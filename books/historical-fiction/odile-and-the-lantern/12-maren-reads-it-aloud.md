## 12. Maren Reads It Aloud

The book fit inside Ivo's coat the way a secret ought to: flat against her ribs, warm from the press, smelling faintly of linseed and the tallow candle she'd worked by until the third hour of the morning. She had wrapped it in brown paper and tied it with string like a parcel of buttons, and all the way across the frozen square she kept one hand pressed over it, so that anyone watching — and someone was always watching now — would think she was simply a girl with a cough.

The lantern workshop stood at the crook of the river road, where the town bent its back against the water. Maren's father had built it sixty years ago, and every winter since, the windows had glowed with the festival lanterns: paper and horn and wire, each one holding a small stubborn flame against the dark. This year there were fewer lanterns than Ivo remembered from childhood. Some of the hooks along the eaves hung empty, and nobody had said why out loud, because everyone already knew.

She knocked twice, then once more, the little rhythm they'd fallen into without ever agreeing on it.

Maren opened the door with solder smoke still clinging to her sleeves. She was a broad woman made broader by aprons, with forearms that could bend an iron frame back into true and hands nicked in a dozen places from years of trimming tin. Her hair was going grey at the temples in two clean wings, as though even her body were precise about things.

"You look," she said, "like a woman who hasn't slept."

"I've slept some."

"Liar. Come in before the frost does."

* * *

Inside, the workshop was warm in the particular way only lantern shops are warm — the heat of small fires tended patiently, over years. Lanterns in every stage of becoming hung from the ceiling beams: wire skeletons, papered bodies, finished ones glowing softly even in daylight because Maren never quite trusted the sun to do the job properly. The workbench ran the length of the room, littered with snips, spools of wire, jars of glue gone amber with age.

Ivo had been coming here since midwinter, first to help rehang the festival lanterns after the storm tore half of them down, then for no reason she could name except that the workshop was the one place in Vellmuth where silence didn't feel like surrender.

They had a whole language of not-talking between them now. Maren would hand her a lantern to hold steady; Ivo would hold it steady. Maren would mutter at a stubborn joint; Ivo would pass the mallet before she asked. Once, three weeks ago, Maren had paused in her work, looked at the empty hook nearest the window, and said only, "That was Tam's." And Ivo had said nothing, and handed her the next length of wire, and that had been the entire conversation, and it had been enough.

Today Maren looked at the brown-paper parcel under Ivo's arm and raised one eyebrow.

"Buttons?"

"Something like that."

"Mm." Maren wiped her hands on her apron with great ceremony. "I've known you four months, Ivo Eastwick. In four months you've lied to me exactly twice, both times about buttons. Sit down. Tea first, or whatever it is?"

"Whatever it is first."

Maren poured something from a dented kettle that tasted of rosehips and possibly iron, and Ivo sat with the parcel on her knees while the fire ticked, and found that her rehearsed speeches had all evaporated. So instead she simply untied the string.

"It's a children's book," she said. "That's what it looks like. That's what it has to look like."

Maren took it the way she took everything — firmly, without fuss. She turned it over. The cover board was plain grey, and pasted to it was a woodcut Ivo had cut herself, working nights: a lantern hanging in a window, its light falling in long rays, and beneath it, in letters rounded and friendly and utterly innocent:

**ODILE AND THE LANTERN**
*A Book of Rhymes for Little Ones*

"Odile?" said Maren.

"My grandmother's name. Nobody will ask questions about a grandmother."

"Hm. My grandmother was called Grieta and she bit a tax collector. Books about grandmothers would be dangerous indeed." Maren opened the cover.

Ivo watched her read the first rhyme. She had read it so many times herself that she could no longer see it fresh, but she knew what Maren saw: a nonsense verse about a girl named Odile who carries a lantern through a winter fair, counting stalls and snowmen and dogs. Twelve lines, singable, the kind of thing a mother might chant to a child on the walk home. Harmless as bread.

But if you knew where to look — if you counted the stalls, and matched them to the grain requisitions of the twelfth of October — Odile's little market inventory became a ledger. *Twelve sacks of barley, eleven sacks of rye, ten fat geese, nine good hens* — and the army's own receipt, which Ivo had copied in her neat clerk's hand, said exactly the same, item by item, date by date. Every plate hid its account inside its counting rhyme. Every rhyme scanned sweetly enough to be sung.

Maren read it twice. Then she looked up, and her face had changed — not dramatically, not the way faces change in plays, but the way ice changes when the sun first touches it. Something shifting underneath.

"How many of these are there?"

"Forty-one copies. It's slow work. The old press in the cellar is older than my father and twice as temperamental."

"And who else has seen it?"

"No one. You're the first."

Maren was quiet a moment. Outside, a soldier's boots crunched past on the river road, unhurried, bored, and both women sat perfectly still until the sound faded, which told Ivo everything about how far the occupation had reached into the ordinary business of sitting in a warm room.

Then Maren turned the page.

* * *

Ivo had meant to warn her.

She had planned it on the walk over — *tell her gently, tell her there are names in it, give her time* — but the plan had dissolved somewhere between the door and the tea, and now Maren was reading aloud in the voice she used for rhymes, half-singing really, the way she probably sang to the neighbor's children when they came to watch the lanterns being made:

*"Oh, Odile walked by the water,
where the winter willows lean,
and she counted seven lanterns
hanging where the boats have been—"*

She stopped.

The workshop went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to hold its breath.

Seven lanterns. Hanging where the boats had been. And underneath, worked into the counting like thread into cloth, the numbers that were not numbers: the seventh day of the month, the seventh hour, the seventh man taken from the ferry landing in November, in front of witnesses who were later encouraged to forget.

Maren's thumb moved across the page. Slowly. Like touching something hot and not able to stop.

"There's more," Ivo said quietly. "If you want it. You don't have to—"

"Read it," Maren said. Her voice was steady in the way that cost something. "Read me the rest."

So Ivo read it. The rest of the rhyme unspooled: six geese on the water, five bells in the evening, and then the lines that weren't about lanterns at all unless you knew — *four names the winter swallowed, three days the ferry stayed* — and then the last stanza, which Ivo had rewritten eleven times because it had to scan perfectly, perfectly, because a rhyme that stumbled would be read twice, and a rhyme that flowed would be memorized and carried away in the head like a tune, and no search could confiscate what a person remembered:

*"And Odile lit her little flame
against the cold and dark,
for a name that's written down in ink
is a name that leaves its mark."*

Silence.

Maren set the book down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when their hands aren't trustworthy. Then she turned away toward the window, toward the row of lanterns and the empty hook beside them, and her shoulders began to shake.

Ivo didn't go to her. She wanted to — every part of her wanted to cross the room and put an arm around those broad shaking shoulders — but she had learned from her father, who had learned it from forty years of customers weeping over printed things, that some grief wants witnesses, not rescue. So she sat, and waited, and let the workshop hold them both.

When Maren finally spoke, her voice was wrecked and furious and clear all at once.

"The eighth of November." She wasn't asking. "They told us he deserted. They came here — two of them, polite as priests — and they said Tamlin Voss had deserted his post and fled south, and that there would be no compensation, no inquiry, because a deserter forfeits his name in the record. Forfeits his *name*, as if they'd lent it to him." She laughed, a terrible bright sound. "He was seventeen. He ferried people across the river, Ivo. That was his post. He couldn't desert a boat."

"He didn't desert anything," Ivo said. "It's in the rhyme. The seventh hour, the ferry landing. Two soldiers and a corporal, the eighth of November. I got the date from the garrison duty roster — Vane signs off on it himself, every week, his signature's on the order that took your son off that landing."

Maren turned around. Her eyes were red and dry now, which somehow was worse, and she looked at Ivo the way she looked at a lantern frame that had come back from the fire bent — assessing whether it could be made true again.

"Say that again."

"His signature. On the order. I've seen it. I can print it."

For a long moment Maren just breathed. Then she crossed the workshop, took the book off the bench, and held it up to the light of the nearest lantern, tilting it so the page glowed, and Ivo realized she was doing exactly what any mother in Vellmuth would do with a picture-book: checking it was well-made. Checking it would survive small hands. Checking it was worth keeping.

"This," Maren said, "is going to get you killed."

"Probably."

"And me."

"That's why I came to you second, and not to anyone else."

Maren snorted — actually snorted, wetly, through the wreckage of her composure — and something in the room loosened. "Second," she repeated. "Flatterer. Who was first?"

"My father. He inked the press himself. His hands shook the whole time, and he did it anyway." Ivo hesitated, then gave her the truth whole, because this woman had earned it. "He printed a suppressed pamphlet once, thirty years ago. About the flood levy. They broke his brother for it. Uncle Piet — my father doesn't say his name, but it's in the type case, the original sorts, hidden under false bottoms. He gave them to me last week. He said—" She stopped, and heard her father's voice saying it, mild as ever, as if discussing weather. "He said, *lead remembers longer than men do.*"

Maren set the book down and looked around her workshop — at the lanterns, at the hooks, at the empty hook by the window — and Ivo watched an idea take shape behind her eyes with the same patient competence she brought to everything else.

"Every house in this town hangs a lantern in winter," Maren said slowly. "Even now. Even with half the hooks empty. Especially now. Do you know how many lanterns I repair in a season? Do you know how many come back to me in spring, cracked, water-stained, needing new paper?" She tapped the cover of the book. "Paper goes out of this shop every week, Ivo. Into houses. Under floors. Inside walls. Nobody searches a lantern-maker. We're furniture. We're *festive.*"

Ivo felt her heart begin, very carefully, to hope — which was the most dangerous feeling she'd had all winter.

"You're offering—"

"I'm offering the obvious. Copies leave from here. Wrapped as lantern paper, packed in repair orders, delivered to houses that owe me a visit anyway. Widow Brandt on Tanner's Row, the Kessler sisters, old Hobb who pretends he can't read but reads everything — half the town owes me a lantern and I intend to collect." She was pacing now, tin snips forgotten, arms moving as she counted. "And not just Vellmuth. The bargemen. The ferrymen. Tam's friends, the ones they didn't take. A book goes downstream in spring same as anything else does, if it's packed right."

"Maren." Ivo said it gently. "What happened to Tam — if they find these here, they won't be polite as priests the second time."

Maren stopped pacing. She picked up the empty hook's neighbor, a finished lantern with horn panes and a brass cap, and turned it in her hands, and for a moment she was silent.

"Do you know what I did the night they told me he'd deserted?" she said. "I went to the garrison office with his ferry logbook. His actual logbook, every crossing in his own hand, and I put it on the counter and I said, *here is proof.* And the clerk looked at me with such pity, such gentle, administrative pity, and he said, *madam, the ledger is the record. Paper proves what the ledger says it proves.*" She set the lantern down with a click. "They taught me that day what paper is worth. So now I'm going to teach them."

* * *

They worked out the details over the dregs of the rosehip tea, and it was strange, Ivo thought, how quickly terror becomes logistics. Forty-one copies existed; Maren would take twelve, wrapped in waxed lantern-paper inside three repair orders, starting with Widow Brandt because the widow had a loud voice and no fear and a kitchen where people gathered. Ivo would keep printing — the cellar press, the nights, the cracked forme her father swore could be coaxed along if she spoke to it nicely. New copies would come to the workshop disguised as returns for repair, and repaired lanterns would go out carrying their cargo of rhyme.

"One rule," Maren said, holding up a scarred finger. "You don't deliver anywhere near the garrison. Not ever. If they take you, I know nothing, you're a customer, and this book is your grandmother's recipe collection."

"She bit a tax collector, my grandmother."

"Perfect. Consistent."

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, two grown women solemnly shaking hands over treason among the lanterns, and then Maren did something Ivo hadn't expected. She reached up to the beam above the bench and took down a small lantern Ivo had noticed before and never asked about — plain tin, dented, older than the others, its glass fogged with age.

"Tam's first attempt," she said. "He was nine. It leaks like a sieve and the hinge's wrong and I've kept it lit every winter since." She lit it now, from the stub of a candle, with hands that were almost steady. "You'll take it home. Hang it in your shop window."

"Maren, a lantern from this shop in the printer's window—"

"—is a lantern from this shop in the printer's window," Maren said, "and nothing else, because half the town has one and nobody thinks twice. But *you'll* know what it means. When the press fights you at three in the morning and you want to stop — and you will want to stop — you'll look at it and remember whose name is in your book, and how many more names there are, and that they're waiting." She pressed it into Ivo's hands. Its warmth sank straight through her gloves. "Grief keeps badly alone, Ivo. It spoils. Turns to poison or to stone. But shared out—" She gestured at the workshop, at the forty-one copies, at the whole ridiculous fragile scheme. "Shared out, it keeps the whole town lit."

Ivo carried the lantern home through the blue dusk, holding it against the book under her coat, and it seemed to her that the streets of Vellmuth had changed since morning: every window she passed had a hook above it, most of them empty, all of them waiting, and she caught herself counting them the way Odile counted stalls — one, two, three — and understanding, with a thrill that was equal parts joy and dread, that she had stopped writing a book and started building a network, one grief at a time.

Her father met her at the cellar door, wiping his hands, and his clouded eyes went straight to the lantern.

"From Maren's shop," he said. Not a question. News traveled strangely fast between the two oldest trades in town.

"Yes. Papa — she's in. Twelve copies, this week. And she says the bargemen—"

He held up a hand, gentle, and tilted his head toward the street. Down the lane, two soldiers were making their slow rounds, lantern-checking, door-checking, bored and thorough. Ivo stood still in the doorway, the contraband warm against her ribs, and waited for them to pass.

When they had gone, her father leaned close and said, very quietly, with the ghost of a smile:

"Before you tell me the rest — you should know a rider came at noon. Garrison courier. There's to be a proclamation posted tomorrow." He paused, and the smile faded into something older and colder. "Announcing that when the ice breaks, the historians are coming. To compile the final record."
