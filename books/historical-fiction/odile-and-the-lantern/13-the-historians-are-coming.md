## 13. The Historians Are Coming

The news came, as most news came that winter, by way of the bakery.

Ivo had gone down for the morning loaf — a small errand she'd claimed for herself since the occupation began, partly because her father needed the walk and partly because the bakery was where Vellmuth did its thinking. The ovens kept people honest. It was very difficult to lie convincingly while holding something warm.

Frau Beckel was already holding court behind her counter when Ivo pushed through the door, stamping snow off her boots. The baker's flour-white arms were folded, and her face wore the particular expression of a woman who has been waiting all morning for an audience.

"There she is," Frau Beckel announced to the room at large, as though Ivo were a delivery she'd been expecting. "The printer's girl. Come to hear it straight."

"Hear what straight?"

"The commission." Frau Beckel lowered her voice with the delicacy of a stage actress who has learned that a whisper carries further than a shout. "The historians. They're coming from the capital. When the ice breaks."

Someone near the window made a sound — not quite a word, more the noise a town makes when it swallows.

"Historians," repeated Herr Ostrow, the clockmaker, from his corner by the stove. He said it the way other men said *plague* or *taxes*.

"That's what my cousin's boy heard, down at the customs house," Frau Beckel continued, warming to it now, kneading the air as if the rumor itself required proofing. "A whole commission of them. Scholars. Men with ink on their fingers and letters after their names. They're going to write the official account of the occupation. Of everything." She wiped her hands on her apron. "And they'll take their record from the ledger. Our ledger. The one the captain has."

Ivo stood very still among the elbows and coats, and felt the bread in her hands go suddenly heavy, as if it too understood its new significance.

"When?" she asked.

"When the river opens," said Frau Beckel. "That's the word. They come with the first barges. Everything settled before spring is over. Every name written down and *finished*." She drew the word out like thread. "Sealed, my cousin's boy says. Sealed with wax. After that, whatever the ledger says is simply what happened. Forever."

*Forever,* Ivo thought, walking home through the falling snow, the loaf clutched to her chest like a small warm heart. *They're coming to make forever.*

* * *

Her father was at the big press when she got back, squinting at the type bed with his face close enough to fog the metal. His eyes had gotten worse again — the winter light did that, or so he claimed, though Ivo suspected the winter light was only the latest in a long line of excuses his eyes had offered him for forty years of stubbornness.

"Papa," she said. "Put the paper down. There's news."

He listened without interrupting, which he almost never did, and when she finished he sat down slowly on the stool by the composing stick, the one that creaked in a note like a low F and which he refused to let her fix because, he said, a man should know when he's sitting down.

"So," he said. "When the ice breaks."

"When the ice breaks."

"And how long does an ice break take? In your professional judgment."

Ivo looked at him. He was smiling, but it was the thin kind of smile, the kind he wore like a coat against weather.

"Three weeks," she said. "Four if the frost holds. The old-timers say this winter's been slow to loosen. But Papa—" She stopped, started again. "They'll read the ledger. All of it. And they'll write it into the record, and then it will be *true*. Not just true here, not just true for us. True everywhere. In the schools, in the histories. A hundred years from now some child will learn that the Aldring family never existed, and no one will ever tell her different, because everyone who could have will be—"

"Dead, or quiet, or both," her father finished. He rubbed his jaw. "Yes. I know the arithmetic."

"Do you think Vane knows they're coming?"

"I think Captain Vane has known since before we did. Men like that receive their invitations early." He turned back toward the press, and Ivo saw his hand rest on the frame the way you'd touch a horse's neck. "Well. Then we have until the river opens."

"Until the river opens *what*?" she asked, though her stomach already knew.

"Everything, love. Everything there is." He looked up at her, and even through the blur of his failing sight she could feel the weight of his attention, the way she always could — her father saw her better than anyone, blurry as he was. "We've been printing a book, haven't we. Quietly. Page by page, like mice building a cathedral. Perhaps it's time we stopped being mice about the schedule."

* * *

Time, Ivo discovered, had a sound.

She had never noticed it before. Time had always been something that happened to other people — her father aging, the town changing, wars arriving in distant provinces and then, abruptly, at the door. But now, in the weeks after the bakery, time became audible everywhere. It dripped.

It dripped from the eaves of the shop each afternoon when the sun found the south-facing roof and the icicles loosened, one bright drop at a time onto the frozen gutter. *Tik. Tik. Tik.* Like a slow press counting impressions.

It dripped inside the house, in the walls, where the pipes her grandfather had laid — badly, her father always said — began to groan and shift as the frost retreated from around them.

And it dripped in Maren's workshop, where Ivo went twice a week now, ostensibly to help with lantern frames and actually to sit in the warm smell of tin and tallow and be, for an hour, a person instead of a secret.

Maren had heard the news too. Everyone had. The town wore it like a stone in a shoe.

"They say the scholars are famous men," Maren said, soldering a seam with the steady hands that had once, Ivo knew, steadied a son. "Men whose books are taught. Imagine that. Your whole life read by a stranger and put in a chapter." She lifted her mask, blew on the joint. "My Tam would have liked that, once. To be in a book." Her voice didn't waver; it had gone past wavering months ago, into the flat country beyond. "Instead he's a rhyme in a primer. 'The little lantern crossed the stream.'" She set down the iron. "Though I suppose that's a kind of chapter."

"It's a better one," Ivo said fiercely, and Maren gave her the ghost of a smile, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the eaves outside dripped their patient count.

"How many pages are done?" Maren asked at last. "Of the true ones."

"Nine spreads set. Seven printed clean. The lantern plates take twice as long as ordinary blocks — Papa insists on the old cross-hatching, and his hands aren't—"

"Your father's hands are fine," Maren said. "His pride is what's shaking. Let him have both." She turned the lantern she was mending, catching the light through its horn panes. "How many books do you mean to loose before they seal the record?"

Ivo had done the sums a dozen times, on scraps she burned afterward. "Forty, if the paper holds. Forty copies, hidden in five hundred harmless primers. Enough that they can't burn them all. Enough that one reaches somewhere wet and green and far from here."

"And the commission arrives when?"

"When the ice breaks."

Maren nodded slowly. "Then you'd best pray for a late thaw," she said, "and work as if you won't get one."

* * *

The thaw, treacherously, obliged them by being late — and then punished them by beginning anyway, in its own sweet time, indifferent to deadlines human or otherwise.

February leaned into March. The river, which had lain under its white lid all winter like a held breath, began to mutter. Ivo heard it at night through the floorboards: long groans from the channel, cracks that ran out like rifle shots across the flats, the deep shifting complaint of a thing too large to hurry and too old to be hurried by anything, least of all history.

In the shop, they worked like people possessed, which Ivo supposed, in the gentlest sense, they were.

Her father took over the press entirely now — she'd stopped arguing about his eyes, because he'd solved the problem his own way, working by touch, reading the type with his fingertips the way a blind man reads a page. "Forty years at these cases," he said when she marveled at it. "I know this alphabet better than I've ever known your mother's face, God forgive me, and hers was worth ten alphabets." He inked, he pulled, he stacked the damp sheets with the tenderness of a midwife. His tremble, which Ivo had feared, seemed to vanish when he worked, as though the press lent him its own stability.

Ivo set type. She cut new sorts for the names — she had begun cutting the names individually now, giving each disappeared person their own small leaden body, their own weight in the case. The Aldrings. Tam Osterhale. The ferryman's twin daughters, gone eleven months and unexplained. Each letter of each name got her full attention, got filed and spaced as carefully as if it were going into a king's coronation oath, because, she thought, it was going into something nearly as permanent.

Once, setting Tam's name, she caught herself talking to it. *"Hold still," she whispered to a stubborn lowercase h.* "You've held still long enough. One more page and you can move."

She decided not to mention this to anyone.

The proclamations still had to be printed, of course. The occupation did not pause for literature. That week's order was a notice on grain quotas, decorated with the army's eagle and a border of printed wheat so idealized it bore no resemblance to anything that had grown in Vellmuth soil in living memory. Ivo set it in her neatest hand and pulled every impression clean, and between notices she set rhymes, and the rhymes carried names, and the two kinds of truth passed through the same press within minutes of each other, and she tried not to think about which one the ink preferred.

On the twelfth of March, Captain Vane sent a note.

Not a summons — a note, courteous as ever, written in that upright, unhurried hand she'd seen on the requisition orders. *The Commission for the Historical Record will arrive with the first navigation. The printing office will be called upon to produce commemorative materials. Please ensure the shop is in good order. — A.V.*

"Good order," her father read aloud over her shoulder, breathing on his spectacles and putting them on and taking them off again, his three-way method of seeing. "What do you suppose constitutes good order, in the captain's estimation?"

"An empty cellar," Ivo said.

"Mm. Then we shall have to disappoint him. He strikes me as a man who collects disappointments the way other officers collect medals." He tapped the note. "He signs everything, you know. Did you ever notice? Every requisition, every alteration, every order — his signature, large as life. Most officers delegate their signatures. Not Vane. He wants his name on things."

"Why would a man want that?"

"Because he believes the record will remember him kindly," her father said, "and he intends to hold it to the promise."

* * *

That night the river woke the whole town.

Ivo came up out of sleep into a sound like a giant turning over in bed beneath the earth — a long, grinding exhalation, followed by a crack that ran up through the foundations and rattled the type cases in their racks. Down the street, dogs began, then stopped, then thought better of it.

She dressed and went to the window. Moonlight lay on the rooftops, and beyond them, above the line of the warehouses, she could see the pale bulk of the river ice, and — she pressed her forehead to the cold glass — she could swear she saw it move. A shudder passing downstream, like something swallowing.

Her father appeared beside her, wrapped in his blanket, his hair standing up in the way it did when sleep abandoned him mid-argument.

"Hear that?" he said.

"The whole street heard it."

"That wasn't the ice cracking." He smiled, and in the moonlight the smile looked young and old at once. "That was a deadline. Walking toward us."

Below in the dark, the eaves dripped. *Tik. Tik. Tik.* Faster than they had in January. Ivo counted without meaning to, and made herself stop, and couldn't.

* * *

Two days later, Tomas came to the back door.

This was unusual. Their arrangement was strictly neutral-ground — the smuggler did his business at the tannery steps, cash and paper changing hands with the impersonality of a bank. Him appearing at the print shop's rear entrance, hat in hand, glancing up and down the alley before knocking, meant either very good news or very bad news, and his face suggested he hadn't yet decided which.

"Word from downriver," he said, without coming in. "Barge pilots. The commission's traveling heavy — six boats, clerks, soldiers, a portable strongbox for the documents. They mean to compile on the water and seal at the capital." He paused. "Also, the pilot says the ice at the bend will go within the fortnight. Warm rain upcountry. Big melt."

"A fortnight," Ivo said.

"Maybe less. Maybe the river surprises everyone and goes tonight." Tomas shrugged, and for once there was no calculation in it, only a tired honesty. "Look, printer-girl. I move goods. I don't move sermons. But whatever you've got that needs to be on those barges — you'd best decide soon what it weighs, and who carries it, and what happens to them if it goes wrong." He put his hat back on. "Because after the sealing, there's no unsealing. Not for a hundred years. I've seen records sealed before. Places forget they ever argued with themselves."

He left through the alley, and Ivo stood in the doorway a long moment, listening.

The river was talking to itself out there, deep and restless, rehearsing. And along the eaves of the shop, in the gray afternoon, the meltwater kept its small relentless arithmetic — *tik, tik, tik* — each drop an impression pulled, each drop a name set, each drop one tick closer to the day the strangers in fine coats would open her town's ledger and write forever in it.

She went inside, washed the ink from her hands — it never fully came off; it lived in the creases now, a second skin — and sat down at the case where Tam Osterhale's name waited in its galley, half a spread from finished.

"Fortnight," she said aloud to the empty room. "Right. Then we don't sleep much."

From the cellar, faintly, came the creak of her father's stool, the low F note, and beneath it the soft regular breathing of the press, waiting to be fed.
