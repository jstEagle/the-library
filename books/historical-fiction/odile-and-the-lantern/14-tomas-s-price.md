## 14. Tomas's Price

Tomas arrived the way weather arrives in Vellmuth in late winter — announced by nothing but a change in the air, and impossible to keep out once he'd decided to come.

Ivo heard him before she saw him: the particular scrape of his boots on the alley cobbles behind the shop, a rhythm she had learned to distinguish from a soldier's march or her father's shuffle. Three uneven steps, a pause, three more. He walked like a man who had spent his life on boat decks that moved under him and never fully trusted solid ground again.

She set down her composing stick, wiped the ink from her fingers — an old habit; Tomas noticed hands, and hands told stories whether you meant them to or not — and went to unbolt the cellar door.

"You're early," she said.

"I'm always early." He ducked under the lintel, a lean man wrapped in a coat that had once belonged to someone broader, with a scarf wound around his neck so many times it gave him the silhouette of a man being slowly strangled by his own caution. "It's the late ones who end up in ledgers."

He said things like that. He'd been saying them for six weeks now, ever since their first transaction, and Ivo still hadn't decided whether they were jokes or invoices. Probably both. With Tomas everything was probably both.

"Paper," she said.

"Paper." He settled onto the upturned crate he always claimed as his own, the one with the wobbly leg he refused to let her fix because, he claimed, the wobble kept him honest. He produced the bundle from inside his coat with the tenderness of a man presenting a grandchild. "Thirty sheets. Good stock — mill overrun from upriver, no watermark anyone will look twice at. And four reams of the cheaper stuff for packing pages between, since you insist on pretending you're running a legitimate bindery down here."

"I am running a legitimate bindery."

"You're running," Tomas said, unwinding half a turn of scarf so his mouth could operate, "a children's book of lantern rhymes that makes grown men learn to read very carefully." He said it lightly, the way you'd comment on the weather, and watched her the way you watch ice.

Ivo kept her face pleasant and empty, the way she kept it when officers came into the shop. It was a skill she'd refined over months now, this face, and it was getting good exercise. "It sells well. Children like lanterns."

"Mm," said Tomas. "Children do like lanterns."

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles did, at the arithmetic stage.

"The price has changed," Tomas said.

They had a system. Paper came in, travel passes went out — neat little documents on the shop's finest stock, stamped with a seal Ivo could cut in lead better than the army's own engravers, filled out with names Tomas supplied and dates Tomas chose. She had told herself, in the beginning, that this was simple barter: paper for paper. Ink for ink. The occupation had made every document in Vellmuth a lie anyway; what was one more, and this one let people slip through checkpoints instead of through walls?

But she knew what she was doing. That was the thing about knowing how to print. You couldn't un-know it.

"How much has it changed?" she asked.

"Doubled." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Before you make that face — and it's a very good face, very reproving, your father must be proud — consider the market. There are two more checkpoints on the north road than there were in January. The passes are inspected twice now, sometimes three times, which means my risk has doubled, which means—"

"—which means your price doubles. Yes. I've read the proclamations too. They have a whole section on fair compensation."

"They do not mean the same thing," Tomas said, "but the structure is similar. I admire it, actually. Whoever writes those proclamations understands that a lie holds better if you build it out of true words."

Ivo thought of Captain Aldous Vane's handwriting, the flourishing signature he pressed onto every requisition order like a man signing a dance card. *A lie built out of true words.* She wondered if Vane had ever met Tomas. She suspected they would get along terribly, which was to say perfectly.

"Sixteen passes," she said.

"Twenty-four."

"Eighteen, and I'll cut the seal finer. Your last batch got a second look at the ferry crossing. I heard about it."

That landed. She watched it land — the small shift in his shoulders, the recalculation behind his eyes. Information was currency in Vellmuth these days, and she had just paid him some, and hated herself a little for how naturally the transaction had come.

"Nineteen," Tomas said, "and finer seals on all of them, and we never speak of the ferry crossing again."

"Nineteen."

They shook on it. His hand was dry and quick, a hand that touched things briefly so as not to leave prints.

* * *

He didn't leave, though. That was the new part.

Usually he took his passes and vanished back into the town's underside like a fish going deep. Today he stayed on his crate, working the wobbly leg with his boot heel, looking around the cellar with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory.

The press stood against the far wall — her father's old second press, the one from before, dragged down here years ago and left to sleep under canvas. Now its bed was scattered with type, and the drying rack along the wall hung with printed sheets, and the whole cellar smelled of ink and lamp oil and the faint sweet rot of the river seeping through the stones.

"Lantern rhymes," Tomas said.

"Mm."

"For children."

"Mm."

"My sister's boy is seven," Tomas said. "He likes a rhyme. What sort of rhyme is it? Give us one."

"There's one on the rack there if you're curious," Ivo said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because curiosity was a door and she had just unlocked it for a man whose entire profession was walking through unlocked doors.

He rose, crossed to the rack, and lifted a sheet. She watched him read it — really read it, lips moving slightly, the way careful readers move when they've learned that the surface of a text is where lies go sunbathing.

The rhyme was one of hers. *Count the lanterns, one by one / Hang them high till winter's done.* And beneath, worked into the counting, woven through the numbers like thread through cloth: nine names, nine dates, nine small true facts about nine people who had been subtracted from the master ledger as though subtraction were a kind of mercy.

Tomas read it twice. Then he set it down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down something that might be armed.

"Well," he said. "That's a fine primer."

"Yes."

"Very educational. All those names. Children love learning names." He turned around. In the lamplight his face had lost its market-day looseness; underneath it was something older and harder, a riverbed face. "You know what this is."

"I know what it is."

"Do you know what it's *worth?*"

And there it was. Not hidden, not even especially disguised. Ivo had known this conversation was coming for weeks; she had felt it approaching the way you feel a tooth decide, somewhere in the quiet of the night, to become a problem. Tomas dealt in risk and compensation. Everything in Vellmuth was requisitioned eventually — grain, boats, ledgers, loyalties. Why should a smuggler's silence be exempt?

"That depends," she said, "on who's asking."

"Don't play trader with me, girl, you'll lose. You count type for a living; I count men." He sat back down on the crate, and for a moment he looked tired — genuinely tired, in a way that had nothing to do with money. "Captain Vane would give me more than I've earned in ten years of honest smuggling for one sheet of that paper and the name of the hand that printed it. More, and a guarantee, and guarantees are worth more than money right now. You've seen what happens to men without guarantees."

"I've seen what happens to men who sell them."

"Yes," Tomas said. "So have I. That's rather my point." He rubbed his jaw. "Here's my situation, Ivo Eastwick, since nobody in this town tells me anything straight. Vane's men stopped my cousin on the mill road two weeks ago. Asked him questions. Friendly questions, the kind with weights in them. About river traffic, about paper moving after dark, about who buys what from whom. My cousin knows nothing, thank whatever gods listen to liars, but they'll ask again, and next time it won't be my cousin, and my cousin's luck doesn't extend to me."

"So you want more money."

"I want to not hang," Tomas said simply. "Money is how I've been buying that so far. But money's losing value faster than I can earn it. Which leaves information." He looked at her, and his eyes were steady and sorry about it. "Tell me what I'm carrying. Tell me the shape of it. How many copies, where they're meant to go, when the river opens — that's all. Not your name, not your father's press. Just the shape. So that when the day comes that I have to buy my life with something, I have something to sell that isn't *you.*"

"And if the day comes," Ivo said quietly, "that selling the shape isn't enough?"

Tomas was silent a long moment. Somewhere above them, her father's footsteps crossed the shop floor, slow and deliberate, each step a small negotiation with the world. The river, under everything, made its low grinding sound, like a giant turning over in sleep.

"Then we'll find out together what I'm made of," Tomas said. "I'd rather not find out. But I'd also rather not promise you a wall and deliver a door. A man who promises walls ends up hanged for doors." He stood, wound his scarf back up to full strangulation capacity, and tucked the nineteen passes into his coat. "Think about it. You're a printer; you understand debt. This is a debt. And debts compound."

"Everything compounds," Ivo said. "That's what the occupation taught us."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at the drying rack, at the innocent-looking sheet with its lanterns and its buried dead.

"One thing for free," he said. "Since you paid me true at the ferry. The commission's clerks arrived yesterday. Four of them, lodging at the Custom House, young men with clean cuffs. They've been sent ahead of the historians to prepare the ledger for review." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Preparing a ledger means deciding what stays in it. So whatever you're printing, girl — and I still don't officially know — you'd best print faster."

Then the door closed behind him, and the cellar was just a cellar again, smelling of ink, holding its secrets badly, the way cellars do.

* * *

Ivo climbed the stairs slowly and stood in the dark of the stairwell for a while, thinking.

Her father was at the front bench, squinting at a job of wedding invitations through the thick lens he now wore chained around his neck like a saint's medal. He sensed her before he saw her — fathers of printers develop sonar — and patted the bench beside him.

"Tomas?"

"Tomas."

"Price went up."

"Price went up."

Her father nodded, unsurprised, and continued setting a capital T. "Everything does, this time of year. Even honesty." He fitted the letter into its place with a click. "What did he ask for?"

Ivo hesitated. Six weeks of secrecy sat in her throat like a swallowed coin. But her father had given her the cellar press. Her father had inked the first page with shaking hands and said nothing about why his hands shook. There were debts in this family too, and they compounded in the other direction.

"He wants to know the shape of the book," she said. "How many copies. Where they're going. He says he wants it so he can sell something smaller than me, if it comes to that."

Her father considered this, the way he considered a crooked line of type: patiently, professionally, without panic.

"And what did you tell him?"

"Nothing yet."

"Hm." He pulled the composing stick toward him and began on the next line. "Your grandfather lent money to a man once, during the last bad winter. Never asked what it was for. Man turned out to be buying guns. Your grandfather always said the mistake wasn't lending — it was not asking, and then pretending he'd been generous instead of afraid." He squinted at the stick. "Ask questions, Ivo. Even of men you trust. Especially of men you trust. A question costs nothing, and it teaches you what a man flinches at."

"What do you flinch at?" she asked.

He smiled, faint and dry, still reading his type by touch. "Lately? Very little. Old men have already lost most of what fear can take." He set another letter. "Go on. Count your copies. Decide what Tomas may know. And then come to bed — you've got that look, the one your mother used to call 'the arithmetic face.' Nothing good was ever solved by it after midnight."

* * *

But she didn't go to bed. She went down to the cellar with a candle and a slate, and she did the thing she was best at in all the world, the thing no one noticed: she counted.

Two hundred and forty sheets remained. At eight pages a copy, with wastage, that was fifty-one finished books — fifty-one lanterns, fifty-one sets of names, hanging in the dark waiting for someone to count them. Fifty-one was not enough. She had known it wasn't enough since the night Maren read her son's name aloud in a voice like something breaking open, and had begun, in the way of people who cannot stop a flood, building sandbags: names of clerks' wives who might read, barge captains who owed old favors, a schoolmistress, a ferryman, two soldiers' letters home that passed through obliging hands.

The list ran to nearly two hundred now. Two hundred readers, and paper for fifty-one books.

And Tomas wanted to know the shape of the distribution. And Tomas's cousin had been questioned on the mill road. And four clean-cuffed clerks were at the Custom House tonight, deciding what stayed in the ledger, and the ice on the river was groaning louder every night, audible now even from her bedroom, a countdown written in water.

Trust, she wrote on the slate, just to see it in her own hand. Then, beneath it: interest. Then, beneath that, because she was her father's daughter and could not help herself, a small column of figures that meant nothing and everything: what Tomas knew, what Tomas guessed, what it would cost him to sell her, what it would cost him not to.

The columns wouldn't balance. That was the whole trouble with trust. It was the only currency in Vellmuth that you spent without ever seeing what you bought, and the only one whose price could change overnight, and the only one that, once counterfeit, could never be recalled.

She thought of Tomas's face when he read the rhyme — the way he'd set it down gently, like something armed. He hadn't asked for the book itself. He'd asked for the shape of the danger around it. A man planning to sell her outright would have asked different questions. Wouldn't he? Or was that exactly what a clever seller would want her to think, the way a forged pass was printed on the finest stock precisely so it would pass the finest inspection?

She wiped the slate clean. Then, because indecision was a luxury the river wouldn't allow, she wrote one line at the bottom:

*Tomorrow: give Tomas the shape of the routes, and nothing else.*

Routes, not destinations. Numbers, not names. Enough truth to keep him honest, not enough to hang anyone but her. It wasn't generosity. It wasn't wisdom. It was the best bargain available, and in Vellmuth that winter, the best bargain was all any of them had.

She blew out the candle. Above her, muffled by floorboards, the river ground and shifted, and somewhere out in the frozen dark a crack ran a hundred yards downstream with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Three days later, she would learn what Tomas had done with the shape she gave him — and it would be neither the betrayal she feared nor the loyalty she hoped for, but something stranger, something that would send her running through the slush to the lantern workshop with her heart going like a press under strain, because Maren's window was dark, and the guards were at the door, and the thaw had finally come for all of them.
