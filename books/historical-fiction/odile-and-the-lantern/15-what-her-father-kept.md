## 15. What Her Father Kept

The knock came at the wrong hour — that was the first thing Ivo noticed. Not a customer's knock, which arrived with the smell of the street and a hand already reaching for the bell, but three slow raps, spaced like a man deciding something between each one.

Her father was in the back room, where he had taken to sitting after supper these past weeks, mending the good chair with glue and string because his eyes could no longer judge a straight seam and he refused, on principle, to admit it. "That's level," he said, when she looked at him over the wobbling armrest.

"It's a spiral," said Ivo.

"Chairs can be spirals."

She sat down across from him. The lamp between them was turned low, the way he liked it now, though she suspected it was less for the sake of his eyes than for the sake of not seeing how much of the room he couldn't see.

"Someone knocked," she said. "Front door. They've gone round the side."

He set down the glue pot with great care, as if it were a sleeping animal. Then he said, in the tone of a man commenting on weather, "It's time you knew about the cellar."

"The cellar has a press in it. I've known about the cellar since I was six. It's where you send me when I'm being annoying."

"You were never annoying. You were sent there because you asked questions faster than I could lie." He rubbed his hands together, and she saw that they were shaking — not the ordinary tremor of age or drink, but something older, something that had been waiting in him for years like water behind a dam. "Not that cellar. The other one."

"There is no other cellar."

"There is now."

* * *

What her father kept was behind the coal store, behind a wall of brick that was not brick but plaster painted convincingly for forty years to look like brick, and behind that — after he worked loose a stone that came away easier than any stone should, as if it had been waiting too — a cavity barely taller than a kneeling man, lined with oilcloth, dry as a sermon.

Inside lay type.

Not the type of the shop above, which was brass-cased and sorted and honest. This was lead, hundreds of pounds of it, wrapped in sacking and waxed paper, and among the sorts — she lifted one to the lamp with fingers suddenly unsteady — letters she did not recognize. A fatter face. An older cut. The serifs worn soft, like teeth of a man who had laughed all his life.

"That's the Grellet face," her father said. "Cut by old Grellet himself, before your grandfather was born. There's maybe three sets left in the world and two of them are rusting in Vane's requisition warehouse right now." He knelt, grunting, and unwrapped another bundle with the tenderness men usually reserve for infants. "This is what printed the pamphlets. All of them."

Ivo's mouth went dry. "Pamphlets."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm about to tell you I once stole a horse." He sat back on his heels. In the lamplight his ruined eyes looked almost young. "Sit down. No — kneel. Kneeling's better for this story. Everything important in my life has happened below the waist of a house."

* * *

The story, as her father told it, went like this:

There had been a brother. Odilon Eastwick — called Dill, because a boy named Odilon in a river town will be called nothing else — four years older than her father, quicker, funnier, the kind of man who could talk a customs officer out of his own boots. When the last bad government fell — "not this one, girl, the one before it; governments here are like weather, always the same storm wearing different coats" — the victors of that day had done what victors do. They took the grain. They took the ledgers. And they rewrote.

Dill Eastwick had been a bargeman with a loud opinion. He'd objected, publicly and repeatedly, to the new commission's habit of striking drowned men from the rolls so their widows' pensions could be quietly pocketed. One night in the autumn of — her father gave a year Ivo had never heard attached to anything — Dill was taken from the Salt Anchor inn between one song and the next. No trial. No body. And within a month, no record: the ledger showed no such man had ever been registered in Vellmuth, no barge license in his name, no debt, no kin. Their mother went to the clerk's office eleven times. On the twelfth visit the clerk wept — genuinely, her father said, that was the worst part, the man was crying as he told her the page had never existed — and their mother stopped going to offices forever.

"So your uncle printed pamphlets," Ivo said slowly.

"Dill printed nothing. Dill was gone. *I* printed. Sixteen years old, hands still soft, and angry in a way I didn't have words for yet." He touched the lead type the way you touch a scar. "Your grandfather's shop printed almanacs and wedding notices. But at night, I set the Grellet face, and I printed four hundred copies of a little thing called *A True Account of What Was Done to the Men of the River*, and Tomas's father carried it downstream on the ice barges, and for one winter every clerk from here to the capital had a copy hidden in his desk drawer, and some of them even read it."

"What happened?"

He was quiet long enough that the lamp guttered and steadied. "Two things happened. The first is that they never caught me. Never knew me. That's the whole art, Ivo: not courage — invisibility. A printer who is famous is a dead printer. The second thing—" He exhaled through his nose. "The second thing is that it didn't work. Not then. The commission finished its record, sealed it, shipped it off, and the official history says the winter of that year passed unremarkably. My brother is still not in any book in this country except the ones I made." He tapped the oilcloth bundle. "Except those."

Ivo knelt in the cold secret dark beside him, and understood, with a feeling like the floor tilting, that everything she had thought was hers alone — the fury, the private account kept in her own hand, the plan folded up inside the children's rhymes — had been walked before her, by this stooped man who glued chairs badly and complained about the price of ink.

"Why didn't you ever say?"

"What would I have said? 'Goodnight, Ivo, mind the type, also your Uncle Dill never existed'? You were a child. Then you were a sensible young woman, and sensible people don't need more reasons to be afraid." He smiled, and it crooked exactly the way hers did, which she had never noticed before and would never un-notice again. "Then the army came, and I watched you copying receipts with your jaw set like a woman biting down on a nail, and I thought — ah. There it is. Same jaw. Same stubbornness. It skips generations like a curse."

"I'm not brave," she said. "I'm frightened all the way down."

"Good. Brave people get caught. Frightened people get clever." He began rewrapping the bundles, and his hands had stopped shaking. "But listen to me now, because this is the part I got wrong, and I won't let you inherit my mistakes along with the lead."

* * *

His mistake, he said, was that he had printed once and then waited. He had put four hundred pamphlets on the ice and gone back upstairs to almanacs and wedding notices, telling himself the truth was planted and would grow on its own. Truth, he said, is not a seed. Truth is a fire, and fires need feeding, and he had starved his own.

"They sealed the record because nobody was still printing when the sealing happened. The historians came on a spring barge just like this lot will, and the town had already gone quiet, and quiet is the same as agreement as far as commissions are concerned." He stacked the last bundle and looked at her — at her, directly, though she knew he could only see the shape of her against the lamp. "So whatever you're doing — and don't insult us both by denying it, I found the lantern plates under the flour bin weeks ago, and might I say the woodcuts are lovely — whatever you're doing, don't stop. Print until the ice goes out. Print while they're reading. Keep printing after. That's the whole inheritance. That, and the type."

He pressed the bundle into her arms. It weighed as much as a sleeping child, cold through the sacking, and she held it the way you hold something alive.

"The Grellet face," she said. "Why hide it instead of using it all these years?"

"Because a face that printed sedition once gets remembered. Any clerk worth his salt knows it on sight. Which is why—" and here he grinned, and for a moment he was sixteen again, furious and invisible, "—you'll use it only for the names. Every name of every struck-out soul, set in a face nobody's seen in forty years, buried inside a nursery rhyme. Let the historians squint at it. Let them feel it tickle something they can't place. Old type has a memory, Ivo. So does everyone who ever read it."

They climbed up out of the false cellar into the coal dust and the ordinary dark, and he fitted the stone back into the wall, and smeared it with ash, and stood back admiring a job so seamless that Ivo felt a small grief for the forty years it had fooled her.

At the top of the stairs he paused, one hand on the rail.

"One more thing," he said. "Tomas. Whatever arrangement you've made with that smuggler, whatever he's carrying for you — watch him. His father was honorable. Honorable men don't reliably breed true." He said it lightly, almost as a joke, and patted her shoulder, and went up to bed.

But Ivo stood a long moment in the stairwell with the type heavy in her arms and the words turning over, because tomorrow morning Tomas was due at the shop to collect the third batch of pages, and for the first time since the ice had begun to creak out on the river, she found herself wondering what exactly he was asking about them — and why he'd started asking at all.
