## 16. Ice Cracking at Night

The first sound came sometime past midnight, when the lamps in the shop had burned down to their last finger of oil and Ivo was still awake, listening to the house settle around her like an old man shifting in his chair.

It was not a loud sound. That was what made it terrible. A groan, low and long, as if something enormous beneath the town had turned over in its sleep and found the bed too small. The windowpanes shivered in their frames. The type case nearest the wall gave a faint metallic hum, a thousand pieces of lead whispering against each other, and Ivo sat up in the dark with her heart going like a press run.

*The ice,* she thought. *The ice is going.*

She had lived her whole life beside that river and had heard it crack every spring of them — the great booming reports like cannon fire, the shriek of plates grinding, the groaning she was hearing now — and every year it had meant nothing but spring. This year it sounded like a clock.

*The commission comes when the ice breaks.* Everyone in Vellmuth knew it by now; Vane had made sure of that, posting his notices on every door as though he were announcing a festival. *By order of the Provisional Administration: the Commission of Historical Record shall convene upon the opening of navigation.* As if history were a barge you could schedule.

Another groan, longer this time, rising at the end like a question. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark, then another, and then half the dogs in Vellmuth were barking at a river, which was, Ivo supposed, exactly the sort of thing dogs were for.

She got up and went downstairs in her stocking feet. Her father was already there, standing at the dark front window with his coat over his nightshirt, his face close to the glass though the glass showed him nothing but black water and blacker sky.

"You heard it," she said.

"Whole street heard it." He didn't turn. "Your mother used to say the river talked in its sleep in March. Said if you listened close you could hear it counting."

"Counting what?"

He smiled faintly. "I never stayed out long enough to find out."

They stood together while the ice groaned again — a sound like a door in a very large house being forced — and somewhere along the bank, faintly, came the sound of voices. Other sleepless people. By the time the sky went grey, half of Vellmuth would be standing at the railings looking at the ice as if staring could hurry it.

"I should sleep," her father said, without moving.

"Yes," said Ivo, also not moving.

Neither of them slept. They drank tea instead, standing at the cold stove, and neither mentioned the crates stacked under sacking in the cellar, nor the four hundred copies of *Odile and the Lantern* hidden inside flour sacks and blanket chests across three streets of the town, nor the fact that every groan of the river was one groan closer to the day the historians would open their ledgers and read a town that no longer existed. Some things, once said aloud, became heavier. They kept them where they were.

* * *

By midmorning the whole town had migrated to the banks, the way iron filings migrate to a magnet, or the way gossips migrate to a wedding.

Ivo went with Maren, who had insisted on walking the long way round through the market so she could buy chestnuts "for warmth," which everyone understood to mean for eating, and which cost twice what they should have because the chestnut man had also heard the ice and decided spring justified surcharges.

"He's a thief," said Maren cheerfully, burning her fingers on the paper cone.

"He's an optimist," said Ivo. "There's a difference. Thieves don't smile."

The bank at Coal Steps was packed shoulder to shoulder — fishermen who hadn't fished since autumn, children hanging off the railing posts, old women with folding stools who had clearly done this every year of their lives and regarded the crowd as amateurs. Below them the river lay under its winter lid, white and grey and ridged like a scoured floor, except that now the lid had begun to talk.

Everyone listened. It was the strangest thing about Vellmuth in thaw: two hundred people standing in silence, listening to water.

*Grooooon.* A sound from deep midstream, felt in the boot soles before it reached the ears. A cheer went up from the children. An old fisherman near Ivo shook his head gravely, as though the river had said something disappointing.

"Too slow," he told no one in particular. "Cracking from the banks first. Means a late break. My father always said—"

"Your father always said everything means a late break," said his companion. "You'll be saying it over my grave."

"It *did* break late the year of the mill fire."

"The year of the mill fire it broke early and you've told it both ways so many times even you believe—"

Ivo let their argument wash over her and watched the ice. There was a seam opening along the eastern channel, a black line thin as a hair, widening by inches. Water showed through it, moving. Moving water in March was the most hopeful sight in the world, and she felt it go through her like brandy.

Downstream, she thought. Four hundred books, and more coming off the press each night, drifting into hands she would never see, borne by peddlers and pilgrims and one extremely unreliable smuggler toward towns whose names she only half knew. The ice was holding them all in place like a hand pressed flat on a table. When it broke, everything moved at once — the barges, the grain, the commission, the truth.

Dread and release, braided together like rope. She couldn't feel one without the other anymore. It was, she was beginning to suspect, simply what hope cost now.

"They say the commission arrives within the week of the break," Maren said quietly. Her breath smoked. "Vane's criers have been at it all morning. *Within the week of the opening of navigation.* He does love his phrases."

"He loves anything that sounds inevitable."

"Mm." Maren cracked a chestnut with her thumb, a trick Ivo had never mastered. "And he loves your shop, doesn't he? There are soldiers outside it this morning. Two of them. Very upright. Very bored. One of them has been counting the bricks."

Ivo went still. "Guards?"

"Protection," said Maren, in the tone that put the word in quotation marks. "For the print shops. Didn't you hear? There was a proclamation nailed to the guildhall before dawn. All presses to be placed under guard until the commission has 'secured the administrative record.'" She snorted softly, smoke curling from her nose. "Secured. Like hens."

*Like witnesses,* Ivo thought.

Every printing shop in Vellmuth. Not just hers. Which meant either Vane suspected everyone equally — possible; suspicion was the cheapest thing he manufactured — or someone had told him something. And the short list of someones contained exactly one name, and that name owed her for forty travel passes and had been asking, lately, questions about the book's contents with a little too much interest, the way a man asks questions when he's pricing something.

She pushed the thought down where she kept it, next to the fear, in the drawer that stuck.

"Come on," she said. "Let's look at the ice properly. If we're going to stand here freezing we might as well freeze usefully."

* * *

The proclamation was read aloud from the guildhall steps at noon, by a clerk with a voice like a wet slate, while Captain Aldous Vane stood behind him on the top step looking like a portrait of himself.

Ivo heard it secondhand, from the edge of the market crowd, close enough to see and far enough to slip away. Vane wore his dress uniform, the one with the braid, and had arranged his face into benevolence, which was somehow worse than any expression he'd ever worn naturally. The clerk read: the commission of historians, empowered by the Provisional Administration, would convene in Vellmuth upon the opening of navigation — *within the week*, said the criers, and the crowd murmured — to compile the definitive record of the occupation from official documents, chiefly the master ledger, which had been "maintained with exemplary care."

A ripple went through the crowd at that. Not laughter. Something quieter and more dangerous than laughter — the particular silence of two hundred people all remembering the same dead man marked as a debtor. Ivo felt it pass through her like current. *Exemplary care.* Somewhere in the requisitioned strongroom of the customs house sat a book with her town's name in it, being rewritten entry by entry, and the men who were rewriting it intended to hand it to scholars as gospel.

Then Vane stepped forward, and the crowd quieted the way a room quiets when the wrong person stands up.

"Citizens of Vellmuth," he said, and his voice carried beautifully; he'd have made a fine reader of proclamations himself, or a fine preacher, or a fine actor, all the professions that consist of making falsehoods comfortable. "In six days — perhaps fewer, if the river is generous — distinguished scholars will arrive among you. They come to write down what happened here. I ask you all to assist them. To speak with them freely. To trust, as I trust, that the record will show the occupation in its true light."

His gaze traveled over the crowd, unhurried, and Ivo had the unpleasant certainty, from thirty feet back in a mob of hundreds, that it paused on her for exactly as long as it paused on anyone else, which was to say not at all and completely.

"To that end," Vane continued, "all printing establishments in the town will remain under military protection until the commission's work is concluded. The press is an instrument of great power, citizens." A small smile. "Great power deserves careful keeping."

Beside Ivo, an old woman muttered, "Careful keeping, hah. Same way he kept the grain," and was shushed by her daughter with real fear, and Ivo's chest ached with something that was almost pride and almost terror.

Six days. Maybe fewer. The guards were already at the shop doors — she'd seen the pair outside Eastwick & Son on her walk down, one young and nervous and counting bricks, one older and watching faces, which was the dangerous one. Every hour the river groaned, the deadline shortened, and the cellar press still had eleven pages to go.

She turned for home and nearly walked into Tomas.

He materialized the way he always did, out of crowd-thick air, smelling of wet wool and river mud, with his cap pulled down and his grin pulled up, and the combination of the two expressions made him look like a man apologizing in advance.

"Ivo Eastwick." He fell into step beside her as if they'd arranged it, which they absolutely had not. "Standing at the ice like everyone else. You'd think after twenty winters people would know what thawing looks like."

"People like rituals," said Ivo, not looking at him. "What do you want, Tomas?"

"Want? Can't a man greet a friend in the market?"

"A friend wouldn't have found me in a crowd of four hundred."

"Ah." His grin slipped a notch. "Well. Then let's say a businessman. The passes you owe me for the last shipment — I'll want those tonight. And the next batch, when it comes. And—" he glanced sideways, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Tomas was the most frightening register he owned, "—and I'd take payment in news, these days, same as coin. News keeps its value. Coin doesn't, not with soldiers everywhere."

"What kind of news?"

"Oh," he said lightly, eyes forward, "whatever a printer knows. What she prints. What she hides. Who helps her hide it." He stopped at the corner where their ways parted, tipped his cap, and his face did something complicated — guilt, maybe, or its cousin, self-preservation. "River's going soon, Ivo. Everything moves when the river goes. Best decide what you're carrying before it does."

Then he was gone into the crowd, and Ivo stood in the cold with the chestnut cone going limp in her hand, understanding perfectly well what she had just been shown: a bill, presented politely, with the interest already calculated.

* * *

That night the river shouted.

Ivo was at the press — the old cellar press, her father's first, the one that had printed trouble decades ago and was now printing it again — pulling sheets of the lantern book page by page, when the sound came up through the foundations. Not a groan this time. A report, sharp as a pistol, then another, then a rolling series of them like artillery up the valley, and the lamp flames leaned, and dust sifted from the ceiling joists, and her father, inking the plate with his ruined eyes and steady hands, said without looking up:

"There she goes."

They climbed up into the street. Everyone in Vellmuth was in the street. Nightgowns and coats over nightgowns, lamps held high, the whole town pouring downhill toward the banks like iron filings after all, drawn by the one magnet that had ever mattered here. The ice was talking continuously now — cracks racing along it visible in the lamplight as white seams, splits opening with sounds like doors breaking, and out in the black middle distance, something vast beginning, slowly, majestically, to move.

"It won't go tonight," said the old fisherman, who had followed the crowd like a prophet attached to his congregation. "Banks-first ice holds till the third day. My father—"

"Your father," said three people simultaneously, and even he laughed.

Ivo stood at the railing with her father on one side and Maren on the other, and below them the river worked in the dark, and she felt the braided rope in her chest pull tight from both ends. *Six days.* Guards on the presses. Tomas circling. Eleven pages left, and a cracked platen, and a town full of hidden books waiting for water.

Maren's hand found hers on the railing. Neither of them said anything. Grief and courage had long ago stopped bothering to introduce themselves to each other; they just arrived together now, like old friends.

Out in the dark, the ice boomed, and a seam of open water ran silver from bank to bank, and the whole crowd made a sound — a soft collective exhale, half awe and half relief — as though the river had finally said the thing it had been counting all along.

Ivo counted too, silently, all the way home: pages, hours, coins, risks. And on the shop door, when they reached it, she saw the guard shift his weight and look away, embarrassed, as people do when they catch themselves watching a house too closely — and inside, on the workbench, she would find the note that Tomas had slipped under the door while the whole town stood at the railings:

*A man came asking about you today. Not a soldier. Worse. Come see me tomorrow, or don't come see me at all.*

The river roared in the dark like a promise, and like a warning.
