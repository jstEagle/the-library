## 17. The Betrayal

The knock came at half past four in the morning, which was the hour when knocks stop being knocks and become verdicts.

Ivo heard it from the cellar, where she had fallen asleep at the press again, her cheek printed faintly with the mirror-image of a lantern rhyme. She woke with the words backwards on her skin and did not understand them for a moment — *odile odile light the way* — and then the knocking came again, three blows of a rifle butt against the shop door above, and every word she had ever set flew out of her head at once.

Her father was already moving. That was the thing about him that surprised people who thought blindness made a man slow: he moved like water finding a crack. He pressed the loose board back over the old type, laid the rag over it, and by the time Ivo reached the top of the stairs he was standing in the shop doorway in his nightshirt, blinking like an old man rudely awakened, which he was, and which was also the best performance of his life.

"Soldiers," he said mildly, as if reporting weather. "At this hour. The army's hours have always been a scandal."

There were six of them, and behind them, held by two more, stood Tomas.

She had never seen Tomas look small before. He was a man who filled doorways the way barges fill locks — comfortably, with room to spare, taking up his space in the world as though the world had agreed to it. Now the soldiers had folded him into himself, arms pinned, coat hanging open, and his face wore the particular gray of a man doing arithmetic he is losing.

"Master Eastwick." Captain Vane's voice came from the street, unhurried, pleasant. He did not enter; he preferred thresholds, Ivo had learned. Thresholds were where power lived. "Forgive the hour. We're conducting inquiries."

"At this hour," her father said again, "inquiries ought to be asleep."

One of the soldiers laughed and then stopped laughing very quickly.

Vane stepped into the lamplight. He looked tired, Ivo thought, and that frightened her more than anything else, because tired men make decisions quickly and regret them slowly, if at all. His eyes went past her father, past her, cataloguing the shop — the type cases, the press, the stack of primers drying by the window, innocent as bread.

"We've had a report," Vane said. "Of seditious material passing through this town. A book, they say. A children's book." He smiled at the absurdity of it, inviting them to smile too. "Rhymes. Lanterns. And underneath the rhymes—" he tapped his own temple— "names."

"Children's books are full of names," her father said. "Jack and Jill. Very seditious pair. Always falling down hills."

"Father." Ivo touched his arm.

Vane's gaze settled on her then, and it stayed there a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Miss Eastwick. Your type work on the commemorative broadsheet was excellent. I said so at the time." He let the compliment sit there like bait in clear water. "You'd know, I imagine, what passes through a print shop. What gets printed after hours. What paper goes missing."

"Paper goes missing everywhere," Ivo said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Mice. The river damp. My father blames the mice; I blame the damp; between us we've never once blamed the truth."

Something flickered across Vane's face — not anger. Interest. It was worse.

He turned to the street. "Bring him forward."

They pushed Tomas up onto the step, into the lamplight, and for one long moment he looked at Ivo, and she looked at him, and the whole history of their dealings passed between them: the paper bales, the passes, the questions he had never asked because not asking was the service she paid for.

Then Tomas looked away first.

"The lantern workshop," he said. His voice was flat, scraped clean of everything. "On Tanner's Row. The woman Maren. She's been keeping them there. Copies. Under the floor where the wax is rendered."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way it does in the instant after ice cracks.

"Tomas," Ivo said, and it wasn't a plea exactly, or maybe it was entirely a plea, or maybe it was just his name, which was all she had left to give him.

"Don't," he said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Don't say my name like that. You knew what I was when you hired me. I'm a smuggler, girl. Smuggling's just loyalty for sale, and somebody finally offered a better price than silence." He swallowed. "They have my sister. In the garrison. They showed me her boots." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "Her boots, do you understand? As if to say, we have the rest of her too."

Vane watched this little drama with the patience of a man at theater. Then he inclined his head to Ivo. "You'll want to dress warmly, Miss Eastwick. I believe you'll want to see this."

* * *

They marched them both — her father insisted on coming, and Vane, perhaps sensing that an audience was part of the punishment, permitted it — through streets still blue with pre-dawn, past shuttered windows behind which Ivo could feel the town holding its breath. Frost furred the pump handles. Somewhere a dog gave one bark and thought better of it.

Tanner's Row smelled of beeswax and tallow even in winter, Maren's whole trade baked into the brickwork. The lantern workshop's windows glowed — Maren rose early, always had, said grief slept badly and so might as well be useful — and Ivo's heart broke a little at the sight of that warm light, because warm light was about to become evidence.

They were already inside when the group arrived. Ivo heard the crash of something — a mold, a jar of pigment — and then Maren's voice, high and furious and utterly unafraid:

"—careful, that's forty years of work, you great oxen, you'll answer for every lantern you break if this town ever sees morning again—"

"Maren," Ivo said, and stumbled through her own doorway.

The workshop had been turned inside out. Soldiers pulled boards from walls. One had found the wax-rendering floor and was prying at it with a bayonet, and beneath the flagstone — beneath the place where Maren had hidden them these three weeks, wrapped in oilcloth against the tallow smell — lay the books.

Not many. Twenty-two copies. Ivo counted them as the sergeant stacked them on the workbench, because counting was the only thing her hands knew how to do while the rest of her screamed. Twenty-two. She had printed thirty-one.

*Twenty-two*, she thought, with a coldness that felt like falling. *Nine unaccounted for.*

Maren stood in the middle of her ruined workshop in her shawl, hands bound in front of her with a soldier's belt, and when she saw Ivo she did not cry out or accuse. She looked at her the way she had looked at broken lantern glass a hundred times: assessing what could be mended.

"It's all right," Maren said. To Ivo. In front of all of them. "It's all right, child."

It was not all right. It was the least all-right thing Ivo had ever witnessed, and Maren said it the way she hung lanterns — steadily, at height, in bad weather.

Captain Vane entered last. He picked up one of the seized copies from the bench and turned its pages with genuine, almost scholarly curiosity. "*Odile and the Lantern,*" he read aloud. "Charming. 'Odile, Odile, light the way, / the river keeps what rivers may—'" He paused. Read it again, silently, lips moving. Ivo watched him arrive at the edge of it — the sense that the rhyme scanned too neatly, meant too much — and then watched him set it down, because a man who has decided a book is harmless cannot easily un-decide it in front of his own soldiers.

"A primer," he said. "Hidden under a floor. With the wax." He looked at Maren. "Why hide a children's book, madam?"

"Children's books are precious," Maren said. "Ask any mother."

Vane studied her for a long moment. Whatever he suspected, suspicion was not proof, and proof was what a commission of historians would demand. "Take her," he said instead. "And the books. She'll answer questions when the commission arrives. They'll want witnesses." He said *witnesses* the way other men said *kindling.*

As they led her out, Maren paused beside Ivo. Their shoulders almost touched.

"The festival lanterns," Maren murmured, quick as a pickpocket. "The ones I repaired with you, in autumn. Did you count how many hang on the bridge?"

Fourteen, Ivo wanted to say. Fourteen lanterns on the bridge, and Maren had shown her, weeks ago, that the brackets behind three of them were hollow — had shown her casually, talking of nothing, the way she did everything that mattered.

"I never counted," Ivo said aloud.

"No," Maren agreed. "Sloppy of you." And then they took her out into the blue morning, and the door closed, and Tanner's Row was silent except for the drip of thaw off the eaves, each drop landing like a small clock.

* * *

They walked home through streets going gray-gold with dawn. Her father said nothing until they were inside with the door barred, and then he sat down heavily at the workbench and put his face in his hands, and Ivo understood that the performance was over and the fear had arrived to collect.

"They took twenty-two," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, thin and far away. "We printed thirty-one. Nine are unaccounted for. Three are in the bridge lanterns. Six—" She stopped. Counted again, because arithmetic was the rope she was climbing down. "Six are with the six families. The Kesslers. Old Pieter. The widow Anselm. The ferryman's wife. The two brothers at the mill. Six families who promised."

"And the ninth?" her father asked quietly.

Ivo opened her mouth. Closed it.

The ninth copy was the master — the first perfect impression, the one she had kept wrapped in oilcloth in the cellar, under the loose board, beside their father's forbidden type. She had not given it away. She had not dared. It was the original, the true account, the one with every date and every name and — since two nights ago — the fresh page recording the search itself, the defaced proclamation, her father's struck cheek, all of it set in type while her hands shook.

"It's safe," she said. "It's in the cellar. They didn't find it. They didn't even look — Vane decided the book was a primer, Father, he *decided* it, in front of his own men, and now he can't un-decide it without admitting he missed it."

Her father lifted his head. In the gray light his blind eyes looked toward her with terrible accuracy.

"You're proud," he said. "I can hear it. Don't be. Pride is what the ledger eats first."

"He betrayed us." The fury came up in her then, hot and sudden, scalding her throat. "Tomas. After everything — after I paid him, after I trusted him — he sold Maren for a pair of boots in a garrison window—"

"He sold Maren for his sister," her father said. "Say it properly. There's no virtue in getting the arithmetic wrong."

Ivo turned away. She busied herself at the stove, striking flint, feeding kindling, doing useful violent things to innocent wood. "His sister might not even exist. It might have been a trick. Boots, Father. They showed him *boots.*"

"And if it was a trick?" Her father's voice was gentle, which made it unbearable. "Would you have done differently? Be honest, Ivo. You've been honest with ink your whole life. Be honest with yourself for one minute."

She crouched by the stove and did not answer, because the honest answer was *no, I would have traded Maren too, God forgive me, I would have traded anyone for Da,* and the knowledge of it sat in her chest like swallowed lead type, heavy and poisonous and impossible to cough up.

"That's the occupation's true invention," her father said, to the room, to the years. "Not the altered ledger. Not the proclamations. It's this. It's making neighbors into currency. Every arrest buys another betrayal, every betrayal buys another arrest, and the whole town spends itself." He sighed, an old man's sigh, long-practiced. "Your brother — my brother, Tam Eastwick — was sold the same way. By a man who cried while he did it. Crying didn't help Tam."

Ivo fed another stick to the fire and watched it catch. "What do we do?"

"What printers do." He rose, joints cracking like the river ice. "We print. They took twenty-two copies; we'll make the next run truer and larger. They took Maren; we'll put her arrest in the book itself, tonight, while the ink of it is still wet in the world. They think they've cut the head off the thing." He smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a man remembering a suppressed pamphlet and a lost brother. "But a book isn't a head, Ivo. It's water. You can't arrest water. You can only fail to drink it all."

* * *

She went down to the cellar as the sun came up proper, gold on the frost, ordinary as anything. The loose board was undisturbed. The master copy lay where she had left it, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed.

She unwrapped it and read her own pages — the requisitions, the prices, the names of the dead marked as debtors, and now Maren's name, which she would add tonight: *arrested on the testimony of a smuggler whose price was his sister's freedom, held without charge, charged with nothing, guilty of a children's book.*

Guilt sat with her in the cellar. It did not shout. Guilt never shouts; it itemizes. *You hired him. You paid him in crime. You knew what he was. You spent a corrupt coin and are astonished it bought corruption.* She let it itemize. She had learned that much from the ledger: you cannot argue with an account. You can only balance it.

And the balancing, she thought, looking at the cracked old press her father had offered her, the press that had once printed treason and could again — the balancing was arithmetic too.

Twenty-two copies seized. Nine surviving. One town waking. One commission arriving when the ice broke, hungry for documents, and one document waiting in a cellar that would make their official record a lie with a title page.

They had taken Maren. They had taken the books they could find. They had not taken the type, the press, the paper hidden in the flour barrel, or the nine.

Ivo banked the stove under the cellar kettle, rolled up her sleeves, and began sorting type by feel in the dimness, letter by letter, the way her father had taught her when she was small enough to believe letters were only for spelling.

Outside, the river groaned — a long, low note, like a struck bell underwater — and somewhere on Tanner's Row, in a cell, a lantern-maker was counting the hours until spring.

So, Ivo thought, setting the first capital of Maren's name, was everyone else.
