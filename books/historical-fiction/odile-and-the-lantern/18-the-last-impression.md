## 18. The Last Impression

The cellar had a smell Ivo had known all her life — oil and lead and the particular dustiness of old paper, like the inside of a pocket that has held letters for forty years. Her father called it *the smell of work done properly*. Vane's men would have called it nothing at all. They had searched this room twice and seen only what they expected to see: an old press, a dead trade, a half-blind man's clutter.

That was the whole art of hiding things, her father liked to say. You did not hide them. You let them be boring.

Ivo sorted type by feel in the light of two lanterns hung low, their flames turned down so small they were more memory than light. The cellar was cold enough that her breath showed, but her hands were steady. They had been steady for eleven days now, since the night Maren was taken and Ivo had stood in this same spot and set the first capital of her friend's name into a composing stick.

*The nine.* That was how she thought of them now — the copies Tomas hadn't found because Tomas had never been told about the flour barrel. Nine finished books of *Odile and the Lantern*, each one a children's primer on its face and a true account underneath, names and dates threaded through rhymes about a girl who carried a light through a dark winter town. Nine copies. It sounded absurdly few when she said it aloud, even alone, even to herself.

But nine was not the end of the arithmetic. Nine was only the proof.

Because tonight they were printing the last pages — the ones no rhyme could soften. The full account. Every requisition with its date and tonnage. Every arrest. Every altered entry in the master ledger, copied out in Ivo's careful hand over weeks of pretending to sort receipts, and every one of those entries bearing the same flourish at the bottom, the looping, confident signature of Captain Aldous Vane, who signed for grain the way other men signed love letters.

"You'll want the small caps for the dates," her father said from across the room, without looking up. He was oiling the old press — the cellar press, the one officially sold as scrap metal eleven years ago to a dealer in Corvess who had never existed. "Small caps. Dates read better in small caps. Like whispers."

"Like whispers," Ivo agreed. "Papa, you've said that four times."

"I've said it four times because you ignored me three times." He straightened up, pressing a hand to his back, and grinned at her in the lantern light. His eyes had gone milky at the edges these past years, but his grin was unchanged — the same one he'd worn when she was six and had dropped a tray of sorts and spent an evening sorting *b* from *d* by touch while he told her stories until she stopped crying. "Also, you're standing on my good foot's side of the room, which is theft."

"It's your bad foot's side too. You own the whole room."

"A man can dream of neighbors." He lowered himself onto the bench beside the press and ran his palm along its iron frame the way another man might stroke a horse. "Now then. Let's see if the old girl remembers her manners."

* *

They worked the way they'd always worked, without needing many words. Her father inked; Ivo fed the pages. The rhythm was older than either of them — pull, release, turn, feed — a heartbeat made of iron and grease, and Ivo found, to her surprise, that there was room inside it for something almost like peace.

It didn't last, because peace was not on tonight's schedule.

The first sheet came out clean. She held it to the lantern and read what the town of Vellmuth would read, if any single page survived:

*On the 14th of Frostmonth, one hundred and twelve sacks of barley were taken from the granary of Aldous Beck, who received a receipt signed A. Vane. On the same day, the family of Hessa Brann was struck from the master ledger, all five of them, though four were living when last seen.*

Four living. Ivo had checked. She had walked past the Branns' shuttered house three times before she dared believe it, and the fourth time, a curtain had moved.

"They'll hang us for this," her father said mildly, reading over her shoulder. Not a lament. An observation, in the tone he used for weather.

"Probably."

"Well." He wiped his inky hands on his apron, leaving black handprints like the ghosts of handprints. "Your mother always said we Eastwicks do our best work under threat of hanging. She said it about the pamphlet year."

"The pamphlet year," Ivo repeated. He had finally told her, three nights ago, everything — the suppressed broadsheet of '39, his brother Willem whose name had been scraped off the guild rolls until even the stone mason forgot which grave was his. Her uncle had become a blank space in a record, and blank spaces, Ivo now knew, were how towns died without anyone noticing.

"She'd have liked this book," her father went on, feeding the next sheet. "All the rhymes. She couldn't abide a serious thing that couldn't also be sung."

"Maren will sing it," Ivo said, and then her throat closed, because Maren was in a cell on Tanner's Row and nobody had sung anything on Tanner's Row in a long time.

Her father said nothing, which was his way of saying a great deal. He just pulled the lever, and the press spoke instead — the great wooden groan, the satisfying thump, and then the quiet hiss of the screw, like a long exhale.

* *

By the second hour, the cold had crept from the walls into their bones, and Ivo's humor began to fray at the edges.

"The *g*s are wearing," she complained, holding a sheet up. "Look. Odile's grandmother looks like she's melting."

"Everything melts in spring. It's thematic."

"Papa."

"Fine, fine. There's a spare g in the job case, third row, behind the broken asterisks." He squinted at her sheet. "Although honestly, girl, a melting grandmother might improve the story. Mine never once melted and look how dull she was."

Ivo laughed despite herself — a short, startled sound in the cold cellar, quickly smothered. Sound traveled in this building. Above them, the shop floor creaked sometimes when the wind pushed at the shutters, and every creak was, these days, possibly boots.

But the laugh loosened something, and they fell back into rhythm, and the stack of printed sheets grew: requisitions, arrests, prices of bread month by month climbing like a fever chart. And then the pages she had saved for last, the ones her hands had trembled setting three nights ago:

*Maren Ostra, lantern-maker of Candlewick Lane, arrested on the 3rd of Thawmonth. Her son Joren Ostra, drowned in the mill race on the 9th of Deepwinter while in custody of the occupying garrison, entered in the ledger as a deserter who never existed. The entry altering his record bears the signature A. Vane.*

Every entry bore it. She had counted. Forty-one signatures, each one a small, arrogant sunburst of ink. When the historians came — and they were coming, everyone said so, riding behind the ice-break like crows behind a plow — they would open the ledger and find a tidy, settled truth. Unless somewhere, somehow, a truer document reached them first.

Nine books. Plus tonight's run. Maybe thirty copies by dawn, if the paper held and the press held and their luck held, and none of those things was guaranteed.

"Feed it slower," her father said suddenly.

"It's not me. Listen—" Ivo tilted her head. Under the familiar groan of the pull, there was a new note. A thin, complaining creak from deep in the frame, the kind of sound wood makes when it is being asked a question it cannot answer.

Her father heard it too. He put his palm flat against the press's cheek like a man taking a fever. "She's straining."

"We have fourteen more pages."

"We have fourteen more pages," he agreed. "And she has maybe ten in her. This press is older than both of us together, Ivo. Older than the town charter. She was old when your grandfather bought her secondhand and lied about it."

"So we go gently."

"We go gently," he said, "and we accept that we may lose her."

Ivo's hands went still on the next sheet. It wasn't sentiment, exactly — or rather, it was exactly sentiment, and she refused to apologize for it. This press had printed her birth notice. It had printed the festival bills she'd grown up chasing down Windmill Street. It had printed her first clumsy attempt at a poem, age nine, which her father had framed and hung in the privy, where, he said, all poetry belonged.

"If she cracks—"

"Then she cracks having done the most important work of her life." Her father pulled the next impression anyway, gently, and the thin creak answered him. "There are worse deaths. Ask anyone in that ledger."

* *

Midnight passed. Then the hour after midnight. The lantern oil sank. Somewhere above, the river kept up its groaning conversation with itself, ice plates shifting and grinding, the thaw arriving day by day whether the commission arrived or not.

Ivo's world narrowed to the size of a page. Feed, pull, release, check. Feed, pull, release, check. Her shoulders ached. Her fingertips were gray with ink that no longer washed off but simply accumulated, layer on layer, so that her hands looked like the hands of someone much older, someone who had been printing forbidden truths for decades instead of weeks.

At some point her father began humming. It took her a while to place the tune, and when she did, her chest hurt: it was the lantern-song, the one Maren hummed in her workshop, the one every child in Vellmuth knew — *carry the light where the water goes, carry it down where the dark river flows.* He hummed it off-key, as he hummed everything, and Ivo joined in under her breath, and between verses they printed the arrest of the woman who wrote it.

Page twenty-two came out crooked. Page twenty-three smeared. On page twenty-four, the press gave a crack like a knuckle popping, loud in the little cellar, and both of them froze.

"That was a warning," her father said. "Not the big one. The big one sounds like a rifle."

"How do you know?"

"Because I heard the big one, in the pamphlet year, when they broke into Dorn's shop and smashed his press with sledgehammers." He said it lightly, the way he said terrible things, and returned to inking. "Dorn cried. Not about the arrest. About the press. I understood him only tonight."

They were down to the final leaf — the colophon, the last page, where Ivo had hidden the boldest thing of all. No rhyme here. Just plain text, small and neat, the way a printer signs her work when she has decided she is done being anonymous:

*Printed in Vellmuth, in the last winter of the occupation, by someone who lives here.*

No name. But *someone*. After months of erasure — names struck, families vanished, a boy unmade into a blank line — the whole book ended by insisting on the opposite: *someone exists. Someone saw. Count us.*

She fed the last sheet. Her father drew the ink roller across the form one final time, tender as a man combing hair, and looked at her.

"Together?" he asked.

"Together."

They pulled the lever side by side, his hand over hers on the bar, and the press groaned its longest groan, gathered itself, and delivered the last impression with a thump that seemed, in the exhausted silence afterward, very like a bell.

Ivo lifted the sheet. Perfect. Every word sharp, every date legible, the signature *A. Vane* reproduced forty-one times across the preceding pages in damning, looping fidelity.

"Papa," she started, "it's—"

The crack came then.

Not like a rifle — worse, more intimate, a splintering groan from the heart of the frame, and the whole press shuddered and sagged to the left like a tired animal lying down. A wedge of ancient oak split away from the main beam and clattered to the floor. The screw hung crooked. The platen listed at a sad, permanent angle, the way a smile lists after a stroke.

Neither of them moved for a moment. The lantern flame swayed and steadied.

"Oh," her father said softly. He limped over and laid his hand on the broken frame, and Ivo watched his mouth work around words that wouldn't come, and finally what came out was: "Well. She held. She held to the last sheet, the vain old darling. She wanted a dramatic exit."

Ivo found she was laughing, or crying, or some compound of the two that her body had negotiated without consulting her. "She's dead, Papa."

"She's retired. There's a difference. Dead presses get melted down." He patted the iron cheek. "Retired presses become furniture. She'll hold the kettle. She'll hold umbrellas. She'll hold whatever a press holds when it's earned its rest." He turned to Ivo, and in the failing lantern light his milky eyes were bright. "Thirty-one copies, girl. I counted. Thirty-one true books in a town that was supposed to have none. Now. What do we do with them?"

Ivo looked at the stack. Thirty-one slim volumes bound in plain gray boards, titled *Odile and the Lantern*, innocent as lambs. The flour barrel would hold perhaps ten more than it already hid. The rest needed to travel, and travel soon, because tomorrow — or the day after, or however long the ice had left — the historians would arrive, and Vane would present them the ledger like a finished meal.

And there was only one delivery that mattered. Only one crate in this town that was guaranteed to be opened, page by page, by the very people whose job it was to decide what Vellmuth had been.

"The commission's document crates," she said slowly. "They'll bring empty crates for the ledger. For the official papers. Everyone knows commissions travel with clerks who file everything."

Her father's eyebrows climbed. "You mean to walk into the middle of that parade and put your book in with theirs."

"I mean to deliver primers," Ivo said. "The shop does deliver primers. We're printers. It's practically our civic duty." Her heart was hammering, but her voice came out level, and she recognized, distantly, the feeling of it — the dangerous thrill from chapter after chapter of this long winter, the feeling of writing what is true, now upgraded to carrying it. "Vane searches print shops. He doesn't search deliveries to his own commission."

"He'll have guards."

"He'll have *clerks*," Ivo corrected. "Clerks are busy men. Busy men don't look closely at gray children's books."

Her father studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded once, the way he nodded when a customer's proof came back approved. "Then we'd best make you look bored enough to be invisible. Nothing hides better than a bored shop assistant. Can you look bored?"

"Papa. I've been looking bored my entire life. You said so at my naming-day dinner."

"I said no such— well. Possibly." He began gathering the broken wedge of oak from the floor, cradling it like a relic, and paused. "One condition. If it goes wrong — if they take you — you say nothing, you admit nothing, and you remember that your mother's people were famously stubborn and mine were famously lucky, and you contain multitudes of both."

"Yes, Papa."

"And Ivo." He set the oak fragment down and took her ink-stained face in his ink-stained hands, so that they matched, a matched set of printers' hands framing a printer's daughter. "Whatever happens at that dock — you were counted. Whatever they write or strike out, you counted. Thirty-one times."

Outside, faint through the stone, the river cracked and boomed, a long plate of ice letting go somewhere upstream, and beneath the sound — unmistakable now, arriving — came the far-off clang of the harbor bell ringing out barges' signals. Preparations. The town was waking to the thaw, and somewhere beyond the bend, moving with the meltwater whether they knew it or not, were the historians, coming to seal the ledger shut.

Ivo banked the lanterns, wrapped the books in oilcloth, and went upstairs to find the plainest dress she owned.

Bored, she practiced in the dark glass of the kitchen window. Bored. Invisible. A shop assistant delivering primers.

In the glass, a woman with ink to the wrists looked back at her and did not quite manage it.
