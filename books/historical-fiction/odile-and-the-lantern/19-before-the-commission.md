## 19. Before the Commission

The historians arrived the way winter does — first a rumor, then a certainty, then a great gray mass of them filling the square and complaining about the mud.

Ivo watched from the print shop window as three carriages ground to a halt in front of the customs house. Out of the first came a man built like a lectern: tall, narrow, and clearly designed to be stood behind. He wore a coat the color of wet slate and carried a leather satchel against his chest the way other men carried infants.

"That'll be Doctor Hessel," her father said, peering over her shoulder at a range that could not possibly resolve anything smaller than a cathedral. "Or someone pretending to be him. Historians all look like they're apologizing for something."

"They are," Ivo said. "For being late."

The commission had been announced for weeks, nailed to every door in Vellmuth in Captain Vane's best ceremonial type — type, Ivo noted with professional bitterness, that she herself had set. THE HONORABLE COMMISSION OF INQUIRY AND RECORD, it read. COME TO PRESERVE THE TRUE HISTORY OF THE OCCUPATION OF VELLMUTH.

She had set the word *true* in the same face as the rest of it, which felt at the time like the most obscene thing she'd ever done with her hands. She had since done considerably more obscene things with her hands, but none of them had been printed.

* * *

In the days before the carriages came, Vane transformed. Or rather, he revealed that the transformation had always been available to him, folded up inside his uniform like a dress shirt in a trunk. The man who had signed requisition orders with a flourish now stood on the customs house steps in his cleanest coat and welcomed the commission with a speech about hardship nobly borne, about a town that had *received* its protectors with gratitude, about a record — he gestured to the ledger, displayed on a table like a relic — kept faithfully through difficult seasons.

"Every entry," he said, "verified."

Ivo stood in the crowd with flour on her apron — a deliberate touch; nobody interrogates the flour girl — and listened to the ledger described as settled truth while she knew, page by page, exactly where the lies lived. Entry forty-one. Entry sixty-three. The whole run of the M-names, where a boy called Piet Aldemar had been struck out so cleanly you'd think the paper itself had never known him.

Beside her, a woman from the mill whispered, "They say the historians can compel testimony."

"They say," Ivo agreed, and said nothing else, because saying things was how people ended up in entries.

* * *

That evening her father laid out the plan on the workbench the way he once laid out galleys: everything in order, everything reversible until the moment it wasn't.

"The commission will need printing," he said. "Summons forms. Subpoenas. Certified copies. Historians are worse than bishops for paperwork." He tapped a notice that had come by courier, embossed with Vane's seal. "They've commissioned the town shops to supply primers and blank books for their clerks. Blank ledgers, Ivo. For their own record-keeping."

"And?"

"And," her father said, with the mildness he reserved for observations that were actually orders, "a clerk's blank ledger is the last place anyone looks for anything. Clerks write in them. Nobody reads them until it's too late."

Ivo looked at him. His eyes had gone nearly white at the edges this past year, but there was nothing blind in the way he looked back.

"You want me to put the book *in* their crates."

"I want you to deliver primers," he said, "as a shop assistant would. What happens to be bound between those covers is between you and your conscience, which I understand is a working relationship."

* * *

She spent two nights preparing the delivery copies. They were beautiful, and she permitted herself to know it. *Odile and the Lantern* wore its disguise proudly: a children's book of lantern rhymes, woodcut plates of festival light, a cover stamped in cheerful red. Inside, beneath the rhymes, in the smallest type she could cut without going mad, ran the true account — names, dates, grain tonnages, arrest orders, and the signature A. Vane appearing on altered entries like a stain coming through wallpaper.

Her father inked the press. His hands shook — age, and the memory of a soldier's rifle butt from the search weeks ago — but the impressions came off clean.

"Good pull," he said, examining one. "Better than anything I did at thirty."

"At thirty you were printing pamphlets in a cellar."

"Yes, well. Rebellious youth is wasted on the young."

* * *

The morning of the delivery, Ivo dressed the part. Her father's spare apron, dyed shop-brown. Her hair pinned under a cap. A delivery docket written in her own hand, because forgery of a humble kind is still forgery, and she had decided some time ago that if she was going to hang, it wouldn't be for something small.

Tomas's betrayal had cost them Maren and most of the workshop stock. The soldiers who raided the lantern-maker's found crates of the book stacked beside the festival lanterns, and hauled them away triumphantly, and Ivo had spent three sleepless nights doing arithmetic: how many seized, how many hidden, how many left. The answer was *enough*. It was never enough. But it was enough.

She loaded a handcart with twelve primer bundles and wheeled it toward the customs house through a town gone strange with ceremony. Bunting on the customs house — bunting, in Vellmuth, where the last bunting had gone up for a harvest festival two years before the army ate the harvest. Guards at the doors. Clerks hurrying in and out with armfuls of paper, looking harried in the universal manner of clerks everywhere, a tribe Ivo understood better than any other: people who believed that what was written down became real, and who were not entirely wrong.

At the goods entrance, a sergeant checked her docket, frowned at it, and looked at her.

"Eastwick Print Shop," she said. "Primers for the commission clerks. Signed order's on top."

He read slowly, lips moving. Ivo waited, heart performing something arrhythmic behind her ribs. She had faced soldiers before. She had faced Vane across a counter while he complimented her kerning. But this — walking the truth into the enemy's own filing system, wearing her father's apron as a costume — this was different. This was audacity wearing flour, and the flour was thinning.

"S'pose it's fine," the sergeant said. "Third door on the left. Don't touch nothing."

* * *

The corridor smelled of ink and cold tea, and Ivo nearly laughed at the familiarity of it. War rooms and inquiry commissions were apparently indistinguishable from print shops, right down to the tea gone cold beside unfinished work. She passed an open door where three clerks sat copying pages from the master ledger onto fresh sheets — the official transcription, the one that would become history. One clerk yawned enormously. Another had drawn a small horse in the margin of his draft and was shading its mane when he thought no one was looking.

She delivered four bundles to the clerks' room, as instructed. Then she saw the document crates.

They stood along the far wall: slatted wooden boxes, stenciled COMMISSION OF INQUIRY — PROPERTY, each one half-full of papers awaiting cataloguing. And beside them, a stack of empty crates waiting to be filled for transport downstream — because the commission would compile its record in Vellmuth, but the record itself would travel to the capital, sealed and certified, where it would become the only version of events anyone would ever read.

Downstream. On the spring barges.

The river was cracking outside. She could hear it through the wall — long groans, like a giant turning over in bed — and the sound went through her the way it went through everyone in Vellmuth, dread and hope in one chord.

A guard stood at the corridor's end, absorbed in picking his teeth with a quill, which Ivo felt said a great deal about the commission's security arrangements.

She worked quickly. Four bundles delivered openly; eight more distributed into the open crates, slid flat beneath legitimate papers, covers down, spines disguised among summons forms and certified copies. Into one crate she tucked the book beside the ledger's fresh transcription — let them lie together, she thought, the lie and the truth, sharing a box, sharing a ride, like passengers who don't yet know they're traveling together.

Into another crate, deliberately, she placed a copy on top, cover visible, innocent as bread. Let some junior clerk pick it up to amuse himself. Let him read a lantern rhyme to his fellows on a slow evening. Let him get to the third verse and go quiet.

Her hands shook on the last copy. Not fear now, or not only. Something closer to vertigo — the sensation of standing at the very edge of a thing and knowing there is no stepping back from it, that the next hour will either be nothing or everything, and that she has already leaned forward.

She thought of Maren in the cell they'd put her in, arrested on Tomas's information, held for possession of a book about lanterns. She thought of Piet Aldemar's name, struck out. She thought of her father, who had lost a brother to exactly this arithmetic and had spent fifty years pretending the loss was weather.

She placed the last copy in the crate marked for the commissioners themselves, face-up, and walked out past the guard with the quill, who nodded at her the way men nod at aprons: without seeing.

Outside, the air tasted of thaw. The square was full of historians in slate-colored coats, and Captain Vane was showing Doctor Hessel the ledger, both men bent over it with the tenderness of priests over scripture, and neither of them knew — couldn't know — that the truth was at that moment sitting in their own luggage, wearing a red cover, humming a nursery rhyme.

Ivo wheeled her empty cart home through streets that dripped. At the corner she stopped, turned, and allowed herself one long look at the customs house.

"Go downstream," she whispered. "All of you. Go downstream."

Behind her, faint but unmistakable, the river gave a crack like a rifle shot, and somewhere in the town a dog began to bark, and in the clerks' room of the Honorable Commission of Inquiry, a tired young man with ink-stained fingers picked up a cheerful red book and opened it to the first rhyme, reading aloud to no one:

*Odile lit the lantern bright / to count the boats that pass by night—*

—and stopped counting boats, because beneath the verse, in letters small as seeds, was a list of names, and one of them belonged to a boy from the lantern workshop whom the ledger said had never existed at all.
