## 20. The River Opens

The knock came at dawn, and Ivo was at the door before her father could rise from his chair, because a knock at dawn in the third week of an occupation meant one of two things, and she had decided she would rather face either of them standing.

It was not soldiers. It was Maren.

She stood on the step with her shawl pinned crooked and her hands bare and red from the cold, and behind her the sky over the river had gone the color of pewter, that particular heavy gray that meant the ice was thinking about giving up. Ivo had imagined this moment — Maren walking free — perhaps forty times since the arrest, and in every imagining there had been weeping, and falling into arms, and some speech about endurance. What actually happened was that Maren looked her up and down and said, "You look terrible."

"You've been in a cell for nine days," Ivo said. "I've been sleeping badly. We are not the same."

"I slept *wonderfully*," said Maren, stepping inside. "Best sleep of my life. No lanterns to make." And then her voice went out from under her like a stool kicked away, and Ivo caught her, and the two of them stood in the doorway of Eastwick & Son, Printers, holding each other among the smell of ink and oil, while old Eastwick pretended very loudly to be absorbed in cleaning a brayer that did not need cleaning.

* * *

They gave her tea, and bread with the last of the honey, and Maren ate like a woman who had been making up for lost meals and intended to keep making up for them for some weeks yet. Her account of the arrest was brief and unsentimental: four soldiers, a cart, a cell with a window too high to see out of, three days of questions from a lieutenant whose name she refused to learn on principle. The copies they'd seized from her workshop — eleven of them, stacked neatly by a soldier with more tidiness than sense — were carried off to Captain Vane's office.

"And then?" Ivo asked.

"Then nothing," Maren said. She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Three days of nothing. Then the lieutenant comes back looking like a man who has been asked to swallow something large, and he says they're releasing me for lack of evidence. Lack of evidence! I told him exactly what the book was. I read him a rhyme. I said it was a primer for children, lantern rhymes, nothing more, and he wrote it down, and I thought, this man is writing down my words, oh, if only he knew — but Ivo, he *did* know. That's what I keep turning over. He knew what he was holding and he let me go anyway."

"He couldn't prove it," Ivo said. "A rhyme isn't evidence."

"A rhyme isn't evidence," Maren agreed. "But he'd held the pages, girl. He'd have felt the weight of them. There's a difference between paper and paper." She looked up, and there was something in her face Ivo had never seen there before — not grief, not the flat endurance she had carried since her son's death, but a kind of startled, dawning delight, like a woman finding spring flowers under late snow. "Unless," she said, "he wasn't the one deciding."

* * *

The answer came to them, as answers do, sideways and absurdly, through the commission's laundry.

The historians had arrived with the thaw's first promise and taken rooms at the Gilded Pike, and their clerks — a dozen young men in good coats with ink-stained fingers — sent their linen out to Widow Kessel, who washed for half the town and reported on all of it. By Thursday, Widow Kessel's front room had become the most reliable news bureau in Vellmuth, superior in accuracy to anything the garrison produced, and Ivo went there ostensibly to collect mending and actually to sit near the stove and listen.

"—wouldn't say what it was," Widow Kessel was telling a neighbor, folding a shirt with the crisp authority of a magistrate. "Only that the young one, Herr Brandt, had been up half the night with it and missed the morning session entirely, which Captain Vane remarked upon, *publicly*, in front of the senior men. And then yesterday the other clerk — the pale one with the cough — asks me, casual as you please, whether I knew any of the families mentioned in the requisition rolls. Families *mentioned in the requisition rolls*. I said, sir, everyone in this town is in those rolls, dead or alive or otherwise, and he went quiet as a closed book."

"Otherwise," the neighbor said. "That's what they call them now? Otherwise?"

"That's what the ledger calls them," said Widow Kessel, and snapped the shirt flat. "Struck out. As if a line drawn in ink could stand where a person used to."

Ivo gathered her mending and left before her face could tell anyone anything, but her heart was going like a drum in a parade. A clerk had been up half the night with something. A clerk was asking after names in the requisition rolls. Somewhere in the commission's own crates, in the commission's own quarters, in the commission's own careful hands, the book was doing what books do when they are true: it was refusing to stay shut.

* * *

Her father heard her account of it without interruption, sitting in his chair by the cellar door with his ruined eyes turned toward the sound of her voice. When she finished, he was silent so long she began to think he'd drifted off, which happened now, gently, several times a day, his mind mooring itself in quieter waters.

Then he said, "Your grandfather always said paper is patient."

"Did he?"

"He said ink dries, wax seals, men die, governments fall — but paper waits. Paper sits in a crate for thirty years and then someone opens the crate." He smiled at some interior sight. "He was a gloomy man. Right, though. Gloomy and right."

"There's a difference," Ivo said, "between paper waiting in a crate and paper walking around town in a clerk's coat pocket asking questions."

"That," her father said, "is why yours is better than his."

* * *

The ice broke fully on a Friday, in the night, with a sound the whole town later claimed to have heard differently.

Ivo woke at the first groan — a long, deep, wooden complaint, like a house settling, except it was the river, the whole river, a league of locked water deciding at once to be water again. She was at the window before she was properly awake, and her father came leaning on her shoulder, and together they watched the dark, listening. Cracks ran through the silence in long reports, some sharp as whips, some rolling like distant cannon. The river humped and shrugged. Down at the banks, lamps were coming alight one by one as the town stumbled out into the cold to witness it, because in Vellmuth the breaking of the ice had always been an occasion — the year's true new year, the moment the town exhaled.

By morning the channel at midstream had opened, black water showing through white, and the floes were moving, grinding past each other with a sound like furniture being rearranged in a giant's house. Children ran along the bank throwing sticks onto the pans to watch them race downstream. The ferries would run within days. The barges — the first barges, loaded with the requisitioned grain, sealed and manifested and bound for the capital — would follow within the week.

And with them, riding the same current, going the same direction, tucked into document crates marked with the commission's own seal: the truth.

Ivo stood in the crowd at the bank with her hood up and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since autumn. It was a strange feeling, and it took her a while to name it. Not triumph — triumph was too loud a word, and anyway the thing was not done. It was closer to watching a letter leave your hand at the post. The moment when the outcome passes out of your keeping entirely, and all that remains is to hope, and to be ordinary, and to be nobody, which for once felt not like erasure but like safety.

"Look at that," said a voice beside her, and it was Tomas, hooded and unshaven, hands in his pockets, watching the floes with the professional interest of a man whose livelihood floated on that water. He didn't look at her. "First barges load Tuesday. Grain, timber, the captain's personal luggage, and, I'm told, eleven crates of administrative documents, very carefully packed." A pause. "Very carefully packed indeed. Almost as if someone wanted them to arrive."

"Tomas—"

"Don't," he said mildly. "I'm not fishing. I sold you once already; I'm not selling you twice, it's bad for trade." He still didn't look at her, but his mouth twitched. "Besides. I hear the pale clerk with the cough has been buying rounds at the Gilded Pike and asking barge crews whether they can read. Whether they'd *want* to read, given something worth reading. Crews remember things like that. Word travels on water faster than on roads, always has." He finally turned his head, just enough. "Whatever it is you put in those crates, printer's daughter — it's loose. Loose things don't come back."

He touched his cap and melted into the crowd, and Ivo stood a moment longer, watching a pan of ice the size of a barn door turn slowly in the current and slide out of sight around the bend, carrying nothing but itself toward the sea.

* * *

Maren's workshop smelled of wood shavings and tallow again, and the lanterns hung for the winter festival — the ones that had never been lit, that winter, because the town had been under curfew when the festival should have come — still hung from the beams like a flock of sleeping birds.

"They never got their festival," Maren said, moving among them, touching frames here and there. "The army postponed it. Then canceled it. 'In light of the security situation.'" She snorted. "As if light were the situation's enemy." She took one down — a plain hexagonal frame, glass panes fogged with a winter's dust — and blew the dust off, and held it up to the grey window light. "So I've been thinking. The commission's clerks walk past this street twice a day on their way to the ledger office. Past a shop full of lanterns that were never lit."

"Maren."

"I'm not proposing anything reckless."

"You're proposing everything reckless; it's your native language."

"*One* evening," Maren said. "One evening of lanterns in the windows, all down this street, the way it should have been in winter. Nothing written on them. No gathering, no speeches. Just light in windows, which is not against any law I've ever seen, and if the captain objects to light in windows then let him explain to the commission why the town must be dark." She set the lantern down and faced Ivo squarely. "And every lantern gets a page inside it. One page. From the book. Where a flame would go, a page — folded small, readable if you open the lantern, invisible if you don't. The clerks walk home past twenty lit windows, and any one of them might lift a latch."

Ivo opened her mouth to list the dangers, and found, going down the list, that each item was smaller than the last, and that at the bottom of the list was the fact — undeniable, inconvenient, warming her like a stove — that she wanted it. She had spent a winter learning to hide truth inside innocence, rhymes inside primers, names inside verses. Light inside lanterns was only the next size up.

"All right," she said. "But the pages get folded so the verse shows first. If a soldier opens one, he sees a children's rhyme and nothing else. The names only come for people who unfold."

"People who *look*," Maren corrected softly. "There's a difference between paper and paper."

* * *

That evening, printing pages by lamplight in the cellar, Ivo paused over the type case and allowed herself, for the first time in months, to count what they had made.

Two hundred and six copies of the primer, of which eleven sat in Vane's office being slowly, patiently read by clerks who had stopped pretending not to understand them. Thirty-one distributed through Maren's network of the bereaved — mothers, widows, an uncle, two sisters, a boatman who had rowed a disappeared man's family across the river once and never forgot it. Fourteen slipped into the commission's own document crates on delivery day, wearing the disguise of routine consignments. And one copy, the first, kept wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards, which Ivo sometimes took out at night just to hold, the way you hold a child asleep.

The press beneath her hands was cracked along the cheek — she could feel the flaw give slightly on every hard pull of the bar, a small creak like a joint complaining — and she knew, the way you know a horse is nearly spent, that it had perhaps a few hundred impressions left in it. Enough. Enough for the lantern pages, enough for one final run if it came to that, and then the old press could lie down with honor.

Her father appeared at the top of the stairs, descending carefully, and settled onto the stool beside her without being asked, and began, unbidden, to ink the plate with his shaking hands, missing the edges and not caring, because his part these days was not precision but presence.

"Word from Widow Kessel's?" he asked. "Any word today?"

"The pale clerk bought a round for three barge captains," Ivo said. "And the junior one, Brandt, requested the original requisition manifests from October — the ones predating Vane's amendments. Vane told him those were lost in transit."

"Lost in transit," her father repeated, savoring it. "From a locked office fifty feet from his desk."

"Clerks notice things like that."

"Clerks live on things like that." He rolled the brayer, slow and even. "Your grandfather would have liked these clerks. Gloomy and right, the both of us." He worked in companionable silence for a while, and then said, quietly, not looking at her: "When the barges go — when it's truly loose, past calling back — what will you do?"

Ivo pulled the lever. The press spoke its familiar complaint. She lifted the frisket and looked down at the fresh page: a lantern rhyme, innocent as milk, and folded within its future creases, the date and true manner of death of a boy named Anders Moll, who had been struck from the record of his own town and was now, line by line, being written back into it.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Hide, probably. Be nobody for a while. Let the paper be patient." She smiled down at the page. "But Da — whoever they come looking for, they won't find a printer. They'll find type dust and a cracked press and a shop assistant who delivers primers. I've gotten very good at being no one."

Her father patted the cracked cheek of the press, gently, the way you'd steady an old friend.

"No one," he said, "with ink under her nails."

* * *

On Monday evening, the lanterns went lit.

Not all at once — Maren was wiser than that. They came alight one by one as full dark fell, down the lane and along the quay road, until twenty-odd windows burned with the soft gold of festival light that was eight months late, and the town, passing below, slowed, and looked up, and said nothing, and understood everything. Soldiers on patrol frowned at it. An officer came to Maren's door and was met with a smiling old woman offering spiced wine and an explanation about making up for the canceled festival, and what law precisely prevented a shopkeeper from lighting her own goods, and the officer, having no answer that would survive being written down, withdrew.

And the clerks walked home from the ledger office through that lane of gentle light, past twenty windows each holding a folded page where a flame should be, and Ivo — watching from the shadowed doorway of the print shop, anonymous, unafraid, her breath smoking in the cold — saw one of them stop.

The pale one. The one with the cough. He stood before Maren's window a long moment, looking up at the lantern hanging there, and then, glancing left and right with the guilty quickness of a man stealing something worthless and priceless at once, he stepped up, lifted the little latch, unfolded the page inside, and read.

He read it twice. Ivo saw his shoulders change. Then he folded it back along its creases with great care, latched the lantern, replaced it exactly, straightened his coat, and walked on — faster than he had come, with the cough suddenly forgotten, a man carrying a coal in cupped hands toward a very dry field.

The ice groaned out in the dark, shifting, opening. Somewhere downriver the first barges waited for morning.

Ivo stayed in the doorway until her feet were numb, watching the lit windows and the clerks' departing backs and the black water running free between the floes, and she thought: *it's out of our hands now.* The record was slipping its keepers, page by page, pocket by pocket, barge by barge, and no signature — not even Vane's beautiful, flourishing one — could sign it back.

Then she saw, at the far end of the lane, a figure standing motionless in the lamplight, watching the windows with an expression she could read even at that distance, because she had spent a winter learning to read it.

Captain Aldous Vane had come to see the light for himself.

And as she drew back into the shadow of the doorway, heart slamming, she saw him turn — slowly, deliberately — and look directly at the darkened windows of Eastwick & Son, Printers.
