## 21. What the Victors Read

The commission arrived on a Tuesday, which Ivo thought was fitting. Tuesdays were, in her experience, the day most likely to produce bad news delivered with good manners. The historians came up from the barge landing in a procession of grey coats and important satchels — nine of them, plus a clerk whose job appeared to be carrying things and looking apologetic about it — and Captain Vane met them at the customs house with his hat off and his smile arranged just so.

Ivo watched from the print shop doorway with her father's spectacles pushed up on her head, an affectation she had adopted because it made her look busy and slightly deaf, both of which were useful.

"They've brought their own ink," her father said behind her. He was sorting type by touch these days, his fingers reading the little metal letters like a blind man reading faces. "Did you see? Little traveling cases. A man who brings his own ink to a town full of printers is a man who has already decided what he's going to write."

"Perhaps they're particular about their ink."

"Everyone is particular about their ink," her father said. "That's how you know what they are."

The commission settled into the customs house for three days without producing anything visible, which Ivo learned was called *deliberating*. On the fourth day, the clerks began carrying crates of documents between the customs house and the requisitioned granary where the master ledger now lived under guard, and Ivo understood that the machine had finished warming up and was beginning to turn.

She had done her part already. Three crates' worth of primers — *Odile and the Lantern*, cheerful cover, innocent rhymes, forty copies slipped into document consignments over two weeks of deliveries she had made herself, wearing her shop apron and her best expression of dull competence. The commission's own quartermaster had signed for them. She still had the receipt. She kept it in the same drawer as her private account of the occupation, because there was a certain poetry in filing Vane's world and her truth side by side.

Now there was nothing to do but wait, and waiting, Ivo had discovered, was the hardest work of all. Setting type was patience, but it was patience with a purpose; you could feel the page assembling itself under your hands. This was patience with nothing in your hands at all except a broom you did not need to sweep with.

"You'll wear a groove in those boards," her father said.

"I'm inspecting them."

"You've inspected them eleven times."

"Twelve," said Ivo. "You missed one."

* * *

It happened on the sixth day, in the market square, in front of the fishmonger's stall, of all places — and Ivo would learn afterward that the fishmonger never stopped telling the story, embellishing it a little more each winter until the junior historian stood knee-deep in trout and read aloud like an angel announcing a birth.

His name was Pell. That much the town would extract from him eventually: Junior Historian Ambrose Pell, third from the bottom of the commission's hierarchy, twenty-three years old, and burdened with the task no senior historian wanted — cataloguing the seized ephemera. The pamphlets, the proclamations, the broadsheets, the crates of printed matter pulled from shops and homes during the occupation. Someone had to sort it. Someone always had to sort it.

He had been given a crate. In the crate, mixed among Vane's commemorative broadsheets and grain-requisition notices, were four copies of a children's book.

Ivo was not there when he opened it. She was across the square buying eels she had no intention of eating, because the fishmonger's stall had the best view of the customs house steps and buying something gave her a reason to stand there. Her father had told her this was paranoid. Maren had told her this was sensible. Maren had spent one night in a cell and had earned the right to be believed.

Pell sat down on the crate itself, as tired young men do, and opened the book the way one opens anything expected to be dull — quickly, with the impatience of a man who has ninety-nine more items after this one.

And then, because he was twenty-three and the rhyme was short and the afternoon was long, he read it aloud. To himself, mostly. But the square was quiet in the way squares are before evening, and sound carries over water, and Vellmuth was built on water.

*Odile hung her lantern high / to count the boats that passed her by—*

"One, two, three, four," Pell recited, counting the numbers buried in the verse the way children were meant to. And then he stopped. Because Pell was young, but he was not stupid — the commission had hired him precisely because he noticed things, though they had imagined he'd notice useful things — and he had spent three days inside the requisitioned ledger, and the numbers in the rhyme had snagged against the numbers in the ledger like a nail against silk.

Ivo watched him go still. Watched him look at the page. Watched him take out a pencil — a pencil! a historian's most honest tool! — and write something in the margin.

Then he read the next rhyme aloud, slower, and this time half the fishmonger's row heard it:

*Seven lanterns, six remain, / one went dark on Vellmuth lane—*

And old Widow Kessel, standing three feet away with her basket, said, in the clear flat voice of a woman who has waited months to say a thing: "That was the ninth of November. My nephew. He went dark on Vellmuth lane."

Ivo bought her eels. She paid too much for them. It was worth every coin.

By nightfall, the story had crossed the town the way stories cross river towns — faster than any barge, arriving damp and exaggerated. A historian had found names in a nursery book. A historian had found *dates*. The ledger said one thing and the lantern book said another, and the lantern book was sitting open on a crate in the customs house yard while three senior historians argued around it in voices that carried.

Vane, Ivo heard from the baker's boy (who charged nothing for news but accepted bread), had been summoned from his quarters twice before supper, and had returned the second time looking, for the first time since the army marched into Vellmuth, like a man who had mislaid something and suspected it might be himself.

* * *

The official record did not stop — records of that kind rarely do; they are heavy machines with momentum — but it *stalled*, and a stalled record makes a very particular noise, like a wagon with a bad axle deciding whether to complain or collapse.

The commission's morning session ran long. Then longer. Clerks came in and out with their arms full. Pell, Ivo observed from a safe and patient distance, was suddenly invited to sessions he had previously only been permitted to fetch paper for. A senior historian named Corvess — tall, grey, with the air of a woman who had audited empires — was seen reading the lantern book by lamplight well past midnight, making marks in a notebook with increasing velocity.

And through the town, the copies moved.

There were only forty, and Ivo had distributed thirty-one before the raids took the workshop copies, so the arithmetic was tight. But tight arithmetic is what riverside towns do best. A copy traveled from the chandlery to the ferry house in a sailor's coat pocket. Another turned up in the schoolmaster's desk, and the schoolmaster — a cautious man who had spent the occupation teaching approved mathematics with the windows shut — began, very quietly, to use the lantern rhymes as reading exercises. The children loved them. Children always love a thing with a secret in it, and Vellmuth's children learned the dates of the disappeared faster than they learned their multiplication tables, chanting them in the lanes like skipping songs until their mothers hushed them, gently, and then taught them more.

Maren came to the shop on the second evening, moving through the back streets with her collar up out of habit, though nobody had looked sideways at her in a week. She brought lamp oil as payment, which Ivo protested and then accepted, because Maren's dignity was a load-bearing wall and you did not knock on it.

"Corvess sent for me," Maren said, sitting on the type case with her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she wouldn't drink. "The historian. Not Vane. *Her.* She asked me about my son. His name, the date, the road where—" She stopped. Started again. "She wrote it all down. In her own book. She said the commission keeps its own record, separate from the ledger, and that discrepancies between them get..." Maren smiled, and it was a terrible and wonderful thing to see. "*Adjudicated.*"

"Adjudicated," Ivo repeated.

"That's the word. Like a debt." Maren looked at her. "You put my Tam in a nursery rhyme, Ivo Eastwick, and now a woman with grey hair and a government satchel is adjudicating him. Back into existence."

Ivo's throat was too tight for eloquence. "Someone had to count him."

"Someone counted him." Maren finally drank her tea. "Forty books. You counted him forty times."

* * *

On the fourth day after Pell's discovery, Captain Aldous Vane began searching for the printer.

He started, sensibly enough, with the print shops, of which Vellmuth had three: the Eastwick shop, Hobb's stationers on the canal street, and the defunct works of old Brech, dead two years, whose presses had been sold for scrap. Soldiers visited Hobb's in the morning and emerged with ink on their gloves and nothing else; Hobb printed invoices and funeral announcements and had not had an original thought since the coronation. They visited the Eastwick shop in the afternoon.

Ivo had been ready for this visit since autumn, and readiness, she had learned, mostly consisted of tidiness. The cellar press stood where it had always stood — cracked bedplate, split platen, the fracture running through it like a river on a map. It looked exactly like what it was: an old press that had died mid-job, surrounded by the ordinary debris of a working shop. Type dust lay over everything, fine and grey, the sediment of decades. There were formes of harmless matter locked on the stone: a price list for the chandler, a wedding announcement, the next week's proclamation proofs, because one did not stop printing the occupier's proclamations merely because one was undermining him; business was business.

Vane came himself. Of course he did. He entered the shop the way he always did, removing his gloves finger by finger, and Ivo noted with a professional's eye that his hands were not steady. Small comfort. She took it anyway.

"Master Eastwick." He nodded to her father, who sat by the window doing his slow, public, blameless sorting. "Mistress Eastwick. I understand your establishment produced a children's book this winter."

"Several establishments did," Ivo said. "Primers sell well when families are indoors. We printed for Hobb's, for the ferry house, for the schoolmaster. Children being unable to read is a problem everyone wants solved, Captain, whatever else is happening."

"And this *Odile*." He picked up a copy from the display stack — the display stack, deliberately placed, containing the six remaining sale copies, innocent as lambs. He turned it over. Read the cover. Opened it to the first plate, where Odile held her lantern over a bridge rendered in warm browns, and beneath the bridge, worked into the shadow-lines of the stonework in a hand so fine it read as texture: nothing. Nothing at all. The innocents' plates carried no hidden text. Ivo had learned that lesson from the raid; the copies that circulated bore the truth, but a copy on open display in a searched shop must be able to survive a search.

"It's charming," Vane said. "My sister would have liked it." He closed the book with a soft clap. "A printer in this town set names into these pages. Dates. Accounts of requisitions. A full narrative of the occupation, disguised as verse. I am going to find that printer."

"The town has three hundred years of printing history, Captain," Ivo's father said mildly, without looking up from his type. "Half the gravestones were set in this room. If someone hid names in a book, it wouldn't be the first time a printer put the dead where the living could find them. That's half of what printing *is*."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — recognition, perhaps, or its unwelcome cousin. He looked at the cracked press. At the type dust. At Ivo, who gazed back with the frank, faintly bored expression of a tradeswoman with work waiting.

"You cut a clean letter, Mistress Eastwick," he said. "I said that once before."

"You did."

"I wonder if you know how clean."

"I know my trade," Ivo said. "It's all I have time to know."

He stood there a moment longer, and Ivo watched him perform the arithmetic she had been performing all winter in reverse: if the printer was here, in this room, then the evidence should be here, and the evidence was not here, therefore the printer was not here. It was flawless logic. It was the logic of a man who believed records described reality rather than the other way round, and it was, she reflected as he replaced his gloves, the single greatest gift anyone had ever given her.

At the door he paused. "When the commission finishes its work, this town will have an official account of the occupation. One account. Authoritative. It would be a shame if... unofficial versions continued to circulate."

"Unofficial versions," Ivo said, "tend to die out on their own, Captain. Nobody reads yesterday's primer."

Vane looked at her for a long moment — and here was the strange thing, the thing she would think about later: he almost smiled. As if some part of him, some buried apprentice-part that had once loved the trade too, knew exactly what she was doing and admired the craft of it even as it undid him.

"Good day, Mistress Eastwick."

"Good day, Captain. Mind the step. The frost heaves it every spring."

* * *

That night the whole family of conspirators that wasn't — Ivo, her father, Maren — sat in the shop with the shutters closed and the good lamp lit and let themselves, cautiously, unbelievingly, be happy.

"He searched the shop," Ivo said, "and found type dust."

"Type dust," her father agreed, "which is what a press leaves behind when it dies. Which is what we wanted him to find. Though I confess I didn't plan the crack in the bedplate; the press planned that itself. Give credit where due."

"The press sacrificed itself," Ivo said solemnly. "It deserves a mention in the true account."

"It got one. Page nineteen. 'The press cracked under the strain, as did many quieter things that winter.'" Her father reached over and patted the old machine's frame the way one pats a good horse. "It earned its line."

Maren laughed — actually laughed, the first time since Tam — and then pressed her hand to her mouth as if laughing were also contraband. Perhaps, Ivo thought, it had been, until quite recently.

Outside, the river ran loud and free. The ice had gone to broken floes grinding downstream, and the barges were loading at the landing — grain, timber, wool, and, stacked in the commission's document crates with the official seals on their lids, thirty-one copies of a lantern book bound for the capital, for the archives, for desks and drawers and shelves in cities Ivo had never seen, where someone someday would lift a lid and find a nursery rhyme and count the boats.

"Pell stays till Friday," Maren reported. "Corvess has extended the whole commission's stay. Vane wanted them gone by the thaw and Corvess said — the baker's boy swears this is verbatim — 'The record will leave when the record is accurate, Captain, and not one barge sooner.'"

"She sounds formidable."

"She asked me," Maren said, "if I knew who printed the book. I told her the truth: that a book is like a lantern, you light it and hang it out and you never see who stops beneath it. She wrote that down too. In her own book." Maren turned her mug slowly. "I don't think she believes it. I think she knows there's a printer in this town. I think she simply hasn't decided yet whether knowing is the same as saying."

"Then we keep the type dust fresh," Ivo's father said, "and the press looking dead, and our faces looking bored. Boredom, girls, is the great camouflage. Nobody suspects a bored person of anything."

"I've been practicing boredom since October," Ivo said. "I may be the finest bored person in the province."

They sat a while in the lamplight, the three of them and the dead press and the living type, and Ivo felt something settle in her chest that she had no word for yet — not vindication, exactly, because vindication was supposed to be louder than this, trumpets and banners, and what had actually happened was a tired young man reading aloud over a fish stall while the town leaned in. Quietly. Without a name attached. Just as she'd wanted it.

Almost just as she'd wanted it. Because there was a small voice in her — the same voice that had once longed to matter, back when she was only cutting type nobody noticed — that whispered that someday, maybe, she might sign her work.

But that was a thought for a warmer season. Tonight there was tea to finish, and a river to listen to, and in the morning, Corvess had requested — politely, officially, in writing — that the Eastwick print shop provide the commission with a quotation for printing work. Official work. Commission work.

Ivo read the request twice by lamplight while her father pretended not to watch.

"They want *us* to print for *them*," she said.

"Well," said her father, smiling at his type cases. "Isn't that interesting. What shall we charge them?"
