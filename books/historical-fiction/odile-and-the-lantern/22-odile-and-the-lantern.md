## 22. Odile and the Lantern

The swallows came back to Vellmuth on a Tuesday, which Ivo Eastwick took as a good omen, since Tuesdays were the day her father had always declared best for setting type. "Lead settles on a Tuesday," he liked to say, squinting at the case with his clouded eyes as if lead were the sort of thing that could be seen settling at all. "Everything else in this town is unsettled, but lead knows its business."

He was wrong about everything else being unsettled, actually, though it had taken the better part of two months for the town to prove it. Spring had come up the river like a slow apology. The barges that had carried away her book in March had come back carrying flour, salt, and — more remarkably — clerks. Not soldiers' clerks with their rubber stamps and their inkpads the color of old bruises, but commission clerks, pale men from the capital who wore their spectacles on chains and asked questions in soft voices and wrote down the answers.

The ledger had been reopened.

Ivo learned of it from a customer, old Frau Behm, who came in for funeral cards and stayed for gossip. "They've struck through the strike-throughs," she said, delighted, laying a wrinkled hand flat on the counter. "My cousin's boy — the one they said drowned at Alderford when everyone knows he never went near Alderford — his name's back in. In proper ink. There's even a margin note." She leaned in conspiratorially. "In red."

"A red margin note," Ivo repeated gravely, as though this were news of a coronation.

"Three lines long! You could read it across the room!" Frau Behm paid for her cards and left glowing, a woman who had spent a winter learning that names were not safe and who now walked through town like a person whose name had been personally vindicated.

Ivo went back to her composing stick. Her father was grinding ink at the back bench, humming something tuneless, and she found herself watching him — the way his hands knew the stone without his eyes, the way he leaned into the work like a man leaning into a wind only he could feel. He had lost a brother to exactly this kind of forgetting, decades ago, and had said nothing for thirty years, and then had handed her the hidden original type of his first press one night in February with hands that shook and eyes that did not.

"You're staring," he said without looking up.

"Admiring the technique."

"The technique," he said, "is knowing when the ink is ready before you can see it. Same as most things worth knowing." He scraped the brayer clean and glanced over at her, and there was something in his face she was still getting used to: not pride exactly, or not only pride, but recognition. As if for thirty years he had been printing beside a stranger and had only just been introduced.

* * *

Maren's son came home in April, in the way that mattered.

Not his body — that was under the willows past the mill, where Maren had always known, because mothers know — but his name. On a gray morning in the second week of April, a junior clerk from the commission came to the lantern workshop with a document folder under his arm and an expression of a man delivering something fragile. Ivo happened to be there, mending the hinge of the workshop door, and so she saw it happen.

The clerk unrolled the page on Maren's workbench, between the solder pots and the glass scraps. "Petra Mollen," he read. "Recorded the fourteenth of November, cause of death entered in error, entry amended by order of the Commission on Records. Compensation claim reinstated. Back payment authorized." He cleared his throat. "There's a signature page. It wants your mark."

Maren looked at the page for a long time. The light through the workshop windows came greenish, filtered through hanging lanterns waiting for repair, and it made her face look like something underwater.

"He was real," she said at last. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't addressed to the clerk either.

"Yes, madam. The record now says so."

She signed. Her hand was steady. Then the clerk rolled up his papers and left, and Maren stood at her bench among the lanterns, and Ivo pretended very hard to be interested in the door hinge until Maren said, quietly, "Come here and let me cry on your shoulder, girl, you're standing there like a fencepost," and Ivo came, and it was fine. It was more than fine. Grief that has been answered has a different weight than grief that hasn't; it can be set down.

"Fourteenth of November," Maren said into her shoulder. "That's what your rhyme said. That's how I knew it was true, before anyone told me anything. Some stranger's little poem knew my son better than his own government did." She pulled back and wiped her face with a rag that smelled of flux and lamp oil. "You'll print the next edition properly now. Under its own name."

It was not a question either.

"It needs a title," Ivo said. "A real one."

Maren looked around her workshop, at all those lanterns hanging in their rows, each one holding its small dark flame-shaped absence. "Well," she said. "You already know it."

* * *

Setting the title page took her three days, which anyone who understood type would find excessive and anyone who didn't would find baffling. But a title is a promise, and Ivo had learned — the hard way, the way you learn things when soldiers search your shop and strike your father for surliness — that promises want to be kept precisely.

**ODILE AND THE LANTERN**

Twelve letters tall in the old display face her father had cast before Ivo was born, the O with its counter punched deep so the letter held a perfect circle of white in its middle, like a lantern seen from very far away in the dark.

Below it, smaller:

*A true account, for children and other careful readers.*

Her father approved of the subtitle immensely. "Insulting and accurate," he said. "Best combination in printing."

They printed two hundred copies on the good paper Tomas no longer supplied — Tomas being, at present, a guest of the civil authorities in a matter involving travel passes, a subject on which Ivo felt the appropriate amount of nothing, having done her grieving for him in February alongside her fury. The new paper came from a cooperative of small printers downriver who had read the first edition, passed it hand to hand along the barge routes all spring, and finally sent a letter addressed, magnificently, to *the printer of the lantern book, Vellmuth.* No name. Just the trade.

"They think I'm a man," Ivo said, reading the letter aloud in the cellar.

"They think you're a press," her father corrected. "Which is better."

* * *

The new edition differed from the smuggled one in ways that mattered. The rhymes were still there — Odile the lamplighter's daughter walking her rounds through a town that might have been any town, lighting lantern after lantern while something cold followed behind her snuffing them out — but now the accounts beneath the verses were set openly, in plain roman type, with dates and names and the phrase *by order of Capt. A. Vane* appearing eleven times where it needed to appear once, because repetition, Ivo had decided, was the honest form of emphasis.

And on the last page, where the first edition had ended with Odile hanging her final lantern and the rhyme trailing off into hope, Ivo had added a colophon. She turned it over for days. Printers' colophons were traditionally modest — place, press, year — and hers began that way.

*Printed in Vellmuth, at the sign of the cracked press, in the spring of the thaw.*

But then, because she had spent a winter learning what erasure cost, she added the rest:

*Set and printed by Ivo Eastwick, who is counted.*

Five words. Her father read them and said nothing for a long moment, and then he said, "Your mother's stubbornness and my spelling. No wonder you turned out like this," and went upstairs to make tea, which was how he said things that mattered too much to say facing someone.

*Counted.* That was the word the whole winter had taught her. Not safe — she was not safe, and suspected she never would be entirely, and had stopped minding. Not important, even; the commission's report ran to nine hundred pages and mentioned the lantern book twice, in footnotes. But counted. Present in the record. One name among the town's names, entered in proper ink, with a margin note in red if you knew where to look — because the commission, in its dry bureaucratic way, had cited her book as a *source document,* and sources got their notes in red.

Frau Behm reported this news within the hour of it becoming public, breathless, having walked from the commission offices faster than any woman her age should walk uphill.

* * *

On the last evening of April, Ivo hung the lantern.

It was one of Maren's — the first of the new season's making, brass and horn-glass, with a wick that burned steady and low. Maren brought it herself to the shop, wrapped in sacking like something newborn, and stood in the doorway while Ivo fixed the bracket above the window.

"Left a bit," Maren said. "No — my left. There. Now it faces the street."

"It's supposed to face the street?"

"Every lantern faces the street. That's the whole profession." Maren stepped back to admire the work, arms folded, and Ivo climbed down off the stool and stood beside her, and together they looked at the small flame burning in the glass with the dusk coming blue behind it.

Inside the shop, her father had lit the lamps over the cases, and the type lay in its trays gleaming dull silver, and the new edition sat stacked on the counter in its neat hundreds, the title page uppermost, the O holding its circle of light even unlit. Through the window the whole arrangement showed plainly to the street: the books, the press visible in the back room beyond, the lantern above it all. Anyone passing could see exactly what was sold here and who did the selling.

For years Ivo had arranged her life the opposite way. Cut the type well and don't be noticed; keep the head down; let the work speak and stay silent yourself, because silence was safety and safety was the only currency that couldn't be requisitioned. She had believed it the way you believe weather — completely, and without ever having examined it.

Then a captain had signed for a ledger with a flourish, and she had learned what silence bought: a town unmade on paper, line by line, while everyone watched politely. Silence, it turned out, was also currency. It just spent worse.

"I keep thinking about the first night we hung the festival lanterns," Maren said. "Before. When Petra was alive. Everyone complained about the ladder, do you remember? And the mayor gave that speech nobody listened to, and the lanterns caught, all at once, and for a minute nobody talked at all." She smiled at the memory, and it didn't break her face the way it would have in January. "That's what I wanted them for. Not the light exactly. The minute where everybody sees everybody."

Ivo thought: *that's the whole profession.* Not the printing, maybe. The minute where everybody sees everybody. Two hundred copies going out on the spring barges, and the ones after that, and somewhere downriver a reader opening the cover and finding inside not just the dead restored and the vanished named but a sentence on the last page saying *who is counted,* meaning: *you are too, whoever you are, that's the point of the book.*

A boy came running up the street — the cooper's son, ten years old, one of the children who had adopted the first edition as genuine literature despite its alarming habit of containing arrest records. "Frau Eastwick! Fraulein — miss — the commission clerk says the amended ledger's going on exhibit. In the market hall. With the original pages. They're letting people read it!"

"Imagine that," Ivo said. "A record people are allowed to read."

"And he says—" the boy caught his breath, delighted, savoring it— "he says the red notes cite a *children's book.* My da says he'll buy me a copy if I can find one."

"They're in the window," Ivo said. "Under the lantern."

The boy pressed his nose briefly to the glass, counted the stacks with a merchant's instinct, and tore off down the street to spread the news, and Ivo watched him go, and Maren laughed beside her, low and warm, and inside the shop her father called out that if the tea went cold again because of street conversations, it would be the third time this week and he was keeping count.

*Keeping count.* Well. Someone in that family had to.

The lantern burned above the window. Down at the docks the first barge of May was loading, and somewhere in its cargo hold, wrapped in sacking between bolts of cloth, rode forty copies of *Odile and the Lantern* bound for towns Ivo would never see, where readers she would never meet would open the cover by lamplight and find, at the bottom of the last page, five words in small type, and — if the commission clerk's rumor was right, and rumors in Vellmuth traveled faster than barges — a summons was coming upriver even now for a Captain Aldous Vane to answer some questions of his own, in proper ink, before witnesses.

Ivo went inside to her tea. Above the door, the lantern faced the street, and burned.
