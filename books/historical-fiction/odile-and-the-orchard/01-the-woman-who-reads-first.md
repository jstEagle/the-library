## 1. The Woman Who Reads First

The letters came on Tuesdays, mostly, and I read every one of them before they reached Miss Marchand's hands.

I want to be clear about this from the start: it was diligence. Anyone who says otherwise has never driven a motorcar down the coast road in April with a woman of sixty-two in the passenger seat who expects you to know things — who expects you to know *everything* — without ever having been told. Odile Marchand did not tolerate ignorance in her employees. She tolerated a great many other things — my Spanish temper, my habit of singing hymns off-key when the engine flooded, my conviction that marmalade was a food group — but not ignorance.

So I read the letters. It was practically a professional obligation.

I told myself this every Tuesday at half past nine, standing in the post office at Wren Hollow with the morning fog still dripping off my sleeves, while Mr. Petch the postmaster watched me over his spectacles with the expression of a man who had long ago decided that whatever I was up to was none of his business so long as I didn't do it in front of him.

"Three today," he said, sliding them across the counter one by one, like cards dealt to a losing hand. "Two local. One foreign."

Foreign meant Germany. Foreign meant the blue airmail border and the careful, upright handwriting that belonged to a Dr. Greta Lindqvist of Heidelberg, whose name I knew better than my own mother's, though we had never been introduced. Foreign meant I would have to steam it open over the kettle again, which I had gotten quite good at, and which I told myself was also diligence.

"You'll want to mind the roads past Marlow," Mr. Petch said. "They're resurfacing."

"They're always resurfacing," I said. "This country is permanently under repair. It's how it avoids thinking about anything else."

He gave me the look he reserved for my foreign opinions, and I took my letters and went.

* * *

Let me explain what I was, in the spring of 1914, because I did not know myself, and perhaps you will see it sooner than I did.

I was twenty-three years old, born in Cádiz to a father who repaired clocks and a mother who believed England was a country made entirely of Jane Austen novels and honest weather. My father died; my mother followed him two years later out of what the doctor called grief and what I called stubbornness, because she had told me all my life she would die before she learned to cook English food, and she kept her word. At nineteen I crossed the water alone with a trunk, forty pounds, and an accent that made landladies purse their lips. At twenty I answered an advertisement in *The Vote*: *WOMAN DRIVER WANTED, MUST BE SOBER, MECHANICALLY INCLINED, AND DISCREET.*

I was two of those three things, and I lied about the third, which turned out to be the only qualification that mattered.

Odile Marchand hired me on the spot. She interviewed me in her study at Wren Hollow, a house that looked like it had been dropped into the Sussex countryside by a careless giant and left there to grow ivy. She asked me three questions: could I drive, could I keep my mouth shut, and what did I think of women voting?

"I think," I said, "that if men can vote after drinking all day at the Dog and Duck, the argument is already lost."

She laughed — a short, surprised bark, as though laughter were something she'd have to sign for — and said, "You're hired. The wages are modest. The work is not."

The work, it transpired, was this: I drove her to meetings in Lewes and Brighton and once, memorably, to a hall in Portsmouth where a man threw a herring at her and she caught it, examined it, and handed it back saying, "Thank you, but I've eaten." I maintained the car, a 1911 Daimler with opinions. I carried banners, bandages, bail money, and occasionally Miss Marchand herself, who weighed nothing and argued like a cathedral falling on you.

And I handled her correspondence. That was the discreet part.

Miss Marchand received an enormous quantity of mail. Suffrage generated letters the way a wet log generates smoke — angry ones, pleading ones, unhinged ones, one memorable series from a gentleman in Tunbridge Wells who wrote weekly to inform her that women's brains were smaller than men's, each letter written in a smaller hand than the last, as though his own brain were shrinking in sympathy. There were letters from Members of Parliament, letters from prison (we had friends there), letters from women in villages I'd never heard of asking questions like *how does one begin?*

My job was to open, sort, summarize, and file. That was the arrangement, stated plainly, on my first morning. "You will read everything," Miss Marchand said. "If there is anything requiring my attention beyond a summary, you will say so. If there is anything dangerous, you will burn it. Do you understand?"

I understood. What I did not tell her — what I have never until this moment told anyone — is that long before she asked, reading other people's letters was already my favorite thing in the world.

* * *

Here is why, and here is where I ask you to be kind, because I was young and lonely and history had never once happened to me.

When you come to a country at nineteen with no family and a funny accent, you learn quickly that you are a spectator. England happened around me like weather happening to someone else's garden. The great events — the suffrage marches, the Parliament debates, the strikes, the speeches — these occurred in London, in newspapers, to people whose names got printed. I drove the car. I held the banner. I stood at the edge of the photograph. And at night in my little room above the stable I read the letters, all of them, even the ones marked personal, especially the ones marked personal, and through them I lived dozens of lives that were not mine.

Mrs. Ainsworth of Battle, who wrote every fortnight to report on her branch's membership ("We are seven now, counting the vicar's wife, who counts conditionally"). The prisoner in Holloway who signed herself *Nightjar* and described the force-feeding in language so calm it made your teeth ache. The girl of sixteen in Rye who wanted to know if wanting to vote was the same as wanting too much, and to whom Miss Marchand dictated replies I copied out fair, answers so patient and exact that I used to copy them twice, once for the file and once for myself, folded into my coat pocket like a talisman.

I read them all. I told myself it was diligence. Diligence, I find, is a very comfortable word. It fits over curiosity the way a glove fits over a stolen apple.

* * *

But the German letters — the letters from Dr. Greta Lindqvist — those were different, and I knew they were different, and I steamed them open anyway.

They arrived roughly monthly, blue-bordered, addressed in that upright hand to *Miss O. Marchand, Wren Hollow,* with no title, no honorific, nothing that would tell a censor or a curious postmistress what the contents might be. Inside, the handwriting loosened. The letters were long. They were, I realized early on, strange.

Not improper-strange. Strange in their subject matter. Here is a specimen, from March of that year, which I copied into my notebook because something about it itched:

*My dear O — The gagea is up already, weeks early, and I have pressed a sprig for you though I know customs men confiscate flowers now as though a petal could carry a revolution. The botanic garden is reorganizing its alpine section and Herr Direktor has given my greenhouse to a man who cannot tell moss from mold. I have said nothing. You would be proud of my silence, or alarmed by it, knowing you as I do. Tell me about the orchard. Is the medlar alive? You never answer about the orchard directly, which I have decided to find charming rather than evasive. The swallows returned Thursday. Yours, G.*

That was typical. Four pages of botany, weather, petty academic grievances, and swallows. Not one word — not in four years of letters, and I had gone back through the files, I confess it freely — not one word about the Reichstag, the Kaiser, the naval estimates, the arms race, any of the thunderous subjects filling the newspapers. While the whole of Europe talked of nothing else, these two women wrote to each other about moss.

I found it restful, actually. That was my verdict at the time, delivered to myself with the confidence of a magistrate: *restful.* Everyone else's correspondence crackled with the age — votes, prisons, hunger strikes, dreadnoughts — and here was one corner of the world where two old friends discussed whether a medlar tree had survived the winter. I remember thinking, folding that letter closed, sealing it with the little stick of gum I'd learned to soften just so, that it must be nice, to have a friendship so far above politics. So safe. So beside the point of everything.

I want you to notice, as I did not, what I had just done. I had read four years of another woman's private letters, decoded nothing, questioned nothing, and concluded that the absence of evidence was evidence of safety. That is not diligence. That is a spectator's arithmetic: *nothing is happening here, therefore nothing is happening.* History, I believed firmly, happened elsewhere — in London, in Berlin, in capitals, in capitals letters — to people whose names appeared in print. It did not happen in kitchens. It did not happen in gardens. It certainly did not happen to a chauffeur reading stolen mail over a kettle.

I was wrong in every particular, and the bill for being wrong arrived within the year. But in April of 1914 the kettle whistled, the seal lifted clean, and I read about swallows.

* * *

At eleven I carried the tray up to the study, as I did every morning: coffee, toast, the sorted letters in their order of importance, and my summaries on a card in my small neat hand. Miss Marchand sat at her desk beneath the window, where the light came in gray and silver off the Downs, doing the Times crossword in ink, which she said was the only honest way to attempt anything.

"The Lindqvist letter," I said. "Botanical. The swallows are back in Heidelberg."

"Early," she said, without looking up. "It was a warm March there. Fill in six across for me. *Firmness of purpose*, eight letters."

"Steeliness?"

"That's nine and it's ugly. Try again."

"Resolve? No, that's seven. *Tenacity.*"

"*Tenacity*," she repeated, writing it in with a flourish. "Yes. Tenacity. You see, Miss Vasquez, the puzzle-setters and I are in agreement about the qualities required." She set down the pen and took up the German letter, and I watched her face while she read it, because watching her face was part of my education, though I'd have denied it under oath.

Her face did very little. That was the thing about Odile Marchand: she had spent thirty years learning to keep it still, in courtrooms and committee rooms and once, famously, on the floor of the Albert Hall while a government minister called her sex a national embarrassment. But I had two years of mornings banked now, and I could read her the way she read a crowd. And that morning, holding the letter about swallows, something moved behind her stillness — quick, low, like a fish under ice. Her thumb rested on the signature a moment longer than reading required.

"She mentions the orchard," I said. It was not in my summary. I hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way things come out of me when I've been quiet too long, which is sideways and slightly too loud.

Miss Marchand looked up. For a moment — I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left — for a moment she looked at me the way one looks at a locked door one had forgotten was in the house. Then it passed, and she was merely my employer again, amused, faintly ironical, entirely closed.

"Do you know, Miss Vasquez, you have a genius for summary. You compress a four-page letter into a sentence and somehow the sentence contains everything and nothing." She tapped the card. "'Botanical. Swallows.' Magnificent. Like a telegram from a very discreet planet." She smiled. "Very well. Draft the reply. The usual courtesies. And add that the medlar is alive, since she asks."

"It doesn't say she asks—"

"It asks," said Miss Marchand, and turned back to her crossword, and that was the end of the audience.

* * *

I drafted the reply that afternoon in the study while she was at her committee in Lewes, and I will admit — since we are being honest now, since honesty is apparently the theme — that I added the line about the medlar exactly as instructed, and then sat looking at it for a long time, feeling the way you feel when you've walked into a room and everyone stops talking.

*The medlar is alive,* I wrote, in my small neat hand, at the dictation of a woman who was not present. *It fruited poorly last autumn but the frost has done it good, I think.*

Why did that sentence itch? I have had five years to answer, and the answer is this: I had read the files. I knew what the outgoing letters contained, because I typed them. Courtesies, health, meeting reports, the usual. In four years, neither woman had mentioned the orchard. Not once. And yet Dr. Lindqvist wrote *you never answer about the orchard directly* — which meant she had asked before, repeatedly, and been deflected, deliberately, by a woman who now ordered me to break the pattern in a single sentence about frost.

There was a code in those letters. I understood this dimly that afternoon, the way you understand a change in the weather — not as knowledge but as pressure behind the ears. Something between those two women ran underneath the swallows and the moss, something old enough to have rules, important enough to hide, and gentle enough, I thought then, to be none of my business.

I sealed the reply. I posted it Wednesday. I forgot it by Friday, or rather I filed it, in the drawer of my mind labeled *Curiosities, Harmless,* which was, I am sorry to say, a very full drawer.

Because that was April, and the world was still arranged the way I liked it: history over there, happening to other people, and me at the edge of the photograph with the engine running.

On the last Tuesday of the month, Mr. Petch slid the letters across the counter and said, "Two local. No foreign this week." And then, because postmasters are the historians of small places and he knew more than he ever let on, he added, mild as milk: "Funny, that. First week in four years she's missed."

I told him people are busy. I told myself the same, all the way home, past the resurfaced road, past the downs going green, telling myself a story about swallows and silence that I very much wanted to believe.

The next foreign letter came eleven days late. It was thinner than usual. And the first line, which I read over the kettle with the fog pressing at the windows, was not about botany at all.

*My dear O — They are asking questions at the garden now, and I find I have less talent for silence than you credited me with. There is something I must ask of you, and I ask it badly, in haste, and about trees — you will understand, you have always understood —*
