## 2. Letters from Across the Water

I had developed a theory about Odile Marchand and her letters, and the theory was this: that she loved Greta Lindqvist more than any person I had ever seen love anybody, and that she would sooner have admitted to theft than said so out loud.

The evidence was considerable. It lived in the way she held the pen when she signed her name at the end of each dictated reply — slower than she did anything else, as though the signature were a small ceremony no one had told me about. It lived in the drawer of her writing desk, which stuck in damp weather and which she always opened with her hip while pretending she hadn't. And it lived most of all in the letters themselves, which arrived from Heidelberg in envelopes gone soft at the corners from crossing the sea, and which Odile read standing up by the window before breakfast, holding them at arm's length like a woman checking herself for faults in a mirror.

My role in all this was officially that of secretary. Unofficially — and here my diligence again, you understand, my thoroughness — I was the one who wrote down what she said.

"Begin," she would say, settling into the chair by the cold fireplace she refused to let me light because "the smoke gets into the paper," though we both knew the paper lived upstairs in the desk. "'My dear Greta.'"

She always began with *my dear Greta*, even when the letter she was answering had made her laugh until she'd had to set down her tea. Even once — and I confess I looked up from my page for this — when it seemed to have made her press her knuckles briefly against her mouth, in the manner of a woman stopping up a word before it could escape.

"Tell her the wisteria has finally done something worth reporting."

"The whole sentence?" I asked. "Or shall I improve on it?"

Odile gave me a look over her spectacles that could have curdled cream at forty paces. "You are not employed to improve on me, Miss Vasquez."

"No, ma'am."

"You are employed to take down what I say and to drive without frightening the horse."

"We don't have a horse, ma'am."

"That is how good your driving is."

I wrote: *The wisteria has finally done something worth reporting.* And then, because I was twenty-three and constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone, I added, "Shall I tell her it's climbed as far as the gutter?"

"It has not climbed as far as the gutter."

"It is touching the gutter."

"It is *near* the gutter." A pause. The fireless hearth ticked. Somewhere outside a rook argued with another rook about nothing, the way rooks do. "Oh, put it down. She'll only write back asking, and then I'll have to answer, and the whole business will grow by two pages over a plant with the moral character of a burglar."

So I put it down. *Near the gutter,* I wrote, and felt the small warm satisfaction of a lie told for a good cause, which is a feeling I would come to know rather better later on, though I didn't know it yet.

* * *

Here is what struck me, in those first weeks of taking dictation, and what I turned over in my mind like a coin I suspected of being foreign currency: the letters never mentioned politics.

Not once. Not a syllable.

This was 1913, remember. Europe was not yet aflame, but it was certainly smoking — you could smell it in the newspapers, which arrived at Wren Hollow smelling of ink and grievance. Every week there was some fresh alarm. Fleets being built. Morocco, Agadir, the Balkans doing what the Balkans did. Parliament shouting itself hoarse. And Odile Marchand, who could not pass a newsstand without buying three papers and reading all of them in the car while I drove and she tutted, Odile wrote to her dearest friend in Germany about wisteria.

About apples, too. About soil. About whether a particular variety of plum would survive a frost, about grafting, about the correct depth at which to set a young tree's roots ("as deep as grief, no deeper" — that one I copied twice just for the pleasure of it). About the angle of light in October. About birds.

Never about Kaiser Wilhelm. Never about navies. Never about suffrage, either, which was its own curiosity, since Odile gave speeches on that subject with such regularity that I had memorized half of them through the wall of my room, and had once been moved to applaud a paragraph on taxation while alone in the scullery, which I hope says something about her powers and something about my loneliness too.

"Greta isn't interested in politics," Odile said once, when I'd asked — carefully, the way you ask about weather that might turn. "She's a botanist."

"I thought botanists liked arguments. All that classification."

"She likes plants." Odile's pen hovered. "Plants don't build dreadnoughts."

"No," I agreed. "Terrible naval architects, the rose family."

And Odile laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and for a moment she looked younger than her fifty-odd years, and I understood that I had been permitted to see something, though I couldn't have said what.

But here is the thing I noticed and did not yet understand: it wasn't only Odile who avoided it. Greta's letters came through the same filter. Four years of correspondence between two brilliant women — one of whom chained herself to railings for causes, if rumor was true, and the other of whom ran a research garden full of specimens other scientists traveled across borders to see — and between them, across all that water, they discussed *compost*.

It was as if two people had built a house together and agreed, without ever saying so, that certain rooms would stay locked. And kept the keys somewhere neither of them would look.

* * *

The dictations followed a ritual, and I will describe the ritual, because rituals are how people tell you things they cannot say.

Wednesdays, mostly. Odile would appear in the morning already dressed for battle — hat pinned, collar high — but instead of ordering the car she would go to the desk, unlock the sticky drawer with her hip, and take out the newest letter from across the water. She read it standing by the window, where the fog off the Channel made a gray wash of the garden. Then she sat. Then she said, "Begin," and, "'My dear Greta.'"

The replies took an hour, sometimes two. She dictated slowly, in complete sentences, correcting herself rarely — she composed the way some women knit, evenly, without looking at their hands. But every few paragraphs she would stop and look out at the fog and be silent for so long that I learned to recognize these silences as part of the composition. Not pauses. Passages. As though the letter had movements, like music, and some of them were played by nobody.

During one of these silences — November, it would have been, the fog thick enough to lean on — I committed what I still consider my greatest act of espionage, and my smallest.

I said, "How long have you known her?"

Odile didn't turn around. "Thirty years."

"Goodness."

"We met at a lecture on alpine flora in Zurich. She corrected the lecturer. Publicly. From the third row." Now she turned, and there was something in her face I had not seen before and would spend years learning to read. "He was wrong about gentians. She was right. She was twenty-two years old and terrified, and she stood up anyway, and I thought — *there*. That is the bravest thing I have ever witnessed, and it was performed over a flower."

"And you've written ever since."

"Every week. Missing perhaps six weeks in thirty years." She counted them, silently — I watched her lips move — and then dismissed the count as inaccurate, or unbearable, or both. "When we began, a letter took nine days each way. Now it takes eleven. We are losing the race against the post, Greta and I, by a day per decade."

"What happens when you lose it entirely?" I asked. I meant it as a joke.

Odile looked at me for a moment too long. "Nothing happens," she said. "We simply keep writing. Letters aren't a race, Miss Vasquez. They're a rope. You hold your end and trust the other woman to hold hers, and the distance stops mattering, because the distance is *held*."

Then she cleared her throat, embarrassed — Odile Marchand, embarrassed, by her own tenderness — and said, "Where were we. 'The plum question.' Take this down properly this time and stop looking at me as though I were a specimen."

I took it down properly. Mostly.

* * *

I want to record one exchange exactly, because I got it nearly word for word in my notebook, and because I have read that notebook so many times the pages have gone soft as cloth.

It was a Sunday evening, out of schedule, which itself was strange — Odile did not break her schedules. There had been a letter that morning, arriving late, and she had read it twice by the window, and then instead of putting it away she had carried it around the house with her all day like a hand warmer, tucked in her sleeve, and I had seen her touch her sleeve perhaps a dozen times to confirm it was still there.

At seven she appeared in the kitchen doorway, where I was eating bread and cheese standing up, which she disapproved of and secretly found funny.

"Paloma."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Take a letter tonight."

"Now?"

"Unless you have an engagement."

I had an engagement with the rest of the cheese. I set it down with the heroism of the truly devoted.

In the study she did not sit by the cold fireplace. She stood at the window a long time, letter in hand now, not in sleeve, and the lamp made a small gold island of us, and beyond the glass the fog pressed like something wanting in.

"'My dear Greta,'" she said. And stopped. Started again. "'My dear Greta — the frost has come early this year, and I find myself thinking of the orchard.'"

There is no orchard near Wren Hollow. Or rather, there is — I knew of it even then, a tangle of abandoned ground past the second hill, which the village called the old Marchand place and which everyone treated as scenery, the way you treat a ruin. But Odile had never once taken me there, and I had never asked, and I filed the mention away in the growing drawer of things I noticed and did not understand.

"'You will remember,'" Odile went on, and her voice had changed — lower, steadier, the voice she used for the ends of speeches, when the hall had gone quiet on its own, "'you will remember what we swore under the fourth tree. I remember it every autumn now. Age does that — it turns promises into furniture. You bump against them in the dark.'"

She paused. Outside, the fog shifted against the glass like an animal settling.

"'I am an old woman in most respects,'" she continued, "'and a coward in several, and I want you to know that I have never regretted the oath, only the years spent honoring it at a distance. When this frost passes — when the season turns — I intend to stand in that ground again. And if the world permits it, I intend to stand there with you. If the world does not permit it'—"

Stop, I almost said. Because her voice had caught on something, a splinter in the sentence, and I had never heard Odile Marchand's voice catch, not chaining herself to railings, heckled by men with rotten vegetables and worse logic.

"If the world does not permit it," she repeated, quietly, "then I will keep the promise alone, and it will be enough, because a promise kept alone is still a promise kept."

Silence. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, keeping its own accounts.

"Sign it," Odile said at last, roughly, in her ordinary voice, as if waking from sleep. "No — wait." She crossed to the desk, took the pen from my hand — took it *from my hand*, which she had never done — and bent over the page herself. For a full minute she wrote in silence, adding something after the signature, small and private, shielding the paper with her arm like a schoolgirl. I looked pointedly at the ceiling. I am proud of that, actually. Even now.

Then she sealed it, and handed it to me, and said only, "First post tomorrow. Don't read this one on the way."

"Ma'am," I said, "I always read—"

"I know what you always do." And she looked at me, tired and fond and older than her years in a way that had nothing to do with age, and said, "That's why I'm asking."

I did not read it on the way. I want that recorded too. Whatever else I was in those days — nosy, lonely, self-deceived in comfortable layers — I could keep a door shut when someone asked me to keep it shut. It was doors that were never marked as doors that I opened.

* * *

The letter went out on Monday's post, eleven words heavier for whatever she had added after signing, and I settled back into the rhythm of our Wednesdays, and the world went on smoking gently at its edges, and I went on believing what I believed: that history was a thing that happened in capitals, to ministers, to men whose names printed in bold. That it happened *elsewhere*. That Wren Hollow was a harbor and harbors are not where storms occur, only where boats wait them out.

On Wednesday she dictated the wisteria correction. On Thursday we drove to Lewes for a suffrage meeting where she spoke for forty minutes and a man in the back row called her a disgrace and she thanked him for his attention with such sweetness that he sat down confused, and on the drive home she hummed, which she only did after victories.

And on Friday morning, coming back from the village with the post, I found among the bills and the pamphlets a thin envelope, foreign stamps, soft-cornered, addressed in that upright continental hand I had begun to know as well as my own.

Greta Lindqvist, answering.

I carried it inside. I set it on the hall table. I even — mark this — left it there, unopened, while I went to fetch Odile from the garden where she was inspecting the wisteria's criminal ambitions.

But as I passed back through the hall, I saw that Odile had come in ahead of me, silently, and was standing over the table with the letter in her hands, and she had not opened it. She was holding it flat between both palms, the way you'd hold a bird — firmly enough that it couldn't fly, gently enough that it wouldn't break — and she was reading the outside of it. The address. The stamps. The postmark.

And her face, in the gray light of that hallway, wore an expression I had never seen on it and would not see explained for a very long time.

It was the face of a woman holding her end of a rope, feeling it pull.
