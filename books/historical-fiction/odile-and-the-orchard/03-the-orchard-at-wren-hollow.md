## 3. The Orchard at Wren Hollow

### Paloma

There was a particular sound Odile Marchand made when she was about to be unreasonable, and on the last Tuesday of June, Paloma heard it through the study door before breakfast had even been cleared.

It was a small hum — two notes, descending — like a woman negotiating with herself and losing.

"You're humming," Paloma said, setting down the teapot.

"I am thinking."

"You hum when you're about to cancel something important."

Odile looked up from her desk with the expression of a duchess caught shoplifting apples. "I have decided," she said, "that today we do nothing whatsoever for the Cause."

Paloma blinked. In three years of service she had seen Odile work through influenza, through a sprained ankle, through the death of her second cousin once removed, and once — memorably — through a wasp sting inside her glove during a deputation to Westminster. The Cause did not take Tuesdays off. The Cause barely took Sundays off, and those only because Odile believed God deserved one day of quiet before He was argued with again.

"It's Thursday," Paloma said.

"Then all the more radical." Odile stood, and there was something in her face that stopped Paloma's next remark before it formed. Not mischief exactly. Something older, and softer, like light coming through a curtain that hadn't been opened in years. "Fetch your walking shoes. The ugly ones. And don't ask questions until we're past the churchyard."

"I wasn't going to ask questions."

"You were composing them. I could hear it."

This was true, so Paloma went upstairs and fetched the ugly shoes.

They walked out of Wren Hollow by the back lane, past the smithy where Mr. Cobb raised his hammer in salute and Odile returned it with a formality that suggested the salute was part of some ongoing negotiation neither party wished to discuss. The village thinned. The hedges grew taller and less disciplined. And then, quite suddenly, the lane ended at a rusted gate leaning against its own post like a drunk against a lamppost, and beyond it lay an orchard.

Not a tidy orchard. An abandoned one — grass to the knee, bramble encroaching from the east wall, apple trees standing in loose ranks like soldiers whose war had ended without anyone telling them. Some had gone wild, throwing up sucker shoots from their bases. Some bore fruit so small and sour it made your jaw ache just to look at it. A stone wall ringed the whole of it, and beyond the wall, the downs rolled green and empty toward the sea.

"Well?" said Odile.

"It's beautiful," said Paloma, and was surprised to find she meant it. There was a quality to neglected places — they didn't perform beauty the way gardens did; they simply were, extravagantly, without supervision.

"We planted six of these trees ourselves," Odile said. "Greta and I. In 1908, when we were students and knew nothing about anything." She pushed the gate open; it groaned like an old man asked to dance. "Come along. Mind the nettles. They hold grudges."

### Odile

The orchard smelled as it always had — grass, bruised windfall, the green copper scent of apple leaves crushed underfoot. Twenty years since she'd stood here last, and the smell had not changed at all, which felt like a rebuke: places remember us better than we remember them.

She walked the row slowly, counting under her breath. First tree — the Cox, planted crooked because Greta had insisted on arguing about soil drainage mid-hole. Second — the Egremont Russet, which Greta had named after her landlady as a joke too obscure to explain and therefore funnier for it. Third and fourth, planted in a single long afternoon while rain came down sideways and both of them were soaked to the skin and laughing so hard they could barely dig.

"Greta," Paloma said behind her. It was not a question. The girl read everything; of course she had kept the name.

"The very same." Odile touched the russet's bark. The tree had grown enormous, its branches heavy, unpruned, reaching out over the wall as if trying to see what had become of the world. "We were nineteen. We'd been reading about American orchardists — how they planted in rows, scientifically, spacing calculated for light. Very modern. We thought we were revolutionizing English agriculture." She smiled at the memory of their own magnificent ignorance. "We had one spade between us, a book borrowed from the college library that we never returned, and forty shillings scraped together from allowances."

"And the sixth?"

"The sixth is over there." Odile pointed to the far corner, where a tree stood apart from the others, smaller, twisted, growing at an angle as though it had spent its whole life listening to something. "The pear. We quarreled over it. Greta wanted a pear; I wanted another apple. She won, because Greta always won arguments conducted in German, and my German was terrible then." It was better now. Fourteen years of letters had taught her. "The tree sulked for a decade. Look at it now — it fruits every year, stubborn as its advocate."

"Where is she now?" Paloma asked. Carefully, the way you'd lift something fragile. "Germany?"

"Jena, last I had word. She runs the botanical garden there. Orchids, mostly. She writes me—" Odile paused, hearing the sentence before finishing it, choosing among truths. "She writes me long letters. About orchids, mostly."

*About orchids,* Paloma thought, and Odile saw the thought cross her face like a bird crossing a window, and pretended not to. There would be time for what the letters held. Today was not for holding things. Today was for giving something away.

"There are seven positions in the row," she said instead, walking to the gap at its end, where a seventh hole had been dug in 1908 and never filled. Grass had long since reclaimed it; only she and Greta would ever have known it was there. "We planned seven. Planted six. The seventh was left for—" She stopped. Straightened her hat. "For later. We always meant to come back and plant it together."

"When later comes?"

"That was the general theory, yes."

Paloma crouched by the empty spot, and Odile watched her run her fingers over the grass as if reading it. The girl had hands that noticed things. It was why Odile tolerated the letter-reading, which she was quite aware of and had never once mentioned — because a woman who reads closely enough will eventually understand everything worth understanding, and that, in a secretary, was worth more than discretion. Discretion could be hired. Understanding had to grow.

"Why today?" Paloma asked. "Why bring me here?"

Because, Odile thought, I have carried this place alone for twenty years and I am tired of carrying it alone. Because you have been three years in my house reading my mail and you still believe history happens somewhere else, to other people, in newspapers. Because this ground is where my history actually happened, and I want one person to know it before—

Before what? She didn't know. That was the trouble with the present tense: you never got to read ahead.

"Because it's Thursday," she said, "and I told you we're being radical," and offered her arm, and Paloma took it, and they walked the rows together like two women inspecting troops who had long ago stopped expecting inspection.

### Paloma

They sat afterward against the wall in the sun, sharing bread and cheese and a thermos of tea that Odile had ordered cooked that morning without explanation — which, Paloma now understood, explained itself.

An orchard. Six trees and an argument. Two girls with one spade and forty shillings. It should have been a small story, the kind people tell once at dinner and forget. But the way Odile told it — the crooked Cox, the landlady-named russet, the sulking pear — it came alive in Paloma's chest like something planted there too.

Paloma had belonged to places before, technically. A flat above a bakery in Bermondsey that smelled of yeast and other people's dinners. A room in a boarding house in Clapham where the landlady counted the towels. She had lived in eleven places by age twenty-four and belonged to none of them; belonging, she'd assumed, was something that happened to people with gardens and grandmothers, not to girls who read other women's mail for a living.

But sitting here, her shoulder almost touching Odile's, the bees working the wildflowers, the pear tree leaning its crooked ear toward them — she felt the strange, alarming sensation of a root going down. As if she'd been a cutting in water all these years and someone had finally put her in soil.

"The seventh position," she said. "You've really waited sixteen years?"

"Seventeen this autumn."

"What are you waiting for?"

Odile was quiet a moment. A bee investigated the cheese, reconsidered, left.

"Some promises aren't mine to keep alone," she said at last. "That's rather the point of them." She turned, and her eyes had the look Paloma had learned meant a decision had been made somewhere below decks and was rising. "Someday — perhaps soon — I'll tell you the rest of it. What the seventh tree is for. Why we left it." Her mouth curved. "You'll probably read it in a letter first anyway."

Paloma felt her ears go hot. "I don't—"

"Paloma." Odile's voice was mild as milk. "In four years you have never once sealed a letter before reading it. You lick the envelope like a woman defusing a bomb. I have eyes."

"I read them for diligence. If a letter contains something requiring action—"

"—you must know it requires action. Yes. Diligence." Odile passed her the thermos cup with great solemnity. "Drink your tea, Miss Vasquez. Your diligence has earned it."

And because there was no defense, Paloma drank her tea, and found she was smiling into the cup, and let herself smile.

They stayed until the light went long and gold across the rows. Odile showed her how to tell the varieties by leaf. Paloma learned the difference between a sucker and a watershoot and would retain approximately none of it. On the walk back, at the gate, Odile paused with her hand on the rusted iron and looked back at the six trees standing in the low sun, and her face did something private and old.

"Sixteen years," she murmured, "and it's still waiting for us," and Paloma understood she was speaking to someone not present, and said nothing, and kept the silence the way she kept everything else — carefully, and first.

### Odile

That evening, back at the house, Odile sat at her desk with the day still warm in her bones and did a thing she had not done in months: she wrote to Greta about the orchard.

*The russet has gone vast and ungovernable. It reaches over the wall now like a scandal. The pear still leans — you'll be pleased — and the seventh position waits, as patient as you were impatient. I brought my secretary to see it today. She has good hands and reads everything, which sounds like an accusation but is in fact the highest praise I know how to give. You would like her. You would disapprove of her politics and adore her entirely.*

She signed it, weighed it, and set it in the outgoing tray for Paloma to post tomorrow — knowing, smiling slightly, that it would be read tonight by lamplight, and that somewhere across the North Sea a woman with ink-stained fingers would laugh at a joke about a scandalous apple tree, and that this — this slow conversation conducted in fruit and seasons and small domestic news — was the realest thing she possessed.

Outside, the summer dark came down gently on Wren Hollow. In the village, a man was nailing a notice to the parish board. Odile did not see him. Nobody much did, at that hour.

The notice concerned a census. It concerned Sunday next. And it contained, in its small printed list of questions, one question that no one in the village gave a second glance — a question about names, and households, and correspondents — that would reach into the warm heart of that evening's letter and close around it like weather changing.

### Paloma

Paloma posted the letter herself the next morning, walking to the pillar box on the high street in the new sunlight, and she did not seal it unread. She read it twice — once for diligence, once for the pleasure of it — and folded it back along its creases with something that felt, for the first time in her life, less like snooping and more like keeping.

*You would disapprove of her politics and adore her entirely.*

She wondered, licking the envelope with unaccustomed gentleness, who *her* was supposed to be. She wondered why the thought of Greta Lindqvist, whom she'd never met, made her feel like a character in a story she hadn't been told the beginning of. She wondered why Odile had chosen yesterday — of all days — to open a door she'd kept shut for three years.

History, Paloma believed, happened elsewhere. In newspapers. In London. To other people.

On Sunday, a census taker would arrive at the door of Wren Hollow, and begin, politely, to teach her otherwise.
