## 4. One Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the days nothing happened at Wren Hollow — the days Odile spent hunched over her correspondence and I spent pretending the motorcar didn't need its tires looked at again. A Tuesday was a day you could count on. And so of course that was the day a man in a grey suit came up the drive with a leather satchel and an air of official purpose, asking questions.

I saw him first from the kitchen window, where I was engaged in my usual morning negotiation with the stove, which had opinions about coal and expressed them by smoking. He was walking up the gravel with the measured tread of a man who has been given a list of houses and no particular affection for any of them. Behind him the hedge moved in the wind, and beyond the hedge the downs went on being beautiful in their indifferent way, as they always did, as though history were something that happened strictly below sea level.

"Paloma," I called through to the study, because Odile insisted I call her Paloma-less things like *Miss Marchand* in front of strangers and I insisted back that warning her was only courteous. "There's a gentleman coming up the drive."

"What sort of gentleman?" Her voice floated out, distracted. She was mid-sentence in a letter to Mrs. Fawcett's office, or possibly mid-argument; with Odile the two were often indistinguishable.

"The sort with a satchel."

A pause. Then, sharper: "What kind of satchel?"

I went to the door and squinted. "Leather. Buckled. He has a pencil behind his ear like a carpenter and a face like a Sunday sermon. I'd say government, but government usually sends nicer coats."

That got her out of her chair. Odile Marchand could ignore a bishop at her gate without troubling herself, but she rose for bureaucracy the way other women rose for fire alarms.

* * *

His name was Mr. Pellow, and he was perhaps forty, with the damp handshake of a man who washed his hands often and dried them never. He set his satchel on our hall table as if it were a small animal he was tired of carrying.

"Census of 1911," he announced, producing a ledger with the reverence of a priest unveiling relics. "Every person who slept under this roof on the night of April the second must be entered. Names, ages, occupations, places of birth." He smiled at us both, a smile with the warmth of a receipt. "It is a legal requirement, madam."

"Yes," said Odile, in the tone she used when agreeing with something she intended to survive rather than endorse. "We've had the papers through. Sit down, Mr. Pellow. Paloma, tea."

I went, because going gave me a reason to come back slowly, and coming back slowly gave me a reason to listen.

Here I should confess something, since this account appears to be a confession from beginning to end: by April of 1911 I had been reading Odile's post for two years, and I had long ago stopped calling it diligence even in the privacy of my own head. It was not diligence. Diligence does not know which correspondents use violet ink. Diligence does not have favorites. I read the letters because Wren Hollow was quiet and I was lonely, and because every envelope that crossed our threshold seemed to promise that somewhere, someone's life was happening — real life, with stakes in it — and reading it secondhand was the closest I had yet come to having one myself.

Odile knew. I was nearly certain Odile knew. She had never said a word about it, which either meant forgiveness or strategy, and with Odile those were frequently the same thing.

So when Mr. Pellow uncapped his pencil and began, I listened the way I always listened: openly, habitually, telling myself it meant nothing.

"Name of head of household?"

"Marchand. Odile Marchand."

"Occupation?"

"Writer. Lecturer." A beat. "Suffragist, if your form has room for the truth."

Mr. Pellow's pencil hovered. "I'll put *lecturer*," he decided, with the pragmatism of a man who had forms to finish before dark. "Others in the household?"

"One. Paloma Vasquez. Chauffeuse and secretary. Spanish father, English mother, born — Paloma, what did we decide? Lewes?"

"Lewes," I confirmed from the doorway, teapot in hand, though we had decided nothing of the kind and both of us knew my papers said Bristol. There are loyalties one owes an employer, and there are loyalties one owes oneself, and in April 1911 I had not yet learned the difference between them; I simply answered *Lewes* because Odile had said it first, and because saying it made me, briefly, a woman with a tidy history.

Mr. Pellow wrote it down. The pencil scratched. Outside, a wren sang in the hedge with more conviction than either of us would manage all morning.

"And do you receive correspondence from abroad?" he asked.

I want to be fair to Mr. Pellow. He asked it lightly. It was, after all, a reasonable question — the census form did ask about persons temporarily abroad, and he was a thorough man doing a thorough job, and the sun was shining and the kettle was warming and nothing, nothing at all, was wrong.

But the room changed. Not dramatically. That was the thing I would remember later, in the years when I remembered everything: the room changed the way weather changes, not with thunder but with a shift in the light, a stillness in the hedge, a pressure behind the ears that says *rain coming* to people who grew up watching skies.

Odile's teacup paused halfway to its saucer. It completed the journey half a second later, perfectly, but I had seen the pause, and Mr. Pellow had seen the pause, and I think all three of us sat inside that half-second together like passengers in a carriage whose horse has faltered.

"Correspondence," Odile repeated pleasantly. "Everyone receives correspondence, Mr. Pellow. I receive bills. My secretary receives bills. Between us we keep the Post Office solvent."

"I mean letters," he said, still pleasant, still writing nothing. "From overseas. The form asks after household members abroad, you see, and some folk keep up friendships across the water. It helps the enumeration." He said *enumeration* the way some men say *duty*, as a shield against interruption.

"Why ever would it matter?"

"It doesn't matter, madam." He smiled his receipt smile. "It's only a question."

And here is what I understood, sitting very still with the teapot: that it was only a question, and that it was not only a question, and that I could not have explained the difference to save my life. Dread arrived in me quietly, the way weather arrives, and like weather it did not require my permission or offer reasons.

"Greta Lindqvist," said Odile.

My heart performed a small, unauthorized maneuver.

"A botanist," Odile continued, in the bright, level voice she used for hecklers at lectures — the voice that said *I am answering you and you will regret having asked*. "Of the Jena Institute. We were students together, years ago. She writes to me about orchards. You'll forgive me if I fail to see the national significance of grafting techniques."

"Not at all, madam." Pellow wrote the name down. I watched him write it — *Lindqvist, Greta* — and watched the way his pencil slowed on the word *abroad*, and the way his eyes flicked up afterward, quick and professional, taking inventory of us the way a customs man takes inventory of luggage. "German institute, you said?"

"Bavarian training, Swedish family, German appointment. Europe is full of such mixtures, Mr. Pellow; most of them harmless."

"Mm." He wrote something else, smaller, in the margin. I know margins. I live in margins. I could tell you the exact shape of that marginal note, though I never saw its contents: it was the shape of a man filing something away for a future self.

I poured the tea. My hands were steady. This surprised me. Inside, some part of me had begun pacing like a cat shut in a room, but my hands poured four years of practice into three cups without a tremor, and I thought, absurdly, *this is what chauffeuring teaches you — the road can frighten you all it likes, but your hands belong to the wheel.*

"Is Miss Lindqvist currently abroad?" Pellow asked.

"She is in Germany," Odile said. "Where she lives. Where she has always lived. Where I have never lived, and where, I suspect, neither of us plots anything more treasonous than the pruning season."

He laughed. It was a polite laugh, and it fooled neither of us, and I don't think it was meant to. That was the strangest part — how civilised it all was. No accusations. No drama. A man drank our tea, complimented the marmalade (which was my marmalade, and deserved the compliment), gathered his ledger, buckled his satchel, and took Greta's name away with him as casually as a man pocketing a windfall apple.

At the door he turned. "Oh — one more thing, madam. The letters from Miss Lindqvist. They come regular, do they?"

"They come when she writes," said Odile.

"Only the village postmistress mentioned—" He stopped himself with professional grace. "No matter. Good day to you both. Thank you for the tea."

He walked down the drive with his measured tread, and the hedge swallowed him, and the wren started up again as though nothing had happened, because wrens have the luxury of believing nothing happens.

* * *

For a full minute after the gate clicked, neither of us spoke. Then Odile said, "Your marmalade impressed him," and I said, "He took notes on it," and we both laughed, too quickly, the way people laugh when the alternative is asking questions.

She carried the cups into the study. I followed. I always followed; following was my profession.

"He'll write it up tonight," she said, standing at the window with her back to me. "Names, dates, the marginal note. Some clerk in London will file it, and no one will look at it for years, and it will mean nothing whatsoever."

"You don't believe that."

"No," she agreed, turning. "But isn't it remarkable, the things one can say aloud that one doesn't believe? It's practically the national pastime."

"Odile." I never called her Miss Marchand when we were alone; she had forbidden it in the first month, on the grounds that we shared a house, a motorcar, and an unreasonable quantity of secrets, and secrets make colleagues of anyone. "Why did he care about the letters?"

"Because the world is beginning to care about borders," she said, "and Greta lives on the wrong side of a line that hasn't been drawn yet. Men like Mr. Pellow don't ask questions because they suspect anything. They ask them because someday they may want to have already known." She said it lightly, tossing it off like a coat, and then she sat down at her desk and picked up her pen, and that was that, apparently.

But I stood in the doorway a moment longer, and I looked at the writing table — at the stack of unopened post with its foreign stamps, at the drawer where the answered letters lived, at the whole quiet architecture of a friendship conducted in ink across a stretch of cold water — and I felt the weather changing in earnest now. Not rain. Something with more weight behind it, moving slow and far off, the way you feel a storm before you can name it.

That night I did something new. I took the most recent letter from Greta Lindqvist — the one with the violet ink and the pressed sprig of blossom between pages three and four — and instead of merely reading it, I copied out the address on a slip of paper and put the slip in my coat pocket, where it stayed, small as a ticket, for a long time afterward. I couldn't have told you why. Habit, I told myself. Diligence.

It was neither. It was the instinct of a woman who has just realized that the mail she reads is not other people's history arriving at her door.

It is hers, already, sitting on the hall table, smelling faintly of violets.

Two nights later, Odile burned nothing and said less, and the newspapers began to shout. But that is getting ahead of the story. On the evening of the census, we ate supper as usual, and Odile told a story about Greta falling into the Rhine chasing a specimen, and we laughed properly this time, and outside the stars came out over the orchard, all seven trees of it, patient as ledgers, waiting to be counted too.
