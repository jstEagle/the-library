## 5. War Is Declared Elsewhere

Odile Marchand had always believed that the great events of history would announce themselves with some dignity — a hush, perhaps, or a bell, or at minimum a decent interval of gravity. Instead, they announced themselves with Mr. Hobbes, the village newsagent, standing on a chair in the middle of Wren Hollow's high street, bellowing like a man selling fish.

"WAR!" he cried, waving a newspaper over his head as though it were on fire and he was proud of it. "GERMANY REFUSES! WAR DECLARED AT ELEVEN O'CLOCK!"

The church bells took up the announcement before anyone had time to be properly horrified about it, which Odile thought was cheating. A person deserved at least a quarter hour of silence to absorb the end of the world. Instead she got the bells of St. Aldhelm's and Mrs. Penhallow weeping theatrically into her good shawl while also, somehow, arranging for everyone to see that she was doing it.

Odile stood quite still beside her motorcar, which Paloma had just polished to an obscene shine, and felt the strangest sensation: as though the ground had shifted under her feet and yet nobody else had noticed. They were all cheering.

That was the part she could not get past. Not the war itself — she had watched it assembling for weeks the way one watches weather come up a valley, grey and patient and inevitable. She had read Greta's letters from Berlin with growing dread, the botany increasingly thin, the silences between paragraphs increasingly loud. No. The war she could have accepted, in the grim way one accepts hail. It was the cheering she could not survive.

A boy ran past her with a paper hat made from yesterday's *Express*. Somewhere a gramophone struck up — she recognized it with horror as "Rule, Britannia." An elderly veteran whom Odile had known for fifteen years as a quiet man who grew excellent marrows was now beating time with his cane and shouting something about teaching the Kaiser a lesson.

*Elsewhere*, she thought. That is what they believe. War is declared elsewhere. It happens elsewhere, to other people, in newspapers, in capitals whose names they cannot spell. And then one day it is a boy from the next farm face-down in Flanders mud, and everyone acts astonished, as though history had crept into the village wearing a disguise.

She knew better. She had known since Sunday, when Greta's last peacetime letter arrived — postmarked the fourth of August, written in a hand noticeably less steady than usual — and said nothing about botany at all except one line: *The herbarium has been closed by order of the government. They say my specimens are now enemy property. As am I, apparently.*

*As am I, apparently.* Greta Lindqvist, who had once spent an entire summer teaching Odile to tell a hawthorn from a blackthorn by taste alone, was now a category. Enemy property. Filed somewhere in Berlin alongside confiscated crates and shuttered laboratories.

Odile looked down at the newspaper someone had pressed into her hands — she did not remember who — and saw the headline shouting about German atrocities already committed, already proven, already beyond doubt, though the fighting had barely begun. She folded it neatly. She did not crumple it. She would remember later that she had folded it neatly, and take a strange comfort in that.

"Miss Marchand!" Mrs. Penhallow had recovered sufficiently to seize her arm. Her eyes were red but her grip was triumphant. "You'll sign the pledge, of course. We're drawing one up at the vicarage. Every loyal household."

"A pledge," Odile said.

"Of loyalty! To King and country. And against—" Mrs. Penhallow lowered her voice to a register that was somehow louder than her speaking voice, "—the enemy within. You know how it is. One can't be too careful. My Herbert says half these foreign professors are spies."

"How many foreign professors do you imagine there are in Somerset, Mrs. Penhallow?"

"Well. None that we know of," said Mrs. Penhallow, with the air of a woman delivering a devastating point. "That's exactly what makes them so dangerous."

Odile smiled. It was an old smile, well maintained, the one she wore at garden parties and suffrage meetings when a heckler got going — the smile of a woman listening patiently while privately composing her rebuttal. It had served her through twenty years of public life. It served her now.

"I shall consider the pledge," she said, "at length."

Which meant no, and Mrs. Penhallow probably knew it meant no, and both women pretended otherwise, because England in August 1914 was already learning to run on pretending.

* * *

Paloma drove them home along the coast road with the top down, because Odile had asked for it and Paloma, who understood everything without being told, had not asked why. The wind off the Channel was warm and smelled of salt and cut hay, and it was very hard to believe in a war. Behind them the village bells went on ringing, fainter and fainter, until Odile could almost pretend they had been ringing for something else entirely — a wedding, a fete, an ordinary Tuesday.

"You didn't cheer," Paloma said at last. She kept her eyes on the road. She always kept her eyes on the road; she drove the way she did everything, with a fierce competence that Odile found quietly moving and never mentioned.

"No," Odile said.

"The Marrow Man was crying, actually. Mr. Tregose. I don't think anyone noticed but me."

Odile turned to look at her driver — this young woman with the Spanish name and the English stubbornness who had been reading her mail out of habit for two years now, thinking herself invisible, and who had evidently noticed old Tregose weeping among the flag-wavers when nobody else had.

"Why do you suppose he was crying?" Odile asked.

Paloma considered the question properly, which was one of the things Odile loved about her — she never gave the quick answer when the true one was available. "Because he's fought," she said finally. "And everyone else hasn't. It's like watching people applaud a play you've seen. And you know how it ends."

Something tightened behind Odile's ribs. *It's like watching people applaud a play you've seen.* Yes. And Odile had seen this particular play before, in another theatre, in another lifetime — 1870, she had been a girl in a boarding school near Dieppe, and the Franco-Prussian war had come, and her dearest friend at school had been Prussian, and Odile had learned at sixteen what it was to love someone the newspapers had decided was your enemy. The friend's name had been Ilse. Ilse had been sent home in disgrace, weeping on the platform at Dieppe, and Odile had stood there holding their shared copy of a botany primer, and the whole world had taught her its first great lesson: that nations are invented things that eat actual people.

She had sworn, at sixteen, never to let it happen twice.

And here she was, fifty-three years old, with a letter in her writing desk from a German woman she loved more than she had ever loved anyone she was permitted to say so aloud, while the bells rang and Mrs. Penhallow drew up pledges and the whole green island braced itself to be brave.

"Paloma," she said, "when we get home, I should like tea. And then I should like not to be disturbed for the rest of the afternoon."

"Right you are, miss."

"And Paloma — the post. If anything comes today, or tomorrow, or any day after — bring it to me directly. Unopened."

There was a pause long enough that Odile glanced over. Paloma's face did something complicated and then settled into blankness, which from Paloma was practically a confession.

"Unopened," Paloma repeated.

"Yes. All of it. From now on."

"As you like, Miss Marchand." A beat. "Though I'll say the post's been thin lately. Since the ports got slow."

"I know," Odile said gently, and Paloma flushed to the ears, and Odile looked back at the road ahead and let her have her dignity.

* * *

The house at Wren Hollow was small and ugly and Odile adored it — a squat Georgian box her father had bought as a joke because it came with four acres and a ruined orchard, and which she had kept because ruined orchards, like certain friendships, only need tending to come back. Paloma brought the tea up to the study herself and set it down and, for once, left without commentary, which told Odile that her own face must have been saying something.

She locked the door anyway.

Then she sat at her desk and unlocked the lower drawer and took out the letters.

Four years of them. She had kept every letter Greta Lindqvist had ever sent her, arranged chronologically, tied with garden twine, because Odile was a sentimental woman who disguised herself as a practical one and fooled nearly everybody. She untied the twine now and spread the most recent ones across the desk blotter and read them again, all of them, hunting — as one hunts in a dream, knowing the thing is there and unable to find it — for anything dangerous. Anything that could hang a woman. Anything Mrs. Penhallow's pledge or a zealous constable might make much of.

There was nothing. There was, deliberately, magnificently, nothing.

*The plum trees at Dahlem are fruiting badly this year. Greta blames the late frost; the director blames the pigeons; I blame the director.*

*I have enclosed seeds of the white campion. Do not plant them near the wall; they sulk.*

*You ask after my health. I am forty-nine and my knees have opinions. Otherwise the same as ever, which is to say: missing you, and refusing to write so.*

Four years of orchards. Of seasons. Of trees and frosts and birds and soil, and never — not once, not in four years — politics. Anyone intercepting this correspondence would conclude that two middle-aged women had nothing between them but horticulture, which was precisely what they were meant to conclude. What no interceptor could know was that the horticulture was the language, and that beneath every sentence about plum trees ran a second conversation, conducted in a code two women had built together over thirty years, in orchards and laboratories and one unforgettable night in 1910 under a half-planted row of apple trees, when they had sworn an oath that seemed sweet and safe at the time and now sat in Odile's chest like a stone.

*Pacifism*, whispered through botany. Peace societies, and congresses, and names passed carefully, and pamphlets carried inside seed catalogues — all of it dressed up as weather and fruit. Greta had written it; Odile had distributed it through the suffrage network, which moved papers across borders the way rivers move leaves, unremarked, because who watches a parcel of seeds?

Nobody. So far, nobody.

But that was before. Before eleven o'clock this morning, when war stopped being elsewhere.

Odile rose and stood at the window with her tea going cold, looking down at the orchard. The six trees she and Greta had planted — six of seven, the row unfinished these four years — stood in the evening light, patient as ever, asking nothing. Plant the seventh tree, Greta had written once, years ago, laughing: *when we are old and the world is sensible, we will finish the row, and the apples will be our monument, and no historian will ever know what we actually meant by it.*

The world was not sensible. The world had gone mad at eleven o'clock this morning, loudly, with bells.

And here was the thing Odile could not explain to Mrs. Penhallow or the vicar or even, fully, to herself: she was not afraid of being caught. Well — she was, of course, in the ordinary way, the way one is afraid of falling. But underneath that fear lay a stranger one. She was afraid of becoming the kind of woman who burned letters. She had watched the century arrive full of men talking about purification and loyalty and the enemy within, and she had understood early that the first act of every such regime is always small and domestic: a hearth, a match, a handful of paper. History begins with burning letters. Everything after is administration.

So she did not burn them. She sat down instead and read every word again, slowly, like a miser counting coins, and then retied them with the garden twine and put them back in the drawer and locked it, and sat with her hand flat on the wood for a long moment, the way one rests a hand on the flank of a sleeping animal one intends to protect.

* * *

The next days established a rhythm that frightened Paloma — Odile could see it frightening her, though neither of them said so — because it was the wrong way round from what everyone expected.

In the village, the war grew louder daily. Recruitment posters bloomed on the noticeboard like aggressive fungi. The vicar worked *duty* into every sermon. Young men Odile had known since they were boys stealing apples came by the house in their new uniforms to say goodbye, grinning, embarrassed, wanting to be told they'd be splendid, and Odile told them, because what else does one tell them, and hated herself for the ease of it. Mrs. Penhallow organized a knitting circle for socks. Someone put a German-made piano out in the lane and smashed it, and the crowd cheered, and Odile stood at her gate and watched the wreckage and thought: *there. There it is. First the pianos.*

But at Wren Hollow, Odile went quiet. She answered no letters — there were none to answer; the mails to Germany had stopped as if a door had been shut in the face of the entire continent. She wrote nothing for the suffrage papers, pleading pressure of work. She declined three engagements and gave no reason. She walked the orchard every morning at seven, alone, whatever the weather, and stood before the six trees as if taking attendance.

And she burned nothing.

Paloma, who had lived through enough of Odile's rages to expect fire — Odile in a temper was a conflagration, doors slammed, inkwells endangered, at least once a hat sacrificed — had clearly braced herself for flames. Odile caught her watching the fireplace in the study with an expression of professional vigilance, waiting for the burning to start.

It did not start. Nothing started. Odile simply went on, day after day, folding her hands, drinking her tea, walking her orchard, smiling her public smile at the village and saying nothing true to anyone.

On the sixth day Paloma cracked. Odile heard her before she saw her — the particular clatter of a tray set down harder than necessary — and looked up from the seed catalogue she was not reading to find her driver standing rigid in the doorway with fury coming off her like heat off a road.

"Beg pardon, miss," Paloma said, in the voice of someone begging no pardon whatsoever, "but I've brought the post, and I'm to bring it unopened, and I've done it, three weeks running, and I'll go on doing it. But I want it noted" — her chin came up; she had a chin like a figurehead and used it accordingly — "that you've stopped writing altogether. Not a line. Not to your editors, not to your sister, nobody. And you don't burn anything either. And I don't know which frightens me worse, if you want the truth, because a person who burns things is angry, and a person who burns nothing is—"

She stopped. Odile waited, hands folded, heart going oddly.

"—is waiting," Paloma finished, quieter.

The clock ticked. Out in the orchard a blackbird was making its opinions known.

"You're quite right," Odile said at last. "I am waiting. Though not, I think, for what you fear."

"For what then?"

For the knock, Odile did not say. For the constable at the door, polite and terrible, asking after correspondence abroad. For the moment when the war stopped being elsewhere and arrived, as it always arrives, as a uniform on the doorstep and questions about one's private life. She was waiting the way a house waits for weather it can hear coming up the valley.

But she looked at Paloma Vasquez — twenty-four years old, loyal past all sense, reading mail she wasn't supposed to and lying badly about it and standing in the doorway demanding truth with the whole force of her small furious body — and Odile thought, with a pang that was equal parts terror and tenderness: *she doesn't know what she's guarding. When the knock comes, she'll open the door before I can stop her. And whatever she says to them, it will cost her something she hasn't learned to pay yet.*

"I'm waiting for autumn," Odile said, "and the apple harvest, and better news, in whichever order they choose to arrive. Now — thank you, Paloma. Leave the tray. And close the door on your way out, if you would."

Paloma held her gaze one second longer than was strictly employable, then went. The door clicked shut.

Alone, Odile unlocked the drawer, took out the newest letter — the last one, postmarked the fourth of August — and read the final paragraph again, the one she had read forty times, the one she had not shown anyone and would not show anyone:

*If this reaches you, keep it. Keep all of them. Whatever comes, whatever they ask — you were never anything but my fellow gardener, and I was never anything but yours. Plant the seventh tree, Odile. Finish the row. Promise me the world will have to dig through thirty years of apples to reach us.*

Outside, far off down the valley, a car engine that did not belong to any neighbour turned in at the Wren Hollow lane.

Odile put the letter back, locked the drawer, and stood up, smoothing her skirt, arranging her face into calm. Through the window she could see the motorcar — dark, official, unfamiliar — coming up the drive at a speed that suggested the driver had somewhere better to be.

Not the army. Too soon, she thought. Too soon, and yet exactly on time.

"Paloma," she called, pleasantly, through the door, and her voice did not shake at all, which she counted among the day's small victories. "Paloma, we have a visitor."
