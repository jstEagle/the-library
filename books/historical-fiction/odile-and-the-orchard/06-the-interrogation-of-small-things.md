## 6. The Interrogation of Small Things

The constable arrived on a Tuesday, which felt somehow appropriate. Tuesdays were the day Odile Marchand paid the tradesmen's bills, corrected proofs, and generally conducted the small administrative machinery of her life with the brisk efficiency of a woman who believed civilization could be maintained through punctuality alone. If authority was going to come knocking, it ought to come on a Tuesday.

I heard the trap before I saw it — the particular clop of a police horse is heavier than a farmer's cob, more deliberate, as though the animal itself understood the gravity of its employment. I was in the kitchen scalding milk when the sound came up the lane, and by the time I reached the front step, Constable Prewitt was already dismounting, his boots landing in our gravel with two crisp reports like distant gunfire.

I want to say I knew immediately why he'd come. I did not. My first thought was that someone had reported the motorcar for speeding through Wren Hollow village — I had been guilty of this twice the previous week, once with Odile beside me telling me to go faster. My second thought was that Mrs. Tilling from the post office had finally filed her complaint about my habit of collecting the mail at half past ten rather than ten o'clock sharp, an offense I had assumed would one day bring ruin upon us all.

"Miss Vasquez?" He consulted a small notebook even though there was only one of me standing there, and I was not a difficult identification. "Constable Prewitt. Is Miss Marchand at home?"

"She's writing," I said. "She doesn't like to be interrupted before noon."

"I'm afraid I'll have to insist." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, or it was shaped like one, the way a paper flower has the shape of a rose. "It won't take but a few minutes. It's about the census."

My stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution, the way it had done on the ferry crossing to the Isle of Wight years ago, when I'd been eleven and certain the water intended to keep me.

*The census.* Of course. It had been taken in April, while I'd been away in London seeing my sister through a difficult confinement, and Odile had filled out the household schedule herself, alone, with no one watching over her shoulder. She had written down what she had always written down: herself, myself, the gardener Hobbs who came three days a week. But the census man that spring — a Mr. Aldous, nervous and ink-stained — had asked questions. And Odile, I now understood, had answered them honestly, because honesty was her religion, and she had never imagined her honesty might be collected into a ledger and read back to her by a man on a horse.

"Shall I tell her you're here?"

"You shall," said Constable Prewitt, and then, because he was not entirely a machine, "though if she's mid-sentence, I've interrupted better writers than her. My wife reads her column in *The Suffragist* aloud of a Sunday. Doesn't always agree with it," he added hastily, "but she reads it."

I left him on the step with his horse steaming gently in the cold morning air and went up through the house, taking the stairs two at a time and then, on the landing, forcing myself to take them one at a time, because whatever was coming deserved to be walked into, not run from.

Odile was at her desk in the morning room, pen moving, spectacles low on her nose. She looked up at my face before I said a word — she had a genius for reading faces; she said it came from decades of addressing hostile crowds, where the whole audience was one enormous face that must be read line by line.

"Paloma. What is it?"

"A policeman," I said. "About the census. About—" I hesitated, and in the hesitation she went very still, the stillness of a deer that has caught a scent it cannot yet name.

"Show him in," she said quietly, "and stay."

*Stay.* As though I were a maid whose presence would be furniture, invisible and reassuring. Later I would understand that she wanted me there as witness, as memory, as the person who would remember exactly what was said and how. That morning, I simply took my place by the window with the mending basket I had carried everywhere since girlhood as a prop — nobody interrogates a woman with a darning needle — and Odile rose to greet the law of England with her hand extended.

"Constable. You find me at my least eloquent hour."

"Ma'am." He shook the hand, sat when offered the chair, declined tea when offered tea, which marked him at once as either very honest or very cautious. "It's a matter of some routine, I assure you. The census schedules have gone to the County Records Office, and certain... particulars have been flagged for follow-up. Nothing alarming. A formality."

"Flagged," Odile repeated pleasantly. "How modern. I didn't know the census could wave flags."

He laughed, dutifully. Then he opened his notebook, and the laughter died between us like a fire starved of air.

"Now then. The schedule for Wren Hollow records three persons resident on the night of April the second. Yourself. Your driver and secretary, Miss Paloma Vasquez. And..." He turned a page with unnecessary care. "There's a fourth entry. In a different ink. Added after the original schedule was submitted, by way of amendment. A 'G. Lindqvist,' recorded under 'visitors present or expected.'"

The room went quiet enough that I could hear the clock in the hall, which Odile kept deliberately five minutes fast and which ticked now like a small impatient heart.

"My friend Greta Lindqvist visited in March," Odile said. "She departed for the continent on the fourth of April. I amended the schedule to remove her name. The amendment appears to have been misfiled."

"Misfiled," said Constable Prewitt, without inflection, making a note. "And Miss — or is it Mrs.? — Lindqvist. Her nationality?"

"Swedish by birth. Naturalized German, many years ago, before anyone thought it mattered." Odile's voice was level and warm, the voice she used on hecklers, the voice that made them feel foolish for having raised their own. "She is a botanist. A distinguished one. She holds a chair at the University of Heidelberg. Her interest in England extends to apple varieties and nothing whatsoever beyond them."

"Apple varieties," the constable repeated. He wrote this down too, and I watched his pen move and thought, absurdly, *he is writing a poem and does not know it, a terrible little poem about apples and empires.*

"Yes. We studied together, as young women, in Zurich. We planted an orchard together, as it happens, not two miles from this house. There are six trees in it still. You may verify this at your leisure, Constable; I will draw you a map."

Something flickered across his face — the look of a man who had prepared for evasion and been ambushed instead by candor. He rallied. "Very thorough, ma'am. Now, the correspondence. The schedule notes correspondence with an address in Heidelberg, ongoing over — let me see — four years' duration. Weekly frequency."

"We write every week," Odile said. "We have written every week since 1910. Mostly we write about weather, trees, and the various failings of our respective postal services. If the Crown wishes to read four years of letters about grafting techniques, I will happily provide the bundle. I warn you, Constable: it is duller than sermons."

I nearly laughed. I caught it behind the mending basket, but not quite, and the constable's eyes came to me for the first time — properly came, weighing, cataloguing.

"And you are Miss Vasquez. The driver."

"And secretary," I said.

"You handle her correspondence?"

"Some of it."

"Which some?"

"The bills," I said. "The publishers. Fan letters, when they come. Miss Marchand answers her personal mail herself. Always." This was true. It was also, I realized with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there, not entirely true — because I read everything first, and I delivered everything, and four years of Greta Lindqvist's letters had passed through my hands like water through a mill, turning something, grinding something, and I had never once told a soul.

The constable nodded and made another note, and I understood suddenly that my whole life was being distilled into notes in a book by a man whose wife disagreed with the suffrage column but read it aloud anyway, and that these notes would travel upward through offices I could not picture, into drawers I could not open, and lie there waiting, patient as seeds.

"There's just the one further question," Prewitt said, closing the notebook halfway, which I would learn was a technique — close the book, relax the room, ask the real question. "In recent months, has any parcel arrived for Miss Marchand from abroad? Scientific materials, publications, printed matter of any kind? Anything you'd describe as unusual."

"No parcels," Odile said.

"None," I said.

They came out of both of us at once, smooth and simultaneous, like harmony lines in a hymn, and I heard my own voice say *none* and knew it for the first lie of my life, spoken fluently, without heat, in defense of — of what? Of a shipment I had never seen? Of a promise I did not yet know existed? Of Odile's privacy, which I had spent four years violating in the gentlest possible way and now defended like a fortress?

The constable looked from her to me and back again, and I watched him decide something. I don't know what. Perhaps that spinsters and their drivers were loyal creatures after all. Perhaps that apples were apples. He stood, tucked the notebook away, thanked us for our time, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth, ma'am," he said, "my wife says your piece in June about the Cat and Mouse Act was the truest thing printed all year. She cut it out." He cleared his throat. "Mind how you go, all the same. These are times when minding how you go matters more than usual."

Then he was gone, hoofbeats fading down the lane, and Odile sat back down at her desk and picked up her pen, and did not write a single word.

* * *

That evening she sent me to the post office early — quarter past ten, Mrs. Tilling's sacred hour be damned — and I came back with three items: a bill from the seed merchant, a circular from the National Union, and nothing from Germany. Nothing from Germany had become, in the last weeks, its own kind of correspondence. The silence arrived weekly, on schedule, and we received it together.

I found Odile in the kitchen, of all places, standing at the window with a cup of tea gone cold in her hand.

"He'll write it up," she said, without turning. "Whatever he writes will go to some clerk, and the clerk will file it, and if things worsen — and they will worsen, Paloma, they are worsening as we stand here drinking cold tea — someone will pull the file. Four years of letters to Heidelberg. A German national entered on my census schedule. They'll come back with a warrant next time, and it won't be a notebook, it'll be a search."

"Let them search," I said, surprising myself with the ferocity in it. "There's nothing here but apples."

Odile turned then and looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in her face I had never seen directed at me before: a kind of sorrowful gratitude, as though I had handed her a heavy package and she wished I hadn't.

"Is there?" she said softly. "Is there nothing here but apples?"

And she kissed my forehead — she had never done that, in three years of service, not once — and went up to bed, leaving me in the kitchen with the ticking clock and the three-year-old question that had followed me upstairs ever since:

What was in those letters?

I had read them. I had read all of them. Every week for four years I had opened Greta Lindqvist's envelopes and run my eyes over words about *the late frost damaging the perry pears* and *the new rootstock from East Malling* and *do you remember the spring the whole hillside smelled of blossom, we were twenty-two and immortal.* And I had filed them away in the oak cabinet in Odile's study, in order, tied with string by year, thinking: *what devoted friends, how they love their orchard, how they fill their letters with trees.*

Four years. Two hundred letters, give or take. Not one word of politics. Not one mention of armies, of Kaiser or King, of the suffrage cause Odile bled for, of anything that lived outside the orchard rows.

I had always found it charming.

Sitting alone in that kitchen, with the constable's notebook and its patient pages somewhere on the road to London, I began — slowly, reluctantly, the way dawn comes in winter, gray and undeniable — to find it something else entirely.

Nobody writes two hundred letters about apples.

I untied my apron and hung it on its hook, and I made myself a promise that felt less like loyalty than like hunger: tonight, tomorrow, as soon as the house slept, I would take down the bundles from the oak cabinet and read them properly — not as a secretary delivering mail, not as a busybody feeding her loneliness, but as a woman trying to learn, at last, what she had been carrying all these years.

The letters, I suspected, had never been about the trees.

The trees had been about something else.
