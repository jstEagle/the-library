## 7. What the Letters Never Said

I did not sleep that night, which was not unusual, but I slept badly in a new way — not the restless way of a girl with too many thoughts, but the way of a woman who has discovered a locked door inside her own house and cannot remember when it was built.

The constable had gone by teatime, satisfied and slightly flustered, the way men are when a small Spanish driver smiles at them too steadily. "Enemy correspondence," he'd said, as if letters could carry a nationality the way dogs carry distemper. And I had lied — smoothly, cheerfully, with a little laugh placed exactly where a laugh should go — and Odile Marchand had sat in the corner chair with her hands folded and let me do it.

That was the thing that kept me turning over like a pancake that refuses to brown on one side. Not the lie. The letting. She had watched me lie for her and had not blinked, had not flinched, had not so much as adjusted a cushion. A guilty woman flinches. An innocent woman protests. Odile had done neither. She had done what she always did with anything uncomfortable: she had made herself into furniture.

So at half past two in the morning, wearing my coat over my nightdress because Wren Hollow's corridors hold their cold like a grudge, I went down to the study and took out four years of letters.

* * *

You should understand something about how I read mail. It is a habit I fell into during my first month, when Odile's hands were bad with rheumatism and she asked me to open things for her, and it hardened into ritual because — I told myself — a driver who reads is a driver who knows where she's going. If a letter says *come at once to Bath*, I need to have petrol enough for Bath. That was the story I told myself, and it was even true, mostly, the way most self-serving stories are.

But there is reading and there is reading. There is the reading you do standing up, skimming, looking for addresses and urgency. And then there is the reading you do at three o'clock in the morning with the lamp turned low and nobody to perform for.

I spread them across the desk in order. Four years of correspondence between Odile Marchand of Wren Hollow and Greta Lindqvist of — the address had changed twice, Heidelberg then Jena — two women who had apparently never once, in all that time, discussed the war that was coming, the suffrage fight Odile bled for, or anything at all that a reasonable person would call news.

Here is what they discussed instead.

*March 1911.* *The plum trees have come into flower early this year. You would say the blossom is too proud, standing there before the leaves have given it anything to stand on.* And back from Germany: *Our plums here are late and shy. They wait for the leaves. I have always trusted trees that dress before they boast.*

*September 1912.* *The apples dropped heavy and unasked-for, more than we could use. We gave baskets away until the village ran out of gratitude.* Reply: *We lost half the crop to a storm in June. The trees that survived lean eastward now, as if listening.*

*June 1913.* *The new grafts took. Seven of nine. The seventh is the stubborn one — it leafed late and small, but it lives.* Reply: *Keep the seventh alive. Promise me nothing about the others.*

I sat back. My breath had gone strange and shallow, the way it does when you've been holding your stomach in for someone's portrait and forget to let it out.

Because I knew orchards. Not well — I was a mechanic's daughter from Bristol before I was a driver, and my father's idea of agriculture was a well-greased axle — but I knew enough to know that no two women, writing across a continent, described their fruit trees in matching metaphors by accident. Blossom too proud. Trees leaning eastward as if listening. *Keep the seventh alive. Promise me nothing about the others.*

These were not letters about orchards. These were letters *disguised* as letters about orchards, the way a smuggler disguises brandy as communion wine.

And once I saw it, I could not stop seeing it. The whole archive rearranged itself in front of me like a magic lantern show snapping to a new slide. When Odile wrote *the frost came late and spared the south wall*, she was telling Greta something had been narrowly escaped — an arrest, a raid, I didn't know what. When Greta replied *our winters are long here; we keep our roots warm and our branches quiet*, she was not discussing climate. She was describing caution. Survival. The discipline of keeping your head down under a government you did not choose.

Four years. Hundreds of pages. Two women conducting a conversation in front of every censor in Europe while pretending to be two harmless middle-aged ladies with opinions about grafting.

I should have felt clever. Instead I felt the specific, sickening cleverness of a person who has just realized the joke everyone was laughing at included her.

* * *

Because here is the other thing, the smaller and sharper thing, the splinter under the nail: they had done it in front of me.

Every letter I opened. Every letter I read first, standing in hallways and kitchens, telling myself I was being diligent. Every dictated reply I copied out in my round hand while Odile paced behind me saying *the cherry walk needs thinning this year, Paloma, put that down* — and I put it down, obedient as a typewriter, never once asking why a suffragist who hadn't walked her own garden since 1911 cared about the cherry walk.

They had used me. Not against them — with them, unknowingly, as a kind of living cipher wheel. My ordinary eyes, my honest face, my habit of reading everything and understanding nothing. I was the proof of innocence. A letter about plum trees, read aloud by a cheerful foreign girl in a kitchen in Kent — what censor on earth could trouble himself?

Betrayal is supposed to feel hot. This felt cold, and worse, it felt *admiring*. Because it was elegant. It was beautiful, in its way. Two women, oceans apart, hiding a conversation inside the seasons themselves, and the seasons are the one subject no government has ever thought to censor.

By four o'clock I had stopped being angry and started being curious, which is a dangerous progression, and by five I had stopped being curious and started being determined, which is worse still.

At seven, the kitchen clock struck, and upstairs a floorboard announced that Odile Marchand was awake and performing the fiction of a normal day.

I made tea. I carried it to the study, set it down, and did not leave.

She looked up from her blotter, took in my face, and — to her eternal credit — did not pretend to misunderstand. "Ah," she said. "You've read them properly."

"You knew I would."

"Eventually." She gestured at the chair opposite, and I sat, and for a moment we regarded each other across four years of correspondence like two generals who have both just realized the other one has artillery. "I want you to know," she said, "that I argued with Greta about involving you at all. I said you were too young, too honest, too — "

"Too useful?"

"Too *visible*," she said. "A woman who reads everything and suspects nothing is worth ten conspirators. I am sorry for it. I would do it again."

Most people, confessing, offer some softening clause. Odile offered arithmetic. I found, to my considerable irritation, that I respected her for it.

"The seventh tree," I said. "In June 1913. *It leafed late and small, but it lives.* What does it live *as*, Miss Marchand? Because it isn't a tree, and I'd wager my wages it isn't in any orchard."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the fog was doing its slow grey work on the garden, erasing the wall, the gate, the road, leaving only the house floating in white like a ship that has lost its bearings — which is, I suppose, what we all were.

"It lives in Jena," she said quietly. "In a filing cabinet belonging to a German botanist. Alongside eleven years of writing that neither of us will discuss in this room, because this room has ears I have not yet identified." She folded her hands. "You have read our letters, Paloma. All of them. So tell me — what do you think we were saying?"

And I opened my mouth to answer — I had answers, I'd spent the whole night assembling them like a kit — and that was when the knock came at the front door. Three knocks. Brisk, official, entirely unlike a constable's apologetic tap.

Through the frosted glass of the hall, a shape. A hat. And on the mat below the door, slid through the slot a moment later, an envelope — thick, foreign, postmarked weeks ago, held up somewhere in the machinery of two empires learning to distrust their own mail.

Odile rose to fetch it. Her hand, when she picked it up, was not steady, and I noticed — because by then I had learned to notice — that she read the postmark before she read the name.

Her face went white in a way I had only ever seen on other people.

"Paloma," she said, without turning around, "fetch your coat."
