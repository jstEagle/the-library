## 8. Greta's Last Letter

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Odile would remember afterward as the last ordinary day of her life, though of course she did not know it at the time. Tuesdays were for the market. Odile Marchand had kept Tuesdays sacred for eleven years — no meetings, no speeches, no committee work, nothing but the slow business of choosing apples and arguing with the fishmonger about the freshness of his herring, an argument she lost every week and enjoyed losing, because Mr. Pemberton took such visible pleasure in winning and a man needed so little, really, to be happy.

She was in the garden when Paloma brought it out.

"Postmark's odd," Paloma said, holding the envelope the way she held all envelopes now — a beat too long, thumb already working at the flap before some internal discipline caught her hand. "Hamburg. But it's been opened and resealed. Censor's mark."

Odile set down her trowel. The word *censor* sat strangely in the October air, among the dahlias going brown and the last defiant roses. War had been declared two months ago, and the village had celebrated with bunting and a brass band, and Odile had smiled at the band and gone home and sat very still in her study until dark.

"Give it here," she said, and heard how carefully flat her own voice came out.

Paloma gave it over. The girl had been with her three years now — driver, secretary, reader-of-mail-when-she-thought-nobody-noticed — and Odile had learned to read Paloma's silences the way farmers read clouds. This silence said: *I want to ask what's in it. I won't ask what's in it. The not-asking is costing me something.*

"Run along and see about the coal," Odile said gently. "We'll want more than usual this winter, I think."

Paloma went. She went slowly, craning back once from the gate like a dog asked to wait outside a butcher's shop, but she went. Odile waited until the kitchen door clicked shut before she turned the envelope over in her hands.

It was Greta's handwriting. That alone nearly undid her — four months of nothing, four months since the last letter had come through in June, when the world still pretended to be a place where letters traveled freely between friends, and Odile had told herself every day that the silence meant nothing, that mail simply got slow in wartime, that Greta was busy with her specimens and her students and her stubborn refusal to own a telephone because telephones were "eavesdropping machines with delusions of grandeur."

But the postmark. Odile tilted it toward the light. July the twenty-eighth. The war had begun on the fourth of August. This letter had been written in the last days of the old world, and had spent three months wandering through whatever purgatories mail passed through now — inspected, stamped, delayed, forgiven, released.

And the censor's resealing was clumsy. There was a second letter inside. She could feel it, thin as a wafer, slipped beneath the first.

Odile carried it into the study and did not open it immediately, which would have surprised anyone who knew her reputation. Odile Marchand, who could dismantle a heckler's argument in forty seconds flat; Odile Marchand, who had once read a government minister's own statistics back to him from the platform until he sat down looking like a man who'd been shown his pockets were empty. That Odile feared many things and admitted to few. But she feared envelopes from Hamburg, now, in the way one fears a knock at the door late at night — knowing that whatever is behind it has already happened, and all that remains is to be told.

She made tea first. Ritual was armor. She warmed the pot, measured the leaves, let it steep exactly four minutes while standing at the window watching Paloma argue with the coal man in the lane, gesturing at the scuttle with the authority of a woman half her age. Good girl. Stubborn girl. Odile had opinions about how much of each quality a person ought to have, and Paloma exceeded the recommended dose of both, and Odile had stopped minding somewhere in the second year without ever announcing that she'd stopped.

Then she sat, and broke the seal, and read.

*My dearest Odile,*

*I will write quickly because the doctor says I tire, and because I have decided to be the sort of patient who ignores doctors, which you will recognize as your influence.*

*The situation is this: my heart is misbehaving. Not fatally — Dr. Weiss uses words like "management" and "rest," by which he means he does not know, and I mean I have stopped asking. The trouble is less the heart than everything around it. Travel is forbidden. The university has dismissed its foreign members — yes, even honorary ones, even botanists, apparently we are a threat to the Reich with our ferns — and so I am stranded in a city that has stopped being mine, writing letters I am not certain arrive, to a country that has stopped being anything but "the enemy."*

*You will want to send money or arrange passage. Don't. I mean it, Odile. I can read your intentions across an ocean and four years of correspondence, and I am telling you: any attempt to move me will fail, and the attempt itself may be noticed, and neither of us can afford to be noticed. We are, I regret to inform you, extremely noticeable women.*

*So instead I ask for one thing. One promise. You will know which, because we swore it under the half-planted row in 1910, and you laughed at me for making it formal, and then you swore it anyway, which is the story of our entire friendship: you pretending my solemnities are ridiculous right up until the moment you honor them better than I do.*

*Plant the seventh tree.*

*Not in spring, when it would be easy. Now. In autumn, in bad ground, against all horticultural advice, because the whole point — do you remember? Do you remember what we said the seventh tree was for? It was never about the tree, Liebling. It was about planting something in the season when nothing wants to grow, and insisting on it anyway. I find I need to know it is done. I need it the way other people need prayers said for them. I am not ashamed of this. Well. I am slightly ashamed of this. But I am ill and German and fifty-eight years old, and I have earned the right to one superstition.*

*Tell no one why. Tell no one when. Just put it in the ground, and stand there a moment after, and think of me arguing with you about drainage.*

*Burn this letter if it seems wise. You always burn things when you're frightened, and you are always frightened, and you are always wise, and I love all three of those facts about you equally.*

*Your Greta*

Odile read it twice. Then she became aware of the second letter — the thinner one beneath — and drew it out.

It was dated the same day but written in a different mood entirely, the handwriting looser, hurried, the ink blotched where a drop had fallen and been smeared rather than blotted, which Greta never permitted in her scientific notes and only ever allowed in her most honest ones.

*O. — the censor reads the first letter, so I have written what I could say there and must keep this brief. If you are reading this, it survived him, which means either he was careless or he understood and chose mercy. Pray for carelessness; understanding is rarer.*

*Three things, and then I stop, because my hand is failing me tonight.*

*First: the shipment. My research collection — thirty years of specimens, the pressed series, the notebooks — left Hamburg in June aboard the freight route through Rotterdam. It will be held at your end. It will be called enemy property. There is a letter among the crates addressed to you personally. When they release it — and they release personal effects eventually, there are procedures, the Germans have their procedures and so do you — it must be collected in person, by someone whose name gives them no reason to hesitate. Not mine. Not yours. Think on this.*

*Second: whatever you hear about Germany in the papers, multiply the suffering by ten and divide the glory by a hundred, and you will approach the truth. I say this not to make you grieve for us but because I know you, and you will carry both countries in you now, and it will hurt, and I want you to know the hurt is accurate. You are not wrong to feel torn. Torn is correct.*

*Third — and here my hand truly does fail me, so forgive the scrawl —*

*In 1910 you asked me why the seventh tree mattered, and I told you, and you said, "That is the most unscientific thing I have ever loved." I have thought about that sentence every day since. Because it is true, Odile. Everything we planted was science. Only the seventh was faith. And I am finding, sitting here with a misbehaving heart in a country gone mad, that faith is the only crop that grows in autumn.*

*Keep the promise. Keep the letter. Keep yourself. In that order, and do not argue about the order, I know you are already composing the argument.*

*Yours, in all seasons,*
*G.*

Odile sat for a long time with the pages in her lap. Outside, the light went from gold to grey to the particular blue-grey of English October that makes even the faithful doubt spring exists. Somewhere below, Paloma was building the fire and moving objects around the kitchen with unnecessary force, which was how the girl prayed.

The promise. Of course. The seventh tree — the empty space at the end of the row at Wren Hollow, the gap they'd left deliberately sixteen years ago, two young women drunk on botany and each other's company, swearing an oath that had seemed charming then and now arrived, via a censor's clumsy resealing, wearing the full weight of everything it had always meant.

*Plant something in the season when nothing wants to grow, and insist on it anyway.*

Greta was ill. Greta was stranded. Greta was asking her — not for money, not for rescue, not for the grand gestures Odile's whole life had been built on, but for one tree in frozen ground and a moment's standing-still afterward.

And the second letter. *There is a letter among the crates addressed to you personally.* A letter inside a letter, a secret nested in a secret, and instructions to send someone inconspicuous to collect it — which meant Greta believed the contents were dangerous, or precious, or both, and Greta did not use the word *personally* lightly.

Odile rose and went to the desk and took out the box where she kept the things she did not burn. Four years of Greta's letters lay there, tied in ribbon, and above her, in the wall, the chimney stood cold and empty, holding nothing, burning nothing, and somehow that was worse — that the temptation to destroy existed and she refused it, sat with all this paper evidence of a friendship with a German national stacked in a country at war with Germany, refusing, out of pure mulish love, to give history the satisfaction.

Someone knocked. Two knocks, brisk, apologetic. Paloma.

"I've made supper," came the voice through the door. "It's the trout, so it's either eaten tonight or buried with honors tomorrow. Also the coal man says hello, and also that you owe him an apology about 1911, which I told him was between you and God."

Odile looked down at the letters. Then she folded them together — both of them, the public one and the private one — and slid them into the box, and closed the lid, and put her hand flat on it for a moment like a woman taking an oath.

"Coming," she called.

But at the door she stopped, one hand on the handle, and stood in the darkening study with the fire unlit and the orchard invisible beyond the window — seven trees and a gap, six planted and one owed — and felt the promise settle onto her shoulders with the specific gravity of a debt that cannot be discharged by anyone but the debtor.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would tell Paloma they would need the motorcar, and good boots, and a spade. Tomorrow she would begin explaining — or begin not explaining, which might be safer, and harder, and more like her.

Tonight there was trout, and a girl downstairs who read other people's mail out of loneliness, and a promise sixteen years old coming due in the worst possible season.

She turned the handle and went down to supper.

* * *

Paloma knew within four minutes.

This was not a compliment to Paloma's powers of detection, though they were considerable. It was simply that Odile Marchand, who had faced hostile crowds and hostile ministers and one genuinely hostile goose outside a hall in Norwich, could not eat trout and conceal a letter from Hamburg at the same time. Her fork slowed. Her answers to questions developed pauses. When Paloma mentioned the coal delivery, Odile said, "Yes, good," in the voice of a woman agreeing to something about coal while thinking about something else entirely, and then added, apropos of nothing, "How are your boots?"

"My boots?"

"For walking. Rough ground. Are the soles sound?"

Paloma set down her knife. Three years of reading this woman's mail had taught her that Odile asked questions in straight lines only when the real question was bent. "Miss Marchand. What's happened?"

"Nothing has happened."

"You've read a letter and nothing has happened, and now you're asking about my boots."

"The letter was from an old friend. She is well."

Paloma looked at her — that direct, unhurried look that had unnerved Odile for three years, the look of a girl who had grown up watching adults lie for a living and had developed an ear for the false note. "That's three statements," Paloma said, "and I don't believe one of them, which I think is a record."

For a moment Odile almost laughed, and almost told her everything — the illness, the stranding, the tree, the nested letter, the whole impossible architecture of love and distance and war. The words assembled themselves. She felt them queuing.

What stopped her was not caution. It was the picture of Paloma's face if she knew — the girl who believed history happened elsewhere, who drove through a world catching fire and narrated it to herself as weather happening to other people. To tell her was to hand her a piece of the fire. And Odile had spent two months watching the country celebrate that fire with bunting, and she could not bear to watch it catch in anyone she —

Anyone, she corrected herself firmly. Anyone at all.

"There will be a journey soon," Odile said instead. "To the coast. Nothing dramatic. I'll explain when I can."

"When you can," Paloma repeated, without inflection, and returned to her trout, and the silence that followed was the loudest conversation they had ever not had.

Later, upstairs, Odile took out the letters once more and read the last line of the private one again. *Keep the promise. Keep the letter. Keep yourself.* She had argued with Greta about the order for sixteen years, in her head, rehearsing. She suspected she would spend the rest of her life losing the argument, and that Greta, wherever she was, in whatever state, already knew it.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north. Winter was assembling itself in the dark like a patient carpenter.

The seventh tree, she thought. In frozen ground. Against all advice.

Well. She had never once taken good advice in her life, and she did not propose to start for a promise.

She slept badly, and dreamed of an orchard in summer, and woke with the certainty — arriving quietly, the way dread arrives, like weather changing — that the letter in the crates at the coast was going to require more of her than a spade and a train ticket.

She just did not yet know how much more. Or how soon.
