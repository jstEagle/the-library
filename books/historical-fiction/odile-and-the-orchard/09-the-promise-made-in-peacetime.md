## 9. The Promise Made in Peacetime

*Wren Hollow, June 1910*

The orchard did not exist yet, which was precisely why they had come.

Odile Marchand stood at the top of the sloping field behind the derelict cottage and looked out over a landscape of nothing much — long grass gone silver in the evening light, a broken drystone wall, one enormous ash tree leaning over it all like an elderly chaperone who had given up disapproving. Somewhere below, a wood pigeon said its two dull syllables over and over, as though it were being paid by the word.

"It's perfect," Greta announced.

"It's a ruin."

"It's a ruin *with soil*." Greta knelt where she stood, drove her fingers into the earth up to the wrist, and came up with a fistful of dark loam that she held out for inspection as if presenting evidence at trial. "Look at this. You could grow anything here. You could grow *opinions* here."

"You cannot grow opinions, Greta."

"The Germans have been growing opinions in dirt for centuries. It's called philosophy." She wiped her hand on her skirt without a flicker of regret — her third ruined skirt that term, Odile was fairly sure — and grinned up at her. The grin was the thing about Greta Lindqvist. Everything else about her could be catalogued: the round spectacles, the hair escaping its plait in pale ropes, the ink permanently staining the side of her right hand from writing left-handed and badly. But the grin was not catalogueable. The grin arrived like weather.

Odile felt it arrive now, and hated how reliably it worked on her.

"We haven't bought the cottage," she said, because someone had to be practical and Greta had never once volunteered. "We've made an *offer*. Through a solicitor. Who has informed us there are three other offers."

"Then we plant before we own it," Greta said, with the serene logic of a woman who had once walked into a Cambridge lecture hall uninvited and simply begun taking notes until no one had the heart to remove her. "Ownership is paperwork. Planting is a fact. Facts first, Odile. You taught me that."

"I taught you citation format."

"Which is facts wearing their best coat."

Odile laughed despite herself — the short, unwilling laugh she'd been producing around this woman for four years now, ever since a rainy afternoon in the botanical library when a German student had reached past her for the same volume of Linnaeus and then, instead of apologizing, had read over Odile's shoulder and said, "Your marginalia is better than the text. Are you going to be famous, or merely correct?"

"Merely correct," Odile had answered, and Greta had said, "What a waste. Come and have tea," and that had been, more or less, the whole of it. Four years of tea. Four years of arguments conducted across library tables and resolved over seed catalogues. Four years of Greta writing letters home describing England's suffrage meetings with such affectionate bewilderment that Odile had finally asked whether she was mocking her, and Greta had looked genuinely wounded and said, "I am *documenting* you. There is a difference. Mocking is what I do to Professor Henslow."

Now Greta was hauling a sack of six saplings off the cart they'd hired — apple trees, young ones, bare-rooted things that looked less like future orchards than like sticks with ambitions — and Odile stood watching her friend wrestle them down the slope, and thought, with a clarity that startled her: *I will remember this afternoon.*

It came to her whole, the way premonitions do — not as a picture of the future but as an instruction about the present. *Pay attention. This is one of the rooms of your life.*

She had no way of knowing, standing in the June grass, that the room would one day be locked, or that she would spend years pressing her face against its windows. She only knew that the light was doing something golden and shameless to the field, and that Greta had fallen over, and that the sound of Greta falling over — the theatrical groan, the muttered Swedish curse, the immediate resurfacing holding one sapling aloft like a rescued infant — was the finest sound Odile knew.

"Six trees," Greta panted, upright again, grass in her hair. "One for each—" She stopped. Counted. Looked into the sack. "There are seven."

"There are seven," Odile said. "I ordered seven."

Greta went very still, which for Greta was roughly equivalent to another person fainting. "You ordered seven."

"You wanted six. I wanted six. Seven seemed—"

"Seven seems *correct*," Greta breathed. "Oh, Odile. Seven. Do you know what seven means?"

"A number between six and eight?"

"In the old orchard books — the monastery ones I showed you — seven trees is a *promise*. A planted row of seven means whoever plants them intends to come back. Every year. Until they die. It's in the Benedictine practice — the seventh tree is the one you plant for the person who isn't there yet. For the one who comes after." She was talking faster now, hands moving, spectacles slipping. "Or sometimes for the absent one. For the one who can't be there when it's planted. You plant it anyway, and it waits for them."

"That sounds like superstition dressed in Latin."

"Everything true is superstition dressed in Latin. That's practically my family motto." Greta set the sack down with exaggerated ceremony, as though handling relics. "So. Seven trees. Whom are we planting the seventh for?"

And Odile, who had spent four years learning to say precise things, found herself saying the most imprecise thing of her life: "Whoever needs it."

Greta looked at her for a long moment. The pigeon said its syllables. The ash tree leaned. And then Greta nodded slowly, the way she nodded over a result that confirmed itself, and said, "Yes. All right. Whoever needs it. That's the oath, then."

"Greta—"

"No, no, stand *there*, this must be done properly, I'm Swedish, we take oaths seriously, it's all that cold weather, it makes the blood solemn." She positioned Odile at the head of the slope, where the first hole would go, and took her place beside her, and made her put her hand flat on the sack of saplings while Greta laid her own hand on top of it, ink-stained and earth-grimed and warm.

"This row," Greta said, and her voice had changed — dropped its performance, gone quiet and level, the voice she used only for real things, "we plant in friendship. Six for ourselves, one for whoever needs it. We come back every year while we live. If one of us is kept away — by sea, by war, by illness, by any wall men build — the other keeps the row. And the seventh tree—" she paused, and Odile watched her choose the words with the care she gave to taxonomy, "—the seventh tree belongs to the promise, not to us. Whoever needs it gets it. No matter when. No matter what it costs the one who keeps it."

"What it costs?" Odile repeated. "That's an odd clause for an orchard."

"Every oath worth making has a cost clause. Otherwise it's just a wish with manners." Greta squeezed her hand once, hard, and let go. "Well? Say it back. Oaths don't count unless they're said back. That's also the cold weather."

Odile said it back. All of it — the row, the returning, the keeping, the seventh tree for whoever needed it, no matter when, no matter the cost. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and somewhere in the middle of it the afternoon light went from gold to amber, and the words stopped feeling like a game and started feeling like a document signed in a language she'd only half learned.

They dug until dark. Six holes, six trees, the roots spread with a care that surprised them both — Greta the botanist fussing over rootstock, Odile the activist discovering she liked the plain arithmetic of planting: a hole, a tree, soil, water, done. No committee. No counterarguments. They staked the sixth tree as the moon came up, and then stood looking at the empty space at the end of the row, where the seventh would go.

"Not today," Greta said softly. "The seventh waits. That's rather the point of it."

They walked back along the lane in the blue dark, arms full of tools, shoes ruined, and at the gate of the cottage-to-be Greta said, "You know this makes us sisters. Legally. In Sweden. Possibly."

"Nothing is legally anything in Sweden based on an afternoon in Kent."

"You underestimate Swedish law. It runs on oaths and pickled fish." And then, quieter, bumping her shoulder against Odile's: "Thank you for the seventh tree."

"For whoever needs it," Odile said.

"For whoever needs it," Greta agreed, and the pact settled into place between them like a stone set into a wall — unremarkable, load-bearing, permanent.

* * *

Four years later, Odile stood at her window in Wren Hollow and read Greta's letter for the eleventh time.

The postmark was July the twenty-eighth. Before everything. The paper felt different in her hands now — heavier, though she knew paper didn't change; it was her hands that had changed, since the newspapers had come shouting and the village bells had rung for war and the world had drawn its borders in fresh red ink overnight.

*I am ill,* the letter said, in Greta's slanting, ink-blotted hand. *Nothing dramatic — the doctors say the lungs, which is what the doctors always say, and half the time they're wrong, but I confess this time they may have the right of it. I am stranded in Hamburg. The ships have stopped. Odile, the ships have actually stopped, I keep writing that sentence as though repeating it will make it less absurd.*

*My research shipment is stuck at your blessed coast — the crates with the alpine specimens, and the monograph proofs, and the other thing. You know the other thing. I won't write it plainly, we were always too clever by half with our seasons and our grafts, weren't we? If it reaches you, keep it. If it can't reach you, forget it, and forgive me for ever involving you in my filing system.*

*One promise, then, since promises seem to be the currency now. The seventh tree. Plant it. I know what I said in 1910 — whoever needs it, whenever, whatever it costs. Well. I need it. There, I've said it plainly at last, and you may imagine how much it cost me. Plant the seventh tree for me, Odile, and I will consider every debt between us paid, including the ones you don't know you're owed.*

*Come back to the orchard when the world remembers how to be small again. Bring your girl — the driver, the one who reads your mail, yes, I know, you think I don't notice things, but I notice everything, it's my one talent besides the specimens. Any friend of yours who reads your mail is already family. Tell her the row is hers too.*

*Yours, in peacetime and out of it,*
*G.*

Odile folded the letter along its old creases and did not burn it. She had burned nothing — not the letters, not the photographs, not the seed catalogues annotated in two languages — and Paloma had noticed, Odile knew she had noticed, the way the girl noticed everything and mistook noticing for a small safe sin.

Outside, the orchard stood in the August heat, six trees in their fourth summer, leafed out and heavy with the first green fruit. At the end of the row, the empty space waited, patient as a held breath.

*Plant the seventh tree.* Warmth, when it was spoken under a June sky with a grinning Swede's hand on yours. Debt, now that the sea had closed and the lungs had failed and the words *whatever it costs* had grown teeth.

Odile pressed the letter flat beneath the blotter on her desk, next to the census form she had filled in with careful, defensible truths, and thought about walls men build, and seas, and costs.

She had sworn an oath in peacetime. Peacetime, it turned out, had been the easy party to the contract.

She went downstairs to tell Paloma they would need a spade — and stopped on the second step, hearing the front gate click, and a man's voice in the lane, asking the neighbor's boy, quite pleasantly, whether the lady at Wren Hollow received much correspondence from abroad.
