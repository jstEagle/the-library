## 10. Enemy Property

The letter from the Board of Trade arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except me, because Tuesdays were the days Odile Marchand let me drive her into Ashcombe for the market, and I had learned that a woman's whole character can be read in how she buys apples. Odile pressed every one with her thumb and listened — actually listened — as though the fruit might confess something. She bought bruised ones on purpose, the ones the vendor wanted rid of, and paid full price anyway, and if you think that tells you nothing about a person, then you have never watched her do it.

I was sorting the post when the official envelope came through. I knew it was official before I touched it. Everything about it was gray — the paper, the stamp, the tone of the handwriting, which had the particular lifelessness of a man who is paid by the letter and resents each one. It sat in my hands like a dead bird.

Now, I will be honest with you, since honesty is the theme of this chapter whether I like it or not: I opened it first. I always opened them first. Four years of habit had worn a groove in me so deep that by now the groove *was* me. I told myself it was diligence — a driver must know her employer's affairs; what if it required an answer before Thursday; what if it concerned the motor's registration. The truth, which I only admitted to myself in the dark half-hour before sleep, was simpler and less flattering. I read first because reading second meant being outside something, and I had spent my whole life outside things.

So I stood in the hall of Wren Hollow with the gray envelope open in my hands, and I read:

*Dear Madam,*

*We write regarding certain property consigned to you by one G. Lindqvist, a national of Germany, intercepted at the port of Hamburg and transferred to His Majesty's custody at the coastal depot at Marlow Quay under the Defence of the Realm Consolidation Act. Said property comprises eleven crates of scientific materials, botanical specimens, and personal effects. You are advised that release may be effected only upon personal application by the named addressee or her authorized representative, at the depot, during ordinary business hours. Please bring this notice and proof of identity.*

*Yours obediently,*

*A. Pettifer, Clerk*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing quite still while the hall clock ticked at me like a chaperone.

Eleven crates. Botanical specimens. Personal effects.

And somewhere among them — I felt it before I reasoned it, the way you feel rain coming in your knees — a letter. A letter addressed to Odile Marchand, written by Greta Lindqvist, sitting in a government warehouse between a dried fern and somebody's confiscated microscope, waiting for a clerk named Pettifer to decide its fate.

"Paloma?" Odile's voice came down the stairs ahead of her, as it always did. "Is that the market list? Because if Mrs. Tredwell has quinces again I want three, and I don't care what they cost—"

She stopped on the bottom step when she saw my face. Odile Marchand missed very little, which was inconvenient for both of us these days.

"What is it?"

"It's from the Board of Trade," I said, and heard my own voice go careful, the way it had gone careful ever since the constable's visit. "About the shipment. Greta's shipment."

The change in her was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing about it. She did not go pale or clutch the banister. She simply became stiller, the way water goes stiller over something deep, and she came down the last steps slowly and held out her hand for the letter, and I gave it to her, and she read it standing there in the hall with her hat still on from the garden.

"They have her specimens," she said quietly. "Fourteen years of field work. Pressed plants she collected before I ever met her." Her thumb moved along the edge of the page. "And her letters."

"There'll be letters," I said. "Among the personal effects."

"Yes." She looked up at me then, and I saw something in her face I had not seen since August — not grief exactly, but grief's cousin, the one who does arithmetic. "Marlow Quay is forty miles."

"Thirty-eight," I said. "Thirty-one if we take the coast road past Fenwick, though the bridge at Fenwick has a weight restriction now, so thirty-four, honestly, depending on the state of the road after all this rain."

Odile blinked at me. "You've already worked out the route."

"I work out lots of routes," I said, with dignity. "It's called professionalism."

* * *

Here is what I did not say, because I did not yet fully know it myself: the moment I read *personal application*, something in my chest had turned over like an engine catching. Hope, yes — hope that Greta's things could be reached, that a letter might pass from those sealed crates into Odile's hands. But underneath the hope, humming, a deadline. The words *ordinary business hours* had gone into me like a splinter. Ordinary business hours meant a window. Windows close.

I did not yet know why that mattered so much. I would find out two days later, and the finding-out would change the shape of everything. But that Tuesday, all I knew was that Odile Marchand, who had been moving through October like a woman wading through cold water, suddenly had somewhere to *go*, and I was the one with the keys.

"We'll need the papers," she said. We were in the study now, and she had the notice flat on the desk under the lamp, studying it as though it might contain a second, secret letter between the lines. "Proof of identity. My name is on their lists, Paloma. It has been on their lists since September, whatever the constable pretended."

"Then we'll say I'm your authorized representative."

"You'd be walking into a government depot carrying my name."

"I carry your name everywhere," I said. "It's on the motor's registration. It's on the suffrage correspondence. Half of Kent thinks I'm your niece and the other half thinks I'm your ward, and the remaining half thinks we're running a school for fallen women."

"That's three halves."

"I'm a mathematician of reputation."

For a moment — just a moment — she laughed. It came out of her rusty and surprised, like a door opening in a house long shut up, and I understood that I would have driven to Marlow Quay through fire for another one of those laughs, which should perhaps have told me something about myself that I wasn't ready to hear.

"The papers," she said again, sobering. "There's a solicitor in Ashcombe. Mr. Hale. He drew up the deed for Wren Hollow. He owes me a favor, and more to the point, he owes Miss Pankhurst's crowd a favor, which he'd deny under oath."

"When do we go?"

"Thursday. Business hours begin at nine. If we leave at six—" She stopped. Her hand flattened on the desk, and I watched her do the thing she did, the thing I had watched her do in orchards and committee rooms and once, memorably, in the middle of a village fete when the vicar said something unforgivable about Irish home rule: she gathered herself around a decision the way a fist gathers around a stone.

"If we leave at six," I finished for her, "we're there by nine, with time to spare for Pettifer and his gray envelopes."

* * *

Wednesday was the day I went to see Mr. Hale alone, and Wednesday was the day I learned about deadlines.

It was supposed to be a simple errand. Odile stayed home — she had taken to staying home, watched, she said, by eyes she couldn't name but could feel, and I believed her, because I had started feeling them too. So I took the little Austin into Ashcombe by myself and sat in Mr. Hale's office among the leather books and the smell of ink, and asked him, in my most professional voice, for a letter of authorization naming me Odile Marchand's representative.

Mr. Hale was a small round man with kind eyes and the caution of someone who had survived forty years of small-town law by knowing exactly which questions not to answer. He wrote the authorization without complaint. But as he blotted it, he said, in the offhand voice men use when the information is anything but offhand:

"You'll want to move quickly, miss."

"Quickly?"

"On the collection." He squared the paper, tapped its edges together. "There's a new order coming down from the Admiralty. Confiscated enemy property at the coastal depots is to be catalogued and shipped out — redistributed, auctioned, some of it sent abroad for the war effort. Specimens, instruments, papers. All of it. The depots have been given until the end of the month to clear their holdings."

My stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, the way it does on stairs when you miscount.

"Shipped where?"

"Wherever the need is greatest, they say." He handed me the authorization across the desk. "Nobody says where the need is greatest, miss. They just say it, and things disappear. I had a client lost a case of French wine that way in '14. Never saw it again." He peered at me over his spectacles. "You've got until the tide takes it, I shouldn't wonder. These coastal places, they load by the boat."

I thanked him. I remember thanking him. I remember walking out of his office into the bright cold morning and standing on the pavement of Ashcombe high street holding a piece of paper that suddenly weighed as much as a person, while around me the town went on with its ordinary business — bakers, butchers, a boy whistling, a recruiting poster of a pointing man whom I had hated on principle since August and now hated personally.

Until the tide takes it.

I thought of the crates. Eleven of them, sitting in a warehouse that smelled of rope and salt, and somewhere in the middle of them a letter with Odile's name on it, written by a woman four hundred miles away who was ill, who was stranded, who had asked — I remembered the autumn letter, I had read it first, I always read first — who had asked Odile to keep a promise. And the sea was coming for that letter. Not soldiers, not censors, not anything so dramatic. Just a tide, and a clerk named Pettifer, and an order about redistributing property to wherever the need was greatest.

History, I had always believed, happened elsewhere. To other people. In capitals, on frontiers, in the pages of newspapers read comfortably by firesides. It had taken me four years and one gray envelope to understand that history also happened in warehouses, in the space between *ordinary business hours* and the morning tide, and that it was happening, right now, to a letter addressed to a woman I —

Well. To a woman I drove.

* * *

I got back to Wren Hollow at four o'clock and found Odile in the orchard.

This had become her place of refuge since September, though she never said so. She would walk out among the six trees — the six old trees she and Greta Lindqvist had planted as students, back when the world made promises easy — and stand there with her hands behind her back like a general inspecting troops that happened to be apple trees. The seventh position in the row stood empty. I had noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth in a smile.

She heard me crunching up the path and didn't turn around.

"Well?" she said.

"Mr. Hale came through." I held up the authorization. "You're officially too important to travel, and I'm officially you."

"An alarming promotion." She took the paper, read it, and I watched her face do the arithmetic again — the same expression from Tuesday, grief's cousin, the one who counts. "Then it's settled. Six o'clock tomorrow. The coast road. We present ourselves to Mr. Pettifer at nine sharp, we collect eleven crates of other people's science, and we come home before dark."

"Before dark," I agreed.

But I hadn't told her yet. I stood there in the orchard with the wind moving through the bare branches and the empty seventh spot gaping at us both, and I hadn't told her about the Admiralty order, about the tide, about Mr. Hale's client and his French wine. And I want to be fair to myself here, because I have had years to examine this and I still don't entirely forgive it: I didn't tell her because telling her would make it real, and if it became real, then the plan — our tidy little plan, six o'clock, nine o'clock, home before dark — might crack, and if the plan cracked, then the letter might truly be lost, and I could not bear that. Not for her sake. For mine. For the girl who had spent her whole life outside things and had finally, through a windshield and four years of opened envelopes, gotten inside one.

"Paloma." Odile was looking at me with those eyes that missed very little. "What aren't you saying?"

And here is where I made my choice, the first of several that week, each one heavier than the last.

"The roads," I said. "After the rain. We should leave at five instead of six. Just to be safe."

She studied me for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the hedge a pheasant shouted its idiot announcement, and the light was going gold and thin the way October light does, and I stood there and lied to her with my whole honest face.

"Five o'clock," she said at last. "You're learning deviousness, Paloma Vasquez. I'd be proud if it didn't frighten me."

"Everything frightens you lately."

"No," she said, turning back toward the house, her voice dropping to something I almost didn't catch. "Not everything. Only the things worth keeping."

That night I packed the motor's kit myself — tools, oil, the spare fan belt, a thermos, and beneath the seat where the maps lived, the authorization wrapped in oilcloth. I lay awake until two listening to the wind come up from the channel, thinking about tides. I had grown up inland and knew nothing about tides except that they were punctual, indifferent, and kept no letters.

At ten minutes to five, I went down to wake her, and found her already dressed, already waiting in the hall with her coat on and a small parcel in her hands wrapped in brown paper and string, and when she saw me she tucked it inside her coat quickly, the way you hide a thing you've promised someone.

"Five o'clock," she said briskly. "Excellent. Beat the milk cart, even."

"Odile," I said. "There's something I have to tell you about the depot."

And I told her — all of it, the Admiralty order, the clearance, the wine, the tide — watching her face as the arithmetic cousin came back and finished his sums. When I finished, she was quiet for the length of three ticks of the hall clock.

"Then we haven't got until the end of the month," she said.

"No."

"We've got until the boat."

"Yes."

She nodded once, and pulled her gloves on finger by finger, and there was nothing left in her of the woman who waded through cold water. Whatever was in that parcel against her ribs, whatever promise rode in those eleven crates forty miles away, she had decided, and I recognized the look because I had seen it aimed at vicars and cabinet ministers and once, gloriously, at a magistrate in Tunbridge Wells.

"Start the engine, Miss Vasquez," said Odile Marchand. "We're going to beat the tide."

I wish I could tell you we did.
