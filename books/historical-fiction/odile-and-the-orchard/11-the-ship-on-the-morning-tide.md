## 11. The Ship on the Morning Tide

We did not beat the tide. We did not even beat the road.

The trouble began eleven miles out of Wren Hollow, where the Daimler's radiator decided it had opinions about the cold and vented them all over the lane in a hissing white plume that looked, from a distance, like a small ghost having a tantrum. I stood over the engine with my sleeves rolled while Odile stood behind me holding the water can and pretending she knew what a gasket was.

"Is it serious?" she asked.

"It's dramatic," I said. "There's a difference."

"Paloma, everything you own is dramatic."

"That's why we get along."

The truth was worse than drama. The hose had split its seam like an overripe plum, and there would be no mending it short of the garage in Ashford, which meant hours, which meant the depot clerk's promise — *your papers will be held for collection through the week* — suddenly looked less like patience and more like a trapdoor. I said none of this aloud. Odile had enough arithmetic running behind her eyes without me adding figures.

A farmer's boy on a cart gave us a lift into Ashford sitting on sacks of turnips, which Odile bore with the expression of a duchess reviewing troops, and by four o'clock the Daimler drank its new hose and we were moving again, smelling faintly of boiled coolant and turnip, both of us too tired to speak. It was full dark when the sea announced itself — not seen but felt, a change in the air's texture, salt laid over coal smoke — and then the town rose up around us with its gas lamps doubled and blurred in the wet streets, and beyond the rooftops, somewhere, black water.

* * *

The Coastal Receiving Depot occupied a long shed at the foot of the harbour road, behind a customs house and a stack of barrels that smelled of tar and herring. A lamp burned over the door. Under it, a notice board listed regulations in a hand so severe the letters seemed to be shouting: no admission without authority; all goods subject to inspection; enemy property handled under Order of the day, etc., etc.

Odile read the board twice. Then she straightened her hat, arranged her face into its most unassailable configuration, and knocked.

The clerk who answered was perhaps sixty, with spectacles thick as jam-jar bottoms and a cardigan worn open over his waistcoat as though he'd given up on warmth as a policy position some years back. He looked at Odile. He looked at me. He looked at our motorcar, parked conspicuously in a town where nobody owned one.

"Yes?" he said.

"Good evening," said Odile. "I'm here regarding a consignment of scientific materials seized at — I believe it was registered under the name Lindqvist. Greta Lindqvist. I understand personal effects may be released to authorised claimants."

The clerk's face did something complicated. It began neutral, moved briefly toward sympathy, detoured through apology, and arrived at regret with its luggage lost.

"You'll be Mrs. Marchand, then," he said. "Or Miss. The letter said Marchand."

"You've had correspondence about this?"

"I have the file." He stepped aside to let us in, and I confess I catalogued the room in the way I catalogue every room now — exits, ledgers, the second door marked PRIVATE, the bicycle leaning against the wall that could outrun any of us. Old habits. The depot office was a cubby of damp paper and paraffin heat, shelves of docketed parcels rising to the ceiling like a library nobody wanted to read. Eleven crates would not be hard to find. They'd have been set apart, labelled, probably stacked nearest the door for loading.

The clerk opened a ledger and ran a finger down a column. "Lindqvist, G. Botanical apparatus, books, papers. Received the ninth. Yes. Here." His finger stopped. And here is the thing about clerks: they are trained to keep their faces still, but their fingers are honest, and his finger pressed down on that line of ink as though trying to hold it shut.

"There's been a change of instruction," he said.

"What sort of change?"

He took off his spectacles, polished them on his cardigan, put them back on. All of it took too long. "These effects aren't being released for local claim after all. There's a directive — came through Tuesday — consolidated shipping. Enemy-owned goods in regional depots are to be sent up to London for central assessment. Saves duplication of effort, they say." He said *they say* the way other men say *God help us.*

"When?" Odile asked. Her voice was perfectly level. I had heard her use it before juries.

The clerk looked at the clock on the wall. It was nearly nine. He looked at the window, where rain had begun again against the glass. He looked, finally, at his ledger, because it was easier to look at than either of us.

"The vessel takes the morning tide," he said. "Seven o'clock, or near enough. She loads from first light."

I felt it before I understood it — a sensation low in my chest, mechanical, like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was installed. Somewhere in that shed forty feet away sat eleven crates of a dead woman's life — no, not dead, ill, stranded, *waiting* — and at seven o'clock tomorrow they would go onto a boat, and boats, once gone, do not come back for letters.

"Surely," Odile said, and her level voice developed, very slightly, a crack in the plaster, "surely a claimant already named in the file —"

"The directive supersedes." He spread his hands, palms up, a man showing he carried nothing. "I don't make it, madam. I copy it out fair and file it. That's my whole war." For a moment his eyes behind those jam-jar lenses were terribly human. "If it were mine to give, I'd give it. But there's a gentleman from the Board coming down tonight to supervise the loading. He sleeps in the customs house. You'll have seen the lights."

"How many men does he have with him?" I asked. The question came out of me sideways, before I'd decided to ask it, and I saw Odile glance at me sharply — not disapproval; recognition.

"One soldier, mostly asleep. And me." The clerk almost smiled. "And I'm sixty-three and frightened of mice."

"Mr. — ?" Odile prompted.

"Pike."

"Mr. Pike. If a letter — a single letter, addressed to me, found among those effects — if arrangements could be made for its delivery rather than its shipping —"

"I couldn't say," Mr. Pike said quickly. Too quickly. And then, quieter, looking at his hands: "I couldn't say anything at all, madam, about what might or might not be done by a person who came round when the office was closed. Which is not a thing I could advise, being that I'd have to report it. If it happened. Which I'm sure it won't."

The silence that followed had a shape. Odile and I had worked together three years, and in that time I had learned her silences the way sailors learn weather, and this one was a storm deciding.

"Thank you, Mr. Pike," she said at last. "You have been extremely thorough."

"I try, madam." He showed us out. At the door, under the lamp, with the rain silvering down between us, he added — not looking at either of us, looking at the harbour, where a dark hull sat at anchor with one riding light burning — "She's a tramp steamer called the *Corvid*. Coal smoke, mostly. You'll hear her engines start about half five. Sound carries on the water." Then he went inside and shut the door, and turned the lamp down, and that was that.

* * *

We found rooms at the Anchor & Rope, the only inn taking lodgers within a mile of the water, and by the time we reached it the landlady had heard enough of our accent to decide we were quality and charge accordingly. Our rooms adjoined. Through the wall I could hear Odile not sleeping — the particular creak of a bed bearing a person who is lying rigidly awake, thinking.

I lay on my own bed in my clothes with my boots off and watched the ceiling, and the clock in my chest ticked.

Half past five, the engines. Seven o'clock, the tide. Between now and then: a Board man, a soldier, a locked shed, a harbour road, and two women with no authority whatsoever except a peacetime promise made under apple trees six years ago, which counted for nothing on any form in the British Empire and everything everywhere else.

I got up and went to the window. The harbour lay below in the rain, black water stitched with reflected lamplight, and the *Corvid* rode at her moorings like something patient. Ships were patient. Ships did not care whose letters they carried or failed to carry. History, I thought — history was that ship. It sat offshore your whole life, and you watched it, and you believed it was bound for other coasts, other people, and then one morning you woke and it was taking on cargo with your name on the manifest.

Except it wasn't my name on the manifest. It was Greta's. And Odile's. And maybe, if that sealed parcel against my ribs ever came to light, mine too.

There was a knock at the connecting door. Two knocks, soft. I opened it and Odile stood there in her dressing gown with her hair down, which I had seen perhaps four times in three years and each time it startled me, how much younger she became, how much more like the girl who had planted trees with her dearest friend in an abandoned orchard.

"You're awake," she said.

"I'm always awake. It's my best quality."

"Come in a moment."

Her room smelled of lavender and cold grate. She sat in the chair by the window and I sat on the edge of the bed, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the rain filled the silence for us.

"She planted the sixth tree by herself," Odile said finally. "In 1908. I had gone back to England for my mother's funeral, and she wrote to me that she'd done it alone, and she said —" Odile stopped. Her thumb moved along the arm of the chair, back and forth. "She said, *one of us must keep planting even when the other cannot come.* I thought it was sentiment. Greta was never sentimental. It was an instruction."

"Mrs. Marchand —"

"Don't. Not tonight." She looked at me then, and her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than tears would have. Dry eyes meant the grief had gone somewhere structural, into the joists. "Tomorrow that ship leaves with everything she owns in the world aboard it, and I am watched, Paloma. You know I am watched. There was a man reading a newspaper outside the churchyard gate on Sunday who turned the same page for forty minutes. If I am seen anywhere near that depot, it confirms every suspicion, and worse — it confirms suspicion of *her*, and she has enough of that already across the water."

"So I go."

"No."

"Then who? The vicar? Mr. Pike's mice?"

"*No one goes.*" It came out harder than she intended; I saw her rein it back. She rose and stood at the window with her back to me, a silhouette against the lamplit rain. "You don't understand what they do. To women, especially, caught doing things they shouldn't. You've seen the newspapers call me a hysterical spinster for asking to vote. Imagine what they call a woman caught breaking into a customs depot during wartime. It isn't prison, Paloma. It's worse than prison, because it follows you. It gets into the papers. It gets into *his* papers." She didn't say whose papers. She didn't need to. "I won't have it. I forbid it. As your employer, I forbid it."

The word landed between us like a dropped plate. Employer. Three years, and she had never once used it, not even in anger — not the day I crashed the Daimler into the ha-ha, not the day I read half a letter aloud before noticing it was private. She had kept that coin unspent precisely so it would buy something someday, and now here it was, spent, on keeping me safe.

I should have obeyed her. Let me tell you honestly: in that moment, I intended to obey her. I lay back on her bed and said, "Yes, Mrs. Marchand," and she turned and looked at me with relief and something else — something like grief — and wished me goodnight, and went through the connecting door, and I listened to her settle and go still.

Then I lay in the dark and thought about Greta Lindqvist, whom I had never met, who had written four years of letters in a code of orchards and seasons, who was ill in a country sliding into winter with her life packed in eleven crates on an English quay. I thought about Mr. Pike's honest finger pressing down on a line of ink. I thought about the soldier, mostly asleep. I thought about half past five.

The clock in my chest ticked louder.

Here is what nobody tells you about fear. You imagine it will arrive like a storm, all at once, and flatten you. Instead it arrives like the tide — quiet, incremental, filling every low place in you until you look down and discover you are standing in it, and have been for some time, and the ground you thought was solid is gone. I was afraid. I was afraid of the Board man and the soldiers and the newspapers and the ha-ha of my whole respectable life. And underneath the fear, steady as keel-ballast, was something I had never felt before in my comfortable, diligent, letter-reading life:

*I was needed.*

Not useful — needed. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was Greta Lindqvist, and also Odile Marchand, and possibly, though I would not admit it until much later, me.

I got up. I dressed. I took from my carpet bag the things I had packed without letting myself know I was packing them: my driving gloves, the spare coil of wire I kept for the starter motor, and the little canvas satchel that had carried my lunch every working day for three years and would carry something else tonight.

Through the connecting door, Odile slept at last, or pretended beautifully to. I stood over her a moment. Her face in the lamplight was young again, and stubborn even in sleep, jaw set like a woman arguing with the century itself.

"Forgive me," I whispered. "As your driver, I outrank you on the road."

I let myself out.

* * *

The inn stairs complained under me and the landlady's cat, a vast grey tyrant named Admiral, opened one eye from the landing and assessed my intentions and decided, mercifully, they were beneath his interest. The front door's bolt was stiff; I lifted it with both hands and eased it back inch by inch while my heart performed percussion.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a drifting mist. The harbour road shone wet and empty under the lamps. Somewhere ahead, the customs house windows glowed yellow, and beside them the long black shoulder of the depot shed, and beyond both, the water, and the water's patient cargo.

I checked the little watch pinned inside my coat. Twenty past twelve. Six hours and ten minutes until the tide.

Six hours. One locked shed. One Board man, one sleepy soldier, one mouse-frightened clerk who had told me exactly when the engines would start, in the tone of a man setting a plate before a hungry stranger and hoping God would call it hospitality.

I pulled on my gloves, finger by finger, the way she'd taught me without teaching me, and walked toward the water.
