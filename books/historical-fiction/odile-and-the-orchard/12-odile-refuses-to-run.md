## 12. Odile Refuses to Run

Odile Marchand had spent fifty-one years learning to hold her face still, and she had thought herself a master of it. She had held it still through the death of her mother, through the failure of three petitions, through the moment in 1908 when a policeman's fist had caught her above the ear at Parliament Square and she had smiled for the photographers with blood drying behind her collar. Her face was a house with all the curtains drawn, and she had always believed no one could see inside it.

Paloma saw inside it. That was the trouble.

It happened at breakfast on the third of November, over eggs that had gone cold because neither of them would admit they were finished eating. Paloma had the depot receipt flat on the table between them, her finger resting on the line that mattered — *personal collection only* — and her eyes had that particular shine they took on when she had already decided something and was merely waiting for permission.

"You can't go," Odile said, before Paloma could say anything at all.

"I haven't said—"

"You were going to. You've been going to since Tuesday. You fold the receipt into eighths when you think I'm not looking, which is a curious habit for a woman who intends to keep it safe." Odile set down her fork with great care, as though it might detonate. "You cannot drive to the coast, Paloma. Not now."

"The letter ships out on the morning tide. After that it's gone — gone to some government warehouse in London, or gone to be pulped, or gone into a drawer where nobody will ever—"

"Gone," Odile agreed. "Yes."

"And you're just going to sit here?"

"Yes," said Odile again, and this time she made her voice into a wall, brick by brick, the way her father had taught her when she was small and inclined toward argument. "I am going to sit here. I am going to answer my correspondence, and wind the clock, and be exactly, tediously, unremarkably myself, until the men who watch this house grow bored of watching it. That is the entire strategy, and it is a good one."

"It's cowardice wearing a sensible coat."

The words landed harder than Paloma likely intended — Odile watched her realize it a half-second too late, watched the color come up her throat. In four years the girl had never once called her a coward. There had been arguments about driving speeds, about the ethics of reading mail aloud, about whether marmalade counted as a meal, but never this.

"Cowardice," Odile repeated, tasting it. "Well. At least you've learned to say what you mean. I used to worry about that."

"Miss Marchand—"

"Odile." She said it automatically; she said it every time, and every time Paloma ignored it, and the ritual of it had become one of the small warm stones at the bottom of her life. But today it came out tired, and Paloma heard the tiredness, and sat back down.

They regarded each other across the cold eggs.

"There is a man," Odile said quietly, "who stands by the gatepost on Thursdays pretending to examine the hedge. He has been examining that hedge for eleven days. The hedge has won, incidentally; there is nothing left to examine but sticks. There is a bicycle that passes the window at half past nine each morning and again at four, though no one in this village rides a bicycle at those hours except him. There was a constable in my kitchen two weeks ago asking after enemy correspondence while helping himself to my biscuits. Do you imagine these gentlemen are decorative?"

"No. I imagine they're waiting for exactly this. For you to run."

"For *me* to run, yes." Odile leaned forward. "And if I go to that depot, with papers, with questions asked, with my name written into another ledger — then everything they suspect becomes true. Then Greta's letters, the ones we burned and the ones we didn't, the whole foolish architecture of four years — it stops being the paranoia of an old woman and becomes evidence. They will not stop at me. They will walk backward through everything I've touched, and everything I've touched includes a suffrage network with two hundred names in it, and it includes—" she stopped. It included you, she did not say. It included a Spanish girl with forged papers in her pocket and no country that wanted her either.

"It includes me," Paloma said, because she was infuriatingly quick. "So don't protect me. Let me decide what I'm worth risking."

"That is precisely what I am refusing to do."

"Because you get to decide? Because you own the motorcar and the house and the moral high ground?" Paloma's voice cracked on high ground, which rather undercut it, but she pressed on. "You've spent your whole life telling women to stand up and be counted. You chained yourself to railings. And now the first hard thing comes to your own door and your answer is to wind the clock?"

The silence afterward had teeth in it.

Odile looked at her hands. They were her mother's hands, growing older around the knuckles, and they were shaking very slightly, which she concealed by folding them. She thought: I have been brave in crowds. Crowds are easy. A crowd holds you up like water; you are carried along on the courage of ten thousand strangers and you may believe, for a shining hour, that fear is something other people feel. This — this girl across the table with her jaw set like her father's tools — this was different. This was fear with a name on it.

"Sit down properly," Odile said at last, "and listen to me, because I will only say this once, and then I intend never to speak of it again."

Paloma sat.

"If you take the papers from the network and drive to the coast, and you are stopped — and you will be stopped, the roads are crawling — then you are arrested carrying documents belonging to a network that is already under observation. Your name goes into a file. Perhaps mine does too, but yours goes in first and stays in longest, because you are twenty-four and foreign-born and alone, and this country has decided that people like you are a problem to be managed. I am sixty-one years old, I am tiresome, I am well-connected, and worst case they confiscate my jam. You do not have my armor, Paloma. You have never had it. You simply haven't noticed yet, because until this year nothing has ever required you to look."

"That's not fair."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. None of it is. Fairness has been on holiday since August 1914 and shows no sign of returning. Which is why we must be cleverer than fair." She rose, went to the window, and drew the curtain back one inch — habit now, the inch of surveillance that structured her mornings. The road was empty. The hedge stood stripped and accusing. "Greta wrote to me in June. Before the last post that crossed. Did you know that?"

Paloma went still. "You never said."

"There was a great deal I never said. It is one of my principal accomplishments." Odile let the curtain fall. "She knew the war was coming before anyone. She wrote — I have it memorized, don't ask me why, the mind keeps what the heart would rather lose — she wrote: *If they seal the borders, promise me you'll plant the seventh tree without me. Promise me the orchard won't wait for better times. Better times are a lie we tell orchards.*" Her voice did something on the last sentence that she despised, a small unravelling, and she mastered it. "So. The seventh tree is the promise. Not the letter. The tree is in our ground, where no clerk can confiscate it, where no ship can carry it away. We plant it when the ground thaws. We plant it and we say nothing to anyone, ever, and we win — quietly, the only way left to us."

"The ground thaws in March."

"Yes."

"And the letter sails away in November."

"Yes."

"Then you'd be keeping a promise to her memory instead of to her." Paloma stood. She had her coat over her arm already; Odile realized, with a lurch, that it had been over her arm for some minutes, perhaps the whole conversation. "You told me once that history happens to other people. You were wrong, and so am I, and I'm done pretending otherwise. I'm going to bed."

She went. Her footsteps climbed the stairs with terrible steadiness, and a door closed with the specific gentleness of someone determined not to slam it.

* * *

Odile did not sleep.

She sat in her study with the lamp turned low and the letters — her letters, the safe ones, the innocuous correspondence of a retired suffragist with opinions about drainage — spread before her, and she read the same paragraph nine times without retaining a word of it. Somewhere above her, the floorboards ticked as the house cooled. Somewhere beyond the garden, the man with the bicycle would be sleeping too, or not sleeping, or dreaming of promotion.

She tried to construct the argument she would make in the morning. Reason, she told herself. Meet fire with reason. She got as far as listing her points — the checkpoints, the files, the arithmetic of risk — and then heard them all delivered in Paloma's voice back at her, dismantled one by one, and abandoned the exercise.

The truth, which she examined the way one probes a bruise, was this: she could bear anything happening to herself. She had proven it repeatedly and at volume, to the admiration of newspapers and the despair of her family. What she could not bear — what sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone — was the image of Paloma Vasquez standing in some requisitioned police hall in a borrowed coat, producing papers that would not survive a second glance, saying her name to men who wrote names down. Paloma, who hummed off-key when she drove. Paloma, who had read four years of private letters and confronted her employer with the wreckage and then, when the truth came out, had not reported her — had chosen her, silently, without being asked, in a way Odile had never earned and could never repay.

Pride, she thought, is just fear that has learned to stand up straight. She had known that for decades. Knowing a thing had never once prevented her from feeling it.

Near two o'clock she heard the floorboards above creak, and pause, and creak again in the direction of the stairs. She did not move. Through the gap beneath the study door she watched the strip of candlelight travel down the hall, hesitate outside her door — a long hesitation, the length of a breath held and released — and continue on toward the kitchen.

Odile gave it five minutes. Then she rose, took the lamp, and followed.

In the kitchen, by the light of a single candle stub, Paloma was packing a satchel with the methodical calm of a woman defusing something. Spare stockings. The depot receipt, unfolded now, smoothed flat. A loaf wrapped in cloth. And beside it all, laid out on the scrubbed table like instruments of surgery: papers. Three papers, in three names, none of them hers. The suffrage network's quiet work, its best forgery, the thing Odile had forbidden and that had evidently been sitting in the coal scuttle or under a floorboard for days, waiting.

"You went to Marlow on Tuesday," Odile said from the doorway. "When you said you were posting parcels."

Paloma didn't jump. She finished folding the cloth over the loaf before she looked up, which told Odile everything about how long this had been decided. "There wasn't time to explain. There still isn't, really."

"I ordered you not to go."

"You did. I remember. I was there." Paloma tucked the papers between the stockings, gentle as laying eggs. "You order lots of things. I've driven you to forty rallies where you ordered ten thousand women to disobey their husbands, their priests, and His Majesty's Government. I've never once heard you suggest obedience was the point."

"This is different."

"How?"

"Because—" and there it was again, the stone, the crack running through the wall of her voice. Odile set the lamp down on the table so that her hands would have somewhere to be. "Because I have buried friends, Paloma. Real ones, in boxes, and I did the arithmetic on every one of them afterward and it never came out even. Because I am old enough to know that courage is cheap at rallies and ruinous in kitchens. Because if you go and something happens to you, I will spend whatever years remain to me in this house, winding that clock, knowing—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "Knowing that I sent you. That I let my promise to a woman across the sea cost me the person across the table."

The kitchen was very quiet. The candle guttered and righted itself.

"Then come with me," Paloma said softly.

"I can't. You know I can't. If I'm seen on that road—" 

"Then stay. And hate it. And I'll go, and I'll be careful, and I'll be back by Thursday, and you'll wind your clock and pretend this was your idea." Paloma shouldered the satchel. At the door she paused, and for a moment she looked so much like a younger version of Odile herself — the same lifted chin, the same refusal — that Odile nearly laughed at the cruelty of it. "For what it's worth," the girl said, "you didn't send me. Nobody sends anybody anywhere. People just finally admit where they were always going."

She was gone before Odile found an answer. Headlights swept the yard, dimmed at the lane out of courtesy to the blackout, and faded. The kitchen door breathed shut.

Odile stood alone among the cooling stove and the folded loaf's absence and the small pale rectangle where the receipt had lain. On the table, tucked beneath the lamp where Paloma had surely left it deliberately, was one page torn from a notebook. Odile picked it up. It was a map of the coast road, annotated in pencil — checkpoint times, the sergeant's patrol schedule, the tide tables circled twice — the work of days, done in secret, offered freely.

At the bottom, in Paloma's blunt handwriting, was a single line:

*If I'm not back by Thursday, burn everything and plant the tree anyway.*

Odile Marchand, who had faced mounted police and prison governors and her own sister, sat down slowly on a kitchen chair and put her head in her hands, and discovered that pride, that sturdy old companion, had quietly packed its bags and left her entirely alone with fear.

She gave herself until dawn to despair. Then she went upstairs, took down her traveling coat, and began, with trembling hands, to plan how an old woman under watch might follow her driver to the sea.
