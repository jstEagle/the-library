## 13. The Road to the Coast

I had driven that road perhaps two hundred times in daylight. I knew every bend the way you know the lines of your own hand — the long curve past Hollis's farm where the hedge grew wild with dog roses in June, the dip before the mill where water crossed the road and splashed the wheels on wet days, the stretch of beech trees that made a green cathedral of the lane in summer.

At eleven o'clock at night, with no moon and every lamp within thirty miles extinguished by order of His Majesty's Government, I knew none of it at all.

The headlamps were hooded, slitted down to two thin blades of light that showed me perhaps ten feet of road and nothing else. The rest was blackness — not the friendly darkness of a bedroom or the honest dark of a cellar, but an oceanic black, a black with weight, pressing against the windscreen as though the whole countryside had leaned in to watch what I would do next.

"You're going too fast," said the papers, from the seat beside me.

I nearly put us both in the ditch. "Don't *do* that."

"I wasn't doing anything. I was sitting here being papers." Mrs. Fennick's voice, dry as August grass, came out of the gloom where she sat bundled in Odile's old traveling coat. "But you were going too fast, Miss Vasquez, and now you know it, because you swerved."

She was right, which was insufferable of her. I slowed. The engine dropped its note obediently, and we crept onward through the black like a pair of conspirators in a very slow getaway.

It had taken me forty minutes to talk my way out of Wren Hollow without waking the entire household, which was to say: I had lied to Cook, lied to the maid, and left a note for Odile that read, in its entirety, *Gone to fetch the thing. Back when I have it. P.* — which was either the bravest or stupidest document ever composed in that kitchen, and I genuinely could not tell which.

Odile had ordered me not to go. She had stood in the hall with her spine straight as a tent pole and said, "If you are stopped, they will ask questions, and if they ask questions, they will find her name in your mouth, and then everything is finished — hers, mine, and yours." And I had stood there and agreed with her, nodding like a sensible woman, while some small stubborn creature inside my chest laced up its boots.

Now the creature was driving.

* * *

Mrs. Fennick had appeared at the garden gate at half past ten carrying a leather satchel and the expression of a woman who had never once been surprised by anything, including her own arrival places.

"The network owes Odile more than she'll admit," she'd said, handing me the satchel through the gate bars like contraband, which, technically, it was. "Papers. Not good ones — don't look at them too closely, the photograph is of a girl named Edith Carrow who has unfortunately got your coloring but not your chin — and a letter of introduction to a man in Marlow who owes me a favor older than you are. Also sandwiches."

"Sandwiches?"

"You can't save the world hungry, dear. It makes people reckless."

I had wanted to refuse her. Some part of me understood, even then, that every person who helped me was stepping one foot further into the same fire Odile was standing in, and that Mrs. Fennick, sixty-one years old and built like a teapot, had no business anywhere near that fire. But she had looked at me over the gate with those pale, unblinking eyes, and she had said, "You'll be stopped at Marlow Bridge. Everyone is. You'll need someone beside you who looks harmless and talks like a headmistress, and I have been told on many occasions that I talk exactly like a headmistress."

So here she was, in the passenger seat, being harmless and headmistressy at the English coastline in the middle of the war, holding forged papers and a tin of sandwiches, and I found — this surprised me — that I was glad of her. Terror, I was learning, is much harder to carry alone. Shared, it becomes something almost like courage, or at least like company.

"Tell me about the depot clerk again," she said.

"Small man. Spectacles. Nervous about his supper." I took the bend past Hollis's farm at a crawl, feeling for it rather than seeing it, and found the hedge exactly where my hands remembered it. "He mentioned the morning tide twice. He wanted me to know the crates would be gone. I think he thought he was warning me off."

"And instead?"

"Instead he gave me the schedule."

Mrs. Fennick made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. "Men rarely notice what they've given away. It's their single greatest weakness and I have built forty years of suffrage work upon it."

* * *

We passed through the first village at midnight.

Blackout, I discovered, does strange things to a place. In daylight, Ashcombe was nothing — a church, a post office, six cottages, a pub with a sign of a leaping stag. In blackout it became a held breath. Every window shuttered, every door sealed against the smallest leak of light, the whole village crouched under the stars like a field of animals pretending to be stones. My hooded lamps swept across a wall and caught, for one instant, a chalked sign: *LIGHTS OUT — THE ENEMY SEES.*

The enemy sees, I thought. Everyone was so busy hiding from the enemy across the water that nobody seemed to wonder what the enemy already knew, or how he'd come to know it, or whether he might simply have read it in a letter — the way I had once read four years of letters and seen nothing, and then, suddenly, everything.

"Pull over a moment," Mrs. Fennick said. "I want to check the papers by feel. There's a torch in the satchel but we daren't light it."

I drew the car onto the verge and killed the engine. The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere a nightjar called, that weird churring sound like a sewing machine run by ghosts, and beneath it the tick of the cooling engine, and beneath that — nothing. The whole world holding still around two women and a satchel of lies.

Her fingers moved through the leather bag, counting by touch. "Papers for Edith Carrow, domestic servant, traveling to see her aunt in Marlow. Letter of introduction. Rail warrant, unused, in case you must abandon the car — you won't, but plans should have doors in them. Sandwiches." A pause. "And this."

Something pressed into my hand. Small, flat, wrapped in cloth.

"What is it?"

"A letter from me to the man in Marlow. If the papers fail, give him that and say Fennick sent you. If the papers succeed—" I could hear the shrug in her voice. "Burn it."

"Why would you do this?" I asked. It came out blunter than I intended. "You barely know me. You like Odile, yes, everyone likes Odile, Odile is extremely likable, but this isn't liking, this is—"

"Treason, possibly. I've never had it formally defined." She settled back into her seat, unhurried. "In 1908 I stood on a platform in Bristol while men threw things at me, and I learned something, Miss Vasquez, that I have never since unlearned. When a law is wrong, obeying it isn't loyalty. It's just obedience wearing loyalty's coat." Her hand patted my arm once, briefly, in the dark. "Odile passed Greta Lindqvist's words because they were true and needed saying. I'm passing these papers because you need driving to the coast and I need to not be the sort of woman who lets a girl do a foolish brave thing alone. None of it is complicated. People only pretend it's complicated so they have an excuse to do nothing."

I sat with that in the dark for longer than was strictly necessary for starting a car again.

Then I turned the key, and we went on.

* * *

The second village had a checkpoint. We saw it before we reached it — a smudge of deeper black where the road narrowed, and then the strike of a match behind a shielded lantern, and a voice calling, "Halt! Who goes there?" in the tone of a boy who had practiced the phrase all evening and was delighted to finally use it.

He was very young. That was my first thought, absurdly: *he is somebody's son, and he is standing in the cold at midnight because the world has gone mad, and he is going to ask me questions I cannot answer honestly.* My heart was slamming so hard I felt it in my teeth.

"Evening!" Mrs. Fennick called out before I could speak, in a voice pitched precisely between cheerful and weary, the voice of a thousand schoolmistresses arriving late from a governors' meeting. "My niece is driving me home to Marlow. Dreadful night for it, but my sister's taken ill."

The boy — sixteen at most, rifle taller than his sense of purpose — leaned into the lamplight spilling through the slit of our hooded lamps. He looked at Mrs. Fennick. He looked at me. He looked, with the thoroughness of youth confronted by its first real mystery, at the bonnet of the car.

"Papers?"

Mrs. Fennick produced them. I heard him fumble with the folds, heard the paper tremble slightly in the cold, heard him clear his throat the way boys do when they want to seem official.

"Edith Carrow," he read. "Domestic... servant." A pause, in which I felt my entire future arrange itself around the shape of my own chin, which did not match the photograph, which he was now, mercifully, holding upside down. "Going where?"

"Marlow," said Mrs. Fennick. "To her aunt. Who is ill. As I said. Do keep up, dear, it's freezing."

"Yes — sorry — ma'am." He handed the papers back through the window, and then, because he was young and lonely and it was midnight and we were the first living souls he'd spoken to in four hours, he lingered. "Quiet night, anyway. Nothing comes through here. Sometimes I think the whole war's happening somewhere else, if you follow me."

"Oh, it isn't," said Mrs. Fennick pleasantly. "It only looks that way from the outside. Goodnight, soldier."

He stepped back and waved us through, and I drove on with my hands shaking so badly on the wheel that I had to grip it in both fists, and it was a full mile before I trusted my voice.

"He didn't even look at the photograph properly."

"They never do. Authority checks what it expects to find and skips what frightens it. It's why the movement works." She unwrapped a sandwich in the dark and offered me half. "Eat. Your blood sugar is audible."

I ate. It was ham and pickle, slightly crushed, entirely the best sandwich of my life. And somewhere between Ashcombe and the coast road, in the blackness between two sleeping villages, the terror in my chest changed its shape. It did not get smaller. If anything it got larger — larger and stranger and steadier, until it stopped feeling like fear at all and started feeling like direction. Like a compass needle that had spent years spinning politely over other people's business and had finally, at last, swung round and fixed itself on north.

I had spent my whole life believing history happened elsewhere. To soldiers in newspapers. To queens in books. To other people, in other rooms, at other hours. Tonight I was breaking the law of a nation at war, in a borrowed motorcar, carrying another woman's name in a satchel and a third woman's secret in my chest, and history was not elsewhere. History was in the seat beside me eating a ham sandwich. History was the boy at the checkpoint who thought the war was far away. History was me.

"Mrs. Fennick," I said.

"Mm?"

"When this is over — whatever happens — I'm going to learn to drive properly. Properly properly. Night roads, bad roads, all of it. And I'm going to stop reading other people's letters out of loneliness and start writing my own."

There was a pause. Then, dry as August grass: "That, Miss Vasquez, is the most revolutionary thing anyone has said to me in six years, and I once shared a prison cell with Emmeline herself."

* * *

We smelled the sea before we saw any sign of it — salt and tar and cold iron on the wind, seeping in through the gap in the window — and then the road began its long descent toward the coast, and ahead of us, faint as breath on glass, the sky over the water showed the first gray suggestion of not-night.

Dawn was coming. The tide was coming. Both on the same schedule, and neither waiting for me.

"We'll reach Marlow Bridge within the half hour," Mrs. Fennick said quietly, folding the sandwich paper into a neat square, because even smugglers tidied up after themselves. "And there will be a sergeant there, not a boy. Sergeant Doyle. He asks questions the way a surgeon asks where it hurts — thoroughly, and while watching your face."

I nodded. My hands were steady now. Whatever was coming, I found I could meet it, because I finally understood what I was carrying and why.

The bridge lights appeared out of the dark ahead of us, low and yellow and absolute.

A figure stepped into the road and raised one hand.

"Stop the car," said Mrs. Fennick. "And let me do the talking — until he asks you something only *you* can lie about."
