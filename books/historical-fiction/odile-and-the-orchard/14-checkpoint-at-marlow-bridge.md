## 14. Checkpoint at Marlow Bridge

The fog came off the river in the hour before dawn, and by the time the headlights of the Austin found Marlow Bridge, it had swallowed everything but the sound of water moving underneath.

Paloma had been driving for six hours. Her hands had learned the shape of the wheel so thoroughly that when she looked down at them in the dark they seemed to belong to someone else — someone competent, someone with a plan. The forged papers sat in her coat's inner pocket, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. They were good papers. Nell Ashworth's cousin in the suffrage network had a gift for ink and stamps, and she had made them out to *Mrs. E. Whitcombe, traveling on family business*, which was a lie with just enough truth folded into it to hold its shape. Odile Marchand was family, if not in any way a census taker would understand.

The bridge appeared out of the fog all at once, the way bridges do — a suggestion of ironwork, then a lantern swinging, then a barrier lowered across the road with a finality that made her stomach drop through the floor of the car.

A soldier stepped out of the white nothing. Then another. The first one raised a hand, palm out, and Paloma brought the Austin to a stop with a lurch that she hoped read as ordinary rather than as terror.

"Evening, miss." He was young. They were all young now; the older ones had gone to France months ago and come back changed or hadn't come back at all. "Sergeant will want a word."

The sergeant materialized beside her window like something out of a bad dream, except bad dreams didn't smell of pipe tobacco and wet wool. He was perhaps fifty, with a mustache that had been magnificent in another decade and a face that suggested he had spent thirty years being disappointed by the public.

"Papers."

She handed them over. Her fingers did not shake. She noted this with a kind of distant pride, the way one might note that a houseplant had survived a frost.

The sergeant read. He read slowly. He read the way a man reads who has been paid to find fault and intends to earn his wage. Somewhere behind him, the river went on talking to itself under the bridge, indifferent to all of it.

"Mrs. Whitcombe." He said the name as though tasting it. "Traveling on family business. At four in the morning."

"The tide," Paloma said. "Family business waits on no clock, Sergeant, but ships do."

It was a good line. She'd been polishing it since Reading. The sergeant did not appear moved by it.

"And where is it you're bound?"

"The coast. Littlehampton way."

"That's a fair distance for a woman driving alone in the dark."

"My husband prefers I travel at night," she said. "He says the roads are quieter."

"Does he." The sergeant looked past her into the car — at the empty passenger seat, at the rug she'd laid over the back seat, at the thermos of tea Odile had pressed on her at the door with hands that would not stop trembling until Paloma had covered them with her own. "And where is your husband tonight?"

"At home. Asleep. Where husbands ought to be at four in the morning." She smiled. It felt enormous on her face, like wearing someone else's hat. "Unless you'd like me to wake him and have him drive down here to confirm my story. I'm sure he'd be delighted. He does love an early start."

This was, she understood dimly, either very brave or very stupid, and the difference between the two was about to be decided by a man with a mustache.

The sergeant did not smile. He walked around the car instead, slow as syrup, and Paloma sat with her hands at ten and two and listened to her own heart doing something unseemly against her ribs. Through the windshield the fog turned the lantern light into halos. Everything looked holy and doomed at once.

He came back around. "Open the boot, please."

*The letter.* The letter was not in the boot — she had sewn it into the lining of her coat three villages back, sitting in a lay-by with a needle from the emergency kit and her heart going like a sewing machine itself — but the boot held other things. A spade. Two sacks of soil. A small potted tree wrapped in sacking, because Odile had said, in that voice that was not to be argued with, *if you're going to fail, fail completely, and plant it yourself.*

"Of course," Paloma said, and got out of the car on legs that had opinions of their own.

The sergeant lifted the sacking. He looked at the little tree — a quince, barely knee-high, its roots balled in Hertfordshire clay — for a long, long moment.

"Moving a tree," he said. "At four in the morning. In wartime."

"It's a memorial," Paloma heard herself say. "For my sister's boy. Killed last spring." The lie arrived whole, unbidden, dressed in mourning crepe, and she hated how easily it came, hated the borrowed grief, hated that somewhere a real woman's real dead boy was being conscripted into service as her alibi. "We're planting it where he used to play. She couldn't face the journey herself. So I'm doing it for her."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the younger soldier by the barrier had stopped shifting his feet.

The sergeant put the sacking back over the roots with something almost like gentleness. "Sorry for your loss," he said, gruffly, and for one shining moment Paloma thought it was over.

Then: "Wait here," he said, and took her papers with him, and walked toward the tollhouse at the far end of the bridge, and the fog closed behind him like a door.

* * *

She stood by the car in the cold and did the arithmetic of disaster.

If he telephoned ahead — and there was a telephone in the tollhouse, she could see the shadow of it through the window — if he telephoned Littlehampton, or the depot, or anyone whose business it was to know that confiscated German property was scheduled to sail on the morning tide, then everything ended here on this bridge in the fog, six hours from done. Odile would be questioned. Odile would lie badly, because Odile lied the way a cathedral stands — magnificently but without any intention of moving — and they would see straight through her, and Greta's name would come up, and the letters, and the network, and everything Odile had spent four years threading through the suffrage lines would unravel in an afternoon.

And it would be Paloma's fault. Not for lying — she'd discovered she was rather good at lying, which was its own small horror — but for the tree. For bringing the tree. For sentiment. For wanting to carry a piece of the orchard to the coast as though a promise could be kept by proxy, as though planting something in frozen ground could substitute for saying the true thing out loud.

She thought about turning around. The thought lasted four seconds. Then she thought of Odile standing in the doorway of Wren Hollow with the lamplight behind her, saying *I forbid you to go* in exactly the voice she used for saying *I shall chain myself to the railings*, and Paloma got back behind the wheel and waited, because forbidding had never once worked on anyone in that house, and it wasn't going to start working on her.

The fog thickened. A cart horse clopped past on the towpath below, invisible, its driver humming something tuneless. The world reduced itself to the radius of the lantern.

Footsteps. The sergeant returning — but not alone.

Beside him walked a man Paloma had not seen before, and she would remember afterward that her first thought was entirely practical: *he walks well.* Then she saw the uniform beneath the greatcoat — captain's pips, mud-stained, the uniform of a man recently returned from somewhere the fog had never reached — and revised her assessment downward, because officers were the ones who asked questions with teeth in them.

"This the lady?" the officer said.

"This is her," said the sergeant. "Captain Hale, ma'am. On leave. He was crossing on foot when we stopped you."

Captain Hale looked at her. He was perhaps thirty, with a face that had been handsome before France and was now handsome in a more complicated way, the way a repaired thing is beautiful. His eyes had the flat calm she'd learned to recognize in the men who came back — a stillness that wasn't peace.

"Mrs. Whitcombe," he said.

"Captain."

"Sergeant Prosser tells me you're carrying a memorial tree to your sister's family. At four in the morning. Through a fog you could cut with a knife." He said it without accusation, merely laying out the facts like cards. "And that your papers are in order but your story is — " he glanced at the sergeant — "what was the word?"

"Elaborate," said Sergeant Prosser, with relish.

"Mm." Captain Hale turned back to her. And then he did something no one had done to Paloma Vasquez in years, possibly ever: he looked at her properly. Not at the papers, not at the hat, not at the category of *woman driving alone*, but at her — at whatever it was in her face that she could not manage to make into Mrs. Whitcombe. "Ma'am," he said quietly, "I've spent eleven months learning to tell the difference between people running *from* something and people running *toward* something. The first kind can't look at you. The second kind can't look away from the road ahead." He nodded at the car, at her hands still gripping the wheel through the open door. "You haven't looked away from that road once since I walked up. Whatever you're carrying to the coast, it matters."

Paloma said nothing. There was nothing safe to say, and she had finally learned the discipline of that.

"I'll vouch for her," Hale said to the sergeant.

Prosser's mustache bristled. "Sir, with respect, you don't know her from Adam's housecat."

"No," Hale agreed. "I don't." He took off his cap — a small ceremony, absurd in the fog — and held it in both hands. "But I'll write my name and unit in your book, and if it turns out she's a spy, you can send the bill to the War Office and they can dock my pay, court-martial me, and shoot me at dawn, in whichever order amuses them most. I'm due back at the front in nine days. There are worse ways to spend them than being shot for trusting somebody."

The silence stretched. Down under the bridge the river kept up its low commentary, and somewhere in the fog a church bell struck five, and Paloma understood suddenly that she was watching a man spend something he could not get back — his name, his record, his standing — on a stranger, for no reason he could name, or rather for the only reason that mattered, which was that she had looked like someone worth trusting.

"Why?" she said. It came out rougher than she intended. "Why would you do that?"

Hale considered the question as though it deserved consideration. "Because in France," he said, "I watched a French farmer walk three miles through shellfire to bring our company bread, and when the major asked him why, risking his life for Englishmen he'd never met, the old man shrugged and said, *one learns who people are by what they carry.*" He nodded at the boot, at the sacking-wrapped quince. "You're carrying a tree. In the dark. In winter. Nobody carries trees in the dark in winter for anything but love." He put his cap back on. "That's good enough for me."

Sergeant Prosser sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to forces larger than the King's Regulations. He wrote something in his ledger — Paloma caught the words *papers verified* and *assisted by Capt. Hale* — and handed her documents back through the window.

"Go on then, Mrs. Whitcombe," he said. "Mind the fog on the Arun road. And mind—" he paused, and something flickered under the disappointment that was his resting expression, something that might, in better light, have been kindness— "mind you get where you're going. Tides wait for nobody. My own brother missed one at Southampton in 'ninety-one and swore at the sea for a decade after."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Don't thank me. Thank the captain. I wanted to search the boot twice."

"You did search the boot twice."

"Aye, and I wanted to a third time." He touched his cap and trudged back toward the tollhouse, muttering something about the end of the world arriving on schedule.

* * *

Paloma drove onto the bridge with her heart going like a hammer in a henhouse. Halfway across, she pulled over — there was a wider place by the parapet — and rolled down the window, and Captain Hale, who had resumed walking toward the far side with his collar up against the damp, stopped when the engine noise died.

"Captain."

He came back to the window. Up close, in the lantern light, she could see the tiredness in him properly now, the deep kind that sleep doesn't touch.

"What you did," she said. "You don't know what it cost you. You don't know what I might have been."

"No," he agreed. "But I know what you are. You're someone the fog hasn't beaten yet." He smiled — briefly, crookedly, a smile that had clearly been rationed like everything else in his life lately. "Besides. My mother taught school in Littlehampton for twenty years. If you're headed where I think you're headed, you'll pass her old schoolhouse on the Arun road. Blue door. Give it a nod for me."

"I will," Paloma said, and found, absurdly, that her eyes were hot. "Captain — if I could repay—"

"You already have." He tapped the roof of the car once, gently, as if blessing it. "I'd forgotten what it looked like, people rushing toward things. Go on. You've a tide to catch."

He walked away into the fog, and the fog took him the way it takes everything, gradually and then completely, and Paloma sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, undone.

Because this was the thing — this was the thing she had believed all her life, the belief she'd built her whole careful self upon: that history happened elsewhere, to other people, and that strangers were weather — to be endured, not trusted. That the world was a large cold machine and everyone in it was looking out for their own gear-teeth. Four years of reading other people's mail had only confirmed it: every letter a small fortress, every correspondent a stranger, every act of kindness in print a performance for the record.

And now a man she would never see again had mortgaged his name for her on the strength of a tree and a look, and the whole architecture of her worldview had developed a crack right down the middle, and through the crack was coming something warm and terrifying, which was the possibility that the world was full of people — Prosser and his thrice-searched boot, Hale and his blue-doored schoolhouse, Odile and her impossible promises — all of them, all of them, rushing toward things.

She let off the brake.

The road east ran gray and empty through the fog, and she drove it fast, faster than was wise, the little quince riding in the boot like a sleeping child, the letter flat against her ribs keeping its own counsel. The sky behind her had begun, very faintly, to consider getting light.

By her reckoning she had two hours. Perhaps less.

At the top of the next rise she crested out of the fog all at once, and there below her, when the road bent, was the sea — a thin bright line of it, cold and enormous, and somewhere along that line a ship loading crates marked with red seals, and a clerk with a ledger, and a tide that had never once, in the history of water, waited for anybody.

Paloma pressed the accelerator to the floor.
