## 15. The Depot at Night

The depot sat where the road gave up and became gravel, and the gravel gave up and became mud, and beyond that there was nothing between England and the sea except a fence, two lamps, and a warehouse large enough to hold a cathedral's worth of other people's property.

Paloma killed the engine a quarter mile out and let the car roll. It came to rest against a hedge with a shudder she felt in her teeth, and then the night was very quiet — the particular quiet of coastal towns after dark, which is not silence at all but a low conversation between wind and water that never quite resolves.

She sat with her hands on the wheel.

This was, she understood dimly, the moment at which a sensible person turned around. She had driven through a blackout with forged papers borrowed from a network of women who threw bricks through windows for the vote; she had lied to a sergeant at Marlow Bridge with such fluency that she had frightened herself; and every mile of it had been in service of an errand that was, when one examined it honestly, burglary. She was about to break into a government-sealed warehouse to steal a letter.

And yet her hands would not turn the wheel. They had apparently decided this matter without consulting her, the way her heart had decided years ago to keep reading Odile Marchand's mail.

"One letter," she told the windshield. "I'll put everything back exactly as I found it."

The windshield offered no objection. She took that as consent.

* * *

The fence was the first problem, and the fence solved herself: a stretch of it near the seaward corner had been leaned on by weather until it lay at an angle of polite suggestion rather than enforcement. Paloma went over it sideways, caught her skirt, tore it, swore in Spanish under her breath — a habit from her grandmother that surfaced only in extremity and had, this year, become nearly conversational — and dropped onto wet grass on the other side.

Two lamps burned at the warehouse doors, gaslight behind sooty glass, making halos of the fog. Between them stood a door with a padlock the size of a fist. But warehouses built to hold confiscated cargo are not built by people who expect anyone to want *in*; they are built by people who expect cargo to stay put. And cargo, Paloma reasoned, circling the building with her heart going like a sewing machine, is rarely stored at the front door where the clerks can admire it.

Around the side, past a stack of crates stencilled FRESH FRUIT — PERISHABLE — DO NOT DELAY (a lie, she suspected; the fruit inside had likely perished months ago), there was a loading door. It was fastened with a bar, but the bar lifted if you lifted it, and the door swung inward six inches before something stopped it — a wedge of shadow, a smell of tar and rope and cold paper.

She listened. Wind. Water. Somewhere far off, a dog with opinions. No boots, no voices, no jingle of keys. The night watchman, if there was one, was either asleep or warm somewhere, and Paloma found she could not blame him. She slipped inside and eased the door shut behind her until only a crack remained — enough light to leave by, she told herself, though the truth was she simply couldn't bear the sound of it closing all the way.

Inside, the dark was total and enormous. Her eyes adjusted slowly, and the warehouse assembled itself around her in grays: mountains of crates under dust sheets, ropes hanging from the roof beams like vines, and high up near the ridge of the roof, three small windows through which the fog admitted a faint, sourceless gray light, so that the whole space seemed lit by the memory of daylight.

She stood still and did what she had done for four years in Odile Marchand's parlor: she read the room.

Crates were stacked in rows, chalk-marked. She moved down the nearest aisle reading the chalk as she had once read postmarks — HULL, LIVERPOOL, ROTTERDAM — and names, and dates, and here and there a red seal stamped across a lid like a wax wound. Confiscated. Enemy-origin. Held pending.

Greta Lindqvist's shipment would have come through weeks ago, when the borders closed. Botanical specimens, the papers had said. Research materials. Seized at the depot.

She found them in the third row, set apart from the others as if even the dockhands had sensed they were different: six crates, tall ones, marked in careful German script — *Dr. G. Lindqvist, Botanische Sammlung* — and across each one, the red seal, and beneath the seal, in a clerk's tidy hand, *HOLD.*

Paloma put her palm flat against the nearest crate. The wood was cold and slightly damp, and beneath her hand, when she pressed, she felt the faint give of packed paper — hundreds of sheets, pressed flat, the way botanists press flowers. Somewhere inside these boards were four decades of a woman's life's work: pressed ferns, annotated specimens, the patient green archive of a mind that had spent its whole existence cataloguing how things grow.

"They're just plants," Paloma whispered, and heard how foolish it sounded. Nothing in this war was just anything anymore.

The crowbar was leaning against the wall where some workman had left it, and its presence felt less like luck than like permission, which should have warned her. She worked it under the lid of the smallest crate. The nails shrieked. She froze, counting to sixty, listening. The dog barked twice more and gave up on the world.

The lid came up.

Packed straw, and on top of it, wrapped in oilcloth, a tin box. She almost laughed — of course. Of course Greta Lindqvist, who had spent thirty years pressing flowers between pages, would pack her most important thing the way she packed everything: carefully, in layers, so that whoever found it would have to be looking.

She unwrapped the tin with numb fingers. Inside: a bundle of letters, tied with string. Not pressed flowers. Letters.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Letters were her country. Letters were the one territory where she had always believed herself fluent — four years of reading another woman's correspondence, telling herself it was diligence, telling herself she understood what passed between people on paper. She untied the string because stopping was no longer among her available options.

The topmost envelope was addressed to *Fräulein O. Marchand, Wren Hollow,* and postmarked Hamburg, 1911.

Not to Greta. From Greta. These were Greta's letters home — the copies, or perhaps the originals, kept the way one keeps roots. Paloma told herself she would read only one. Only enough to confirm they were what the manifest said. Only diligence.

She opened the 1911 letter because it seemed safest, oldest, most innocent.

It began with weather. It described an orchard — their orchard, Wren Hollow's orchard, named tree by tree, each sapling reported on like a child: *the Cox is sulking, the Ribston has forgiven us, the seventh we have agreed upon.* Paloma smiled at that, there alone in the dark, and the smile curdled as she read on, because the orchard was not only an orchard. *You may tell your sisters in the north that the grafting takes best in silence, and that the seventh tree will come into bearing when the season turns — they will understand, and the customs men will not.* And lower: *Send the pamphlets inside the seed catalogue as before. Frau Brenner's society distributes well through the gardening clubs. No one searches a woman for seeds.*

No one searches a woman for seeds.

Paloma sat down on an upturned crate because her legs had made an executive decision of their own. She read the next letter. Then the next. The orchard bloomed open before her into something else entirely: seven trees, seven distribution routes; pruning seasons, printing seasons; frost warnings that meant raids; *the birds have been at the young stock in Leipzig, we must replant in the spring* — arrests, replacements, the quiet agricultural persistence of women passing forbidden words from country to country disguised as horticulture, year after year, while Paloma Vasquez read every page and saw only apples.

She thought of Odile dictating replies in her level voice, avoiding politics entirely. Avoiding politics! They *were* politics. They had been nothing else for twenty years, dressed in bark and blossom, and Paloma had sat at the desk beside them sharpening pencils.

She should stop reading. She knew she should stop reading. There was a clock running — a ship, a tide, a morning — and every minute here was a minute stolen from the errand.

But she hadn't yet found the letter she'd come for, and some treacherous part of her knew it, and kept turning pages.

Near the bottom of the bundle, undated in the way of things written without expectation of mailing, she found it. A single sheet, folded once, in Greta's upright hand, addressed on the outside not to Greta at all but to *Odile — for whenever it must be opened.* Paloma hesitated. Then she did the thing she had always done, the thing that had started all of this, the thing that had brought her down a dark road to a dark building in the first place.

She read first.

*My Odile — if you are reading this, then the collection has been taken, and I am asking you now for the promise we made under the half-planted row, and for one thing more, which is harder. You know what I carried for the society, and you know what you carried for me. If they come to question you, let them find nothing but a gardener's sentiment. Burn my letters. Deny me warmly. And keep the list — you know the one — not hidden, for hiding is a confession, but planted, Odile, planted where we planted everything else. When peace comes, dig it up and give it back to the living. You are the keeper, not I. You always were. I only ever held the trowel.*

Paloma read it twice. Three times. The fog-gray light from the high windows did not change; the water talked on outside; and Paloma Vasquez sat in a government warehouse holding a document that could hang the woman whose mail she had spent four years reading, and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was no longer delivering a letter.

She was deciding what to do with a secret.

The letter named Odile. Not as recipient — as keeper. As the node through which pacifist writing had flowed into England, through the suffrage network, through gardening clubs and seed catalogues, for years. Every protective instinct Paloma had — deliver it, get it to the coast, save Greta's legacy — pointed one direction. Every loyal instinct — protect Odile, burn it, deny everything — pointed the other. And underneath both ran a colder current she refused to look at directly: *she never told you. Four years. She let you read every word and never once let you see.*

The crowbar lay on the floor where she'd dropped it. Somewhere above, one of the high windows ticked as the glass contracted in the cold, a small precise sound, like a clock beginning.

Paloma looked down at the letter in her hands, at the red seals on the crates, at the tide waiting out there in the dark with its famous indifference, and she understood that whatever she did in the next five minutes would follow her for the rest of her life.

Then she heard gravel shift outside.

A lantern's glow swung through the crack in the loading door, cutting a bright line across the dusty floor — and along with it came footsteps, unhurried, heavy, and a voice humming something tuneless and cheerful, coming closer.
