## 16. What Odile Hid

The lantern gave a good light for lying to yourself and a poor one for reading truth.

Paloma discovered this sitting on an upturned crate in the sealed warehouse at Marlow Quay, Greta Lindqvist's letter open on her knee, her thumb still resting where it had been when she'd stopped understanding what she was holding.

The letter was not from Greta.

That had been the first shock, though even that had taken a moment. The envelope bore Greta's hand — she knew it now the way one knows a face, having read four years of it — but the paper inside was written in another script entirely, smaller, more hurried, pressed hard enough into the page that the words were legible from the back. And the salutation was wrong. It did not begin *Dear Odile*. It began:

*To the woman who keeps my friend's letters.*

Paloma read it again. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above her, water slapped against pilings in a rhythm too slow to be comforting.

*You will not know me,* the letter continued. *My name is Annelise Brandt, and I taught botany beside Greta at Jena until they removed me for the crime of being half-Jewish and whole-hearted about peace. Greta is ill. She will deny this if you write and ask her; she denies everything now, even her own coughing. So I am writing instead, because there are things that cannot wait for her recovery, and because she trusted you with them once already, whether or not she ever said so aloud.*

Paloma looked up at the crates stacked around her like a wooden forest. Stenciled on every side: LINDQVIST, G. — BOTANICAL SPECIMENS — JENA. Confiscated enemy property, the manifest had said. Sealed pending review.

She had thought, driving through the blackout dark with forged papers warm in her coat, that she was coming for a dying woman's last request. Plant the seventh tree. A promise made in peacetime. There had been something almost sweet in it, in its smallness — history had grown enormous around them, and here was Odile Marchand, sixty-one years old and watched by constables, being asked only to put a sapling in frozen ground.

The sweetness had curdled somewhere between the second paragraph and the fourth.

*Greta's research travels with her letters,* Annelise wrote. *You will have noticed this, if you are the reader I believe you to be — and if you have read four years of correspondence between two women who love each other, then you have read it all, whether you knew it or not. The orchards are not orchards. Or rather, they are orchards, and they are also other things, which is how the best disguises work. My friend understood early what your government is only now discovering: that a letter about apple stock can be read two ways, and that no censor alive suspects a grandmother of encoding pacifist argument in the pruning calendar.*

Paloma's hands had gone cold. Not the cold of the warehouse — the cold of a floor giving way beneath a certainty.

*When,* she thought. When had she first noticed that Odile's dictated replies never mentioned politics? Chapter two of her own private history, years ago now, standing in the parlor with the shorthand pad while Odile gazed out the window and spoke of grafts and frost and the behavior of bees. She had found it restful, then. A refuge. Two old women refusing, together, to let the century into their letters.

It had not been refusal. It had been craft.

*Read the spring letters again,* Annelise instructed. *Every mention of "the trees that bear without permission" is a passage from Greta's pamphlet on conscientious objection. "Pruning what the state has planted" is the argument against conscription, translated into horticulture so thoroughly that even I missed it for a year. And the seventh tree — ah, the seventh tree. That one is not code at all. That one is simply a promise, made under a half-planted row in 1910 by two young women who believed the world would improve if they improved it tree by tree. Some things we kept plain on purpose. Even smugglers need somewhere honest to stand.*

Paloma set the letter down on her knee and pressed both palms flat against her eyes.

*So.* The word arrived unbidden, in the voice she used for herself alone, the voice that had been getting louder since the constable's visit. *So Odile Marchand — suffragist, orchard-keeper, employer, the woman whose mail you opened every morning for four years telling yourself it was diligence — has been running pacifist literature through the suffrage network. Through the same network that lent you those papers sitting in your coat pocket right now. For years. Into Germany. From Germany. During peacetime, when it was merely eccentric, and now during war, when it is—*

She would not finish the sentence. Her mind supplied it anyway, in the constable's voice: *treason*.

* * *

Here is what Paloma Vasquez knew about treason, as of eleven o'clock on that November night: she knew it was a word that lived in newspapers, next to photographs of men whose faces always seemed surprised to be printed. She knew it happened elsewhere. It had always happened elsewhere — Belgium, the Balkans, the Continent entire, that whole smoking hemisphere where history occurred while England drank its tea. She had built her whole philosophy on it. History was a foreign country; she had merely been born within earshot of it.

And now she sat in the middle of a confiscated shipment of it, holding the evidence in her lap, wearing forged papers borrowed from the conspiracy itself.

She picked the letter up again because her hands needed occupation, and read the rest.

*There is a copy of everything in the crates,* Annelise wrote. *Not the originals — those went years ago, carried by hands braver and stupider than mine — but the drafts, the translations, Greta's own fair copies of the arguments she could not publish at home. Your government will call them propaganda. They are not. They are the most tedious kind of documents imaginable: reasoned cases against the war, addressed to reason, which is why they frighten the unreasonable so badly. If these papers burn, the arguments do not die — Greta wrote them twice, three times, she thinks in seasons the way other people think in years — but their author will take it as a verdict. She is ill, Mrs. — forgive me, I do not know your name, though Greta says Odile's driver is "a Spanish girl who reads faster than she admits." She knows you, by the way. You have been a character in our correspondence since 1911. We called you La Lectora. The Reader. We thought it was funny. I hope you find it funny eventually. Tonight you may throw this letter at a wall on our behalf.*

Paloma did not throw the letter at a wall. But she laughed — one short, appalled bark of it that echoed off the tin roof and frightened her more than the darkness had.

La Lectora. Four years of opening envelopes in the morning light of the Wren Hollow kitchen, telling herself she was protecting Odile from frauds and beggars, and all the while she had been a character. A known quantity. *The girl who reads first.* Had they discussed her handwriting? Her opinions? Had Odile, dictating some innocuous passage about codlin apples, paused to say *and Paloma noticed the frost on the south field today, I shall tell her to bank the terrace* — had she been reported across the North Sea like weather?

The strangest part, the part she kept turning over like a stone looking for what lived underneath, was that she did not feel betrayed. Not exactly. What she felt was rearranged. Every memory of the past four years stood exactly where it had stood, but the light on them had changed, the way a room changes when a lamp is moved and suddenly the familiar chair casts an unfamiliar shadow. Odile's silence about politics: not delicacy but discipline. Odile's insistence that Paloma read everything aloud before sealing it: not trust but verification — no, that wasn't fair either. Both things. Both things at once, the way a person can be genuinely fond of you and genuinely use you, and mean both without contradiction.

*Deliver this letter to Odile,* Annelise's closing paragraphs ran, *and let her choose what becomes of the papers. That is all I ask, and it is not a small thing I ask, whatever it sounds like. The authorities here believe the shipment contains dried specimens and dead science. Let them go on believing it. But if the crates are destroyed unread — if the last copies of Greta's work end their life in a customs furnace because a clerk was thorough on a Tuesday — then something will have been lost that cannot be reprinted, which is the particular voice of a particular woman arguing, patiently, furiously, for the idea that ordinary people might refuse to slaughter one another. Greta planted seven trees because she believed in things that grow slowly. Do not let them cut hers down in a warehouse.*

*I remain, madam, your obedient servant in the only army worth serving,*

*Annelise Brandt, Jena*

*P.S. — Greta adds, in a hand much shakier than mine: Tell Odile the seventh tree should be a perry pear, not an apple. She will know why. She will pretend not to. Make her say it.*

* * *

Paloma sat for a long time after that, listening to the water and doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

Because here was the shape of it, and the shape was cruel. Delivering the letter protected Greta — Annelise had been clear enough about that. A letter reaching its destination meant the papers' fate stayed in friendly hands; a letter intercepted meant questions, and questions meant names, and names meant a net drawing tight around two women, one in Jena and one in Kent. But delivering the letter meant carrying it out of this warehouse past a gate, past a sergeant's lamp, into the world where it existed as evidence. Evidence of what? Of correspondence with an enemy national. Of a network. Of Odile.

And Odile could not run. Odile had told her so with that terrible flat calm women of her generation used for saying unbearable things: *If I travel, they will follow, and if they follow, they will find what they are hoping to find.* Odile was watched. Odile was a knot the authorities were waiting for an excuse to pull. And Paloma was holding, at this moment, the loosest thread of that knot, warm from her own pocket's warmth.

Loyalty said: burn it here. Strike a match off the lantern, feed the letter to the flame, scatter the ash among the sawdust, drive home, and tell Odile the crate sailed with everything else. No letter, no evidence, no danger. Greta's papers would go into the furnace on some Tuesday, and Greta would perhaps never know, and Odile would keep her freedom and her watchfulness and her terrible flat calm, and in twenty years they would all be old and safe and nothing would have been risked.

Truth said: she asked you for something. Not the government. Not Odile. A dying woman in Jena whom you have never met, who named you La Lectora and thought it was funny, asked you to carry a piece of paper ninety miles because she believed — on the strength of four years of letters she never knew you'd read — that you were the kind of person who finishes what she starts.

And underneath both voices, quieter than either, a third: *History was never happening to other people. It was happening in your kitchen, in your shorthand pad, in your own competent hands, and you were holding it the whole time without knowing what it weighed.*

Paloma laughed again, softer this time, and it came out closer to crying than she intended.

"You absolute pair," she said to the empty warehouse, to Greta in Jena, to Odile asleep or sleepless ninety miles away. "You could have told me."

But of course they couldn't have. Telling her would have endangered her, and they had spent four years — she could see it now, could see the architecture of it, patient as grafting — keeping her safe by keeping her innocent. Reading her mail. Deciding what she could bear. The realization landed with a strange doubled sting: she had read their letters for years out of loneliness and called it duty, and they had written their letters around her for years out of protection and called it friendship, and none of them — not one of the three women tangled in this — had ever once simply said a true thing plainly to another.

Well. Someone was going to start tonight.

She folded Annelise's letter along its original creases, careful as if the creases were bones. Then she took off her left glove, worked the letter into the lining between wool and silk — the seam there had been loosened weeks ago for exactly this species of cargo, though she'd imagined banknotes, not testimony — and drew the glove back on. Her heart was going like a telegraph key. Against her ribs, the paper made a whisper-thin presence, less weight than a conscience and considerably harder to ignore.

The crates she left as she'd found them, boards replaced, straw resettled, the seal she'd cut pasted down with spit and patience. Let the clerks find nothing amiss. Let Tuesday come and go. Whatever became of Greta's fair copies, it would not become of them because Paloma Vasquez had been careless.

At the mouth of the aisle she stopped and looked back once at the stenciled forest of LINDQVIST, G., standing in the lamplight like the orchard itself, row upon row, waiting out the winter.

"Seventh tree's a perry pear," she told them quietly. "She'll say it. I'll make her."

Then she pinched out the lantern, and the dark came in like a held breath — and with it, from the direction of the gate, came the sound she had been dreading all night and pretending she hadn't heard building for the last ten minutes: boots on gravel. More than one pair. Unhurried. The unhurriedness was the worst of it; men who suspected nothing walked quickly, and men walking toward a warehouse at midnight with all the time in the world had already decided what they would find there.

Paloma stood very still in the black, one gloved hand pressed flat over her heartbeat, and began, rapidly and silently, composing the most magnificent lie of her life.
