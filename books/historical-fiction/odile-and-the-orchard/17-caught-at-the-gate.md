## 17. Caught at the Gate

The trouble with lying, Paloma had discovered in the last hour, was that it required a memory. Truth simply sat where you left it; a lie had to be carried, and carried carefully, like a full cup across a crowded room. She had already spilled some on the constable at the gate coming in — a story about her aunt's chest of linens that had sounded, even as she said it, like something invented by someone who had never owned an aunt. Now she had to get out again with the same story, and she could not entirely remember what she'd said about the aunt's name.

She pressed herself into the shadow between two crates stacked against the warehouse wall and did what Odile Marchand would do: she thought.

It was past midnight. The depot at Harrow Quay slept under a skin of fog that came off the water in slow grey breaths. Somewhere a winch creaked against its chain. The lamp above the main gate burned yellow and small, and beneath it stood a man with a lantern of his own and the unhurried posture of someone being paid by the night rather than by the result. A second man — younger, with a voice like a door swinging — had been making rounds along the seaward fence. She'd counted his footsteps. Forty-one out, forty-one back, then a long pause at the corner where he smoked. She had learned his rhythm the way she had once learned the rhythm of Odile's correspondence: by attention, by patience, by reading what people thought they were hiding.

Her heart was behaving disgracefully. It hammered so hard against her ribs that she half expected the fog itself to shudder with it. She put one hand flat against the crate beside her — *H. Whitcombe & Sons, Agricultural Implements* stencilled on its face in cheerful letters — as though the wood might lend her its composure. Crates, she reflected, were admirable things. They knew exactly what they contained and never once panicked about it.

Inside the nearest crate, wrapped in sacking beneath a layer of Greta Lindqvist's confiscated botanical papers, lay a letter. Not the letter she had come for, exactly — or yes, exactly that letter, but not at all what she had expected it to be. She had spent four years reading other people's mail out of loneliness and calling it diligence. Tonight she had read one letter that was addressed to nobody she had imagined, and it had rearranged the furniture of her whole understanding of the woman who paid her wages.

Odile. The letter named Odile. It named her as a keeper of secrets, as a courier, as — Paloma still couldn't say the word to herself without her mouth going dry — a smuggler of ideas. Greta's pacifist writings, passed hand to hand through the suffrage network, printed in pamphlets with the ink barely dry, travelling from a German botanist's desk to English drawing rooms while Paloma drove the car and read the innocuous letters aloud and believed, with the whole comfortable weight of her ignorance, that history happened elsewhere.

And now the letter was inside her coat.

Not literally inside her coat — she wasn't a fool, whatever the sergeant at Marlow Bridge might have concluded — but folded flat inside a oilskin packet sewn into the lining over her left ribs, where it sat against her side like a coal that refused to go cold. The rest of Greta's papers were back in their crate, re-strapped, re-sealed with wax she had softened and re-pressed with a thimble borrowed from a sewing kit she now blessed with her entire soul. If anyone looked closely they would see the wax was wrong. Nobody looks closely at midnight. She repeated this to herself the way other people said prayers.

Forty-one steps. The young man's boots rang along the fence line and stopped. Match flare. The smell of tobacco drifted through the fog, sweetish and cheap.

Paloma eased out from between the crates.

Her plan, insofar as she had permitted herself one, was simple to the point of insult: walk out the main gate like a woman with every right in the world to be walking out of a sealed government depot at one o'clock in the morning. Confidence, she had decided somewhere between Marlow Bridge and the quay wall, was ninety per cent costume. She had watched Odile wear it for years — watched her straighten her spine before village meetings, before hecklers, before the census taker whose questions had started all of this — and Odile had taught her, without ever meaning to, that the body believes what the spine insists upon.

She got eleven steps toward the gate.

"Oi."

The word came from behind the lamp post, and Paloma's stomach performed a manoeuvre she had previously only experienced when driving too fast down Wren Hollow's hill. The night watchman detached himself from the shadow he'd been part of, and up close he was enormous in the way that furniture is enormous — not menacing, merely present, occupying more of the world than seemed strictly fair. His lantern swung up and caught her face.

"You're the one come in earlier," he said. "Linens aunt."

"I am." Her voice emerged steadier than she had any right to expect. Some merciful instinct had taken over her throat, apparently, and was running the performance without consulting her. "Though I confess my aunt has rather more linen than I anticipated."

He frowned. It was not an unfriendly frown; it was the frown of a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy. "Depot's sealed since Friday. Special order. Nobody in, nobody out, and nothing—" his eyes went, with terrible casualness, to the front of her coat— "carried out."

"The seal is intact," Paloma said. "You may check it."

"Why would I check it?"

"Because I'm asking you to," she said, "which ought to reassure us both."

That, at least, earned a twitch at the corner of his mouth. She felt the lie assemble itself beneath her words the way a bridge assembles itself under load — planks appearing where she needed them, none of them examined too closely. She was aware, distantly, that she was enjoying this, and that the enjoyment frightened her considerably more than the watchman did.

"My aunt," she went on, warming to the fiction, "kept a chest of household linens in storage here — tablecloths, mostly, and a christening gown that has been awaiting a christening for eleven years, which is another story entirely. When the depot took over the lease she moved everything into bonded storage. She's poorly now — her chest, not her spirits; her spirits are indestructible — and asked me to fetch the gown. In case." She let the word *in case* sit in the fog between them, where it did its quiet work. Everyone in 1914 understood *in case*. "I have a receipt."

"You don't," said the watchman.

"I have it in my bag," Paloma said, which was technically true, because she had forged a receipt at a table in the Blue Boar using a pen she had stolen from the landlord, and it was in her bag, and it said *One (1) chest, household linens, removed at owner's request, R. Vasquez*, in handwriting she had practised three times on the back of a railway timetable. Whether it would survive scrutiny was a question she declined to ask herself.

"And your name's Vasquez?"

"It says so on the receipt."

He grunted. Behind him, forty-two steps away, the younger man's cigarette had gone out and his boots were starting up again along the fence. Time was performing its old trick of moving both too fast and too slowly.

"Trouble is," the watchman said, shifting his lantern, "there's orders. Nothing leaves. Not even—" he peered at her bag, a soft leather thing with a broken clasp that Paloma had owned since she was sixteen— "linens. Especially not anything off the German manifest. That's all locked in the east bay, and there's men coming at first light to inventory it, and if anything's missing it's my neck, miss, and I've only got the one."

Something in his tone — the weary honesty of it — gave her a foothold. He wasn't her enemy. He was a man with one neck and a family presumably attached to it, standing in the fog doing a job he hadn't chosen. She thought suddenly of the soldier at Marlow Bridge who had vouched for her at cost to himself, a stranger spending his own credit on her behalf, and how it had undone something in her that she was only now beginning to understand. People were kind. People were also frightened. Both things could be true of the same man holding the same lantern.

"Then I'll save you the trouble of the neck," Paloma said. "Check the seal yourself. If it's broken, fetch whoever you must and let them arrest me — I'll wait right here, and I'll be extremely apologetic. If it's intact, then nothing is missing, because nothing has left, because I—" she held up both hands, palms out, empty except for the bag— "am carrying a christening gown's worth of nothing at all."

The audacity of it sang in her blood. She heard her own voice as if from a little distance, clear and warm and utterly certain, and marvelled at the stranger producing it. Where had this woman been hiding? Through four years of letters? Through the census taker's questions, when she'd stood in the parlour with her hands folded and told the truth because truth was easier? Courage, it turned out, was not a store of something you either had or lacked. It was a muscle, and hers had apparently been waiting all this time for sufficient provocation.

The watchman studied her for a long moment. Fog beaded on the brim of his cap. Then, to her astonishment, he sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to logic rather than to charm, set down his lantern, and said, "Wait here," and walked to the east bay doors.

She waited. Her heart resumed its disgraceful behaviour. The oilskin packet against her ribs felt less like a coal now and more like a heartbeat with opinions.

Here was the arithmetic she ran while his keys jingled: the seal on the east bay doors covered the room, not the crates within it. She had re-strapped Greta's crates and replaced the room's outer wax with her thimble-work, but the inner seals on two of the seven crates she had not dared touch twice — no, that was wrong, she had touched all of them, but two of her replacements were clumsy, the wax slightly the wrong shade of official brown, the impression shallow. If he checked the outer doors, fine. If he wandered in with his lantern and looked at crate four—

He didn't wander in. He bent, squinted at the door seal by lantern light, straightened up, and pronounced, with the gravity of a bishop, "Intact."

"As I said."

"As you said." He retrieved his own lantern and looked at her again, and something shifted in his face — not suspicion, quite; recognition of a kind. "My daughter," he said, apropos of nothing, "is fourteen. Writes letters to a cousin in Toronto. Reads them to me after, sometimes. Says it's so I know what's going on in her life." He paused. "You've got a look about you, miss. Like somebody carrying more than a gown."

The fog pressed close. Paloma found, to her horror, that her eyes wanted to sting, and she mastered them through sheer indignation at their timing.

"What I'm carrying," she said gently, "is a promise. And those weigh nothing at all, which is why everyone assumes they're easy to keep."

He considered this. Whatever he saw in her face made him step aside.

"Gate's the third latch," he said. "Mind the bolt, it bites."

* * *

She was through the gate and forty yards down the quay road before her knees informed her, in writing, of their objections. She leaned against the harbour wall among the mooring rings and the smell of tar and dead fish, and laughed — a small, unsteady, astonished laugh that the fog swallowed whole. She had lied to a policeman at Marlow Bridge and it had cost her a sleepless hour. She had just lied to a watchman with a lantern and a daughter in Toronto, and it had cost her nothing but a christening gown that did not exist, and somewhere in the middle of it she had stopped being afraid and started being *good at it*, and she did not yet know what to make of that.

The letter pressed warm against her side. She thought of Odile — Odile who had burned nothing, which frightened Paloma more than flames would have; Odile who had ordered her to stay home in a voice that cracked exactly once, on the word *stay*; Odile who had spent four years passing dangerous paper through tea parties and pamphlet runs and never once, not in four years of dictated replies to Greta, let her mask slip by a single stitch. All those letters about orchards and seasons and the progress of the damsons — Paloma had read every one, and every one had been true and also a locked door, and she had stood outside it calling herself diligent.

Tomorrow — in six hours, in five — the tide would take Greta's crates away forever, and the letter that mattered was not in them anymore. Which meant the promise had changed shape. It was no longer *deliver the letter to the ship*. It was something larger and far more frightening, and it was waiting for her at home in the person of a woman who would want to know why her driver smelled of warehouse dust and sea fog at dawn.

But first there was the matter of getting off the quay, past the checkpoint road, and into the motorcar she had hidden behind the net sheds with a tarpaulin over it and a prayer under it.

She pushed off the wall and started walking, and that was when the second voice came — the young one, the door-swinging voice, much closer than forty-one steps should have allowed.

"Hold on. You. Miss."

Paloma turned. The young man stood at the corner of the net shed, lantern raised, and behind him, further up the road, a bicycle lamp wobbled toward them — a patrol, an actual roving patrol, at ten minutes to two in the morning, in a town where nothing had happened since the herring failed in 1909.

"You were seen at the gate," the young man said. "Talking to Hobbes. He's not authorised to release anyone after curfew."

"I wasn't released," Paloma said. "I was leaving."

"After being inside a sealed depot."

"Before it was sealed," she said, "from my perspective, which is the one I'm obliged to report from." And then, because courage is a muscle and hers had apparently developed opinions of its own, she added: "Is there a difficulty, Constable, or are we going to stand in the fog discussing perspectives until the tide comes in?"

The bicycle lamp arrived. Its owner dismounted, a corporal with a cold-reddened nose and a clipboard, and the three of them formed a small island of lamplight and suspicion in the middle of the empty road, while behind them, invisible in the dark, the last ship of the morning tide lay sleeping at her moorings with Greta's crates in her hold — minus one letter, plus one secret, both currently residing against the left ribs of a chauffeuse from Wren Hollow who had come to deliver the mail and was discovering, hour by hour, exactly how heavy the post could be.

"Well now," said the corporal, licking his pencil. "Name, business, and destination — and take your time about it, miss. We've got till dawn."

Paloma smiled. It surprised her how easily it came.

"Take mine," she said, "because I haven't got till dawn — and neither, gentlemen, do you."
