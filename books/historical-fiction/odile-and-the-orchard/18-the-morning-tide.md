## 18. The Morning Tide

I had imagined, all the way from Marlow Bridge, that the last mile would be the hardest. It was not. The last mile was the easiest thing in the world — a straight grey road unspooling toward the sea, the engine humming its one faithful note, the letter pressed flat against my ribs where I'd tucked it inside my coat like a second heartbeat. Every other mile of that night had asked something of me: the blackout villages with their shuttered eyes, the sergeant at the bridge with his questions laid out like instruments on a tray, the depot gate and the constable's torch sweeping across my face while I lied with a fluency that would have astonished my mother. But this last mile asked nothing. It only ran downhill, toward water, toward morning, and I let it carry me.

The sky over the harbour was doing that thing it does before dawn in October — not light exactly, but the memory of light, a bruised silver along the eastern edge where the sea begins to consider itself. Gulls were already up and arguing. Somewhere behind the fish sheds, a bell was ringing, though whether it rang for the tide or for breakfast I couldn't have said.

And there was the ship.

She sat low in the water at the end of the long stone quay, a cargo vessel with a name I never learned, her funnel coughing out the first smoke of the day. Steam rose from her decks in slow white columns. Men moved along her rail like ants who had somewhere important to be. On the quay beside her, a crane swung its last crate into the hold with a groan that carried all the way up the hill to me.

I did not stop the car. I did not even properly slow it. I brought it down that road at a speed that would have made Odile Marchand take the Lord's name in vain, which for Odile would have been a considerable theological event, and I skidded onto the cobbles of the quay with a sound like the whole world clearing its throat.

A dockhand jumped back, swore at me with real poetry, and then, seeing a woman alone in a motorcar at half past five in the morning, simply stared.

"Ship," I said. Which was, I grant you, not my finest sentence.

He pointed with his chin. "Goes on the tide. Half six."

I looked at the little clock on the dashboard. Twenty-five past.

Twenty-five minutes. I had driven through the dark of England for nine hours, and now I had twenty-five minutes, and the ship was four hundred yards away, and between us lay a customs shed, a barrier, a cluster of officials in greatcoats, and a locked warehouse that I had left, not ninety minutes earlier, through a window I had closed behind me as carefully as if I were tucking in a child.

I want to be honest here, because I have spent enough of this story lying — first to myself, then to constables, and finally, magnificently, to a sergeant at a checkpoint whose tea I still owe someone — so let me say plainly what I felt in those twenty-five minutes.

I felt certain I would make it.

That is the cruelty of hope. Not that it fails you at the end, but that it holds your hand the entire way, warm and reasonable, whispering *of course, naturally, there's time* right up until the moment there isn't.

* * *

Getting to the barrier was easier than I feared, because the world was still mostly asleep and the men on duty were mostly cold. I walked — I did not run; running attracts eyes, and I had learned that lesson at some cost to my dignity in the warehouse yard — I walked with the brisk purpose of a woman who has every right to be where she is, which is a costume I had put on somewhere around midnight and found, to my surprise, that it fit.

"Papers," said a young man at the barrier, barely looking up. He had a chilblain on one ear and the general expression of someone counting the minutes until his relief came.

I gave him the forged transit pass from the suffrage network's folder, the one with the stamp that Mrs. Frobisher in Tunbridge Wells had assured me "would fool a bishop." It fooled a man with cold ears. He waved me through without ever learning my name, and I remember thinking, absurdly, how strange it is that the largest things in a life can pass through the smallest gates.

The quay stretched out ahead of me, wet stone shining under the growing light. The ship loomed. And now I could see the crates — *her* crates, Greta Lindqvist's crates, stacked on the after deck among the general cargo, marked with stencilled numbers and the word LEIPZIG crossed out by some clerk's indifferent pen and replaced with CONFISCATED EFFECTS. They had loaded her at the end, almost carelessly, three wooden boxes that contained fourteen years of a woman's life: her specimens, her notebooks, her measurements of how light falls through leaves, everything she had learned about orchards and the patience of trees.

Everything except the letter.

The letter rode against my ribs, and my ribs knew it, the way you know a stone in your shoe — not painfully, but constantly, a small insistence that would not be argued with.

There was a gangway. There was a mate at the foot of it with a clipboard. There were perhaps sixty yards of open quay between me and both.

"Passenger?" said the mate, when I reached him, in the tone of a man who has already decided the answer.

"No. I need—" My voice came out wrong, too high, and I dragged it down. "There's a crate aboard. Confiscated effects, consigned Leipzig, re-marked. There's an error in the manifest. A document was included that shouldn't have been — it needs to come off before sailing."

It was a good lie. It was, in its bones, even a true lie, which is the best kind, because the truth underneath keeps the whole structure standing. There *was* a document that shouldn't have been where it was. The error was mine, but errors are errors.

The mate looked at me. Then at the crane, which was swinging another crate — not hers, some farmer's turnips or tins — into the hold. Then at the sky, which had gone from bruised silver to a paler, more apologetic grey.

"Manifest corrections go through the customs office," he said. "Opens at eight."

"The ship sails at half six."

"Aye," he said, with the mild wonder of a philosopher. "That's the tide for you."

"I can go aboard. Five minutes. I'll sign whatever—"

"You can't go aboard." He said it kindly, which somehow made it worse. He was not a cruel man; he was simply a man standing at the exact point where the land's rules ended and the sea's began, and he had no more power over the tide than I did. Behind him, down in the hold, I could see the last of the loading happening, men passing crates hand to hand in the dimness, the work nearly done.

"Please," I said.

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, or at least to whichever chapter of my life comes next: I said *please*, and I meant it more than I have ever meant anything, and it changed nothing. The mate shook his head with genuine regret. A bosun shouted something about the tide. The gulls went on arguing overhead about matters of eternal importance to gulls.

Somewhere in those sixty seconds, I understood — fully, quietly, without drama — that I was going to fail.

* * *

You would think failure arrives like a wall. It doesn't. It arrived like weather. One moment the ship was leaving *eventually*, and the next moment the mooring lines were coming off the bollards, heavy and wet, coiled by hands that had done it ten thousand times, and the gap between the hull and the quay opened by inches, then feet, then a distance no amount of running could close.

I ran anyway. Of course I ran. I ran the length of the quay in boots meant for driving, not sprinting, past the fish sheds, past the customs shed with its eight o'clock promise, past a pair of loaders who watched me with the tolerant interest of men watching a dog chase a ferry. The letter thumped against my ribs with every stride. The engines changed their note — deeper, busier, a sound like the ship clearing her throat before speech.

"Wait!" I called, and heard how small the word was against all that iron and water. "Wait — please —"

The stern swung. The wake began, white and spreading, writing the ship's departure on the surface of the harbour in letters that faded as fast as they were made.

At the very end of the quay there was a bollard, old and green with weed, and I stopped there because there was nowhere further to stop, and I stood with one hand on the cold iron and watched Greta Lindqvist's life steam out toward the open sea without the single most important thing in it.

The strangest part — and I have turned this over many times since, in orchards and kitchens and sleepless beds — is what I felt. Or rather, what I did not feel. I did not feel the tearing grief I had rehearsed all night at the wheel, the picture of myself collapsed and ruined on the stones. I felt something quieter and far more suspicious.

I felt relief.

Not the whole of relief — there was grief in it too, and guilt, and a furious argument just getting underway somewhere below my breastbone. But threaded through all of it, unmistakable as a gold thread in grey cloth, was this: *the letter is still here.* Still warm from my body. Still folded where Greta's careful hand had written *To Odile Marchand, Wren Hollow, to be opened only by her* — and beneath that, in smaller script I hadn't noticed until the warehouse lamplight fell across it: *for she alone knows what must be done.*

The crate sailed. The letter stayed. And some part of me, some part I hadn't consulted, had known since the moment I pulled it from the false bottom of that specimen drawer that this was how it would end — that my hands, given a choice between obeying the dead woman's instruction and keeping the truth close, would keep the truth close.

Had I chosen? That was the question the tide seemed to ask, sliding back and forth against the pilings. I had run. I had lied to a mate with kind eyes and a chilblained clerk and a sergeant at Marlow Bridge. I had broken into a sealed warehouse in service of delivering that letter, and I had failed, and failing had settled the matter for me — and yet it didn't feel settled. It felt like the moment in a story where the heroine discovers she has been the author all along.

Because here is what I knew, standing on that bollard-end with the wind pulling my hair eastward: the letter named Odile. Whatever secret Greta had trusted her with — and I had read enough of those four years of orchards-and-seasons code to know there *was* a secret, a large one, the kind that made pacifist writings move through a suffrage network like blood through a hidden vein — whatever it was, it lived in that envelope now, and it lived in my keeping, and I had just watched the simplest exit sail away without me.

Failure, yes. But also — I tested the word carefully, the way you test ice — *choice.*

The ship grew small. The gulls followed it out for a while, then thought better of it and came back to argue over the fish sheds. The sun finally committed itself, a thin coin of pale gold above the headland, and the whole grey harbour turned briefly to hammered tin.

I became aware, gradually, that the mate from the gangway had come down the quay and was standing a respectful few feet away, holding out a tin cup.

"Tea," he said. "You look like you've raced the tide and lost."

"I have."

"It usually wins." He said it without mockery. We drank our tea side by side, watching the empty water, two strangers united briefly by the ancient human experience of arriving second. "Anything important, what sailed?"

"Somebody's life," I said. "Most of it, anyway."

He nodded as if I'd commented on the weather. Perhaps to him I had. That was the thing about this war beginning everywhere at once — whole lives were becoming weather, cargo, manifest errors, things handled by bored men in greatcoats. History wasn't happening elsewhere anymore. It had a quay, and a tide table, and a mate with a clipboard, and it had just left without me.

I finished the tea. I thanked him. I walked back up the quay to the car, and my legs, which had carried me through nine hours of night roads and one window and one magnificent lie, were suddenly shaking so hard I had to sit on the running board for a full minute, laughing in a way that was not entirely laughter, until a cat emerged from under the fish shed and regarded me with the contempt I deserved, and I got hold of myself.

Then I took out the letter.

I held it in both hands. I did not open it — God knows I wanted to, and God knows that wanting frightened me more than any constable's torch had — but I held it, feeling its weight, which was almost nothing, and its consequence, which was not.

*To Odile Marchand.* To the woman who had ordered me not to come. Who had stood in her doorway with her pride on like armor and said, *if you go, Paloma, do not come back to me expecting thanks.* Who had planted six apple trees with a German girl fifty years ago and had spent every year since pretending that row ended at six because that was simply how orchards are.

Who would, within the day, learn that her dearest friend's last words were sitting in the pocket of a chauffeur she'd hired for her driving and her discretion, and had received, apparently, neither.

I put the letter away. I started the car. The road home climbed away from the sea, and I drove it with the windows down because the smell of salt had gotten into everything and I couldn't bear to sit inside it.

Nine hours back. Blackout again by evening. Odile at the gate, or at the window, or pacing the hall — Odile, who would see my face and know before I spoke, because she always knew, because reading people was the first language either of us ever learned.

What would I tell her? That I failed? That I chose?

Both. Neither. I would tell her the tide won, which was true, and then I would take out the letter and watch her face change, which would also be true, and somewhere in the space between those two truths we would find out what we were to each other now — employer and employee, liar and lied-to, or something rawer that had no polite name yet.

The miles unwound. The letter rode against my ribs like a second heart, stubborn, insistent, entirely unwilling to be delivered anywhere but home.

I only hoped home would still have room for the truth I was bringing it.
