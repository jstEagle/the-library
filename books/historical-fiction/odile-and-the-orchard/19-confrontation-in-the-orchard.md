## 19. Confrontation in the Orchard

### Paloma

The drive back from the coast took four hours and cost Paloma every drop of petrol she had begged, borrowed, or traded for. She did not mind. Her hands were steady on the wheel in a way that frightened her more than trembling would have — steady hands belonged to women who had already decided something.

The letter sat in her coat's inside pocket, against her heart like a hot coal wrapped in linen. All the way home she rehearsed what she would say. *Madam, I have your letter. Madam, I read it. Madam, you are a traitor, and so am I now, apparently, and we ought to discuss it over tea.*

None of it sounded right. All of it sounded like the letters she used to open without thinking, back when history was a thing that happened to other people.

Wren Hollow came into view at dusk, its windows dark except one — Odile's study, where the lamp always burned whether anyone was awake to see it or not. Paloma parked the motor at an angle that would have earned her a lecture any other day and sat with her hands on the wheel, watching the lit window.

She had lied to a constable. She had broken into a government depot. She had stolen — no, not stolen; *kept* — a letter that by rights was sailing toward Germany on the morning tide, or would have been if she hadn't been three minutes too slow.

Three minutes. She could still see the ship's wake unspooling behind it like a thread being pulled from the world.

Inside the house, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and yesterday's bread. Mrs. Tilly had gone to her sister's in the village, which meant the kitchen was cold and the kettle was sulking, and there was nothing between Paloma and the study door but her own nerve.

She knocked. It seemed absurd, knocking in her own — in *Odile's* own house, after everything — but her hand did it before her pride could stop it.

"Come."

The lamp, the desk, the woman. Odile Marchand sat exactly as she had sat every evening for four years: spine straight, spectacles low, a pen in her hand though no paper lay before her. She looked up, and Paloma watched something cross her face — relief first, naked and quick as a bird, then buried so fast beneath composure that a stranger would have missed it entirely.

Paloma was no longer a stranger.

"You went," Odile said.

"You told me not to."

"I did." A pause. "Where is my hat? You've taken the motor without a word and come back without—"

"The letter didn't sail." Paloma pulled it from her coat and laid it on the desk between them. "I was late. Three minutes, perhaps four. So I kept it. Which means I've committed a crime I can't undo, for reasons I don't fully understand, and I would very much like someone to explain them to me. Now. Tonight. All of it."

Odile looked at the letter. Her hands, which had held steady through hecklers at rallies, through constables' questions, through the entire slow collapse of the civilized world, rose halfway toward it and stopped.

"Paloma—"

"No." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "No, madam. Not tonight. I have driven through two checkpoints and lied to a sergeant about whose papers those are, and I have had five years of reading your mail and telling myself it was diligence, and I am done telling myself things. Sit down — you're already sitting down, good — and tell me everything. From the orchard. From 1910. From wherever it starts."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled like an old dog turning over.

And Odile Marchand, who had faced down magistrates and mobs, reached up and removed her spectacles, and looked suddenly, terribly young.

"It starts," she said, "with a girl who couldn't keep her mouth shut. Which will surprise no one who knows me."

* * *

### Odile

Greta Lindqvist talked to trees.

That was the first thing Odile remembered — not the planting, not the oath, but standing in the mud of Wren Hollow's abandoned orchard with her sleeves rolled, watching a German botanist crouch beside a sapling no bigger than her forearm, explaining to it — *to it* — the chemistry of soil nitrogen, in the patient tone of a governess addressing a slow pupil.

"It can't hear you," Odile had said.

"Everything hears you," Greta said, without looking up. "You simply aren't listening when it answers."

They had been twenty-two and twenty-three. Odile had come to Wren Hollow to write pamphlets and escape a father who believed a woman's pen should be limited to thank-you notes. Greta had come for the orchard itself — a forgotten thing on rented land, half-dead, planted generations ago by someone whose name the parish records had misplaced.

"We'll finish the row," Greta said one afternoon, gesturing at the six gnarled elders and the gap at the end. "One tree each spring, until the row is whole. Whoever plants last tends them all."

"A ridiculous oath."

"Say it anyway."

So they had. Hands in the dirt, swearing something neither of them had words for yet — not friendship exactly, not love exactly, though both of those lived somewhere in it, tangled together like roots under a shared wall. A promise made in peacetime, when promises were cheap and the world was long.

For eleven years they kept it. Six trees. Letters crossing the water twice a month, written in a code they never admitted was a code — talk of blossom and blight, of grafting and seasons, of *the seventh* waiting patiently for its turn.

Then August of 1914, and the world went mad, and every word either of them had ever written became evidence.

"I passed her writings," Odile said now, to the girl standing across her desk with fire in her eyes. She had expected anger. Anger she could manage. What she saw instead was worse — hurt, wearing anger's coat. "Her pacifist essays. Before the war, when it was merely unpopular. After, when it was treason. Through the suffrage network — the same channels we used for pamphlets. They went to printers in Zurich, Copenhagen. Under covers of horticulture journals. Nobody reads a treatise on rootstock looking for sedition."

"And nobody opens a letter full of apple-talk," Paloma said slowly, "looking for anything at all."

It was not quite an accusation. It was close enough to sting.

"Yes," Odile said. "You were part of it. Four years, Paloma. Every envelope you carried was a small act of — call it what the law would call it. I let you do it without your knowledge or consent because knowledge is dangerous, and consent—" her voice caught on something, "—consent is a weight I had no right to hand you."

"You had every right to ask."

"Asking gives you the chance to say yes. Saying yes makes you guilty. There is no version of this where I ask and you remain safe." Odile set the spectacles down with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to do something less dignified. "There is only the version where I lie to you, and you stay free, and I carry it alone."

"Well," Paloma said, "that worked beautifully."

Despite everything — despite the fear sitting in her chest like swallowed ice — Odile felt the corner of her mouth move. The girl had been with her four years and still had the nerve of a pickpocket.

"No," she agreed. "It worked terribly."

* * *

### Paloma

Paloma walked to the window because standing still had become unbearable. Below, the orchard was a black suggestion of shapes under the moon, the seven trees — six old, one new, planted in frozen ground barely a week past — holding their winter silence.

"You knew I read them," she said. "From the beginning."

"I suspected within a month."

"And you wrote *for* me." The realization landed like a stone dropping through deep water. "All those letters about grafting and frost. You dictated them knowing I'd read every word. Knowing I'd carry them to the post. You turned me into — into a courier and never told me, because telling me was dangerous, and using me was safer than trusting me."

"Yes."

"Don't you *yes* me like that, madam, as if it's simple arithmetic—"

"It isn't arithmetic." Odile's voice came out rougher than Paloma had ever heard it. "It is the most cowardly and the most loving thing I have ever done, and I have spent five years unable to decide which it was. Do you imagine I enjoyed it? Watching you seal envelopes, wondering if you'd noticed that Greta's 'pruning schedules' arrived suspiciously punctually, that her 'weather reports' grew angrier each year? Some nights I hoped you'd noticed. Some nights I prayed you hadn't. That is what secrecy does, Paloma. It doesn't protect anyone. It just divides one person's fear among everyone who loves them."

Paloma stood at the window a long moment. The coal of the letter seemed to burn through her pocket, through her ribs.

"She's alive." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "In the letter. She writes — she was ill, in the spring, and stranded, and she asked you to plant the seventh tree, and we did, we did that much — but the rest. Does she know what you risked?"

"I don't know." For the first time since Paloma had known her, Odile Marchand sounded her age. "Four years of silence. The last thing I sent her was a warning to stop writing. The last thing she sent me was a request that might get us both imprisoned. And now her research sits at the bottom of the sea in a confiscated crate, and the only piece of her I possess is in your pocket, and I am asking myself whether I have the right to read it."

"It's addressed to you."

"It's addressed to whoever keeps it." Odile's gaze dropped to the letter on the desk. "Which, according to Greta's instructions, may be the point. She always said everything hears you. Perhaps she meant letters too."

* * *

### Odile

The silence stretched until Odile could hear her own heartbeat.

Here it was, then. The moment she had rehearsed in a hundred sleepless variations: the girl demanding truth, and herself choosing between confession and ruin. She had imagined arguing. She had imagined dignity. She had not imagined how badly she wanted simply to be *believed* — not forgiven, not excused. Believed.

"Ask me plainly," she said. "Whatever it is you came in here to ask. I'll answer honestly. It seems the least I owe you, and I find I'm rather out of practice with honesty."

Paloma turned from the window. In the lamplight her face was stripped of all its usual quickness — the wit, the impertinence, the careful armor of a girl who'd learned young that charm kept roofs over her head.

"If I take this letter to the constable tomorrow," Paloma said, "you'll be arrested. Possibly transported. Possibly worse, the way things are going. If I burn it, Greta loses whatever she trusted to it, and I become the sort of person who burns other people's promises. If I keep it—" she laughed, and there was no humor in it, "—then I'm a criminal too, and I don't even know what I'm keeping it *for*, because I haven't read it, because some stubborn shred of me thought maybe I'd read enough of your post for one lifetime."

"You haven't read it?"

"I broke into a warehouse, outran a sergeant, and drove four hours with the damn thing against my chest, and I did not read it." Paloma's chin came up. "Because it wasn't mine. Because everything else I ever read of yours was yours to give and mine to steal, and I wanted — once, just once — to be given something."

Odile pushed back her chair. Crossed the room. Took the letter from the desk and held it out between them, flat on her two palms, the way one presents a sword or surrenders one.

"Then be given it," she said. "Both halves. The letter, and the truth that comes with it. Read it aloud, if you like. Then tell me what I am, and what you mean to do, and I will abide by it. That is not cowardice this time. That is trust, arriving four years late and poorer for the journey, but arriving."

Paloma stared at the letter. Her hands, when they finally rose to take it, were not steady after all — and Odile found, strangely, that this comforted her more than steadiness ever had.

* * *

### Paloma

The paper was thin, the ink faded, the handwriting cramped — Greta had clearly written it in bad light with a failing pen and a failing body, and had refused to stop.

Paloma read aloud, because Odile had offered and because her voice, she discovered, shook less than her hands.

*"My Odile. By the time this reaches you I will have planted my half of our seventh tree in the university garden, where they cannot confiscate a living thing without embarrassment, though God knows they try. Keep yours. When the war ends — and it will end, everything ends, even madness — bring our girl to see it. You know the one I mean. The one who reads first."*

Paloma stopped.

The lamp hissed. Outside, wind moved through the bare orchard, and six old trees and one young one creaked their separate creaks.

"'Our girl,'" she said. Her voice had gone strange. "She knows. She's known. About me reading. She—" Something enormous and simple was rearranging itself in her chest, and she had no furniture for it. "Five years. Five years I thought I was a thief with good posture, and she *addressed* me. She left me the letter. Not you. Me."

"She always liked you," Odile said softly. "From your first week. She said you had the look of someone history would eventually catch up with, and she hoped, when it did, you'd be standing somewhere with good drainage."

A laugh tore out of Paloma — wet, undignified, real. "That is the most Greta thing I have ever heard, and I have never met her."

"You will," Odile said. "If the world holds together long enough. She'll come up the drive complaining about English weather and the state of our topsoil, and you'll pretend you haven't read four years of her correspondence, and she'll pretend not to notice you pretending, and it will be unbearable and perfect."

Paloma looked down at the letter. At the last lines, which she read silently:

*"Tell her she was never outside it. None of us were. History is not a storm that passes over the orchard. It is the orchard."*

She folded the paper along Greta's creases. When she spoke, she found she had stopped deciding anything at all — the decision had simply been made somewhere in her, quietly, the way trees decide to bud.

"I'm keeping it," she said. "Until 1919, if it takes 1919. Until she stands in this kitchen and takes it from my hand. And Odile—" she looked up, and for the first time in four years she called her employer by her Christian name, and neither of them flinched, "—if the constable comes again, I'll lie for you. Gladly. But you'll never lie *to* me again. Not for my protection, not for anyone's. We carry things together or we drop them. Those are my terms."

Odile Marchand studied her for a long moment — this girl she had hired for her driving and kept for her soul — and then nodded, once, like a treaty being signed.

"Together, then," she said. "Now put that letter somewhere safe and come away from the window. There's a frost coming, and I want to check the seventh tree before bed."

They went out together into the dark and the cold, two women and a secret, toward the orchard —

— and stopped at the gate, because lamplight was moving between the trees. Someone was already there.
