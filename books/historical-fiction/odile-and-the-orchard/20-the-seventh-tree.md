## 20. The Seventh Tree

The morning after Paloma demanded everything of me — and I gave it to her, all of it, in the orchard with our breath hanging white between us like something we'd both agreed not to touch — I woke at four o'clock certain that I had lost her.

This is what grief does to a person my age: it makes arithmetic of love. I lay in the dark counting what remained. Greta, three hundred miles away behind a border that had become a wound in the world. My work, scattered or burned or confiscated according to the mood of whichever constable had last read my file. And now perhaps Paloma too, gone down the hill to the police station with my confession still warm in her ears.

I had told her everything. The pacifist pamphlets translated into French and passed through the suffrage network disguised as minutes from committee meetings. The letters sewn into coat linings. The years of it, quiet as planting, treasonous as anything ever was. I had watched her face while I spoke and seen nothing I could read, which frightened me more than anger would have. Anger has a shape. You can brace against anger. But Paloma had simply listened the way she used to listen to the letters I dictated — completely, hungrily, taking in every word — and then she had said, "I need to think," and walked out of the kitchen, and I had heard the front door close with the gentleness of someone who knows the house is listening.

At seven there came a knock. Not the knock of authority; I have learned their rhythms by now, the official triple-tap and the polite pause. This was two quick raps and then a third, apologetic, the way one knocks on the door of a friend who may be asleep or may be weeping or may be doing neither but wishes to be left alone regardless.

I opened the door and Paloma stood there holding a spade.

She was wearing her driving coat over her nightdress, which she had done exactly once before, the day the depot clerk mentioned the morning tide, and I understood immediately that this was a woman dressed for urgency rather than decency. Her hair was pinned badly. There was frost in it.

"Get your boots on," she said.

"Paloma—"

"You didn't report me either," she said. "For the depot. For the forged papers. We're even, and I've thought about it all night, and here's what I've decided: we are going to plant that tree. Today. Before the ground gets any harder, before anybody decides to come up that drive, before either of us can talk ourselves out of it." She looked past me into the hall, at my dressing gown, my bare ankles, my expression — whatever it was. "You promised Greta. I carried her letter five hundred miles to keep it for you. I am not letting a frozen orchard be the thing that breaks an oath."

I should say something here about how I argued. I did argue, briefly, that the ground was iron and the saplings were dead sticks and the whole enterprise was sentiment dressed as action. I said it in the voice I use for committee meetings, the reasonable one, the one that has talked three counties' worth of women out of despair and into canvassing.

Paloma let me finish and then said, "Mrs. Marchand. Boots."

I got my boots.

* * *

The orchard at Wren Hollow in January is not the place it becomes in May, when the blossoms make the air pink and the bees go drunk among the branches. In January it is six bare rows and a silence you could pour into bottles. Six rows, because Greta and I had planted six as students — the crab apple, the two pears, the three eating apples — and the seventh position at the end of the row had stood empty for thirty-one years, waiting on a promise we made when we were twenty-two and immortal.

We had sworn it under the half-planted row, Greta and I, in the spring of 1910, with dirt under our fingernails and cider in a tin cup passing between us. *When the seventh tree goes in,* she had said, *it will mean the war never came.* That was the terms of it. That was the joke, if it was a joke — that we would plant the seventh only when the world had proven itself gentle enough to deserve an orchard's full number. She had been laughing when she said it. I had been laughing too. Neither of us had believed, even dimly, that we were writing the terms of a contract that history intended to test.

Now the war had come, and was coming still, would go on coming like weather, and Greta was ill in a country I could not enter, and the seventh tree was going into frozen ground anyway, because it turned out that promises do not wait for the world to deserve them. They wait for people to keep them.

Paloma had brought the sapling herself. I don't know where she got it — I suspect the nurseryman in the village, whose son she once taught to drive, and I suspect further that no money changed hands and that some debts are now owed quietly in the village that neither of us will speak aloud. It was a quince, which startled me until it didn't.

"A quince?" I said, kneeling at the empty spot at the end of the row.

"Greta's letter said plant the seventh tree," Paloma said. "It didn't say which. So I picked the one that takes the longest to fruit." She set the little whip of a tree against her shoulder, considering it. "Five years, the man said. Maybe seven. By then—" She stopped. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and I loved her for it, and I hated the war for making a young woman learn to leave sentences unfinished.

"By then," I said, finishing it for her, "there will be peace to ripen it in."

The ground fought us. That is the honest truth of it and I see no reason to prettify: the earth at Wren Hollow in late January is a thing with opinions, and its opinion was that nothing should be planted. Our first stroke of the spade rang like a bell and bounced. The second bit an inch deep and stopped. We took turns. Paloma drove the blade with her whole weight, jumping on it, swearing in Spanish under her breath — she thinks I don't know any Spanish, and I know enough to know she was saying things about the ancestry of the soil. I hacked at the edges. Frost flew up like sparks.

Somewhere in the second hour, sweating through wool in freezing air, I began to laugh.

"What?" Paloma demanded, red-faced, spade raised.

"Nothing. Everything. Thirty-one years ago Greta and I dug this row in an afternoon because we were young and stupid and the ground was soft. Now two old women can't get six inches down in a morning." I wiped my eyes, which were watering from the cold, and only partly from the cold. "There's a lesson in it. I refuse to learn it."

"The lesson," Paloma said, driving the spade down again with a grunt, "is that everything worth doing hurts, and everyone tells you it won't, and then it does, and you do it anyway." She glanced at me sideways. "That's the suffrage movement. That's also gardening. Possibly they're the same thing."

"They are absolutely the same thing," I said. "We simply haven't been calling it that."

We struck softer earth below the frost line around noon and the hole went quickly after that, dark and breathing, smelling of cold clay and the deep unbothered life of the soil. Paloma knelt and set the roots in with a tenderness that undid me — this girl who had opened my mail out of loneliness and lied to a constable for me and driven through blackout villages on borrowed courage, kneeling in the mud, tucking roots in like a blanket, murmuring something to the tree that I pretended not to hear and will repeat to no one.

I held the trunk straight while she filled. Or rather, I held the trunk straight while pretending to fill, because my hands had begun to shake and I needed both of us to believe I was useful.

Because here is what I had not said to anyone, not to Paloma, not in the confession she wrung out of me, not even to myself in the privacy of sleepless hours: the letter Greta wrote — the one that came postmarked before the war, the one asking me to plant the seventh tree — had also said *I am tired, Odile, and I am afraid, and if I do not see the orchard again, put my name in the ground where the light is good.* And I had burned nothing and kept everything, because burning felt like killing her twice, and now I knelt in January mud holding a tree upright and understanding that this was the funeral. This was it. No body, no grave, no service — just a quince whip and frozen ground and two women keeping faith with a promise that had been, all along, a way of saying *live* to each other across thirty years and one impossible sea.

"Odile." Paloma's voice, very quiet. "You're crying on the tree."

"I'm watering it," I said. "It's called method."

"It's called mourning," she said gently, "and it's allowed."

And that — that single sentence, offered without pity, without ceremony, the way you'd hand someone a tool — cracked something open in me that thirty years of composure had cemented shut. I cried the way I imagine storms arrive at sea: all at once, without negotiation. I cried for Greta in her boarding house in a country gone mad, for the students we had been, for the pamphlets and the letters and the long careful years of secrecy, for every woman I had ever known who loved someone the government had declared her enemy. Paloma knelt beside me in the mud and put her arm around my shoulders and let me do it, and did not once tell me to be brave, which is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the tree was in. We tamped the earth, mulched it with the rotted leaves Paloma had raked in November and hoarded like a dragon, and staked the little trunk with a post and a strip of sacking so the wind couldn't worry it loose in its first year. Then we stood back, two mud-caked women in the failing light, regarding a stick in a hole.

"It looks terrible," Paloma said.

"It looks like 1910," I said. "That's how they all looked. Greta cried laughing at them. Said we'd planted a regiment of pencils."

Paloma laughed — really laughed, head back — and then sobered, and took off one glove, and pressed her bare palm flat against the little trunk the way one greets a horse. "There," she told it. "Grow. That's an order. I've carried enough for this family."

Then she looked at me, and her face did the thing it does when she has decided to say a true thing and is gathering speed toward it.

"I'm not reporting you," she said. "I want you to hear it plainly, not guessed at. Whatever comes. Whatever they ask me. I read your mail for four years because I thought I was being diligent, and then I read it because I couldn't stop, and then I understood I was reading the truest thing in England — two women loving each other carefully through a code of orchards and seasons because the world wouldn't permit plainer words. I'm not handing that to a constable. I'll lie again tomorrow if I have to. I've discovered I'm good at it, which frightens my mother and delights me."

"Paloma—"

"And you're not dismissing me," she went on, steamrolling my objection with the momentum of a woman who had rehearsed this on the walk up the drive. "Whatever happens next — inquiries, watchmen, worse — I stay. I drive. I read the mail first, openly, and you can dictate your replies in front of me and we'll stop pretending either of us believes the pretense." She stuck out her muddy hand. "Terms?"

I looked at that hand — chapped, filthy, steady — and I thought of a tin cup of cider passed between two laughing girls in 1910, and of a letter carried through checkpoints in a lining sewn by desperate clever fingers, and I understood that the orchard had never really been about trees. It was about who plants with you.

"Terms," I said, and shook it.

We walked back to the house in the blue dusk, arm in arm, leaving two sets of footprints stitched down the frozen row. At the gate Paloma paused and looked back at the seventh tree standing small and stubborn against the last light.

"Five years," she said. "Maybe seven. Quinces take their time."

"Yes," I said. "And when it fruits, we'll make the jelly Greta always ruined. Burnt, every year, and she'd serve it anyway and call it caramelized, and I'd eat it and swear it was the finest in Europe."

Paloma smiled. Then her smile shifted into something else — that alert look I had learned to dread and now, I find, rely upon.

"There's a ship list posted at the stationer's," she said slowly. "Repatriations. Exchanges. The war's grinding toward whatever it's grinding toward, and names move sometimes. I check it every week." She met my eyes. "Odile — last week there was a Lindqvist on it."

The word landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well — I waited for it to strike bottom and it never did.

"Stranded at a port in Denmark," Paloma said. "Waiting passage home. Waiting, Odile. Alive, and waiting, and the list says the exchange ships begin in the spring."

The frost was coming down again, silvering the fence posts, and I stood in the cold with my heart hammering like the spade against frozen ground and understood that the seventh tree was only the beginning of what we would have to keep alive.

"Well then," I said, taking her arm. "We had better write to Denmark."
