## 21. The Years Between

The motorcar had been in the shed so long that Paloma half expected to find a bird's nest in the carburetor and a family of mice holding the lease to the back seat. She found instead that five years of neglect had done less damage than one afternoon of her own stubbornness — she'd spent three days of March coaxing the engine awake, and on the fourth it had coughed itself into life like an old man persuaded against his better judgment to dance.

She drove it now along the lane to Wren Hollow with the windows open, because it was April of 1919 and the air smelled of turned earth and blossom about to commit itself, and because for five years she had driven with the windows shut against blackout curtains and gas warnings and the particular gray smell of a country holding its breath, and she was finished with all of that.

Finished, she told herself, though her hands did not entirely believe her. They still went to her coat pocket at every stop, the way they had gone there through four winters, checking. Checking for the letter.

It wasn't in her pocket. It hadn't been in her pocket since November, when she'd moved it from the tin in the kitchen cupboard — too obvious, Odile said, everyone hides things in tins — to a hollowed space beneath the floorboard under the orchard bench, wrapped in oilcloth, then in wax, then in one of Greta's own botanical drawings for reasons Paloma couldn't entirely explain even to herself. Some things wanted to travel in familiar company.

The letter had waited five years. The telegram had come Tuesday: *GRETA LINDQVIST ARRIVING SOUTHAMPTON FRIDAY STOP TRAVELING TO YOU AFTERWARD STOP SHE KNOWS THE ADDRESS STOP* — as if anyone at Wren Hollow could have forgotten the address, or the woman, or the promise folded up under a bench like a seed nobody had dared plant.

Odile stood at the gate when Paloma drew up, and Paloma saw at once that the war had taken its toll of her employer the way frost takes a wall — not toppling it, but leaving white seams down the mortar. Her hair had gone fully silver. She stood straighter than she had in 1917, though, straighter than she had any right to, and she wore the green scarf, the one Greta had sent from Heidelberg in 1911 with a note that read *for cold orchards.*

"She'll want to see the trees first," Odile said, by way of greeting. "Or she'll pretend to. She'll say something scientific about bud break, and what she'll mean is *I thought I would never stand here again.*"

"Will you let her get away with pretending?"

"I have let her get away with pretending for twenty-two years," Odile said. "I don't propose to change the arrangement now. It's the foundation of the whole structure."

Paloma laughed, and the laugh surprised her — how easily it came now, how the muscles of it had survived disuse. There had been years when she'd wondered whether she'd ever laugh without listening afterward for a knock at the door.

They walked out into the orchard together, because there was nothing else to do with the waiting except walk among the things the waiting was *for*. Six trees in their row, older now, heavier, the apples of them gone from promising to dependable. And at the end of the row, the seventh — planted in frozen ground in December of 1914, by two women with numb fingers and no words left between them, a sapling that should by all reason have died and had instead decided, with the quiet obstinacy of everything Greta Lindqvist had ever touched, to live.

"It's taller than me," Odile observed.

"Everything is taller than you."

"Cheek." But Odile laid her hand flat against the trunk anyway, the way Paloma had seen her do a hundred times and never once commented on, and left it there a long moment, reading bark the way other people read letters.

Which reminded Paloma.

"The floorboard," she said. "Do you want to be the one to—"

"No." Odile said it quickly, and then more gently. "No. You kept it. You carry it. That was never my part of this, and we both know it."

*We both know it.* Five years ago those words from Odile Marchand would have been a door closing. Now they were simply a fact being handed over, the way you hand someone a spade because your own hands are full.

* * *

The motorcar came up the lane at half past three on Saturday — not a taxi but a hired Daimler, and Paloma understood before it stopped that Greta Lindqvist had spent her first English pounds on dignity, because arriving at Wren Hollow after five years and a war in anything less than a proper car would have meant admitting the journey had cost her something.

The chauffeur opened the door. A cane emerged first — a dark wooden thing, new-looking, with a worn grip that contradicted its newness — and then a boot, and then Dr. Greta Lindqvist rose out of the car like a specimen unfolding from a press, tall and thin and gray at the temples, wearing a coat that had clearly crossed borders, oceans, and at least two governments to be here.

For a moment nobody spoke. Paloma, standing behind Odile at the gate, watched her employer's face do something she had never seen it do: absolutely nothing. Every expression arrived at once and cancelled the others out, leaving only a kind of stunned clarity, like sky after lightning.

Then Greta said, in English grown rustier than her letters had ever suggested, "Your orchard is a disgrace. The third tree has canker. I could see it from the road."

And Odile Marchand, suffragist, keeper of treasonous correspondence, seventy-one years old, began to cry — quietly, furiously, the way she did everything else — and crossed the gravel, and the two women held onto each other in the middle of the drive while the hired chauffeur studied the horizon with the professional blankness of his trade, and Paloma stood at the gate with her heart going like a hammer and her hands very still in her pockets, watching history happen at close range and finally, finally understanding that it always had been.

"You're thin," Odile said into Greta's shoulder.

"I'm sixty-eight. Thin is the natural state."

"You're *thin*, Greta."

"And you're old," Greta said, pulling back to look at her, both hands still gripping Odile's arms as if letting go might undo the arrival. "You've gone silver."

"You've gone gray. We match. We always did, eventually."

Greta's eyes came up then, over Odile's shoulder, and found Paloma, and Paloma felt the whole weight of five years shift forward in her chest.

"So," Greta said. "You are the driver who reads."

Paloma had rehearsed a dozen openings for this moment in the small hours of five years — apologies, explanations, the whole architecture of what she'd done with Greta's letters in 1914, opened and read out of habit and loneliness. She found that none of the rehearsals survived contact. What came out instead was simply true:

"I was. I am. I—" She stopped. Started again. "I read them because I was lonely, and then I kept reading because I couldn't stop, and then I kept the last one because—"

"Because I asked you to," Greta said.

Paloma blinked. "You knew?"

"My dear girl, I addressed that letter to Odile, sealed it myself, and posted it from Hamburg eleven days before the border closed. If it reached this house unopened, I should have been deeply insulted. Who else would have carried it?" Greta's mouth did something complicated — humor and grief sharing the same real estate. "I wrote asking her to keep a promise. I did not write asking her to keep it *alone*. Someone had to be the second pair of hands. In every plan I ever made, there was a second pair of hands." Her gaze flicked to Odile. "Usually hers."

"I'm standing right here," Odile said.

"You are standing right here," Greta agreed, "which is such an improbable sentence that I intend to say it several more times today."

* * *

They went to the orchard first, exactly as predicted. Greta walked the row with her cane ticking against the earth, touching each tree in turn, murmuring Latin names like old friends' surnames, and pronounced judgment on the canker (*"prune it hard, burn the cuttings, and stop watering at the crown, Odile, you drown things you love, you always have"*), and when she reached the seventh tree she stopped altogether.

The afternoon light came slantwise through the row and lit the young trunk gold. Greta put her palm against it the way Odile had, and Paloma saw that this too was a language, and that she had been eavesdropping on it for years without knowing.

"It lived," Greta said. Simply. As if reporting data.

"We planted it in December," Odile said. "Frozen ground. I told Paloma it was madness."

"You told me it was *debt*," Paloma said.

Both women turned to look at her, and Paloma realized she had spoken aloud, and decided, with the strange new courage that 1919 seemed to issue to people whether they'd ordered it or not, to keep going.

"That chapter — the one where you two made your oath, in 1910, under the half-planted row. Odile told me about it once, in the worst winter. She said planting the seventh tree was a debt she owed. She didn't say to whom, or for what. She just stood there in the cold with the sapling and the spade, and she said, *some promises aren't yours alone to keep, Miss Vasquez,* and handed me the other end of the tree." Paloma looked at the ground, then made herself look up. "I've been carrying one end of something ever since. I'd like to finally know what it is."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of a room deciding whether to open a window.

"Greta," Odile said quietly. "It's time. It's been time for five years. Give it to her properly."

Greta looked at Paloma for a long moment — and Paloma understood, suddenly, that she was being weighed, and had been weighed for years, sight unseen, across a war and an ocean and a hundred letters she'd never known were testimony.

"Under the bench," Greta said. "Third board from the left. She always hid things badly; the Germans taught me to search floorboards professionally."

"It's not badly hidden," Odile protested.

"It took me forty seconds to find in 1913, and I was *drunk*."

* * *

The oilcloth had kept the damp out. The wax had kept the oilcloth honest. And the botanical drawing — Greta's own hand, a study of apple blossom from 1908, annotated in pencil in two languages — had kept the whole parcel company, so that when Paloma knelt at the bench and lifted the boards and brought the bundle out into the daylight, the first thing either of them saw was Greta's handwriting looking up at them from another century.

Paloma unwrapped it kneeling, because it felt wrong to stand over it. The letter inside was stiff with age, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, the seal broken — broken long ago, in November of 1914, by her own thumb, in the kitchen of this house, by lamplight, with her heart going like a drum.

She had read it twice that night. She had read it perhaps fifty times since. She knew every line the way she knew the lane to the coast. And yet her hands shook now, handing it up to Greta, because knowing a thing and delivering it were different animals entirely.

"This is yours," Paloma said. "I was only supposed to keep it alive until you came."

Greta took it, and didn't open it. She turned it over once, looking at the postmark, the Hamburg stamp, the date that belonged to a world with no trenches in it. Then she looked at Paloma again, and there was something in her face that made Paloma think of the seventh tree — of things planted in terrible conditions that live anyway.

"You read everything," Greta said. "Five years of letters. Four years before that. And then you carried this one through a war, past checkpoints, past a sergeant at Marlow Bridge who I hear required considerable lying-to."

"He was very suspicious," Paloma said. "A soldier vouched for me. I never learned his name. I've thought about him maybe nine thousand times."

"Mm. And at the depot — Odile tells me you broke into a sealed warehouse, stole a letter from confiscated enemy property, lied to a gate guard with what Odile describes as 'magnificence,' and missed the ship besides."

"I missed the ship," Paloma agreed. "That part I did fail at."

"You did not fail," Greta said, and her voice changed — dropped its dryness, set down its luggage. "Child. Listen to me. Everything in that crate was research. Papers, specimens, thirty years of my work — replaceable, eventually, after the peace, and mostly replaced, and none of it worth a single night of your liberty. Except this." She held up the letter. "This was worth everything. And you got it out, and you kept it safe through five years of searches and shortages and men with lists, and you brought it here today, to this bench, to these two ridiculous old women, intact. Do you understand what I am telling you? You did not miss the ship. You *were* the ship."

Paloma had prepared, over five years, a great many responses to this moment — defenses, confessions, the speech about how she'd opened letters that weren't hers, about the code she'd cracked, about Odile's pacifist writings passed through the suffrage network, about everything she knew and everything she'd chosen not to tell. She had imagined being forgiven and she had imagined being condemned and she had built elaborate internal courthouses for both.

What she had never imagined was that there would be nothing to argue. That Greta would simply take the whole weight of it — the reading, the keeping, the lying, the missing — and call it delivery.

"I opened your letters," Paloma said, because honesty deserved its day even on a day of grace. "From the beginning. Before the war. I told myself it was diligence."

"I know," Greta said.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because in 1912 you returned a letter to Odile with a remark about the *Prunus* spelling that could only have come from a careful reader, and Odile wrote to me about it, delighted, and said, *the girl reads us, and I cannot decide whether to dismiss her or adopt her.*" Greta glanced at Odile. "You never dismissed her."

"I was waiting," Odile said, "to see what she'd do with what she read."

Paloma sat down on the bench, abruptly, because her legs had filed a formal complaint. The letter lay in Greta's hands, and the orchard breathed around them, and somewhere down the lane a cart horse clopped along with the sublime indifference of horses to human history.

"What happens now?" Paloma asked. "To the letter, I mean."

Greta considered it. Then she held it out — not to Odile, but to Paloma.

"Now? Now you decide. You've earned the deciding. Burn it, keep it, bury it under the bench for another fifty years — it's yours. That is the trouble with trust, Miss Vasquez. Once given, it doesn't come back. You simply have to live as if you deserve it, and eventually the living makes it true."

Paloma took the letter. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed five years.

"I'm going to keep it," she said. "Not hidden. I'm done hiding things. I'll keep it where I can see it — in the cottage, on the shelf, with the drawing. Because—" and here the truth arrived, the one she'd been walking toward since a census taker asked one question too many, since a constable asked about enemy correspondence, since a sergeant at a bridge looked at a frightened girl with forged papers and chose kindness — "because I spent years believing history happened to other people, somewhere else. And then it happened in a kitchen, and a warehouse, and a lane at dawn, and it was happening *because of me*, and I was holding it in my hands the entire time." She looked up. "I want to keep it where remembering isn't shameful. Where it's just... true."

Neither woman spoke. Then Greta sat down heavily beside her on the bench, cane across her knees, and said, "Good. Because there's rather a lot more where that came from."

Paloma turned. "What do you mean?"

Greta's eyes had gone bright with something that was not entirely grief and not entirely mischief, which Paloma would learn, in the years coming, was simply how Greta Lindqvist's face worked.

"I mean, child, that I did not survive Hamburg, a camp, a border, and an Atlantic crossing merely to water apples. There are others. Letters, names, people who kept things for people who kept things for people — a whole chain of us, scattered from Kiel to Cornwall, most of whom believe the rest are dead. I have a list." She patted her coat pocket with grim satisfaction. "And a trunk of papers I did not declare at customs, and a lifetime of work that somebody will have to help me carry, because my hands are sixty-eight years old and there are only two of them."

"Greta," Odile said warningly, in the tone of a woman recognizing weather.

"And you," Greta continued serenely, "have an orchard, a cottage, a motorcar that I heard misfiring from a quarter mile away, and a driver who reads, lies magnificently, and keeps promises past all reasonable hope. Odile. My dear. We are going to need her."

The sun was going down over the sixth tree. The seventh caught the last of it and held the light the way it always had, stubbornly, against all instruction. And Paloma Vasquez sat on the bench between two old women who had once sworn an oath under a half-planted row, holding a five-year-old letter in her lap, and felt the future open in front of her like a road at dawn — long, unmapped, and entirely, unmistakably, hers.

"Well," she said. "Someone had better teach me German."
