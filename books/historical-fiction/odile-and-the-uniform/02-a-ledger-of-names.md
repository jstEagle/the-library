## 2. A Ledger of Names

The morning letters smelled of coal smoke and cheap paper, and one of them—held to the light, as I always held them—carried a faint sweetness beneath the ink. Lavender water. The woman had dabbed it along the envelope's fold, perhaps so that whoever opened her words would think of her as more than a name on a grievance.

I sat at my desk in the back room of the workshop with the letter flat before me and read it aloud to no one, which was how I began every reply.

*Dear Madam Marchetti,* it said. *I was dismissed from my position at Hessler & Sons on Tuesday last. My foreman asked me plainly whether I had signed the petition for the vote, and when I did not deny it he told me the firm could not employ a woman who made trouble for herself above her station. I have three children. My husband's lungs are bad. I do not regret the signature. I only wish to know if there is anything to be done.*

*Anything to be done.* Four words, and they were the whole of the century asking itself a question.

I took up my pen and began to dictate—not to write, never to write first; writing came second, speaking came first, because speech was honest in a way ink allowed itself to become only afterward—and I spoke the answer into the room as though the room were the woman herself.

"Dear Mrs. Brandt—" I stopped. A coincidence of names, nothing more. "Dear Mrs. Aldermann. Your signature is not regretted by anyone who matters. Regret belongs to those who fired you, though they are too cowardly to feel it. Enclosed you will find five marks from the hardship fund, which is not charity but common property—you helped fill it, or women like you did. And enclosed also is this: your name has been entered in our ledger. It will not be removed. Whatever else they take from you, they cannot take the fact that you signed."

I heard him before I saw him. Ivo Quintana stood in the doorway with a stack of sorted post against his chest, and he had been standing there long enough to hear everything. Most men would have coughed, or retreated, or invented an errand. He simply stood, dark-eyed and still, holding other people's mail like a man holding evidence.

"You may come in," I said, without looking up. "You may even listen. But if you repeat what you hear outside these walls, I shall know it was you, because there is no one else who hears it."

"I wouldn't," he said. And then, because he was Ivo, and honesty with him was less a virtue than a condition: "I already know some of them. The names. From the envelopes. I sort the incoming post."

I set down my pen and looked at him properly. Twenty-six years old, hands scarred from a boyhood spent around engines, a face that gave away nothing except when it gave away everything. He had driven me eleven months without once being late and without once asking why a suffragist needed a driver at all when she could afford a motorcar and the questions it invited.

"And?" I said.

"And I remember them," he said. "I don't mean to. They stay."

There it was. The thing I had suspected for months and never confirmed, confirmed now in nine words spoken with the flatness of a man confessing to a minor theft. *They stay.*

"Good," I said. "Then you may be useful. Bring the post. Sit where you like. If a name comes up that you recognize, say so—we keep two lists, you see. The living and the silenced. A woman who writes to us after losing her work goes on the first list. A woman who stops writing goes on the second, and the second list gets visited."

He crossed the room and sat on the packing crate by the window, the post balanced on his knees, and he did not pretend not to listen, which I decided was the most respectable thing about him.

* * *

The master ledger lived in the bottom drawer of my desk, wrapped in oilcloth, bound in plain grey board with no title on its spine. Anyone finding it would have taken it for an inventory of dry goods. In a sense, they would have been right. It was an inventory of everything this country claimed it did not possess: four hundred and six women, each one entered by hand in my own writing, each one a signature gathered door to door, market to market, laundry line to laundry line, demanding what no government had yet deigned to hear—that women be counted as citizens.

Four hundred and six. I knew the number the way a mother knows the number of her children, and I knew, beside many of the names, the small annotations that were the true history of the movement in this province—the history that would never appear in any newspaper.

*Dismissed — Hessler & Sons, textiles.*
*Landlord served notice, week of St. Martin's Day.*
*Husband forbids further correspondence (her own hand added: he is afraid, not cruel).*
*No reply since June. Visit arranged.*

That last one belonged to Anna Weissl, a widow who kept bees outside town, and I had been meaning to visit her for three weeks and had been meaning to visit her for three weeks because visiting meant admitting I might find an empty house or a closed gate or worse, a face at a window that would not open to me.

Ivo watched me turn the pages. I felt his attention the way one feels sun through glass—warm, patient, slightly dangerous.

"How many," he said. Not *how many are there*. Just *how many*, as though the number were a weight he wanted to hold.

"Four hundred and six."

"And how many lost work?"

"Thirty-one that we know of. Two lost their lodgings. One—" I paused at Anna Weissl's entry, then moved past it. "One we've lost entirely."

"The bees?"

I looked up sharply. "You read the annotations."

"I read everything," he said. "It's a fault. I've stopped pretending it isn't."

Most people, discovering their driver had been memorizing the contents of private correspondence, would have been furious. I found I was something else entirely. I was relieved. There is a particular loneliness in keeping a book of martyrs, and I had kept mine for four years, and in all that time exactly two people had known its full contents, and one of them had married a magistrate and the other had died of influenza. To have a third person know—to have that person be a man who drove a motorcar and remembered names unbidden and confessed his faults without being pressed—was not a breach of security. It was an answer to a prayer I hadn't known I'd filed.

"Then listen," I said, and I turned the ledger toward him, which no one living had seen me do. "This is not a petition. Petitions die in drawers. This is a census of the future. Every name in here is a woman who looked at the world as it is and declined to agree to it. When the vote comes—and it will come, Ivo, not because men grow generous but because we make silence impossible—this book will be the proof that it was asked for. Asked for in writing. By name. While it cost them their bread."

He reached out, hesitated, and touched the edge of a page as though the paper might object. His finger stopped at a name halfway down. "I know this one," he said quietly. "Frau Kessel. She sends a letter every month. Same blue envelope. She asks if anyone else from the mill has written." He looked up. "Has anyone else from the mill written?"

"No," I said. "Not since February."

"So the others were frightened off, and she keeps writing anyway, just to ask."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and I saw something settle in his face—not resolve, resolve comes later, but recognition. The look of a man who has been carrying stones for years and suddenly notices the road they're building.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon. He read the incoming letters aloud while I dictated replies, and somewhere in the second hour the arrangement stopped being an arrangement and became simply how the room worked. He learned quickly which letters went to the hardship fund and which went to the lawyers, which women wanted comfort and which wanted ammunition, and twice he caught things I missed—a date misremembered, a street renamed since the last letter—and corrected me with the apologetic precision of a man correcting a superior officer.

The light went gold and then grey. Marta, the typesetter's daughter, brought tea at four and looked at the two of us bent over the desk with an expression I pretended not to interpret.

The last letter of the day was from a girl of nineteen named Elise Vogel, who wrote in a hand that shook between lines of iron certainty. She had signed the petition against her father's express command. She had been locked in her room for it. She was writing to say that she regretted nothing and wished to know if regretting nothing was foolish.

I dictated the answer with more care than I had given any letter that day.

"Tell her foolishness is a word men use for courage they didn't authorize. Tell her that four hundred and five strangers—"

"Four hundred and five," Ivo said.

"—four hundred and five strangers stood where she is standing and did not step back, and that her name makes four hundred and seven, and that the number is going to grow until it can no longer be called a number but must be called a country."

I signed it. Ivo sealed it. And when he rose to leave with the outgoing sack over his shoulder, he paused at the door and turned back, and I braced myself for a question about danger, about discretion, about whether he ought to know any of this at all.

Instead he said: "The blue envelope. Frau Kessel. When the next one comes, tell her—" He stopped, restarted. "Tell her someone besides you knows her name. That it didn't go into a drawer. That a man who drives a motorcar can recite it."

And he was gone before I could decide whether to scold him for reading ahead of himself or thank him for understanding the assignment before I gave it.

I sat alone with the ledger open under the lamp. Four hundred and six names in my handwriting, thirty-one ruined livelihoods annotated in the margins, one widow with bees unvisited. Outside, the town went about its evening, and somewhere east of us—though none of us knew it yet—men in uniforms were already drawing the maps that would decide which buildings mattered and which could burn.

I dipped my pen and added Elise Vogel's name, the ink still wet when I heard, far off, the sound of boots keeping time on cobblestones. Too regular for drunks. Too many for a patrol.

I closed the drawer softly, as one closes a door on a sleeping child.
