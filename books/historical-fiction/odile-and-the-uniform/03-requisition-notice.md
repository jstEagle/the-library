## 3. Requisition Notice

The notices went up on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the shops opened, when the town belonged to milk carts and the women who swept their doorsteps. Ivo saw the first one because he was early, because he was always early, because a driver who is not early is not a driver but a liability, and Odile Marchetti did not keep liabilities.

He had been wiping the windscreen of the Daimler with his sleeve when he noticed the soldiers at the corner of Hafenstrasse. Three of them, and a corporal with a paste bucket and a brush, working with the brisk indifference of men hanging a poster for a circus. The paper they unrolled was larger than a circus poster. It had a seal at the top, an eagle or something with wings, and rows of type beneath it that Ivo could not read from across the street.

He crossed over anyway. There was no traffic to speak of. A baker's boy watched from a doorway with his basket on his hip.

*By order of the District Military Command,* the notice began, and Ivo's eyes moved down the columns of official German, the language of tax assessments and train timetables, the language that never said anything directly and never meant anything kindly.

*Effective immediately, the eastern quarter of the municipality, bounded by the canal to the north, Hafenstrasse to the west, and the rail spur to the south, is hereby requisitioned for the construction of a munitions depot under the Defence Act of 1907. All residents and commercial occupants shall vacate the premises within thirty (30) days of the date of this notice. Failure to comply will be treated as obstruction of military operations.*

There was more. Compensation procedures, appeals to be lodged at a office that existed only in another town, a signature in a hand so confident it barely touched the page. But Ivo had stopped reading. He was doing geography instead, the quick mental work of a man who knew every street in the district the way other men knew the lines of their own palms.

The eastern quarter. Bounded by the canal, by Hafenstrasse, by the rail spur.

The print workshop stood on Binder Lane, three streets inside that boundary. Number eleven. The building with the crooked chimney and the cellar that smelled of ink and cold stone, where four hundred women's names lay in a ledger no bigger than a hymnal.

Ivo read the notice twice more, though there was nothing in it he had missed. Then he folded his hands behind his back and stood there long enough that the corporal glanced at him, and then he walked back to the car, got in, and sat with the engine off, watching the soldiers move down Hafenstrasse toward the next corner with their bucket and their brush and their eagle-sealed paper.

Thirty days. The words had a clean, administrative finality, like a train departing on schedule whether you were aboard or not.

He thought: *I should tell her now.* And then he thought: *No. She'll want to see it herself.*

That was the thing about Odile Marchetti that strangers never understood, the thing Ivo had learned in fourteen months of driving her and sorting her mail and memorizing what he was never meant to remember. She did not trust secondhand news. Not because she doubted people—she trusted people past all reason, trusted them into trouble half the time—but because she believed a person had a right to meet her own catastrophe face to face, unsoftened, unwarned, with her own eyes. Pity, she said once, was often just cowardice wearing a kind face.

So Ivo started the engine and drove to the house on Lindenweg, and did not say a word, and hated himself for the silence the whole way there.

* * *

She came down at seven-forty, exactly as she always did, in the gray wool dress with the high collar that made her look, she claimed, like a disapproving headmistress, which she considered a useful public costume for a suffragist. She carried her letters in a leather folder and her umbrella though the sky was clear. She paused at the car, looked at Ivo's face, and stopped.

"Something's happened," she said.

"There are notices up," Ivo said. "On Hafenstrasse. Military requisition. The eastern quarter."

For a moment she did not move. Then she set her folder on the seat beside her, deliberately, as if putting down something fragile, and said, "Show me."

He drove her back the way he'd come. The street corner was empty now except for the notice itself, pasted slightly askew, already beginning to curl at one edge in the damp air. Odile got out without waiting for him to open her door, which she never did, and stood in front of the paper reading it while the baker's boy and two maidservants gathered at a respectful distance, the way townspeople gather around anything sealed with an eagle.

Ivo stood beside her. He could have recited it by then. He found himself watching her instead—at the small motion of her lips as she read, the way her finger rose halfway to trace a line of text and then lowered again, denied, as if she refused even to touch the thing.

"The workshop," she said at last. It was not a question.

"Binder Lane is inside the boundary," Ivo said. "So is the whole lane. So are the Kellermanns' warehouse, the tannery, forty houses, the school—"

"I know what's inside the boundary, Ivo. I've lived here thirty years." Her voice was flat, which he had learned was worse than sharp. Sharpness meant fight still in her. Flatness meant the mind was working somewhere below the voice, in the dark, doing arithmetic. "A munitions depot." She said the words slowly, tasting them. "They're going to store shells where my presses are."

"They're going to demolish the presses," Ivo said. "Depots don't keep neighbors."

Odile turned from the notice. Up close, in the flat morning light, he could see the tiredness under her eyes that she hid from everyone else, and he understood suddenly that he was the only person in the town who ever saw it, and that this too was part of his job, though it appeared on no list of duties.

"Get me the colonel's office address," she said. "And then drive me to Binder Lane. If they're going to take my building, I intend to spend the day in it."

* * *

The workshop smelled the way it always did—ink, oil, the mineral coolness of the stone floor—but that morning the smell seemed to Ivo like something already embalmed. Odile walked through the front room where the pamphlets were stacked in bundles tied with string, ran her hand along the big platen press that took up most of the ground floor, and said nothing at all, which frightened him more than shouting would have.

Marta was there, of course. Marta was always there; she practically slept among the type cases. She had heard the news from the tanner's daughter and met them at the door with her chin up and her eyes too bright.

"They can't just—" she began.

"They can," Odile said. "That's what the Defence Act is for. Read the notice. It says *obstruction of military operations* as if it were weather, as if it were something that simply happens to people." She took off her gloves, finger by finger. "Marta, fetch the ledger."

Ivo felt the word go through him like a current. The ledger. He had known of its existence for months—he had listened through doorways to dictation, sorted replies addressed to women whose names he'd memorized against his own will—but he had never seen the object itself. Odile kept it the way other people kept jewelry, which is to say invisibly.

Marta brought it up from the cellar wrapped in oilcloth. A plain book, board covers, cloth spine gone soft at the corners. Four hundred names. Four hundred women who had put pen to paper demanding the vote, knowing some of them would lose their jobs for it, and several of them had. Ivo looked at it lying on the workbench between a tin of ink solvent and a cold cup of coffee, and thought: *that is the most dangerous object in this room, and these soldiers don't even know it exists.*

"We have thirty days," Odile said. She said it the way she might announce a delivery of paper stock. "The army believes that when this building comes down, everything in it comes down with it. The presses can be replaced. The type can be replaced. Marta can set type anywhere in the country, and I—" she almost smiled— "I can be disagreeable in any province they send me to."

"And the names?" Marta asked quietly.

"That," said Odile, "is the question, isn't it."

She looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then she closed it, wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and handed it—not to Marta—to Ivo.

"Take this to the house," she said. "Put it where you put things you're keeping safe. You'll know the place."

He stared at her. In fourteen months she had never once acknowledged, out loud, that he kept things safe, that there was such a category, that he had places. It was as if she had reached into his chest and rearranged something.

"Yes, madam," he said, because his voice would do nothing else.

* * *

That evening the town changed its sound. Ivo noticed it first as he drove home along the canal road: boots. Not the scattered boots of the garrison company that had always been stationed here, polite and mostly bored, but columns of them, measured, new arrivals bivouacked in the Kellermanns' warehouse with straw bedding visible through the doors. Wagons of surveying equipment. An officer on horseback pacing the boundary line with a map case, looking at houses and seeing acreage.

At the corner tavern, men argued in low voices. Old Weiss the cooper had been told his shop of thirty-one years was worth, according to the compensation schedule, less than the wood it was built from. Someone said you could appeal. Someone else laughed, and the laugh had no humor in it, and the conversation died.

Ivo parked the Daimler behind the house on Lindenweg and sat in the driver's seat a while, in the dark, holding the ledger's weight against his ribs where he'd carried it under his coat all afternoon. It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing about it. Four hundred lives, four hundred acts of courage that had cost some of those women their wages and their reputations, and it all fit against a man's chest like a book of psalms.

And in thirty days, a wrecking crew with government authority would stand where it had lain.

He thought about the soldiers posting notices. They hadn't been cruel. That was what unsettled him most. The corporal with the paste bucket had been whistling, faintly, some marching tune. Nobody in that little procession had known what they were destroying, because destruction on that scale doesn't require malice. It requires only paperwork and men who follow it.

*I am the courier of a dead archive,* he thought, and then corrected himself, because the thought was cowardly and he knew it. Not dead. Not yet. Archives died only when no one carried them.

He got out, locked the car, and let himself in through the kitchen. The housekeeper had left a lamp burning low in the hall. Odile's study door stood open, light spilling out, and he heard her voice—low, measured, dictating—and realized she was working, at nine at night, on responses to petitioners whose signatures might outlive the very building where their cause was printed.

He should knock. He should report: notices posted, troops arriving, thirty days confirmed. That was the efficient thing, and efficiency had been his armor for fourteen months—the quiet professional who read everything and admitted nothing, who served and observed and belonged to nothing and no one.

Instead he stood in the hallway with the ledger under his coat, listening to her voice steady as a metronome, and understood with sudden, sick clarity what the arithmetic actually was. Not thirty days until demolition. Thirty days until the army searched, inventoried, and hauled away everything in a building that had housed a printing press used for seditious pamphlets. Soldiers going shelf by shelf. Officers trained to recognize sedition when they saw it. A ledger of four hundred names of women demanding the vote, sitting in plain view on a workbench, wrapped in nothing but oilcloth.

It wasn't the building they would burn. Buildings were incidental. It was the record. The army didn't need to punish four hundred women. It only needed to make sure no one could ever prove they had existed.

Ivo's hands had gone cold around the oilcloth bundle. Down the hall, Odile's voice paused. He heard the scratch of her pen stop.

"Ivo?" she called. "Is that you standing in the dark? Come in. You've been hovering since Tuesday's post and you hover badly."

He went in. She was at her desk with her spectacles pushed up into her hair, and she looked at him—at his face, at the shape under his coat—with the particular attention of a woman who has spent her life reading documents, and Ivo opened his mouth to deliver his report like an efficient man, and heard himself say instead:

"They'll inventory the workshop before they demolish it. Everything gets catalogued. Every pamphlet, every scrap of paper. Your name will be on the seizure order next to the word *materials*."

Odile took off her spectacles slowly.

"Yes," she said. "I know."

"Do you?" His voice came out lower than he intended. "Because four hundred women signed that ledger believing their names would be read, not burned. Some of them lost their jobs for it. And in thirty days a corporal who was whistling this morning is going to stack it all in a wagon marked *salvage*, and none of it will ever—"

He stopped. He had never raised his voice to her. He had barely ever spoken above the level of driving instructions.

Odile regarded him for a long moment across the lamplit desk. Outside, far off toward the canal, a bugle sounded the evening call, and the sound of it seemed to underline everything: the town was already becoming theirs.

"You've read the letters," she said. It was not an accusation. It was stated with the mildness of someone confirming a timetable. "All of them. For how long?"

Ivo felt the floor tilt gently under his certainty. "From the beginning," he said. "I tell myself it's efficiency. Knowing the correspondence means anticipating the routes, the risks—"

"Ivo."

"—but it isn't," he finished. "It hasn't been for a long time."

She nodded once, slowly, as if a suspicion had finally been given its papers. Then she held out her hand, palm up, across the desk.

"Then bring me the ledger," she said, "and sit down. Because we have twenty-nine days, and I need to know if you can drive at night."
