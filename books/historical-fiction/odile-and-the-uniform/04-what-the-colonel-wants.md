## 4. What the Colonel Wants

The colonel arrived at half past ten in the morning, which told Odile everything she needed to know about him. Men who meant violence came early, before the town had gathered its courage, or late, under cover of dark. Men who came at half past ten wanted to be observed. They wanted the shopkeepers at their windows and the children on their doorsteps, wanted the whole street to witness the courtesy of the occupation. Courtesy was a weapon too, though a clumsy one, and she had spent forty years learning the weight of every weapon a man might carry through her door.

He came with an adjutant and no escort, which was itself a performance—the confident man needs no soldiers. He wore his gray uniform as if it had been tailored for him rather than issued, and he had removed his gloves on the steps, folding them over one arm like a man arriving at church.

"Signora Marchetti." He pronounced it correctly, all three syllables of her name given their due. "Colonel Aldous Reiner. I regret the circumstances of our introduction."

"Colonel." She did not invite him in. He came in anyway, the way water comes into a cellar—not through malice, but because the level outside was higher than the level within.

The front room of the workshop smelled of ink and cold coffee. The presses stood silent under their canvas shrouds; she'd ordered them covered three days ago, when the requisition notices went up, not out of surrender but because a covered press looks like furniture and furniture gets left alone longer than machinery does. Every hour bought was an hour won. She had learned that arithmetic from her mother, who had kept a bakery open through two riots by calling her ovens *decorative*.

Reiner walked the room slowly, hands clasped behind his back, reading the spines of pamphlets in the glass-fronted case as though browsing a bookshop. He picked up nothing. That was discipline, and discipline in an officer was worse than cruelty, because cruelty could be predicted.

"You print pamphlets," he said.

"I print wedding invitations, funeral notices, and the parish newsletter," Odile said. "The pamphlets belong to my tenants."

"Your tenants." He turned. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome long enough to stop trying. "Signora, I will be direct with you, because I am told you appreciate directness."

*You are told correctly,* she thought. *And you have been told other things besides.*

"The eastern quarter is now military territory. You will have seen the notices. Thirty days." He let the number sit between them. "Most residents are cooperating admirably. Inventory lists, keys surrendered, departure schedules arranged. The army is not barbaric. There is compensation—modest compensation—for premises vacated in good order."

"And for premises vacated in bad order?"

His smile was thin and genuine, which frightened her more than any threat would have. "For those, there is the depot commandant's discretion, and the commandant has very little discretion and less patience. But that need not concern you." He gestured at the shrouded presses with something almost like affection. "I am here about materials. Party materials. Agitational documents. Registries, correspondence, membership rolls—the apparatus of political printing. If such things were surrendered to me now, formally, with a receipt—" he produced, from his breast pocket, an actual receipt book, leather-bound, and laid it on the table between them like a host setting down bread "—then there would be nothing further to discuss. No inspections. No questions. You would be a widow of letters, Signora Marchetti, retired from the trade, free to go wherever widows of letters go."

"Where do they go?"

"Anywhere," he said pleasantly. "That is the gift. Anywhere at all."

She looked at the receipt book. It was a beautiful object, brass corners, cream paper, the kind of thing a stationer's daughter covets. It occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that this man had rehearsed this speech. In some mirror, in some billet, he had practiced laying the receipt book down at exactly that angle, pausing exactly that long.

"Colonel," she said, "may I ask you a question?"

"Of course."

"When your men posted the notices, did they post one on the chapel door?"

Something flickered across his face—the first honest thing she'd seen there. "The chapel is not in the seizure zone."

"No. But the notice would be read by everyone who attends, and half the eastern quarter attends. Did they post one there?"

"They did not."

"Because the priest objected, or because someone decided the chapel should stay clean of army paper?"

He replaced the smile carefully, the way a man replaces a fallen hat. "Signora, I admire precision. It is rare in civilians. But you are evading my offer."

"I haven't heard an offer yet," she said. "I've heard a purchase. And I don't sell what isn't mine to sell."

"The materials in this building—"

"—belong to four hundred women," Odile said, "who wrote to me in their own hands, with their own names, asking for things the law says they may not ask for but their consciences say they must. I am not the owner of those names, Colonel. I am their custodian. A custodian who sold her trust for safe passage would not be worth the passage." She folded her hands. "But I thank you for the receipt book. It is very fine."

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart passed, and a soldier's voice called something bored and procedural. Reiner studied her for a long moment, and she gave him back the study evenly, letting him look for the fear that wasn't there—or rather, letting him look past the fear that was, because she was afraid, she had been afraid since the first notice went up, and the trick was never to hide it entirely. Men like Reiner trusted what they saw. Show them a woman with no fear and they suspected a trap. Show them a woman with fear who stood anyway, and they concluded—wrongly, blessedly wrongly—that she simply didn't understand her situation.

"Signora," he said at last, gathering his gloves, "you are an old woman running a small print shop in a provincial town. Whatever you imagine yourself to be protecting, I assure you the army has copies of it already, or will within the week. Consider whether your pride is worth the trouble of disproving me."

"I consider it daily," Odile said. "It always comes out worth it."

He inclined his head—a courtesy so perfect it was nearly an insult—and paused at the door. "Thirty days," he said again, gently, like a doctor naming a date. "Good morning, Signora."

The adjutant held the door. The colonel's boots rang on the cobbles, unhurried, and the whole street watched him go, and Odile stood in her doorway until he turned the corner, smiling at the neighbors the way one smiles after a funeral—one has survived the service; the deceased is another matter.

Then she went inside and sat down among her shrouded presses and let her hands shake for exactly one minute. She timed it against the clock on the mantel. Sixty seconds of trembling, granted honestly, paid in full. Then she rose, washed her face in the basin, and went to find Ivo Quintana.

* * *

She found him where she knew she would find him, in the yard behind the workshop, checking the car's tires with a tread gauge he kept cleaner than most men kept their Bibles. He straightened when he saw her, and she watched him do what he always did: assess her face, file whatever he found there, and say nothing. Three years of driving her, and he had perfected the art of being present without appearing to watch—which was, of course, how she had known for a long time that he watched constantly.

"Colonel Reiner visited," she said. "He wants my party materials. He brought a receipt book."

Ivo's jaw tightened. "What did you tell him?"

"That I don't sell other women's names." She came closer, lowering her voice even though the yard was empty except for the two of them and the car. "He said the army has copies already, or will within the week. Ivo, I believe him. Not the copying—but the certainty. Someone has talked. Someone always talks eventually. Which means we are done pretending this building holds only pamphlets."

He set the tread gauge down on the fender with great care, as though the small metal object required his full attention. "How long have you known that I know?" he asked.

The question landed cleanly, without accusation, and she respected him for it. "About fourteen months," she said. "Since the Kesselring letter. You sorted the mail on Tuesdays. You read them standing up, near the window, and you put them back in the order they came, but you always hesitated over the ones with mourning borders. You hesitated because you understood them fastest. Grief is easy to read. Petitions are harder."

"I told myself it was efficiency," he said quietly.

"Yes. So did I, once, about a great many things." She touched his sleeve—briefly, deliberately, the first time she had initiated contact in three years of employment. "It was never efficiency, Ivo. It was the same thing it is in me. Come inside. There's something I want you to see, and I find I'm tired of showing it to no one."

* * *

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of the typesetting cabinet, beneath a false floor Marta's father had built years ago for storing contraband tobacco, back when the workshop's politics had been smaller. Odile knelt, lifted the panel, and drew it out: a thick volume bound in green cloth, its spine cracked from use, pages soft at the edges like cloth worn by washing.

She set it on the composing table, in the pool of lamplight, and opened it to the first page.

Four hundred names. Some in careful schoolroom script, some scrawled, some written by a hand that shook. Dates beside each one. Towns. Occupations—seamstress, laundress, widow, teacher, one *midwife*, three entries marked only with a cross where a signature should have been, women who could not write and had pressed their mark into the paper with a thumb dipped in ink.

"It began eight years ago," Odile said. Her voice sounded strange in the silent workshop, stripped of its public music. "A laundress named Sofia Brandt wrote to a newspaper complaining that she worked fourteen hours and couldn't vote on the hours of her own life. The newspaper printed her name. Her employer dismissed her the same week. So she wrote to me instead, because I had made noise in the papers myself, and she asked me a question I've been answering ever since: *if I sign, will anyone keep it?*"

She turned a page. Another. Names marched down the columns like a procession.

"Every woman who signs knows the cost. Fourteen lost their positions last year alone. One was put out by her own brother. And every time one of them loses something, three more write to ask if they may sign too. Because the ledger proves something the newspapers can't—that we are not five agitators in a provincial town. We are four hundred. We were four hundred before the army came, and we'll be four hundred after, unless this page burns."

Ivo hadn't moved. He stood with his hands at his sides, looking down at the ledger the way men look at altars—with hunger and dread in equal measure. Then he reached out, slowly, and turned the page himself. She let him.

"Sofia Brandt," he read aloud. His voice caught slightly on the second word, and Odile filed that away without understanding it yet. "Number thirty-one. Seamstress." He looked up. "If Reiner gets this—"

"He doesn't get it."

"If the building burns—"

"Then it burns elsewhere first." She closed the ledger and rested both hands flat upon the cover, feeling the weight of it, the four hundred small pressures that had made it heavy. "I showed you this tonight, Ivo, because tomorrow I mean to ask you for something enormous, and I wouldn't ask it of a man I hadn't shown the truth. But tonight I'm not asking anything. Tonight I'm only telling you what I've never told anyone: I am afraid. Properly afraid. And I am glad—I am ashamed of how glad—that I'm not the only one in this room anymore."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere beyond the walls, boots on cobbles, the measured tread of the new world walking its rounds.

Ivo put his hand on the ledger's cover, beside hers, and did not take it away.

"What time tomorrow?" he said.
