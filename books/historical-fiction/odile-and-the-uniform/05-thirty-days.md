## 5. Thirty Days

The notice stayed nailed to the telegraph pole for three days before anyone dared take it down, and when at last a boy climbed up with a hammer and pried it loose, he folded it into his pocket like something stolen from a church.

I read it four times in those three days. I told myself each reading was reconnaissance—knowing the enemy's paperwork was part of a driver's trade—but the truth was simpler and worse: I kept hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable. *By order of the Ministry of War, the eastern quarter is hereby requisitioned for the construction of a munitions depot. All residents and businesses to vacate within thirty days.* Thirty days. The army had a gift for arithmetic that left nothing over for mercy.

The eastern quarter meant the tannery row, the widow Havel's boarding house, the bakery with the crooked chimney. It meant forty families packing their lives onto carts. And it meant the workshop on Binder Lane—the two rooms above the cooper's yard where Odile Marchetti's presses stood, where the pamphlets were inked and folded by lamplight, where the mail came every Thursday in fat brown envelopes that I sorted and, yes, read.

I drove her home from the requisition office the afternoon she went to ask questions no one answered. She sat beside me in the Daimler with her gloved hands folded and her face turned to the window, watching soldiers chalk numbers on doors as though the houses were crates to be shipped. She did not cry. She did not rage. She said, very quietly, "They think we'll scatter like pigeons," and then she said nothing else for six kilometers, and the silence had a sound to it, like a fuse burning underground.

* * *

That evening she summoned me—not asked, summoned—to the workshop. I had never been there after dark. By day it was a print shop with a suffragist's back room; by night, with the shutters drawn and the lamps hooded, it became something else entirely, a ship's hold or a conspirator's cellar, and I understood that I had been driving past a fortress for two years without seeing its walls.

There were seven of us. I knew some by sight: Herr Vogel, who set type with fingers stained permanently black; his apprentice, a girl of sixteen named Marta whose father had died under one of these same presses the previous spring—the army's report had called it an industrial matter, three words that fit on a coin and weighed nothing; Frau Lindqvist, who ran the sewing circle that funded everything; two men whose names I learned and forgot in the same hour because they spoke little and watched much. And Odile, standing at the head of the worktable with the requisition notice spread flat beneath her palm, as if she could pin the army to the wood through sheer pressure of hand.

"Thirty days," she said. "In thirty days they take this building, and everything in it becomes army property. Paper burns easily. You all know what paper we keep here."

I knew better than any of them, though none of them knew that. In the iron cabinet behind the composing stone, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay the master ledger. Four hundred names. Four hundred women who had signed the petition demanding the vote—teachers, laundresses, farmers' wives, a countess, three nuns who had signed against their bishop's explicit instruction—and whose signatures had been entered into official ledgers by clerks who then, I had learned from the letters, forwarded them to employers, to husbands, to parish priests. Women dismissed from posts. Women beaten at home. One woman, a seamstress named Anna Rezek, whose letter I had read twice because I could not believe it the first time, wrote that her brother had put her out of the house in January and her own mother had not spoken in her defense.

Ivo, you are not paid to feel things, I reminded myself, the way I reminded myself every Thursday. You are paid to drive and to sort. But my hands had memorized those names along with the addresses, the way a postman's hands learn routes, and there is no unlearning a thing your hands know.

"The petition itself is gone," Odile was saying. "Submitted, stamped, filed in the provincial archive where it will sit until the building falls down around it. That was always the plan—they don't refuse us, they *shelve* us. But the ledger stays here, and in thirty days the ledger goes into a furnace with the rest." She looked around the table. "Four hundred women trusted this room with their names. I intend to return the trust."

"How?" Frau Lindqvist asked. "You can't move it. If you're stopped with it—"

"We don't move it. We copy it. Every name, every town, every line of what was done to them. Hand copies, dozens of them, mailed out across the country before the deadline. Let the army burn this building. By then the names will be in a hundred kitchens from here to the coast." She said it the way she said most things—flatly, without ornament, which somehow made it more terrible, the way a surgeon describes an incision.

Vogel shook his head slowly. "Do you know how long it takes to copy four hundred entries? Neatly? Legibly?"

"I do," Odile said. "Roughly eleven minutes per page, and the ledger runs sixty-one pages. One copyist working alone: about five weeks. We have thirty days. So we will need more than one copyist."

Her eyes went around the table and stopped on me. I felt it like a hand on my collar.

"You," she said. "Your handwriting is the neatest in this room. Don't look surprised—I've seen your address books. A man who keeps address books that tidy can be trusted with other people's names."

Every face turned toward me. I should have said something modest. Instead I heard myself say, "Sixty-one pages. At eleven minutes a page, three copyists working evenings—that's nine days of copying, leaving twenty-one for distribution. It can be done."

A silence followed, the particular silence of people recalibrating someone. Marta, the typesetter's daughter, tilted her head and studied me with frank, unchildlike curiosity, the way she probably studied misprinted type.

"And distribution," Vogel pressed. "The rail depot is already crawling with military police. They're inspecting outgoing post—you've seen the notices."

"I've seen them," I said. "And I know the roads better than the army does, because I've been driving them since before the army arrived. There are four routes out of this valley and they're watching one." I heard my own voice steadying as it went on, and realized with something like vertigo that I was not performing confidence; I was reporting facts. "The northern road through the pass is closed to civilian traffic, which means it's empty. The mill road floods after rain and they won't post sentries where their boots get wet. And the coach line still runs twice weekly to Halstadt carrying parcels—parcels get searched, but a driver's own effects generally don't, because drivers are furniture. I've been furniture for two years. No one has ever looked twice at me."

"That," Odile said quietly, "is about to change."

"Yes," I agreed. "It is."

I want to record honestly what happened inside me at that moment, because it was not courage. Courage is a word for afterward. What happened was this: for two years I had stood in doorways and read letters not addressed to me, memorized griefs I had no right to, carried other people's secrets like stones in my shoes—and told myself it was efficiency. Now the stones were being taken from me and made into a wall, and I discovered I had wanted to build a wall all along. Fear was still there—it sat in my stomach like swallowed ice—but the fear had found a job. There is no feeling in the world like fear with employment.

* * *

We worked out the system that first night, and refined it over the following week, and I will set it down plainly because plainness was its whole genius.

Copying happened in the cellar beneath the workshop, three at a time, in shifts of four hours, by hooded lamp. Odile copied. Vogel copied. Frau Lindqvist copied. Marta demanded a place and was refused, and demanded again, and was finally given the task of checking each finished page against the original—a compromise that fooled no one, least of all Marta, who treated it as a foothold and intended, I suspected, to widen it.

Each completed page went into a fresh envelope with a false return address—one of six we rotated—and each envelope went into a biscuit tin, and each tin went wherever I decided it went. Under the spare tire. Inside the door panels. Once, in a crate of type metal so heavy the sergeant who lifted it grunted and set it down with respect. I made runs twice a week: north through the pass to the convent school at St. Ambrose, where Mother Superior Kessler—an old friend of Odile's student days, a woman who had signed nothing herself but sheltered everyone who had—received our envelopes and posted them onward one by one from her own mailbag, in her own handwriting, under her own unquestionable collar. East on the mill road to the widow Havel's cousin, a railway clerk who slipped letters into the luggage van. South to Halstadt, where a newspaper editor named Brandeis owed Odile a debt I was never told the shape of.

"Never carry more than three tins," Odile instructed me. "If you're taken with three, we lose three pages and learn what they're looking for. If you're taken with twelve, we lose everything and learn nothing."

"And if I'm taken with three and talk?" I asked. It needed saying. Everyone at the table had gone still.

She looked at me for a long moment. "Will you talk?"

"No."

"Then the question answers itself." And that was all, and it was enough, and I understood that I had just been trusted in a way that could not be unsaid.

* * *

The strangest part was how ordinary the days remained. I still drove Odile to her meetings. I still sorted the Thursday mail. The town still went about its shrinking business while the chalk numbers multiplied on the doors of the eastern quarter, and inside the cooper's yard, above a cellar where three lamps burned in shifts, the presses kept printing pamphlets as though the world were not ending on a schedule.

But small things changed, and the changes were the true story. Frau Lindqvist began bringing food to the copying shifts—dark bread, hard sausage, coffee brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in—and eating with us instead of serving us, sitting on a flour barrel with her sleeves rolled, laughing at Vogel's complaints about his cramping hand until the laugh caught the rest of us. Vogel started teaching Marta the compositor's box between shifts, gruffly, pretending it was punishment, and she absorbed the case layout in a week and began correcting his spelling, which he bore with the wounded dignity of a cathedral. The two quiet men—Kolar and Weiss, I finally fixed their names—turned out to be the ones who knew how to make the false return addresses convincing, aging paper over the stove, matching regional postmarks, and they took a craftsman's pride in forgery that would have alarmed anyone who hadn't spent an evening listening to them argue about ink viscosity.

And Odile—Odile changed toward me, or perhaps only let me see what had been there. She began asking my opinion. Not the driver's opinion, the *opinion*. Whether the Halstadt route could bear more weight. Whether the sergeant at the depot checkpoint was the lazy one or the thorough one. Whether a woman named Petra Voss, whose letters I had read and whose husband intercepted her correspondence, should receive her envelope at her sister's address instead. On that last question I hesitated too long, and she noticed.

"You know something about Petra Voss," she said. Not accusing. Noting.

"I know her letters," I said. "I shouldn't. I'm sorry. I read them because—" I stopped. Every excuse sounded filthy in my mouth.

"Because someone had to know them," Odile said, "and it might as well be the man who carries them." She held my eyes. "Ivo. I dictate those letters within earshot of you. Whatever privacy you've broken, I broke first, and I broke it deliberately, because a movement that hides its own heart from its own people deserves to fail. What matters is what you did with what you read." She picked up her pen. "You remembered. That's what matters."

I drove home that night with the windows down in cold air I didn't feel. *What you did with what you read.* For two years I had thought of myself as a thief of small confidences. She had just reclassified the theft as apprenticeship.

* * *

On the ninth evening, in the cellar, I copied page forty-one and came upon a name I knew outside the ledger: Rosa Altmann, the flower seller who stood at the market corner by the fountain, who had sold me violets for my lapel every Sunday for a year and never once asked why a chauffeur wanted to look respectable. Beside her name, in Odile's precise entry: *Dismissed from stall rental by market board, February 1912, at request of her landlord. Three children.*

I had bought flowers from her four days ago. She had complained about the price of ribbon. She had not mentioned losing her livelihood, her stall, her corner of the world, to a signature I now held in my hand.

I set down the pen. My hand was shaking, and I was ashamed of it shaking, and then I was ashamed of the shame, and somewhere in that spiral Odile appeared beside me—she moved quietly for a woman of such public force—and read the entry upside down, and said nothing, and put her hand flat on the table next to mine. Not touching. Just there, the way you'd rest a hand near a frightened animal.

"That's the whole war," she said. "Not the one coming. This one. They count on each woman suffering alone. Four hundred women who each believed she was the only one punished. The ledger proves otherwise. That's why it terrifies them more than any pamphlet. A pamphlet argues. A list *witnesses*."

I finished the page. My hand had stopped shaking by the last line, and I understood that it wasn't steadiness that had returned—it was purpose, wearing steadiness's coat.

At the end of the shift, Marta counted the finished pages against her checklist and announced, in the tone of a foreman twice her age: "One hundred and twelve pages done. Forty-nine to go. Nine days of copying at current pace. Twenty-one days left on the clock." She looked up. "We're going to make it."

Nobody cheered. We were too tired, and cheering felt like tempting something. But Vogel smiled with his ink-black fingers laced behind his head, and Frau Lindqvist poured the last of the coffee into five cups instead of hoarding it, and Odile laughed—a short, real laugh, the first I'd heard from her since the notices went up—and for one moment the cellar smelled of coffee and lamp oil and warm bread instead of fear, and I thought: *this is what family is. Not blood. Shift work.*

* * *

Two nights later, I came up from the cellar past midnight to find Odile still at the worktable, alone, reading a letter by hooded lamp. She didn't hear me at first, and I saw her face before she composed it: stripped bare, younger, frightened in a way she permitted no one to witness. When she heard my step she smoothed it away like a bedsheet, but not fast enough, and we both pretended otherwise, and the pretense was its own kind of intimacy.

"Everything all right, madam?" I asked, because the old forms were a comfort to us both.

"Post from the north," she said. "Mother Kessler writes that a military postal inspector visited the convent school last week. Asking about unusual volumes of outgoing mail." She set the letter down. "He took a cup of tea, complimented the garden, and left. She thinks nothing of it."

"She thinks nothing of it," I repeated.

"I didn't say I do." Odile looked at the lamp flame for a moment. "Twenty-one days, Ivo. Nineteen after tonight. Keep the tins light and the routes varied. And drive carefully."

I went out into the dark street, and the cold hit me like water, and I stood a moment looking back at the workshop windows—dark now, shuttered, indistinguishable from every other building on Binder Lane—and I thought about inspectors visiting convents, about tea and gardens, about how thoroughly the army had begun to look at paper.

Somewhere behind those shutters lay fifty-eight pages of finished copies and forty-nine pages yet unwritten, and somewhere between here and the coast, a man with a clipboard was learning to count envelopes.

I got in the car and did not start the engine right away. First I sat with my hands on the wheel, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding: nineteen days, four routes, one driver, and a country full of mailbags beginning, very quietly, to close.
