## 6. The Typesetter's Daughter

Marta knew the sound of every machine in the workshop the way other girls knew the sound of their mothers' voices. The Heidelberg platen press had a cough in its left bearing that her father had promised to fix for three years and never had. The paper cutter sang a thin note when it bit through card stock. And the treadle of the small hand press in the back room—her father's own press, the one he'd bought secondhand from a bankrupt printer in Vellbruck—creaked on the downstroke like an old man rising from a chair.

So when she came up from the cellar at half past ten on a Tuesday night with a jar of pickled plums she'd gone down to fetch, and heard the treadle creaking when no one should have been in the building, she did not do what a sensible girl would have done.

She set the jar down on the stairs. She took off her boots so her stockings made no sound on the boards. And she went to see who was using her father's press.

* * *

The back room smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular cold of a building that had been empty of people since six o'clock. The door stood open a hand's width. Through the gap Marta saw lamplight, and a shoulder, and a man bent over the composing stone with a sheet of paper before him and another sheet beside it, copying.

She recognized him immediately, of course. Everyone in the workshop knew Ivo Quintana, the driver—the quiet one who carried Odile Marchetti's letters and never said two words when one would do. He sat with his coat still buttoned, as if he hadn't meant to stay, as if he'd only stopped by on his way somewhere else and the pen had gotten hold of him. His handwriting was small and even, the letters of a man who had learned to write in a place where flourishes were punished.

He was copying names.

Marta read upside down; her father had taught her that before he'd taught her anything else, standing her on his side of the stone and running his finger along the reversed type until the words turned themselves right way round in her head. *Marchetti, Odile. Brandt is not a name I know. Havel, Anna. Kessler, Ruth.* No—those were different pages. On the page under his pen: *Voss, Hedwig. Voss, Hedwig* again, going onto a fresh slip, and beside it the address of a dressmaker in a town Marta had never been to.

Her father was not among them. She checked anyway. She always checked. Four months dead and she still looked for his name in every list, every ledger, every column of print, the way you touch your tongue to a missing tooth.

The treadle creaked. Not the press—Ivo shifting his weight on the stool, and the stool's foot finding the loose board her father had never fixed either. This whole building was held together by things her father had meant to get around to.

Marta pushed the door open.

Ivo's pen stopped. He didn't startle—that was the first thing she noticed about him, the thing she thought about later, lying awake. He simply lifted his head and looked at her with the flat, waiting expression of a man who has already calculated how much trouble he is in and found it survivable.

"You're copying the ledger," Marta said.

"I'm doing inventory," he said.

"On the composing stone. With a pen." She came into the room properly, arms crossed, sixteen years old and five feet nothing of certainty. "Those are petition slips. Those are the addresses we mail the pamphlets to. My father cut that type for the return address himself."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the Ringstrasse a soldier's boot heels went by in pairs, unhurried, the way men walk when the town belongs to them.

Then Ivo Quintana did something she did not expect. He turned the page toward her.

Not all the way. Just enough that she could see the topmost line, and the heading he'd written across it in his small square hand: *Names — first copy.* As if he were showing a receipt. As if he wanted, more than anything, for someone to see what he was doing and tell him whether it was right.

"Four hundred and twelve," he said. "I've done sixty-one."

Marta looked at the slips. Looked at the ledger itself, closed now, sitting under his elbow like a sleeping animal. She had seen that ledger exactly once, a year ago, when Frau Marchetti had opened it on this same stone and run her finger down the columns and said, *Look what they gave us, Marta. Look how many.* Her father had been alive then. He had stood behind her with his hands ink-stained and said nothing at all, which from him was agreement.

"Frau Marchetti knows?" Marta asked.

"She knows."

"And the soldiers don't."

"Not yet."

Marta pulled the second stool—her father's stool—from the corner. Its seat was worn into a shallow shine by eleven years of his weight. She sat down on it and felt the shape of him under her, which was unbearable, and stayed sitting because leaving would be worse.

"Show me how you're dividing them," she said. "If we're doing it alphabetically we're wasting time. Do it by district. All the Vellbruck addresses together, all the river towns together. One envelope per route."

Ivo stared at her.

"My father ran forty thousand pamphlets through this shop last spring," Marta said. "You think I sorted the bundles by watching?"

* * *

They worked until two in the morning.

It went like this: Ivo read a name aloud, low, almost a murmur, and Marta wrote it on a slip in her best compositor's hand—the upright, unornamented letters her father had drilled into her, letters made to survive handling. Then she copied the address beneath it. Then Ivo blotted the master ledger with a scrap of newsprint and turned the page, and they began again.

Somewhere past the hundredth name the rhythm became something else. It became the sound of the shop at night when her father worked late and let her fall asleep on the sack of rag stock by the stove. It became the sound of being useful in the only room in the world where she had ever been entirely herself.

"Kovac, Ilona," Ivo read. "Seamstress. River quarter."

"Kovac, Ilona," Marta wrote. "She brought us cabbage rolls once. During the strike fund drive. She had flour on her sleeves."

Ivo glanced up. "You know them?"

"I know everyone who comes through that door. My father kept the front unlocked twelve hours a day. Women came in to argue with him about kerning." Something caught in her throat and she pushed past it. "Next."

But Ivo didn't say next. He set the pen down and looked at her across the lamp, and his face did the thing faces did when they forgot to guard themselves—a brief, unguarded softness, quickly shuttered.

"Marta," he said. He knew her name. Of course he knew her name; he read everything in this building, apparently, including things not addressed to him. "Your father. The accident."

"The army calls it an industrial matter." She said the words flatly, the way the notice had said them, the way the lieutenant had said them to her mother in their kitchen while holding his hat and looking at the floor. *An industrial matter. Our condolences. The machinery was old.* "The brake on the cutter failed. He'd reported it twice. In writing. The requisition officers took the maintenance log with everything else when they seized the east quarter records." She smoothed a finished slip against the stone. "So there's no proof anymore. There isn't even a record that there was ever a record."

Ivo was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was lower than before.

"That's why the ledger matters," he said. "That's why they'll burn this building rather than leave a single page in it. It isn't the pamphlets. Pamphlets they can reprint anywhere. It's the names. A name written down can't be called an industrial matter. A name has a face, and an address, and a daughter who remembers the flour on her sleeves."

Marta looked down at the slip in her hand. *Kovac, Ilona. Seamstress. River quarter.* Small black letters on cheap paper, worth nothing, worth everything.

"Give me the pen," she said. "You read. I write faster than you do, and my hand won't cramp, because I've been setting type since I was nine and my fingers are liars—they keep working after my head gives out."

He hesitated. She watched him weigh it—watched the whole calculation move behind his eyes, the risk of her, the danger to her, the arithmetic of a man who had spent years making himself invisible and had just been seen. She watched him decide.

He handed her the pen.

* * *

They might have gone on until dawn if the bell over the front door hadn't stirred.

Not rung—stirred. A half-note, muffled, the sound of the door being opened slowly by someone trying not to let it speak. Marta's hand froze mid-letter. Ivo was already moving, killing the lamp with a cupped palm, and the dark came down on them like water.

Boots in the front room. Two sets. The slow scrape of a man dragging his feet because he'd been on his feet since morning and hated it.

"Nothing here," said a voice near the counter. Young. Bored. "Third night running. If the colonel thinks pamphlets grow in the dark, he can come check himself."

"They grow somewhere," said the second voice, older, flatter. "Check the back."

Marta felt Ivo's hand close on her wrist—not pulling, just pressing, a message in itself: *stay*. The cellar hatch was four steps away, under the rag-stock sacks. But the sacks had been moved for the winter coal delivery; there was no time to cover the hatch again. There was nowhere to go at all.

The boots came down the corridor. Stopped at the threshold of the back room.

A match flared. Light bloomed behind cupped fingers, and in its glow Marta saw, absurdly clearly, the face of a young soldier—nineteen, maybe twenty, cheeks still soft, a sergeant's stripes too new to sit straight on his sleeve. He held the match high and looked into the room.

At the composing stone. At the two stools. At the closed ledger sitting in plain view beside a scatter of blank slips and a bottle of blotting sand.

At nothing, Marta realized a heartbeat later. Because Ivo had swept the finished copies into the drawer under the stone in the two seconds between the door and the match, and the ledger lay closed, and a closed ledger in a print shop was furniture. Was inventory. Was nothing.

The young sergeant's eyes crossed hers. Held for one full second—one second long enough for Marta to count her own heartbeats, three of them, four—and then moved off her face as deliberately as a man stepping over something on the road.

"Back room's cold," he said to his companion. "Nobody works in a room this cold."

"Colonel says—"

"Colonel says a lot of things." The match died between his fingers. "Come on. I want my bunk."

The boots receded. The front door opened and shut, and this time the bell rang its full bright note, honest as a struck coin, and then the street took them and the silence came back.

Marta discovered she had been gripping the edge of the stone hard enough to leave marks in her palms. In the dark, Ivo exhaled, very slowly.

"He saw it," she whispered. "The ledger. He looked right at it."

"Yes," Ivo said.

"Then why—"

"I don't know," Ivo said. And then, quieter, in the voice of a man admitting something he'd been carrying alone for weeks: "I think maybe I do. But I don't know."

Marta found the matches and relit the lamp. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake for an hour. Instead they were steadier than they had been in four months, since the day the lieutenant with the hat had stood in her kitchen saying *industrial matter* as though her father had been a crate of spoiled goods.

Because now there was something to do with it. Now the grief had a shape, and the shape was four hundred and twelve names, and one of the names belonged to a girl who could set type in the dark.

"We work by districts starting tomorrow," she said. "And we finish before they burn this place, because they will burn it—you heard the colonel's notice, thirty days, and men like that don't extend deadlines for sentiment." She pulled the next stack of slips toward her and squared its edges with a compositor's precision that would have made her father proud and made her cry, briefly, silently, if she let it, which she did not. "How many are left?"

"Three hundred and fifty-one," Ivo said.

"Then we'd better not waste the night arguing about it." She dipped the pen. "Read."

And Ivo Quintana, the invisible man, the driver who never said two words when one would do, bent over the ledger and read her the names of the women of the country, one by one, like a litany, like a prayer, while outside the window the patrols went by in pairs and the century gathered itself to turn.

It was nearly four in the morning when the bell over the front door stirred again—once, softly—and this time a folded piece of paper came sliding under it across the floorboards, and neither of them heard who left it there.
