## 10. Two Hundred and Sixty-One

The cellar smelled of lamp oil and cold stone, and by the third night it had begun to smell of ink too, because everything in the workshop eventually did. Marta sat at the plank table with a stub of pencil behind her ear and a fresh sheet before her, copying names in her best hand—the hand her father had taught her for setting type, each letter upright and even, as if the words themselves might one day be locked into a composing stick and printed for the whole country to read.

Two hundred and sixty-one.

She knew the number without counting because she kept the count the way other girls kept prayers: morning and night, silently, obsessively. Four hundred names on Odile Marchetti's master ledger. Two hundred and sixty-one now copied onto the thin onionskin pages Ivo would fold into envelopes no bigger than communion wafers. One hundred and thirty-nine to go.

Above her head, footsteps crossed the floorboards—Odile, pacing between the shuttered windows, keeping watch though there was nothing yet to watch for except the ordinary street. The army had not come into the workshop itself. Not yet. They stood at the corner in their gray coats and smoked and looked at the building the way men look at a field they have already decided to plow.

"Another sheet," Ivo said from his end of the table. His voice was hoarse. Three nights of copying had worn grooves into him; she could see them in the way he held his wrist, shaking it out every dozen lines like a man shaking water from his fingers.

Marta took the finished page from under the paperweight—a brass gear from her father's press, the only piece of him that had survived the machine—and set a new one down. The ledger lay open between them, propped on two bricks so both could read it. Four hundred names in Odile's strong slanted script, four hundred women who had signed their names to a demand they knew would cost them something, and had cost some of them everything.

Marta copied the name at the top of her column. *Widow Anna Reiss, laundress.* Then the next. *Frau Katarina Vogel, wife of the butcher.*

Then the next.

Her pencil stopped.

The letters were ordinary. That was what made it unbearable—that the hand of the ledger didn't tremble at all, that Odile's pen had written this name exactly as it had written all the others, steady and fair, as if it were just another woman, just another signature.

*Gerda Hallmann, wife of the schoolmaster.*

Marta stared at it until the lamp flame doubled and swam.

"Marta?" Ivo said.

She couldn't answer. Her throat had closed around something old and sharp, something she had swallowed eleven months ago at her father's funeral and had never let back up.

The cellar door opened above; Odile came down the stairs with a shawl over her shoulders and a pot of coffee balanced against her hip, and Marta watched her the way you watch weather change. Odile set the pot down, poured three cups without asking, and then, seeing Marta's face, went still.

"What is it?"

"She can't be in there," Marta said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "That name."

Odile looked at the ledger. She read the line. Marta saw her understand immediately—of course she understood; Odile Marchetti understood everything about this town, every grudge and debt, the way Ivo understood roads.

"Ah," Odile said softly.

"You know who she is." Marta pushed back from the table. The chair legs shrieked on stone. "You know what she did."

"I know," Odile said.

* * *

It had been February, eleven months ago. Marta could reconstruct the day the way she could reconstruct any page of type—line by line, letter by letter, because grief had typeset it permanently in her memory.

Her father had been dead nine days. The army's report called it an industrial matter: a press coupling that should have been replaced, a guard that had been removed years ago to save seconds per rotation, a man caught where no man should have stood. The officer who delivered the report had not removed his cap. He had left it on, standing in their kitchen with snow melting off his shoulders, reading aloud from a paper as if reciting train times.

There was to be no inquiry. There was to be no compensation beyond a sum so small it was insulting. The army regretted the incident. The army wished the family well.

And Gerda Hallmann—who had lived across the lane from them for nineteen years, who had given Marta cinnamon buns when Marta was small, whose own daughter had once been Marta's closest friend before the girl married and moved to the coast—Gerda Hallmann had gone to the district office and written a statement. Marta knew because Frau Vogel told her, and Frau Vogel had seen the statement herself when she'd gone to petition about her husband's confiscated cart.

Gerda had written that Josef Brandt—the typesetter, the troublemaker, the man who ran pamphlets through his press at night—had long been a disturber of public order, and that his death, while regrettable, was the natural consequence of his own reckless politics, and that the army bore no responsibility, and that certain persons in the neighborhood were spreading ungrateful rumors and ought to be ashamed.

*Certain persons.* That meant Marta. That meant Marta's mother, who had gone door to door asking neighbors whether they would sign a letter demanding an inquiry, and who had collected six signatures before the seventh door was slammed in her face.

Six signatures out of forty households. And one statement against.

"I was fourteen," Marta said now, standing in the cold of the cellar with her fists at her sides. "I was fourteen and my father was in a box, and she—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that the tears came hot and fast the way they always did when she talked about him, as if the tears had been waiting behind a door all day for exactly this moment. "She wrote them a letter. She helped bury it. She helped them bury him twice."

Ivo had risen halfway from his chair, uncertain, a man who never knew what to do with other people's grief—as if it were luggage too heavy to offer to carry. Odile stayed seated. She poured the third cup of coffee, the untouched one, and held it out toward Marta without insistence, the way you hold out a hand to a horse.

Marta didn't take it. But she also didn't leave.

"My mother died asking those doors," Odile said. "Not literally. But almost." She turned the ledger toward herself, gently, and looked down at Gerda Hallmann's name the way a surgeon looks at a wound. "My sister Lena worked in the cotton mill on the river road. You wouldn't remember it; the fire was before your time. Ninety-one women died. The doors had been locked from the outside to keep the workers from slipping out for air. Every single door."

Marta wiped her face with her sleeve. She had heard fragments of this story—in pamphlets, in whispers—but never from Odile herself, never in this low, level voice that was somehow worse than shouting.

"The owner was fined forty marks," Odile said. "Forty marks for ninety-one women. He used the same arithmetic the army uses: a man's life weighed against a machine's output, and the machine always wins, because the machine has friends and the women don't. My mother went to her grave still writing petitions about it. My father stopped speaking to me when I joined the movement. He said I would bring shame on the family. He said the law would handle such things."

"Do you hate him?" Marta asked. It came out blunt, childlike, and she flinched at her own question.

"No," Odile said. "That's the terrible part. I spent years trying to and couldn't manage it. Hate is expensive, Marta. It costs you attention, and attention is the only currency we have. I couldn't afford to spend mine on him. So I spent it on the mill instead. On the doors. On making sure that the ninety-one names got printed, got read, got repeated, until people in three provinces could recite them like a litany." She looked up. "Do you know what happened when we printed those names? Half the town thanked us. The other half said we were exploiting the dead for politics."

"And which half was right?"

"Neither. Both. It doesn't matter." Odile folded her hands on the table beside the ledger. "What matters is that the names existed. That someone wrote them down before the fire took even their record away. A fire takes everything, Marta—it takes the person and then it takes the proof the person existed. Writing is the only thing that burns slower than a life."

* * *

They argued until the coffee went cold.

Marta argued first from grief and then, as the hours wore on, from something colder and more careful that surprised her: principle. If the ledger was supposed to be true, then it should be true. Gerda Hallmann had not signed the petition. Her name was not among the brave. Putting it among them was a lie, and a movement built on a lie deserved what it got.

"That's not what it means," Ivo said quietly. He had barely spoken all evening; when he did, both women turned toward him as if a piece of furniture had offered an opinion. He looked embarrassed but continued. "It's not a roll of honor. It's evidence. It's proof that these women existed here, in this place, in this year, and that the state took the town they lived in and burned the proof. If you take her name out because she doesn't deserve it, then someone else gets to decide which names deserve to survive. And the people who get to decide are never the ones whose names are at risk."

"She denounced us," Marta said. "She'll denounce us again. What if she reads her name in a newspaper and goes straight to the colonel? 'Here, these are the agitators, these are the ones—'"

"Then she does," Odile said.

"You can't mean that."

"I mean it completely." Odile rose, walked to the far wall where the coal shelf held their small store of candles, and stood with her back to them for a moment. When she turned around, the lamplight caught her face from below and made her look older, carved. "Let me tell you the hardest rule I've ever had to keep, and I've kept it for twenty years, and it has cost me more sleep than the police ever have. We take all names or none. No exceptions. No strikes for cowards, no additions for saints. The moment we begin curating, we become the tribunal—and a tribunal that edits its archive will eventually edit anything. Today we strike the informer. Tomorrow someone says strike the wife of the magistrate. Next week, strike anyone whose husband ever bought army boots. Do you see where the staircase goes down?"

Marta saw. She hated seeing. It was easier to be righteous when righteousness required only anger.

"But she betrayed my father."

"Yes."

"After he was dead."

"Yes." Odile came back to the table and sat down across from Marta, close enough that Marta could see the fine lines around her eyes, the tiredness she hid during the day behind the crisp collar and the quick smile she gave officials. "And I will tell you something else, and then you may decide what to do with it. Gerda Hallmann's son drowned at Kiel two years ago. Training exercise. The navy sent a form letter. She has been alone since, in that house across the lane, with a schoolmaster husband who tells her what to think the same way the army told her what to write. I'm not telling you this to excuse her. I'm telling you because you deserve the whole truth before you make a decision that will follow you longer than the grief will."

"That's not fair," Marta whispered. "You can't give me that and then ask me to be merciful anyway."

"I'm not asking you to be merciful." Odile's voice gentled. "Mercy is a feeling. Feelings come and go. I'm asking you to be disciplined. Grace isn't softness, Marta. Grace is the hardest discipline there is—harder than hunger, harder than fear. Anyone can burn a building in fury. It takes iron to copy the name of the woman who hurt you onto the same page as your father's friends, in the same careful hand, and mean it. Because meaning it is how you prove you're different from them. They keep lists to destroy people. We keep lists so people can't be destroyed."

The cellar was silent. Somewhere above, wind moved along the eaves of the workshop, and Marta thought of the presses up there, silent and dismantled-in-spirit, waiting for the army's hands. Thirty days. Twenty-six now, maybe twenty-five—she'd lost track of the exact count and refused to check, as if not checking might slow the calendar.

She picked up her pencil. It had gone cold in her absence; she warmed it in her fist.

"All names or none," she said.

"All names or none," Odile said.

Marta bent over the ledger. Gerda Hallmann's name waited in Odile's even script, patient as stone. Marta copied it. Letter by letter, upright and even, the hand her father taught her, the hand that would set type again someday. *Gerda Hallmann, wife of the schoolmaster.* She wrote it slowly, and she did not cry this time, and somewhere in the writing of it the name stopped being Gerda's name and became simply the archive's—belonging to the record, to the future, to whoever found it after the fires.

When she finished, Ivo slid the page toward himself, squared its edges with the others, and began folding envelopes.

"Two hundred sixty-two," Marta said, and heard her own voice steady.

They worked on. The candle burned down; Odile replaced it without being asked; Ivo's wrist shook and he shook it out and kept going. Past midnight, past the hour when even the soldiers at the corner stamped off to their barracks, past the point where Marta's eyes blurred and the letters swam.

She was reaching for a fresh sheet when the trapdoor overhead creaked.

All three of them froze. Above them, in the dark workshop, a floorboard sighed under weight—not the settling of old wood; weight, deliberate, shifting.

Then footsteps. Slow. Crossing the room toward the stair.

* * *

A knock came at the top of the cellar door—two soft raps, then silence, then two more. Not the army's knock. The army knocked like it owned the door, because it did. This was someone uncertain. Someone who didn't want to wake the street.

Ivo put out his hand flat: wait. He blew out the candle. In the darkness Marta heard Odile move, unhurried, toward the stairs, and heard her climb them, and heard the bolt slide back.

The door opened a finger's width. Gray light from the streetlamp leaked down the steps, and in it stood a figure in a soldier's coat, collar up, cap pulled low—a young face beneath it, drawn tight, holding something out in one hand like a man feeding an animal he wasn't sure wouldn't bite.

Paper.

"Your sergeant," Odile said quietly, taking it, "is going to catch his death out there."

"He won't be missed till morning muster," the sergeant said. His voice cracked on the last word. "Read it fast. And burn it after."
