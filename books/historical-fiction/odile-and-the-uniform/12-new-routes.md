## 12. New Routes

The morning after the mailbag burned, I woke with the taste of ash in my mouth that no amount of coffee could scour away.

I had watched it from the colonel's own square, standing in a crowd of shopkeepers and schoolchildren summoned to witness what the army called *a security measure*. Eleven envelopes, addressed in the careful copperplate hand Ivo used for everything he wrote, fed into an iron brazier while a lieutenant read out the addresses like a list of criminals. A woman from Vellmar. A schoolteacher from Ostrow. My own handwriting on three of them—my name pressed into wax seals I had chosen myself, lavender-scented because I had always believed correspondence should carry some small kindness even when it carried defiance.

The smoke had risen straight up into a windless sky, a gray column visible for miles, as if the army wanted the whole province to understand: *this is what happens to women's words.*

I stood very still through all of it. Beside me, a butcher's wife whispered that it was shameful, and her husband elbowed her quiet. A girl of perhaps nine asked her mother what was burning, and the mother said, *old papers, darling*, in the flat voice of someone who has learned not to see.

But I saw. And by the time Ivo drove us home through streets hung with requisition notices—*THIRTY DAYS REMAINING* pasted over *TWENTY-NINE*, pasted over *ELEVEN*—I had stopped being afraid.

That is not quite true. I was still afraid. Fear had been my companion since my sister Ana died with forty other girls in a locked stairwell of a textile factory, and I had learned that fear is not the opposite of courage but its raw material, the ore from which you hammer something useful if your hands are steady enough. What had changed was this: I understood at last that we were not smuggling letters. We were smuggling proof. And proof, once scattered, cannot be burned twice.

The ledger sat in the cellar behind a false panel Ivo had built, four hundred names in his cramped, patient script—two hundred sixty-one copied before Marta's fingers had found the page that stopped her cold, two hundred sixty-one dispatched before the rail depot seizure cut off our easiest road. The remaining hundred and thirty-nine waited in the dark like patients who could not be moved until the doctor found another way.

Over breakfast—Ivo black-bread and jam, Marta tea she wrapped both hands around as though the cup might be taken from her too—I laid three objects on the table.

A hymnal. A child's coat. A hatbox of the kind traveling saleswomen carried from town to town.

Marta looked at the hymnal first. "Church?"

"Church," I said. "Every Sunday, half the women in the province carry a hymnal to service and carry it home again. No sergeant alive has ever opened one. They search coats for pamphlets, crates for print, mailbags for handwriting. But a hymnal is furniture of the soul. Touching one makes them feel guilty."

Ivo turned the book over in his hands. He had that look he got—the one I had mistaken for mere attentiveness in our first months together, before I understood it was hunger, the hunger of a man who reads everything because he has never been given anything worth reading. "The binding," he said slowly. "If you slit the spine lining and paste the pages flat—"

"Four leaves per book," I agreed. "One name per leaf, written small, in pencil. Pencil fades where ink accuses."

"And the coat?" Marta asked.

"Hems." I lifted the child's garment, turned the frayed edge inside out. "Refugee families are moving west ahead of the depot construction. A seamstress in every town along the road, taking in alterations. A woman brings a coat to be let down for a growing child; the seamstress unpicks the hem, finds a strip of paper sewn into the lining, resews it without ever being told why." I looked between them. "She doesn't need to know. Denial is a form of protection. If she never knows, she can never confess."

"That's—" Marta began.

"Cunning," I said. "And cunning is all we have left, because honesty just went up in smoke in the colonel's square."

Ivo set the hymnal down with the care of a man handling something consecrated. Then he did the thing he does when an idea has been working in him silently for hours and chooses that moment to surface. He spoke without looking up.

"The saleswomen."

"Yes," I said.

"You already have names."

"From before." It was true. Ten years of suffrage work leaves threads across a country, thin ones, frayed ones, but real. Corset sellers and lace peddlers and patent-medicine agents, women who knocked on farm doors and parlor doors alike, who were so thoroughly invisible that men spoke freely in front of them about matters of state, about wages, about grievances—as though invisibility were deafness. Half a dozen of them had once carried petitions for us. Three of them, I knew, would carry anything now, because their own names were in that ledger or their sisters' names were, or because they had simply been waiting ten years for someone to ask them to matter.

"I'll write to them tonight," I said. "Not letters. Nothing with workshop paper, nothing in a hand the post office has seen. I'll write in recipe format."

Marta blinked. "Recipes?"

"A woman named Heske in Brennstadt gets a card asking whether she still makes her almond torte, and whether she'd share the method with a cousin. Inside, folded small: a strip of names. She'll understand. She taught me the trick herself, years ago, when we smuggled strike notices inside preserves."

Ivo almost smiled. Almost. His face held smiles the way a dam holds water—all that pressure, released only in hairline cracks. "Preserves."

"Sugar seals better than you'd think," I told him. "And no soldier in this country has ever confiscated a jar of plum butter."

* * *

We worked for six days.

The cellar became a workshop within the workshop, below the presses the soldiers had catalogued and photographed, below the floorboards they would eventually pry up if Sergeant Brandt's patrols grew curious enough. Down there, by two lamps and a spirit stove, we built our new machinery—not iron but ingenuity.

Ivo handled the hymnals. He had discovered a bookbinder's trick from watching the man repair Odile family Bibles a decade ago (he confessed this with the slight shame of a man admitting to a childhood crime): score the endpaper, lift the marbled lining, lay your slip of paper against the board, paste it flat, weight it overnight under Marta's father's old brass type-case. By morning the hymnal closed with a faint thickness no eye would catch, though a thumb might. So we instructed our carriers: *do not hand the book to any man; let women hold it.*

Marta took the sewing. Her hands, which had learned typesetting at eleven and had been idle and grieving for eight months, remembered themselves at the needle. She sewed hems with strips of tissue paper basted inside—names in pencil, folded to the width of a thumbnail—and then, at my instruction, deliberately sewed some seams badly. "Crooked?" she asked, frowning.

"Crooked," I confirmed. "A perfect hem says a professional made this coat. A crooked hem says a poor woman mending her own. Which one will a sergeant believe carries secrets?"

She thought about it, then nodded slowly, and there passed over her face something I recognized from my own mirror years ago: the moment grief converts to craft. Her father had died under a press the army called *an industrial matter*, and she could not set type anymore without hearing the crash. But she could stitch. She could hide four hundred truths inside the domestic work the world expected of her anyway, invisible work inside invisible work, a Russian doll of concealment, and no one—no one—would think to look.

On the fourth evening, the saleswomen began to answer.

Heske's card came back inside two days—she lived close—with a reply in the same recipe code: *Almond torte confirmed. Send ingredients.* Ingredients meant names. By week's end, three more cards arrived: a corset agent in Lauen who signed herself *Your aunt Mathilde*; a lace peddler working the river towns; a patent-medicine woman whose territory ran all the way to the capital, who wrote back a single line that made me laugh aloud for the first time in weeks:

*Dear cousin: I have room in my case for whatever needs carrying. Tell your friend the doctor sends his regards and charges double after dark.*

The network was becoming a living thing. I felt it the way you feel a fire take in a cold grate—one coal touching another, each catching from the next, until the whole hearth holds heat without any single flame doing the work alone. Every saleswoman recruited brought routes I didn't know. Every route brought towns. Every town brought a seamstress, a schoolteacher, a chapel-goer with a hymnal and a grudge. We were no longer sending names outward from a center; we were weaving a fabric, and fabric, unlike a courier's satchel, cannot be seized at a checkpoint. You would have to arrest the entire country's laundry to stop us now.

I allowed myself, on the fifth night, one hour of something dangerously close to hope.

* * *

It ended on the sixth morning, when Marta put down her needle and said, "I want the eastern route."

I should have seen it coming. I have spent twenty years reading rooms, reading faces, reading the silences underneath speech, and yet she caught me entirely unprepared—which I suspect she intended.

The eastern route ran through the garrison zone itself: four towns inside the cordon around the future depot, where checkpoints stood at every crossroads and every traveler's papers were read twice, where the requisitioned farms had emptied and the soldiers outnumbered civilians ten to one. It was also, not coincidentally, where nineteen of our names came from—farmwives and millgirls whose petitions had gone into the eastern ledgers, whose husbands and fathers now worked army labor for army wages and dared say nothing. If those nineteen names never reached sympathetic hands beyond the wire, then the depot would be built, literally, on top of them: their signatures buried beneath barracks and powder stores, erased by concrete.

"No," I said. Flatly. Immediately. The way my mother said no, the way Ana would have said no.

"It's the only route with no carrier," Marta said. Her voice was level. Sixteen-year-old level, which is a particular and formidable kind of calm, because it has not yet learned to doubt itself. "Mathilde's territory ends at Lauen. The lace woman won't cross the river. And I'm the only one here who can pass as a local going east—I have cousins in Halstedt. Real ones. An aunt who's expecting me for the harvest."

"You are sixteen."

"You keep saying that like it means I'm breakable." She set down the coat she'd been hemming and looked at me with her father's eyes—those steady typesetter's eyes that had measured letterspacing for thirty years without a tremor. "Mrs. Marchetti. I've been dead since March. That's what everyone in this town thinks—that the printer's daughter is a ghost who keeps to herself. Nobody searches a ghost. Nobody suspects a grieving girl of anything except sadness. Sadness is the best disguise in the world, because everyone looks away from it."

I opened my mouth to argue and found nothing there. Because she was right, and I hated her for it, the way you hate anyone who tells you a truth you were hoping to defer.

Behind her, Ivo had gone very still. He does that—he becomes furniture when a conversation exceeds him, and then, precisely when you've forgotten he's in the room, he speaks. "The harvest cart," he said quietly.

Marta turned. "What?"

"If you go east, go as a girl bringing preserves to her aunt. Jars in straw. I'll drive you to the cordon myself—I know the checkpoint rotation, I've memorized the sergeant's shifts." A pause. "Brandt's shifts."

At the name, something moved through the cellar like a draft. Brandt. The young sergeant who had searched our car at the wire three weeks ago and found nothing, who had taken Ivo's papers and returned them unfiled, who patrolled our street with his cap pulled low and his eyes anywhere but the workshop windows. None of us had said his name aloud since. None of us trusted it. And none of us, if we were honest, entirely disbelieved in it either.

"He watches the east gate on alternating days," Ivo went on, in the toneless voice of a man reciting facts he wishes were not facts. "On his days, traffic moves slow. He reads papers carefully. On Lieutenant Weiss's days, it's fast and sloppy. If Marta travels on a Weiss day—"

"On a Weiss day," Marta repeated, "with jars."

"With jars," Ivo said. "Plum butter. Sugar-sealed. And under the false bottom of the basket—"

"—nineteen names," Marta finished, and for the first time since her father died, I watched her smile.

I still said no. I said it twice more that day, and once the following morning, and each time with less conviction and more exhaustion, because I understood that I was not arguing with a girl; I was arguing with the arithmetic of the situation, and arithmetic does not lose arguments. One hundred and thirty-nine names remained uncopied-uncopied... rather, undispatched. Nineteen of them were sealed behind the eastern wire. The window before inspection—before the army turned the workshop inside out—was closing like a fist.

So on the seventh morning I did the only thing left to a woman whose creed is justice outside the law and whose conscience cannot afford martyrs: I negotiated.

"You travel on a Weiss day," I told Marta. "You carry nothing on your body. If the basket is taken, you cry—genuinely, about the preserves, your aunt's disappointment, the wasted sugar. You are a heartbroken niece, nothing else. And you come back the same day, before dusk, because nothing explains itself after dark."

"And if I'm searched anyway?"

"Then you're a girl with too much plum butter and an aunt who'll scold her. That's all anyone finds. That's all anyone *can* find." I held her gaze. "The nineteen names will be under the basket's false bottom, and the false bottom will be built so that lifting it breaks the seal visibly—if a stranger opens it, you'll know instantly, and so will the whole market stall around you."

She nodded. Then, quietly, in a voice suddenly younger than sixteen: "My father would have liked this. The hiding things inside ordinary work. He used to say a compositor hides nothing and shows everything, and that's why they trust him."

"Your father," I said, "is in every letter that leaves here."

Something crossed her face—grief and gratitude braided tight—and she bent back over her sewing, and the needle went on, in and out, in and out, stitching truth into the underside of the world.

That afternoon I drafted the last of the recipe-cards. Ivo ground the pencil lead fine and cut the paper slips narrow. The hymnals went into a chapel-bound deaconess's carpet bag. The coats went west with a refugee family who never learned what warmth they carried besides wool. And Marta stood in the yard at dawn two days later, in her dead father's too-large traveling coat, beside a basket of jars Ivo had packed with the tenderness of a man building a coffin he hoped would never be needed.

"One more thing," I told her at the gate, and pressed into her glove a scrap of paper with a single line: *Ana's name is among the nineteen. Bring her east with you.*

Marta read it, looked up, and understood everything—about my sister, about the fire, about why a woman spends her life collecting other people's names so that no one is ever again a number in a ledger no one reads.

"I'll bring her home," she said.

They pulled away through the fog with the baskets rattling softly, and I stood at the gate listening to the wheels fade toward the eastern wire, toward the checkpoints, toward Brandt's alternating days, and I thought: *we are a living thing now, and living things can bleed.*

I did not yet know how soon.
