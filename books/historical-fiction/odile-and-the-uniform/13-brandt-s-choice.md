## 13. Brandt's Choice

The order came down on a Tuesday, written in the lieutenant's small precise hand, the kind of handwriting that had never once hesitated over anything in its life.

*Full inspection of the Marchetti premises. Premises and all outbuildings. Every room, every crate, every floorboard lifted if necessary. You will have a detail of six men and two days. I want it done before the week is out — before Saturday.*

Brandt read it twice at his desk in the requisitioned schoolhouse that now served as the garrison office, while outside the window a squad drilled in the mud of what had been the children's playing field. The paper was warm from the lieutenant's hand. That was the thing about orders; they arrived still carrying the temperature of the man who'd written them, and you were meant to absorb that warmth as though it were conviction.

Lieutenant Voss had come up from the capital six weeks ago, a thin man with a thin mustache and a talent for making other men feel untidy. He kept a map of the eastern quarter pinned above his desk with red pins marking every structure scheduled for demolition when the depot works began. The Marchetti workshop had three pins in it, which Brandt suspected was more than it strictly required. Voss did not like the workshop. More precisely — and Brandt had learned to read this distinction the way he'd once read weather coming off the hills of his childhood — Voss did not like *her*. Odile Marchetti had smiled at him across his own desk during their first meeting, agreed to nothing, thanked him for his courtesy as though courtesy were something she was doing *him* a favor by accepting, and walked out with her spine straight as a flagpole. Voss had not mentioned her since without his jaw tightening.

"Brandt." The lieutenant stood in the doorway of the inner office, holding a cup of coffee he would not drink. He never drank it. He held it the way other men held a riding crop. "You've been on the workshop surveillance rotation longest."

"Yes, sir."

"You'll lead the search." He set the order on Brandt's desk with two fingers, as though handing over something slightly soiled. "If you find printing materials, pamphlets, correspondence — anything beyond furniture and personal effects — you inventory it and seal it. Nothing gets burned on site this time. We do things properly." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "We are not the mob, Sergeant. We are the army. There is a difference, even if the results occasionally resemble each other."

"Yes, sir."

"The woman will be given notice of the inspection date tomorrow morning. Standard procedure." He turned to go, then stopped. "One more thing. The mail seizures last month — eleven envelopes, burned publicly. Effective, I thought. But the colonel wants assurance the flow has actually stopped, not merely gone underground. If your inspection turns up evidence of an organized distribution network — names, addresses, routes — I want it on my desk within the hour of discovery. Not the next morning. Within the hour." His eyes were pale and patient. "Do you understand me?"

"I understand you, sir."

He left. Brandt sat looking at the order until the ink seemed to darken, and then he folded it along its original creases and put it in his breast pocket, where he could feel it against his chest like a second heartbeat for the rest of the day.

* * *

That night he walked the perimeter.

It wasn't required. The surveillance rotation didn't need him personally; any of his men could count windows and note lamplit hours. But Brandt had discovered, over these months, that he could not think indoors anymore. Thinking indoors had become impossible sometime after the mail burning — after he'd stood in the rail depot square with his hands behind his back and watched eleven envelopes curl in an iron brazier while a corporal read the charge aloud: *circulation of unauthorized printed matter.* Eleven envelopes. He had found himself wondering, with a specificity that felt like a wound opening, whether one of them might have been addressed to a town called Halden, where a woman named Anna Brandt lived above a baker's shop and had once, in a letter to her son, written: *I signed their paper. Your father was furious. I told him my name is the only property I have ever owned outright, and I will spend it how I like.*

She had signed. His mother had signed one of those petitions. He had known it abstractly for months, the way you know a thing you refuse to look at directly. Then he'd read the confiscated pamphlet — he'd been ordered to catalog it, and cataloging meant reading — and somewhere in the third paragraph, in language about wages and locked doors and fires that no one was punished for, his mother's situation had looked back at him from the page wearing different clothes, and he had not been able to unsee it.

So he walked. The eastern quarter at night had a particular quality now: too many dark windows, streets swept too clean, the population thinned by evacuation notices into a scattering of stubborn lights. The workshop sat among them with its lamps burning late, and Brandt counted them the way a man counts another man's breaths.

Tonight there were five figures visible through the frosted glass of the ground floor when he passed at eleven. At midnight, four. At half past, still four. They were working. He knew what they were working at, or he believed he knew, and the belief had been living in him for weeks now like a splinter he couldn't reach — because believing it made him complicit by mere knowledge, and reporting it made him complicit in a different direction entirely.

There was also the matter of the papers.

Sergeant Brandt had confiscated a driver's documents at a checkpoint in October — Quintana, Ivo, chauffeur, employed by Marchetti — and had taken them for verification, which was the procedure, which was what he was supposed to do. And then he had not filed the incident report. He had put the folder in his desk drawer instead, beneath a stack of ration forms, and told himself it was backlog. It was not backlog. Backlog got processed. This folder sat there growing heavier by the day, a stone with a man's whole life written on it, and every morning Brandt opened the drawer, saw the corner of the folder, and closed the drawer again.

He understood now, walking the cold perimeter with his collar up, that the drawer was not a delay. It was a decision he kept making fresh each dawn because he lacked the courage to make it once, finally, in ink.

Saturday was four days away. After Saturday, whatever happened inside that building would happen with the full machinery of the army behind it, and the folder in his drawer, the unsigned reports, the pamphlet he'd catalogued with suspiciously generic summary notes — all of it would surface, because inspections surfaced everything, that was their purpose. When Voss's men went through the workshop, they would find what they found. And when Voss reviewed the file on the Marchetti household, he would find a checkpoint incident with no report attached, and he would ask who had stopped that car, and Brandt's career would end in a room smaller than this street, and possibly worse than his career.

Or.

Or he could walk to that door tonight, tonight, while the lamps still burned, and slide something under it.

He stopped across the street from the workshop. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the frozen ruts. The light in the upper window — the one where the old woman, Mrs. Ferenc, did the binding work — went out. Below it, the long ground-floor glow persisted. Four silhouettes, bent over tables.

He thought about his father, who had spent thirty years as a customs clerk and had died respected, having never once in thirty years decided anything. He thought about Lieutenant Voss's coffee, always full, never drunk. He thought about the brazier at the rail depot, and the way the crowd had been silent — not approving silence, not disapproving silence, but the terrible neutral silence of people memorizing something they expected to need later.

His mother had signed her name. In her only letter about it, she'd written: *A signature is a small thing, Anton. So is a match. It depends what you set it next to.*

"All right," Brandt said to the snow, to nobody, to himself. His breath came out white and hung between him and the workshop like a curtain deciding whether to part.

* * *

He went back to the schoolhouse first. Not for courage — courage, he suspected, was one of those things that evaporated under storage — but for the practical business of composition. An unsigned note was a document, and documents had rules. Too vague and they'd discount it. Too specific and they'd know exactly which soldier had access to exactly which information, and the list was short enough to search by lamplight in an evening. It had to sound like it came from inside the building or beside it. It had to be plausible deniability wearing a coat.

He sat at his desk with the lamp turned low and wrote four drafts and burned three of them in the stove, feeding the pages in one at a time and watching the sentences curl.

The first was too much a soldier's note — dates, unit designations, the number of men in the detail. Anyone reading it would know it came from the garrison, and the garrison was forty men, and forty was too many to search but few enough to frighten. Burned.

The second was too literary. He had tried for anonymity through vagueness and produced something that sounded like an anonymous letter in a novel, all ominous phrasing and no content: *they are coming for you.* Of course they were coming for them; everyone was always coming for someone. Useless. Burned.

The third attempt broke him a little, because in it he tried to explain himself — tried to tell the woman Odile Marchetti why a sergeant of the occupation force was doing this, laid out his mother and the pamphlet and the drawer with the folder in it like evidence at his own trial — and halfway down the page he understood that no warning wrapped in a confession would serve anyone. If they were caught with it, his confession would simply confirm what they already suspected. And worse: it was not for them. However much he might want absolution, this was not the night to go shopping for it. Burned that one too, and sat for a long while watching the fire, feeling lighter and emptier with each draft, the way a man feels shedding weight he hadn't realized was ballast rather than burden.

The fourth draft took him ten minutes, because by then there was almost nothing left to hide behind, and the truth, it turned out, was brief.

He copied it out fair in a hand not his own — he'd learned in training to print block letters for signage, and block letters, impersonal as type, served now:

*FULL SEARCH OF THIS BUILDING SATURDAY MORNING, 0800. EVERY ROOM. FLOORBOARDS WILL BE LIFTED. FURNITURE WILL BE EXAMINED AND MAY BE REMOVED. YOU WILL RECEIVE OFFICIAL NOTICE TOMORROW. WHATEVER CANNOT BE FOUND, MUST NOT BE HERE FRIDAY NIGHT. — A FRIEND*

He read it five times. *A friend.* It was presumptuous, maybe. It was also true in a way that frightened him, and he left it, because a lie would have been easier to write and harder to mean.

Then he considered the piano.

This was the detail that had been sitting in the back of his skull for two days without announcing itself, the way important things do. During the preliminary survey — the walkthrough Voss had ordered last week to plan the search — Brandt had noted the contents of the workshop room by room, as instructed. Presses. Type cases. Worktables. A cot in the back room. Shelves. And in the front room, pushed against the wall under the window, an upright piano with a broken keyboard lid hanging open like a slack jaw, three keys missing, the wood scarred. Voss had glanced at it and said, "Furniture," and moved on, and Brandt had written *piano (broken)* in his survey notes because that was his job, and then he had stood there one moment longer than his job required, looking at it, because something about the instrument bothered him in a way he couldn't articulate. Broken pianos got abandoned. Nobody moved a broken piano twice. Nobody kept a broken piano in a working building unless the keeping of it mattered more than its function — unless it was being kept *because* it was broken, because broken things invited no questions, because a piano was furniture and furniture was invisible.

He was not certain. He wanted to be honest with himself about that: he was not certain there was anything inside that piano but felt hammers and dust. But certainty was a luxury the moment didn't offer, and so he added a line to the note, hesitated, struck it out, wrote it again:

*THE PIANO IS NOT SAFE. IT IS ON THE INVENTORY.*

Which was, technically, a lie — the piano was on his survey notes but not yet on any official inventory — and he accepted the lie the way a man accepts a debt, knowing he'd pay it.

* * *

Friday came, and with it the official notice, delivered by Corporal Ehlers at ten in the morning: inspection of premises, Saturday, zero eight hundred, per authority of the requisition order. Brandt watched from across the street as Ehlers handed the paper to the girl — Marta, the typesetter's daughter, sixteen years old and built out of wire and attention. She read it without expression, thanked him with a courtesy so exact it was nearly insolent, and closed the door.

Then Brandt waited for the day to end, which was the longest wait of his life compressed into ordinary hours: roster checks, a supply dispute, lunch he tasted nothing of, the afternoon drill he supervised on autopilot while his mind ran the same loop — *the note is under the door, they have read the note, they know* — followed always by its twin — *unless the wind took it, unless a boot kicked it aside, unless the girl swept the step at seven and never looked down.*

At dusk he walked past. The door was shut. He could see nothing, prove nothing, and the not-knowing sat in his stomach like swallowed ice.

At nine, he made himself walk again, telling himself it was routine surveillance, telling himself the story well enough that a younger version of himself might have believed it. The street was empty. Snow squeaked under his boots. And as he came level with the workshop he saw it: the ground-floor lamps burning, and shadows moving — not the bent, table-bound shapes of copying work but figures crossing the room quickly, repeatedly, carrying things. At one point the silhouette of the tall woman appeared at the window, and beside her the shape of the piano, tilted — actually tilted, he saw, tipped forward onto its face by two sets of arms, and then righted, and he could not see what they did behind it and did not need to.

They had read the note.

He stood in the snow across the street with his heart going like a drummer boy's and watched the shadow-play of people dismantling their own safety net an inch at a time, and he felt — not heroic. That was the thing no one told you. He had imagined, in the four days since writing the note, some sensation of vindication, some clean warmth. What he felt instead was fear, plain and total, and underneath it a strange fierce clarity, as though he had been looking at his life through fogged glass for thirty-one years and someone had finally wiped a circle in it. Whatever was in that piano — and he would know by this time tomorrow, because he would be the one standing beside it with the inventory sheet — whatever happened Saturday, there was no version of himself anymore that went back through the drawer, filed the folder, saluted, and slept.

He turned for home. Behind him the workshop lamps burned on into the small hours.

And at the edge of the street, where the lamplight gave out, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step beside him — quick, light steps, a young voice pitched low under the wind, and words that stopped him where he stood:

"Sergeant. My name is Marta. We need to talk about what you're going to find tomorrow."
