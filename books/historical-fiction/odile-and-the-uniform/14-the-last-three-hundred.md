## 14. The Last Three Hundred

By the fourth night, my hands had stopped being hands.

They had become instruments—two cramped, ink-stained machines that moved a nib across paper in small, obedient strokes while the rest of me drifted somewhere above the cellar lamp, watching from a distance, half asleep and fully awake at once. I knew this state from long night drives: the body running on rails while the mind floats loose. But never like this. Never with four hundred names depending on the steadiness of those rails.

The ledger lay open before me on two boards laid across flour barrels. To its left, the copybook Marta had bought from the stationer in her dead father's name—the stationer hadn't blinked; grief buys anything in this town now—and to its right, the finished pages, squared and stacked under a stone. Two hundred and sixty-one names copied. One hundred and thirty-nine to go.

I dipped the pen. Wrote. *Katarina Voss, seamstress. Elsbeth Renn, widow.* Dipped again.

"You're smudging," Marta said from the foot of the stairs, where she stood with a lantern in one hand and three women behind her like a tide coming in.

"I'm not smudging."

"Your left elbow is asleep and your letters are leaning east." She set the lantern down and came to look over my shoulder with the merciless eye of a typesetter's daughter. "That's a Voss who looks like a Voss who's been drinking."

"Marta."

"She's right," said one of the women behind her, and then they all came down into the cellar light, and I saw what Marta had done while Odile and I bent over our copying like monks.

She had recruited.

* * *

Their names were Frau Halden, who kept the bakery on Kessel Street and whose arms were as thick as my thigh; Anka Weiss, a schoolteacher of perhaps fifty with spectacles that caught the lamplight like two coins; and a girl only a few years older than Marta named Lisl, who worked in the dye works and whose fingers were permanently stained the blue of deep water. They stood in the damp cellar among the barrels and the smell of lamp oil and looked at the ledger with expressions I recognized, because I had worn them myself a year ago: the look of people discovering that a thing they'd only whispered about was real, and written down, and theirs.

"They'll each carry," Marta said. "Different days, different roads. Frau Halden delivers bread as far as Halbern twice a week. Fräulein Weiss visits her sister in Corvany. Lisl has cousins up the river valley." She turned to them. "You've seen what goes in the hems?"

"We've seen," Frau Halden said, and there was something in her voice—not eagerness, exactly. Resolve with the fear boiled out of it, the way you boil salt out of seawater and are left with something drinkable. "My husband thinks I'm at prayer circle. Let him. He prays too, just quieter."

Anka Weiss took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, and put them back on. "I taught thirty years of children to write their names properly," she said. "It seems fitting I should carry some."

Lisl said nothing. She just looked at the ledger a long time, and I watched her lips move slightly, reading, and I understood that for her this was not about routes or hems or courage. It was about seeing, with her own eyes, that other women had signed. That she was not alone in wanting a life larger than a dye vat.

Marta began the training right there among the barrels. She showed them how the strips were folded—lengthwise twice, then rolled around a pencil shaft no thicker than a knitting needle, then sewn flat into a hem by Odile's own hand, so that even if a coat were turned inside out and shaken, nothing would show but stitching. She showed them how to walk through a checkpoint: not fast, not slow, not looking at the soldiers, not avoiding looking at them. She made Frau Halden practice carrying a basket with a false bottom while answering questions about flour prices.

"And if they search you anyway?" Anka asked.

"Then it's in God's hands," Frau Halden said.

"No," Marta said. "Then it's in your mouth. You smile, you complain about the weather, you let them feel clever. A soldier who feels clever doesn't report finding nothing."

I sat back and listened to this sixteen-year-old drill three grown women in the art of deceiving an army, and I thought: the colonel should be afraid of us. He is afraid of paper. He should be terrified of Marta Brenner.

When they'd gone—Frau Halden first, with forty names sewn into the hem of her good coat, due on the Halbern road Thursday—Marta stayed behind and stood over my copybook.

"How many left?"

"One hundred and thirty-nine."

"At your speed?" She did arithmetic on her fingers, which annoyed me because her answer matched mine. "Four nights. Maybe five, if your hand gives out. The inspection could come any day. Brandt's note said within the week—that was six days ago."

"I know the arithmetic, Marta."

"Then stop doing it alone."

* * *

She didn't mean what I thought she meant. Or maybe she meant more than I thought.

The next evening, when I came down to the cellar after delivering Odile to a meeting across town—a meeting that was really just tea with two aldermen's wives, at which Odile had extracted, by charm or blackmail, a promise of legal counsel should anyone be arrested—I found the second chair occupied.

Odile Marchetti sat at the barrel-table with a copybook of her own, sleeves pinned to the elbow, hair tied back with a ribbon that had seen better decades, writing in a hand far finer than mine. She did not look up. "You're staring, Ivo."

"You're copying."

"I am copying."

"You've never—" I stopped, because I couldn't say it without saying too much: *you've never touched the work. You speak, we carry. That's been the order of things since the day I drove you.* "You have people for this."

"People get arrested," she said mildly. "People get searched. People get their mail read." Now she looked up, and there was lamplight in her eyes and something underneath the lamplight. "A signature can't be confiscated from my memory, Ivo. If they take every page, every copy, every hem and hymnal, I still carry four hundred names in here." She tapped her temple once, precisely, like a typesetter setting a single letter. "But ink on paper is faster than memory. So tonight I am ink."

She pushed a second copybook toward the empty place across from her. Not an invitation. An assignment.

I sat. We wrote. And somewhere in the first hour, without either of us deciding it, we fell into rhythm—she'd read a name aloud in a low voice, almost a chant, and I'd find it in the ledger and confirm the district, and we'd both write it, and the scratch of two pens filled the cellar like rain on a roof.

Around midnight she reached a page near the middle of the ledger and went quiet.

I glanced over. Her pen had stopped. She was reading the same line twice.

"Who is it?" I asked, though I already knew, because I had copied these names weeks ago and remembered the ones that made my own pen hesitate.

"Hedwig Sattler." Odile's voice was perfectly level, which is how I knew it cost her something. "And below her, Greta Sattler. And below that, Ingrid Sattler-Fey." She laid the pen down and pressed her fingertips against her eyes. "Wife and daughters of Alderman Sattler. The man who called us 'a contagion of discontented women' at the council meeting in March. Who proposed the ordinance banning our meetings from public halls. Whose wife and daughters walked past his study door one evening and signed their names in my ledger while he dined with the mayor."

I said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, so I did the only thing I could think of: I dipped my pen and waited.

"He doesn't know," she said. "Or he knows and says nothing. Either way, when the list is printed—if it's printed—he will read his own household in the columns and understand that his ordinance protected nothing, stopped nothing, because the thing he tried to ban was never in the halls. It was at his own dinner table." She picked the pen up again. Her hand had steadied. "Copy them well, Ivo. Hedwig Sattler risked more signing that name than any woman in this book. She risked it inside her own front door."

We copied the Sattlers side by side, three lines each, and I felt something shift in the cellar that I had no vocabulary for then and have only partly found since. It wasn't gratitude, exactly, and it wasn't equality—it was simpler and stranger than both. It was the feeling of two people doing the same small labor in the same small light, and discovering that the labor itself was the conversation.

* * *

The days blurred after that. I stopped measuring time in hours and started measuring it in signatures: ninety-eight remaining, then seventy-one, then fifty-three. Frau Halden came back from Halbern with a grin she couldn't suppress and news that the baker's widow there had wept upon receiving her envelope and immediately asked for two more to send to her daughters. Anka Weiss carried nineteen names to Corvany in the spine of a family Bible, sewn in beneath the binding where only a determined vandal would look. Lisl went upriver with sixty, distributed across three garments and a hymnal, and came back reporting that the sergeant at the river checkpoint had searched her bag, found nothing but laundry, and apologized.

"Apologized," Marta repeated that night, delighted. "He *apologized*. Write that down somewhere. When we win, that gets a monument."

"There's no monument for apologies," Odile said, but she was smiling, tiredly, over her copybook.

The workshop above us grew stranger by the day. With Brandt's warning hanging over us like weather, Odile had ordered the presses partially dismantled—not hidden, just visibly broken down, type cases stacked, rollers wrapped—as if the shop were winding down business ahead of the requisition, which was, after all, the respectable story. Soldiers passed the windows and saw a dying print shop. Below their feet, in a cellar that smelled of flour and lamp oil, four hundred names were being smuggled out of the country of the Colonel's authority one hem at a time.

On the eighth night my right hand seized.

Not cramped—seized, the fingers curling inward like a leaf in frost, the pen dropping from them and rolling off the board. I swore, quietly, the way you do when you're too tired for volume, and shook the hand out, and flexed it, and picked the pen up, and the pen simply fell again.

"That's enough," Odile said.

"It's not enough. There are thirty-one names left on this page and the whole back section—"

"*Enough.*" She said it the way she said it at meetings, the voice that made aldermen sit down. Then, gentler: "Your hands are the only pair that knows the routes, Ivo. If I break yours, we lose the driver, and a driver with a ledger and no carriage is just a man holding evidence." She rose, went up the stairs, and came back down with the bottle of plum brandy that Herr Brenner had kept under the counter for medicinal purposes and that Marta had guarded like a relic since the accident. She poured a measure into a cup and set it before me. "Drink. Flex. Ten minutes. Then we trade."

"Trade?"

"Your hand copies. Mine reads aloud. You'll go faster listening than hunting through the ledger anyway." She settled into the chair beside me instead of across, close enough that our elbows nearly touched on the narrow board. "Begin. *Anna-Lena Fischer, laundress, Mill District.*"

I wrote. My hand obeyed her voice the way a horse obeys a rein, and I understood dimly, through the fog of exhaustion, that this was the trick of it—her voice carrying the weight my hand no longer could. *Margarete Oster, charwoman. Sofia Brandl, teacher.* The names went down onto the page like stitches closing a wound.

Somewhere past two in the morning, between *Teresa Falk* and *Ilse Grunwald*, I heard Odile's voice catch. Just once. A hitch, small as a skipped stitch.

I looked up. She was reading the ledger with her finger under the line, and her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them, and she did not stop reading. "*Ilse Grunwald,*" she said again, steady now, "*laundress, Mill District,*" and I wrote it, and neither of us mentioned the catch, and that was its own kind of tenderness—the kind that keeps working.

Near dawn, Marta came down with bread and hard cheese and stood watching us for a moment from the stairs, lantern in hand, and I saw her take in the scene: Odile Marchetti, who had spoken to crowds of two thousand, reading names aloud in a flour cellar to keep a driver's broken hand moving; the driver, half-asleep, writing like a man praying. Whatever she saw, she decided not to interrupt it. She set the food down without a sound and went back up.

We finished the back section at sunrise. All of it. Every name.

Except forty.

* * *

The last forty were the problem, and the problem was arithmetic again, and this time none of us could argue with it.

Forty names required forty envelopes' worth of copying, folding, sewing, and dispatching—and we had run out of carriers. Frau Halden had made her last run; the army had begun stopping carts on the Kessel road for routine inspection, and sending her again would be spending her life like coin. Anka Weiss was away in Corvany, due back in three days, if the trains still ran on schedule, which lately they did not. Lisl's route was burned; the dye works had been visited by a lieutenant asking questions about absentee employees, and Lisl's uncle had forbidden her the road, and Lisl had obeyed him, furiously, for the first time in her life.

"So we hold the last forty," Odile said, "until Anka returns."

"And if the inspection comes first?" Marta asked. "If they come tomorrow? If they come tonight? Forty names stay in this building and burn with it, and everything—" her voice cracked, just barely, and she hated it, I could see her hate it— "everything we sent out means less, because the record isn't whole. You said all names or none. That was your rule."

"My rule," Odile agreed quietly.

"Then we can't wait for Anka."

I looked at the ledger. At the forty unmarked lines waiting in its final pages like seats left empty. And I heard myself say the thing I had been not-saying for eight days:

"The car."

They turned to me.

"The requisition orders all private vehicles out of the eastern quarter by the deadline. My papers let me drive through the checkpoints—they've been verified, stamped, returned." I tapped the leather folder that lived in my breast pocket, the one Sergeant Brandt had handled and handed back, the one that made me, in the army's ledgers, a man of no consequence whatsoever. "Nobody searches the driver anymore. They search the car, sometimes. They never search the man." I looked down at my ruined, bandaged-adjacent, brandied hand. "And a man can carry forty slips of paper sewn into his coat lining without changing his silhouette."

"You'd do a night run," Odile said. "In the dark. Past three checkpoints. Alone."

"I've driven alone at night my whole adult life, madam. It's the only way I know how to exist."

Something crossed her face—not worry, or not only worry. Recognition. The look she'd given me the night she first showed me the ledger, the conspirator's look, except deeper now, worn smooth by eight days of shared labor. "You'll need the route planned to the quarter-hour. Marta—"

"Already planning it," Marta said. She had chalk in her hand. Of course she had chalk in her hand.

But Odile caught my wrist as I turned to go—gently, mindful of the hand—and held it a moment longer than instruction required.

"Ivo. The ledger stays with you on the run. Not the copy. The ledger itself." Before I could object, she went on, fast and low: "If they take the building while we sleep, the copies in the hems are scattered and safe, but the master dies with it. Four hundred original signatures. The one true thing." Her grip tightened. "You are the courier. Be the courier all the way."

Outside the cellar window, high above, the eastern sky had gone the grey of dishwater, and somewhere along the ring road I could hear the army's lorries starting up for morning drills, engines coughing in the cold like old men clearing their throats.

Three days, I thought. Maybe less. Maybe the lorries turn toward Kessel Street tonight.

I took the ledger off the boards and buttoned it inside my coat, against my chest, where I could feel it with every breath.
