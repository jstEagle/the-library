## 15. Inspection Day

They came at seven in the morning, while the frost still silvered the windows and the kettle had only just begun its low complaint on the stove.

Odile knew the sound before she understood it: boots, many boots, arriving not with the fanfare of trumpets or shouted orders but with the terrible quietness of men who had done this before and would do it again. She was standing at the worktable with a cup of tea she would never drink, watching the steam curl, when Sergeant Brandt's shadow crossed the frosted glass of the door—followed by six more shadows, then ten.

"They're here," she said to the empty room, though of course it wasn't empty. Marta froze at the typesetting case, a line of fourteen-point type trembling between her fingers. Old Vasel looked up from the folding machine. In the cellar below, faint as a heartbeat, Ivo's pen was still moving across its page.

"Put the kettle on," Odile said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "If they've come to search us, let them find women having breakfast."

The door opened without a knock. A lieutenant entered first—young, pink-cheeked, his gloves so new they creaked—and behind him soldiers spilled into the workshop like water finding every low place in a floor. They filled the corners. They filled the doorway. One of them, a boy who could not have been nineteen, stood with his rifle held against his chest the way a child holds a loaf of bread someone has given him and he isn't sure is his to keep.

"Lieutenant Hessler," said the young officer, producing papers with a flourish that suggested he had practiced the gesture before a mirror. "By order of the regional command, these premises are subject to immediate inspection for seditious materials. You are required to stand aside. You are permitted to observe. You are not permitted to interfere."

"Good morning, Lieutenant," Odile said. "Would you like tea?"

Hessler blinked, as men always did when offered courtesy where they expected fear. It was a small weapon and she had honed it for twenty years: politeness as a wall, courtesy as a locked door. "That will not be necessary."

"As you like. Marta, fetch another cup anyway. The sergeant looks cold."

She did not look at Brandt. That was the discipline of it—she had learned long ago, in the years after the fire that took her sister, that the most dangerous moment in any confrontation was the one in which you acknowledged your allies. She poured tea into cups and set them on the worktable among the galleys of type and the ink-stained rags, and she watched Lieutenant Hessler decide how to be offended.

He decided on briskness. "Begin," he said, and the workshop became an instrument being taken apart by hands that did not love it.

* * *

They started with the presses.

Two soldiers lifted the platen press's heavy iron frame apart with the ease of men trained to dismantle things, unbolted from its bed, laid out on the floorboards like the skeleton of some great animal. Vasel made a sound low in his throat—not quite a word, not quite a cry—and Odile put her hand on his arm. The press was older than Marta. It had printed funeral notices and wedding invitations and, for eleven months now, four hundred names copied onto thin paper in ink mixed thin enough to stretch.

"You'll want to reseat the bolts afterward," she told the soldiers pleasantly. "The frame settles if you don't."

One of them actually nodded. Hessler shot the man a look that would have curdled milk.

They moved through the shop with methodical thoroughness. Drawers opened and emptied onto tables—lead type scattered, sorted through, kicked aside. The paper stock was counted and recounted. A soldier pried open the ink cabinet and ran his fingers along the shelves, checking for hollow spaces. Another pulled down the curtains, held them to the window light, felt along their hems.

Marta stood beside Odile with her arms folded, chin up, sixteen years old and made entirely of iron. But Odile saw her eyes track the soldiers' hands the way a mother tracks strangers' hands near a cradle. Somewhere below them—Ivo had gone down into the cellar at the first sound of boots, taking the last uncopied pages with him—there were forty-three names that existed nowhere else in the world except under his pen and inside his memory.

Above them, directly above Ivo's bent head, a soldier stamped his boot on the floorboards testing for hollows, and Odile's heart performed a maneuver she would later be unable to name.

"Madame Marchetti." Hessler had picked up a galley proof from the table, holding it at arm's length as though it might bite. "You print... pamphlets."

"We print announcements. Meeting notices. A calendar, once, for the church bazaar." All true. All useless. The pamphlets—the real ones, the ones with teeth—had been distributed weeks ago or hidden in the false bottom of Vasel's toolbox, which was currently sitting in plain sight because the best hiding place in the world is the thing no one thinks needs hiding.

"Seditious literature," Hessler said. "Calls for civil disorder. Demands addressed to the government in the names of—" he paused, and Odile watched him decide whether to say the word— "*women.*"

"In my experience, Lieutenant, women are part of the civil order. We pay taxes. We bury our husbands. Some of us even read."

A muscle worked in Hessler's jaw. Behind him, one of the soldiers—the boy with the rifle—made a small sound that might have been a swallowed laugh, and turned it into a cough.

"Floorboards," Hessler said abruptly. "Pry up the floorboards."

* * *

They began at the north wall and worked toward the cellar stairs, crowbars singing against nail heads, boards coming up pale and raw-backed from the dark beneath. Dust rose in the winter light like something alive. Each board they lifted revealed only earth, old joists, a dead mouse curled like a comma. Odile stood very still and counted her breathing the way she had once counted the bells after the factory fire—four in, four out—while soldiers dismantled her life board by board.

Under the seventh board they found nothing. Under the twelfth, nothing. Under the nineteenth, a soldier cried *"Here!"*—and it was only a tin of buttons, spilled rolling across the floor, ordinary mother-of-pearl, and Marta laughed, one sharp note she strangled immediately, and Hessler glared at her with such fury that Odile stepped between them without deciding to.

"My late husband's coat," she said. "He saved buttons the way other men save money. Sentimental. Are we finished with the floor, Lieutenant, or shall we excavate to the water table?"

Hessler straightened, dusted his gloves, and made his decision visible: he would not be provoked by a woman today. "The furniture," he said. "Search the furniture."

And there it was. Odile had known it would come to this since the first boot touched her doorstep, and she had spent the night before lying awake in the dark rehearsing this exact moment, turning it over the way you turn over a stone to see what lives underneath.

Against the east wall, pushed into the corner where it had stood for three years, sat the piano.

It had come with the building—an upright, walnut-veneered, its keys yellowed and five of them dead. Vasel had wanted to sell it for firewood. Marta had defended it on sentimental grounds neither of them believed. And eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday Odile remembered better than her own birthday, Ivo Quintana had stood beside it with his head tilted and said, *This back panel comes off, and there is room behind it for a ledger, and no one in the history of armies has ever searched a piano,* and Odile had looked at him—that quiet, watchful man who read her letters over her shoulder and pretended not to—and thought: *he has been paying attention to everything.*

So they had done it. The master ledger—all four hundred signatures, the original book, the one document in the world that mattered—slid flat behind the piano's sounding board, wrapped in oilcloth, resting in the dust of thirty years of forgotten hymns. It had sat there through two previous inspections, through the requisition notice, through the colonel's velvet threats. It sat there now, four feet from Lieutenant Hessler's elbow, while he ordered his men to search the furniture.

A soldier tried the keyboard. Five notes rang out, sour and dying. He played a stumbling scale, shrugged, moved on. Another checked under the lid, felt inside the bench, tipped it over.

"The back panel," Odile said, and her voice was silk stretched over wire. "It's loose. It rattles when the cart goes past. If you're searching properly—"

Every eye in the room turned to her. Marta's breath caught audibly. Down in the cellar, Odile imagined, Ivo had stopped writing altogether.

Hessler studied her. For a long moment the only sounds were the kettle finally boiling and the frost melting off the windows in slow ticks.

"Why would you tell us that?" he said.

"Because if you don't search it, you'll write in your report that you did, and I'd rather you didn't perjure yourself on my account. I have a reputation for honesty. It's the only reputation worth having."

It was a gamble dressed as candor, candor dressed as a dare, and she watched it travel through the young officer's mind like a stone sinking through different temperatures of water. Men like Hessler did not trust honesty; they suspected traps. And a trap was exactly what he needed to believe this was—if the piano held nothing, then she had wasted his time, humiliated him, made him the fool who tore apart a broken instrument on a suffragist's suggestion. His career flickered behind his eyes. His lieutenant's opinion. His colonel's.

"Check it," he said shortly.

The soldier pried at the panel. It gave with a crack of old glue—and Odile's heart went with it, all the way down—and the gap behind the sounding board yawned open, three inches of darkness and dust.

Empty. Empty but for a single dried rose petal, left there by some tenant thirty years dead, which the soldier held up to the light, puzzled, before letting it fall.

"Nothing, sir."

"Reattach it." Hessler turned away, and Odile allowed herself one breath, deep and slow, that no one marked because everyone in the room was breathing again at the same time.

She did not look at the corner by the east wall. She did not look at Sergeant Brandt, who had stood near the door for two hours saying nothing, whose face had been a closed ledger itself. She poured herself the cup of tea she had never drunk and held it in both hands and watched the soldiers reassemble her workshop badly.

* * *

It took them until nearly noon. When it was done, the workshop looked like a room that had survived a modest storm: presses rebolted crooked, type unsorted, floorboards nailed down with bright new heads like scars. Hessler produced his report form and wrote standing at the worktable, narrating as he went in the tone of a man reading his own obituary aloud:

"No seditious materials recovered. Premises inspected in full. Furniture examined. Floor examined. Presses examined." He signed it, blotted it, and fixed Odile with a look meant to be the last word. "You understand, madame, that we may return."

"You know where I live," Odile said. "Everyone in this town knows where I live. That has rather been my problem all along."

Something flickered across Hessler's face—not respect, she thought, but the exhausted recognition of an opponent he could not categorize. He gathered his men. At the door he paused, half-turned, and for one absurd second Odile thought he was going to ask about the tea.

Then they were gone, boots receding down the frozen street, and the workshop settled around them like a bird returning to a branch.

Marta slid down the wall until she was sitting on the floor, laughing and crying in the same breath. Vasel righted the bench with hands that shook. From the cellar stairs, slowly, Ivo emerged with the last pages in his fist and ink on his cheekbone, looking like a man surfacing from deep water.

Odile set down her tea. She walked to the piano, pried off the back panel with her own fingers, and drew out the oilcloth bundle. Four hundred names. Her sister's memory. Every woman who had ever dared put her name to paper knowing what it might cost. She held it against her chest and felt it beating.

"Ivo," she said. "Tonight it leaves this building. Tonight we begin again."

But it was Brandt she caught at the door—or rather, it was Brandt who had lingered, the last soldier on the step, and it was Brandt who said, quietly, without turning fully around, words she would turn over for days afterward:

"The petal, madame. Someone dried that rose a long time ago and left it where no one would find it. Seems to me some things stay safe precisely because everyone believes they're already lost."

Then he walked away down the street, shoulders square, a soldier to the last, and Odile stood in her ruined doorway with the ledger warm against her heart, understanding suddenly that luck had not been luck at all—

—and that somewhere in the ranks of the army that intended to burn her building to the ground, one man had just paid for their lives out of his own.
