## 17. Evacuation Eve

The order came at four o'clock in the afternoon, nailed to the workshop door by a boy soldier who couldn't have been older than Marta and wouldn't look at us while he hammered.

*By order of the District Commandant: all persons and property to be removed from premises no later than six o'clock tomorrow morning. Demolition crews will arrive at dawn. Non-compliance will be treated as obstruction of military operations.*

I read it twice, standing on the step with the wind pulling at my coat, and the strange thing—the thing I still can't explain—was that my first feeling wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. I stood there doing sums like a shopkeeper: three hundred sixty names copied and dispatched, forty remaining, one night, four hands, two pens, one lamp's worth of oil.

The clock would not negotiate. It never had. But for the first time since this began I understood that we had stopped negotiating with it too.

I carried the notice inside and laid it on the composing table where Odile was sealing envelopes by lamplight. She read it without touching it, the way she read everything—eyes only, hands busy—and then she went back to her sealing wax.

"Six o'clock," she said.

"Dawn," I said. "Crews arrive at dawn."

"Then we have until dawn." She tilted an envelope against the candle flame and watched the wax pool red. "Dawn is generous. In my sister's factory they gave the women nothing at all."

She said it plainly, without performance, the way she always spoke of Lucia now that the story had been told once and could not be taken back. That was the change the winter had worked in us, I think—we had spent our secrets the way we'd spent our ink, freely, and there was nothing left to hoard.

Marta looked up from the far bench, where she sat with a pen in each hand because she'd learned to write with either. "How many left?"

"Forty," I said. "Thirty-eight if the last post run made the evening collection."

"Then thirty-eight or forty." She flexed her fingers. "It's the same night either way."

* * *

By seven the workshop had been stripped to its bones. The presses had gone north two days before, disassembled and crated under false manifests, riding in a furniture van Odile had hired from a cousin of a cousin. The type cases were buried in the cellar under coal sacks. What remained was paper, lamp oil, candle stubs, the broken piano that had already survived one inspection by being beneath suspicion, and the ledger itself, which lay open on the composing table like a patient on an operating table, waiting for the last incisions.

We worked in shifts of the body but not of the spirit. Odile wrote names in a hand so fine and upright it looked typeset; Marta copied in her quick slanted scrawl, faster than any of us, faster even than her grief; and I did what I had always done—I handled the paper. I trimmed, folded, sealed, addressed, sorted by route and region, and stacked the finished envelopes into bundles tied with string, each bundle a small bomb of truth waiting to be carried out of the occupied quarter before morning.

At nine o'clock I went out to check the car. It was parked in the alley behind the workshop, and I stood beside it in the cold and listened to the town. The eastern quarter had been emptying for weeks—a family here, a shopkeeper there, carts rolling west with mattresses lashed down and children walking behind holding the corners of tables—but tonight the emptiness had a different sound. It sounded like held breath. Somewhere a dog barked and stopped. Somewhere a shutter banged and nobody closed it. The army's lamps burned along the depot perimeter like a string of yellow teeth, and beyond them the skeleton of the first munitions shed rose black against the stars, growing by the day, fed by the requisition ledger whose twin I had stolen and whose names I had spent eleven months learning by heart.

I knew them all now. Not as a list—as people. Frau Hessler, whose husband beat her and who signed anyway. The two Kessler sisters, dismissed from the mill within a week of signing. Anna Brandt.

That last name still caught in me when it surfaced, which it did less often now, but it surfaced tonight, standing in the cold, because tomorrow morning everything in that building except what we carried out would be ash, and somewhere in a cell across town Sergeant Brandt was counting his own hours, and his mother's envelope—the one Odile had insisted on, the one addressed in my hand—sat in bundle three, bound for a village I could reach in ninety minutes if the roads were clear.

They were clear. That was the one mercy the night offered, and I meant to take it.

* * *

At ten, Odile called a halt—not to rest, but to count.

She made us do it formally, standing around the table like clerks at an audit, because she believed that panic lived in vagueness and died in numbers. Marta counted the finished copies. I counted the dispatched bundles, listed route by route in my head, which was safer than on paper. Odile counted what remained.

"Forty-one," she said at last.

I felt something drop through me. "There were forty this afternoon."

"There were forty this afternoon, and then I remembered Frau Vogel, who signed in March and moved to her daughter's in April, and whose entry is in the appendix, page forty-seven, because I filed her late." Odile turned to page forty-seven and set her finger below the name without shame. "One more. Forty-one."

"It changes nothing," Marta said. "It's one hour of work."

"One hour of work," Odile agreed, "that we do not have, unless someone has invented a way to make the night longer."

Nobody laughed. But something passed around the table anyway—not laughter, exactly; more like the look soldiers must give each other in trenches, the look that says the situation is absurd and we are in it together and absurdity shared is a kind of armor. Marta picked up her pen. I picked up mine. Odile turned to a fresh sheet.

And then she did the thing I think about still, years later, when people ask me what kind of woman she was. She took the appendix page itself—the original, the last uncopied leaf of the master record—and she tore it cleanly along its margin, separating Frau Vogel's line from the rest, and handed the strip of paper to me.

"You copy her," she said. "I'll take the next five. If the worst comes, one of us should be carrying her name out of this building in our own handwriting, not hers. Let the record show we were willing to be responsible for it."

I took the strip. My hands were steady. Eleven months ago they would not have been.

* * *

Midnight came, and with it the cold that gets into stone. We lit the second lamp and then the third, and the workshop glowed like a lantern in a dead quarter of the town, and I remember thinking: let them see it. Let every spy and sergeant and colonel see four lights burning in a building they mean to burn themselves, and wonder what we're writing.

At half past twelve, boots in the alley. Two pairs. I put my hand over the ledger and blew out nothing, because blowing out the lamps would announce guilt louder than leaving them lit, and Odile caught my eye and shook her head once—*keep writing*—and kept writing herself, pen moving, spine straight, a woman copying names in her own workshop at midnight as though midnight were a perfectly reasonable hour for it.

The knock came anyway. Of course it did.

I opened the door on a lieutenant I didn't know and a private I did—one of the depot garrison, young, chapped-lipped, miserable. The lieutenant was polite the way winter is polite. He informed me that the building was to be cleared by six, that lingering civilians would be detained, and that the district commandant wished to avoid, in his words, "an incident."

"We're packing," I said. "My employer's papers. Household effects. We'll be gone by five."

He looked past me into the light—at Odile writing, at Marta writing, at the envelopes in their neat string-bound stacks—and I watched him try to make sense of it. Three people, uprooted from their home, spending their last night in it writing letters. There was nothing in the scene for him to arrest. Curiosity is not contraband. Grief is not sedition.

"The piano," he said finally. "That goes too. Nothing remains. Nothing at all."

"It's going to the scrap dealer at first light," I said, which was true, in the sense that I intended it to be true, in the sense that a broken piano full of nothing but its own broken strings would sit in plain sight until dawn and fool him the way it had fooled the search party in November, because men do not suspect furniture, and because luck—or whatever you call the accumulated silence of strangers—had decided, for one more night, to keep working on our side.

He touched his cap and left. The boots receded. Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Odile said, quietly, "Page forty-eight," and we bent back to work.

* * *

The hours after that blur, the way hours do when the body runs on purpose instead of sleep. I know the sequence of things if not their times: the kettle boiled twice and forgotten both times; Marta's pen splitting and her switching hands without comment; Odile standing once, briefly, at the window, looking north toward the hills where the presses were already safe, and saying to no one, "Lucia, look at this," and sitting back down. I know that at some point my handwriting degraded until my 4s looked like 9s and I had to slow down and discipline myself letter by letter, because a misread name in an archive is a small death, a person erased by haste, and we had not come this far to erase anyone ourselves.

I know that around three in the morning the forty-one became twenty-six, and the twenty-six became fourteen, and the strings on the bundles multiplied until my pockets sagged and the spare tire well behind the car's rear seat was full and I had begun sewing the overflow into the lining of my greatcoat with Marta's needle, badly, pricking my thumb, not caring.

And I know that at half past four, with eight names left, Odile set down her pen and said, "Enough."

Marta looked up, stricken. "Eight left."

"Eight left," Odile agreed, "and dawn coming, and one driver, and one car, and one road north. We finish them at the farmhouse. Ivo carries the ledger. We carry the rest." She was already moving, snuffing lamps, sweeping the last loose papers into her satchel, and her voice had the flat calm I had learned to recognize as its most dangerous register—the voice of a woman who had done the arithmetic and found that the clock had won this round and intended, calmly, to cheat.

"I can be back by six," I said. "Copy the last eight here—"

"No." She said it without heat. "You heard the lieutenant. Nothing remains. And Ivo—" she paused, and in the lamplight I saw how tired she was, tired in the marrow, tired the way only people who have fought institutions get tired, "—nothing remains means the ledger doesn't remain either. It leaves with us, tonight, in your hands, or it burns at dawn with everyone else's history inside it. Which would you rather explain to Frau Hessler?"

There was no answer to that. There never had been.

* * *

We loaded the car in the blue-black hour before dawn, moving fast and quiet, our breath smoking. Envelopes under the spare tire. Bundles sewn into coats. The ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped flat beneath the driver's seat, where my body would sit above it like a lid. The broken piano stayed in the corner, hollowed of everything but dust and one decoy folder of old meeting minutes that Odile had prepared weeks ago for exactly this moment—chaff for the fire.

Last of all, Odile stood in the middle of the empty workshop and looked at it. At the scarred composing table. At the hooks where the pamphlets used to hang to dry. At the floorboard near the cellar stair that Marta's father had replaced the summer before the accident, the wood still paler than the boards around it, a seam of newer grain in the old floor, the last repair of a dead man's hands.

"Five generations of printers in this room," she said. "Mine are the fifth. Whatever they build on top of us, the ground remembers."

Marta put her arm through her mother's—no one had ever said aloud that Odile was the closest thing Marta had left to family, and no one needed to—and I went out to crank the engine, and the cold bit my hands, and the motor coughed and caught.

I was standing at the running board, ready to help Odile up, when she stopped. She had turned halfway back toward the door, and the sky behind the eastern sheds was just beginning to go grey, and she said:

"The last page. Page forty-seven. Did you finish Frau Vogel?"

I had. It was in my breast pocket, in my own careful handwriting, folded once. I told her so.

"Good." She nodded, satisfied, and put her foot on the running board—

—and that is when we heard it: a sound from the direction of the rail yard, low and wrong, a sound I felt in my chest before I identified it, a deep concussive breath, followed by another, followed by a rising orange bloom over the rooftops of the eastern quarter where no furnace, no boiler, no legitimate dawn fire should be burning at that hour.

Odile's foot came off the board. Marta appeared in the doorway, her face lit orange.

The workshop behind her was dark, emptied, saved. But down the hill, between us and the road north, the night had turned the color of the end of something, and smoke was already climbing into the sky over a town that had thirty minutes yet before sunrise—and I understood, with a driver's certainty about distances, that the fire stood directly across our only road out.
