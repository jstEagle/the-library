## 18. Fire

The smell woke me before the sound.

I had been sleeping in the loft above the Kessler farmhouse with my boots on, the way I'd slept for six days now—ready to run, ready to copy, ready to do whatever the hour demanded. In my dream my father was at his case, sorting type by touch in the dark the way he could, each letter a small cold coin finding its slot. Then the dream filled with smoke, and I woke, and it took me three full seconds to understand that the smoke wasn't mine.

I went to the window. The eastern sky over the town was the wrong color—not the gray that comes before dawn but an orange that pulsed, deepened, pulsed again, like something breathing.

Below me, a door banged. Ivo's voice, low and urgent: "Odile. Odile, get up."

I took the stairs too fast and caught myself on the banister. In the kitchen, Ivo stood at the window with his coat already on, and Odile stood beside him very still, her hair loose down her back, her hands flat on the table as if she needed to hold the world to it.

"It's early," she said. Not a question. A statement of fact delivered to no one.

"They weren't due until tomorrow," Ivo said. "Demolition crews at dawn, that's what the notice said."

"That's not dawn." My own voice sounded strange to me, thin and far away. "Dawn is hours off. That's—" I stopped. We all looked at the window together, and none of us said the word.

Because there were two kinds of fire, and we all knew it. There was the fire that happened, and there was the fire that was arranged. The first kind sent up a single column of smoke, ragged, uncertain, climbing. The second kind glowed along its whole base, fed and tended, the way a foundry glows, the way a kiln glows—the way a building glows when men have gone through it first with lamp oil and opened every door between the floors so the air can walk through it like a bellows.

This fire breathed. This fire had been helped.

"The ledger," I said. "Where's the ledger?"

"Here." Ivo touched his breast pocket, then shook his head. "No—in my bag. By the door. I carried it out yesterday evening. All of it. Every page." He said it quickly, the way a man says things he has rehearsed, because he has been afraid someone would ask him exactly this question at exactly this moment and find the answer missing.

"You carried it out an hour before the army burned the building you were hiding it in," Odile said slowly, "and you didn't tell us why."

"I didn't know why." He turned from the window. His face was lit orange on one side. "I told myself it was the piano. They're going to haul the piano out for scrap, and if they move the piano they find the ledger, and if they find the ledger—" He passed a hand over his mouth. "I told myself a hundred reasons. But I'll tell you the truth of it. Last night I couldn't sleep, and I kept seeing Brandt's note under the door, and I kept thinking: a man paid for that warning. He's sitting in a cell right now because he wrote eleven words on a scrap of paper. And if everything he bought with those eleven words burned up in one careless morning, then what he paid was nothing. It had to be worth something. So I took the ledger out to the barn and put it in the feed box under the oats, and I sat with it for an hour, and then I came back inside, and I still couldn't sleep."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the orange light grew.

"We should go," I said. "We should go now, tonight, while—"

"No," Odile said.

And that was all she said, but I knew the tone. It was the tone she used when a petitioner apologized for taking her time. *You will take the time,* that tone said. *Your time is the point.*

"We are going to stand at this window," she said, "and we are going to watch them do it. Because in thirty years, when some official history says the workshop was destroyed by accident in the course of demolition, there will be four people alive who know better, and I refuse to let any of them look away."

* * *

We drove up the hill road in the dark with the lamps unlit, Ivo handling the car like a surgeon, stopping twice behind walls when a patrol passed below. From the bend where the road overlooks the eastern quarter, you can see the whole roofline of the print works, the chimney, the loading door where the paper wagons used to back in, the little dormer window of the room where I slept through the winter of my thirteenth year because Papa worked nights and Mama was dead and nobody thought to make rules about a girl sleeping above a press.

From that bend, at half past four in the morning, I watched my father's workshop burn.

I want to write down what it actually looked like, because everything anyone will ever say about it afterward will be wrong, and somebody should hold the truth even if only in her own skull. It did not look like the fires in engravings. There was no dramatic tower of flame at first. What there was, first, was light coming out of the windows—every window at once, all down the ground floor, a row of bright rectangles like the building had become a lantern, like it was lit from inside for a celebration. For perhaps a minute it was almost beautiful. I remember hating myself for thinking so. I remember thinking: that is the exact light that used to come out of those windows at night when Papa worked late, and he is not in there making it, and the light doesn't know.

Then the roof went. Not all at once—a section near the chimney sagged, held, sagged again, and the chimney stood alone for a while like a finger raised in the middle of the blaze, and Ivo made a sound next to me, small and involuntary, the sound of a man watching something he loved being unmade.

"What's burning?" Odile asked quietly. She wasn't asking about the building.

"Paper," I said. "Tons of it. The pamphlet stock, the letterheads, the—" My throat closed. "Papa's specimen books. Every font he ever cut, every proof he ever pulled. Forty years of type specimens, and the army is using them to heat the sky."

"There were presses in there," Ivo said. "Two Heidelbergs and the little hand press. Those don't burn easy. Cast iron doesn't burn." He stopped. We all understood at once what that meant: whatever was left at morning would tell its own story. Iron frames standing in ash, warped but whole, surrounded by the unburnable facts of a professional print shop—and around *them*, the suspiciously thorough burn of everything else. Any inspector with eyes would read that floor like a page.

Unless there was no inspector. Unless the ashes were hauled away before the sun was fully up. Unless the story was written before the fire, the way our names had been entered into ledgers before anyone asked us.

Down in the town, silhouetted against the glow, figures moved along the street—soldiers, a dozen or more, standing in a loose line with their hands in their pockets, watching their own work with the patient boredom of men waiting out a shift. Nobody was fighting the fire. Nobody had brought water. A bucket brigade takes ten minutes to organize; these men had had an hour.

"Accident," Odile said. She said the word the way you'd spit out a cherry pit. "They'll call it an accident. Lamps left burning by the evacuation crews. Spontaneous combustion in a store of rags—I've read the phrase in coroners' reports, it's a favorite, spontaneous combustion, as though buildings catch fire the way hay does, out of pure temper." Her hands were fists at her sides. I had never seen Odile Marchetti raise her voice, and she did not raise it now, and somehow that was worse. "My father built that wall," she said. "The east wall. He laid the stone himself the summer I was nine, and I handed him mortar, and he let me write my initials in the wet cement at the base where no one would ever see them. M.M., age nine. It's burning right now. Somewhere in that fire, a nine-year-old girl's initials are turning back into powder."

She fell silent. Below us a section of wall folded inward with a sound that reached us a full breath after we saw it happen—a long soft exhalation, like the building finally agreeing to die.

* * *

We stayed until the light changed. That was Odile's rule, and I understood it even as every nerve in me screamed to be moving, doing, hauling, copying—there were forty names uncopied in a feed box under oats, forty names that existed now in exactly one place on earth, forty widowed signatures whose sisters had already been mailed to safety across the country. One fire, one theft, one careless farmhand, and the last of them would be gone the way the workshop was going.

But Odile stood at the wall until the sky behind the smoke turned from black to iron to the pale washed gray of a real morning, and she made us stay with her, and I am glad now that she did, because I learned something standing there that I could not have learned running.

I learned that fury keeps. 

When the fire was young, my grief was all noise—I was shaking, I was crying without deciding to, my teeth wanted to break. But the fire lasted two hours, and grief cannot sustain itself at that pitch for two hours, and somewhere in the second hour the noise burned down the way the flames did, and what was left underneath was quiet and dense and cold, like lead. Like type metal, I thought. Papa melting broken sorts down in the little crucible by the window, pouring the silver liquid into the mold, and what comes out is a new letter. You don't lose the metal. You change what it says.

By full light, the four of us were no longer watching a fire. We were reading a verdict. The chimney still stood. The iron skeletons of the presses stood among the embers, unmistakable, undeniable. And around them, in a wide circle, the ground itself was scorched far past any boundary an accident respects—right up to the property line on the north side, where the grass of the empty lot next door was browned in a neat arc, as though the flames had politely declined to cross onto army-seized land.

"Look at that," Ivo murmured. He nodded at the arc of dead grass. "Fire that knows where the requisition line runs. That's a disciplined fire."

"An obedient fire," Odile said. "It received orders."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly, half sob, and Odile turned and looked at me—really looked, the way she does, the way that makes you feel counted—and she put her arm through mine, and we stood there, the typesetter's daughter and the lady with the list, watching the smoke lean eastward on the wind toward the capital, toward all the places our envelopes were traveling, and I thought: *lean that way. Go on. Tell them.*

* * *

We got back to the farmhouse by seven. The Kesslers were up; Frau Kessler took one look at our faces and put bread and coffee in front of us without asking anything, which is a kind of speech.

Ivo went straight to the barn. I followed him. I don't think either of us decided to; we simply both needed to see it, the way you need to touch your own face after bad news.

He lifted the feed box lid and moved the oats aside with both hands, and there was the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, dusty, entirely ordinary, a fat brown book with a cracked spine that had cost four hundred women their livelihoods and might yet cost us our freedom. Ivo lifted it out and held it against his chest, and his shoulders started to go, and he turned away toward the stall wall, and I pretended very hard to be checking the latch so he could have his minute.

"She's right," he said finally, not turning around. "Odile. About watching. I've spent two years being invisible—driving, carrying, reading letters nobody knew I read—and last night I thought I'd learned my lesson, that invisibility gets people killed, that Brandt is in a cell because he stopped being invisible for one afternoon. But standing on that hill road I realized I've had it backwards. It's not enough to stop hiding. Somebody has to *see*. Out loud. On the record. That's the whole war, Marta. It isn't bombs. It's whether anybody sees."

"Forty names," I said.

He wiped his face with his wrist and set the ledger on the feed box lid and looked at me, and I saw the thing in him that had gotten us this far—the driver who memorized routes, who memorized names, who carried other people's courage in the trunk of a car and called it professionalism. Except he wasn't calling it anything anymore.

"Forty names," he agreed. "Lamplight, pen, ink. We finish today."

"And then?"

"And then the last envelope goes where Odile says it goes." He hesitated. "There's talk it goes to Brandt's mother."

I thought about Sergeant Brandt—about his big careful hands, the way he'd searched our car and found nothing because he'd chosen to find nothing, the eleven words he'd written under our door and the cell they'd bought him. I thought about his mother, some woman in some town who had raised a son decent enough to commit dereliction of duty, who probably scrubbed floors or took in sewing, who had never signed anything in her life because no one had ever told her she could.

"Good," I said. "Let a soldier's family read what soldiers burned."

Outside, the church bells began—the eight o'clock, ordinary, indifferent—and beneath them, faintly, we could still smell the smoke coming off the town, sweet and terrible, like leaves, like endings. Ivo tucked the ledger back into the oilcloth, and I picked up the pen and the ink bottle, and we carried them into the kitchen where Odile waited with the lamplight trimmed and the table cleared, and we sat down among the smell of ashes to write the forty names the fire had been sent to erase.

Somewhere south, a newspaper editor was opening an envelope.

He just didn't know yet what he was holding.
