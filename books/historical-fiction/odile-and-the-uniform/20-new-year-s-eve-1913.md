## 20. New Year's Eve, 1913

The hall had been a dance school once, before its owner fled east with her family and her pianos. Now it was ours for one night—borrowed, like everything we had left. The floor still bore the chalked footprints of girls learning their figures: small arcs and crosses, a language of steps half-erased by boots. Odile Marchetti stood at the window of that hall in the last hour of 1913 and watched the lamplighters work their way down the boulevard, and thought how strange it was that a year could end when nothing inside it had ended at all.

The workshop was ash. That fact sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Eleven days since the fire on the hill road, and she could still smell it if she let herself—the sweet, wrong smell of burnt paper, which is not like other smoke but thinner, sharper, as though the burning pages themselves had cried out. Four hundred signatures had lived in a ledger bound in green cloth. She had carried that ledger out through a side door an hour before the flames took the building, Ivo's hand steady on her elbow, Marta ahead of them with the last armful of type they would never set again.

And now the survivors were gathering in a dead woman's dance hall to mark the turning of a year that had tried to unmake them.

They came in ones and twos, wrapped to the eyes against the cold, watching the street before they knocked. A widow who had signed the petition under her maiden name because her husband's employers read the papers. A schoolteacher with ink stains she could never fully scrub from her fingers. Two sisters from the laundry who had lost their positions within a week of the requisition notices and had walked nine miles rather than miss this night. Frau Heller, whose son drove supply wagons for the army and did not know his mother was here. Old Bess, seventy if she was a day, who brought a jar of plum brandy and announced that she had outlived two emperors and intended to outlive this one too.

By eight o'clock there were thirty-one of them in the hall. Thirty-one out of four hundred. The rest were scattered across the country now, or scattered across something larger than the country—Marta said *scattered across the mail*, which was truer and more frightening. Somewhere between here and every city with a printing press, their names traveled in hems and hymnals and hymn-book covers, in the linings of hatboxes, folded into patterns for dressmaking, sewn into the shoulders of coats. Odile had learned in eleven days more ways to hide paper than she had learned in twenty years of organizing, and every one of those ways had been invented by a woman with no training in conspiracy whatsoever.

Ivo stood by the door taking coats. He had appointed himself to it without discussion, the way he appointed himself to everything—driving, sorting, carrying, standing watch—and Odile caught herself watching him do it, this man who had spent years making himself invisible and could no longer quite manage it. Something had changed in him since the farmhouse. Since the lamplight and the cramped hands and the forty final names. He moved differently. He looked people in the eye when he took their coats, and they looked back, and neither party seemed surprised.

Marta was stringing paper lanterns along the windows. She had made them herself from the flyleaves of damaged hymnals—the pages that had been water-stained past saving—and so the light in the hall came up warm and gold with fragments of psalms glowing faintly through the paper. *Be Thou my vision.* Odile read the words upside down through a lantern shade and decided that God, whatever else could be said of Him tonight, had been given adequate notice.

"Odile." Marta came down off the chair, wiping her palms on her skirt. "The telegraph office closes at ten. If anything's coming, it comes before then."

"I know."

"You've checked twice."

"I'll check a third time," Odile said, "and you will not mock me for it, because you have been to the corner three times yourself."

Marta grinned—that quick, fierce grin that had survived fire and grief and the particular cruelty of being sixteen in a year like this one—and went back to her lanterns. Her father had been a typesetter. His hands had set the first pamphlets; the army called his death an industrial matter; and his daughter now handled paper the way other girls her age handled ribbon, with an intimacy that looked, from a distance, almost tender.

* * *

At half past eight Odile climbed onto the small stage where piano students had once performed their exercises, and asked for quiet, and got it—not all at once, but the way quiet comes among women who have practiced being unheard: a ripple, then a settling.

"We are fewer than we were," she began. "I won't pretend otherwise. We had a building and we don't. We had a press and we don't. Some of us have lost wages, homes, the good opinion of neighbors whose good opinion we were perhaps foolish to want." A ripple of laughter, quickly smothered. "In eleven days I have slept in four houses that were not mine, and eaten at tables where I was introduced as a cousin, a governess, and once—as old Bess will never let me forget—a seamstress."

"Your seams are a disgrace," Bess called out, "and I say that with love."

"With love," Odile agreed, and waited for the laughter to die. Then she let her voice go quieter, the way she did only when what she said next mattered most. "But I want to tell you what else we have. We have four hundred names. Not copies—I mean the names themselves, alive, traveling, doing their work. Every one of those women wrote her name on a line knowing what it might cost her, and some of them paid. We know of eleven who lost work. We suspect more. One of them—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it would have been worse, "one of them is Sergeant Brandt's mother, and that letter went north in November, and whether it has arrived we cannot yet know. But it went. It is moving toward her even now, at the speed of trains and weather, and somewhere in this country a soldier's mother is going to open an envelope and find her own name beside three hundred and ninety-nine others, and she will understand that she was never alone in wanting what she wanted."

She looked out at them—at the widow and the teacher and the laundry sisters and Bess with her brandy—and saw in their faces the thing she had spent her whole life working toward and could never quite name: not hope exactly. Hope was for things not yet begun. This was older than hope. This was recognition.

"The army burned our press," Odile said. "Let them burn presses all winter. Paper is cheap. Hands are many. And a name, once spoken into enough ears, does not go out like a flame goes out. It goes out like a seed goes out—which is to say, it goes into the ground and waits, and the ground does not negotiate."

Old Bess raised the jar. "To the ground, then."

"To the ground," said thirty-one voices, and drank plum brandy in a borrowed hall while outside the century prepared to turn over in its sleep.

* * *

It was Ivo who went for the telegram.

Odile had sent word to the newspaper contact weeks ago—through the saleswoman route, through three changes of hands, the address written in the code of a dressmaker's order—and after that, silence, which was either safety or failure and indistinguishable from the inside. All day she had felt the silence pressing on her like weather. At quarter to ten she could bear the waiting no longer and asked Ivo to run to the telegraph office before it closed, on the barest chance that—

He came back at five minutes past ten, running. She knew before he reached the door. She knew by the way he ran—not the careful, invisible lope of a driver minding traffic, but flat-out, coat flying, hatless, a man who had stopped caring who saw him.

He burst into the hall with snow in his hair and the telegram crushed in his fist, and for one absurd second he simply stood there, breathing, unable to speak, and thirty-one women rose to their feet without knowing why.

"They printed it." His voice cracked on the second word. He held out the telegram and his hand was shaking and he did not try to hide it. "The whole list. All four hundred. Today's edition—front page, below the fold, but the *front page*, Odile—they ran it under 'A Petition Suppressed by Military Authority Is Published Here in Full.' Every name. Spelled correctly." He looked down at the paper in his hand as though he couldn't believe it was real. "Marta's father's shop set type smaller than this. They used nine-point. Nine-point, to fit them all."

The hall went silent in the peculiar way a room full of people goes silent when the sound has nowhere left to go—when joy is too large for the container. Odile took the telegram. Her eyes went to the bottom of the message, where the newspaper had added a note: *List verified against original petition copy supplied by correspondents. Names follow.*

Names followed. Four hundred of them, compressed into wire-speech, abbreviated and numbered, but all of them, all of them, delivered into the permanent record of a nation that had been told, officially and repeatedly, that no such list existed.

Then the sound broke loose.

It began with the widow—Odile never learned her name until that night; it was Anna Voss—who threw both arms around the schoolteacher and sobbed. Then the laundry sisters seized each other. Old Bess stood on a chair, brandy jar aloft, shouting something that was half toast and entirely profane, and Marta—Marta spun in a circle in the middle of the floor, laughing, and the laughter kept breaking and reforming like ice on a thawing river, and halfway through the spin she was crying without stopping the laugh, tears flying off her face as she turned, laughing and weeping in the same breath, sixteen years old and orphaned and lit by psalms in paper lanterns, and Odile thought: *there. That is what victory looks like. No one told me it looks like this.*

They embraced her. They embraced each other. They embraced Ivo, who submitted to it with the stunned patience of a man being hugged for the first time in living memory, and Odile saw him look helplessly over the schoolteacher's shoulder and find her eyes, and what passed between them then needed no dictation and no courier. It said: *we did this.* It said: *they tried to burn it and it is printed in nine-point on the front page of a paper six hundred miles away.* It said: *you read every letter, and every letter was worth reading.*

Frau Heller was standing apart from the celebration, very still, one hand pressed flat over her mouth. Odile went to her. For a moment the older woman didn't speak. Then she lowered her hand and said, "My name is on it. In the newspaper. My son could read it tomorrow at breakfast." Her eyes were wet and terrified and shining all at once. "Good. Let him read it. Let him see what his mother is."

"He'll be proud," Odile said.

"He'll be furious," Frau Heller corrected. "And then he'll be proud. My boy takes things in that order. Always has."

Odile laughed, and the laugh became part of the general noise, and the noise became singing—old Bess started it, some drinking song from another reign entirely, and the laundry sisters harmonized, and the schoolteacher, who had a voice like a chapel organ, lifted the tune above all of them until the windows rattled in their frames and someone downstairs pounded on the ceiling and was ignored.

* * *

Later—much later, near midnight, when the singing had softened and the brandy had gone around twice and thirty-one exhausted women sat in pools of lantern light like survivors of a shipwreck warming themselves ashore—Odile found Ivo at the window again, alone, watching the year die in the street below.

She stood beside him. Neither spoke for a while. Down the boulevard, church bells were preparing themselves; she could feel the midnight coming the way one feels a train arriving, through the soles of the feet.

"My sister's name wasn't on any list," Odile said at last. It was the first time she had said it aloud in months, and it came out level, which surprised her. "No ledger, no petition, no newspaper. The factory burned and the record closed and that was the whole of it. Everything I have ever done—all of it, the petitions, the press, this—" she gestured at the hall behind them, at the lanterns and the sleeping brandy and the women, "—it was all just an attempt to build the record that should have existed for her. To make a world where no one burns unwritten."

Ivo was quiet. Then he said, "Four hundred names, Odile. Printed. Permanent. In nine-point." And there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before—not the careful neutrality of a driver, not even the warmth of these last hard weeks, but something rawer underneath, something that sounded almost like a question.

"It's a beginning," she said. "Not an ending. The depot is built. Brandt sits in a cell for us. There will be other requisitions, other fires. Come spring we start again—north, I think, where the rail lines meet and the printers aren't frightened yet. I'll need a driver."

"You have one."

"Forever?"

"For as long as you'll have me." He turned to face her then, fully, and in the lantern light she saw that his jaw was set in a way she had never seen on him, the way a man sets his jaw before doing the one thing he has been afraid of all evening. "There's something I have to do first. Before we go anywhere."

"What's that?"

He reached into his breast pocket and drew out a folded square of paper, worn soft at the creases, and she knew it instantly—the page he had copied from the ledger's final leaf, the forty names finished by lamplight in the farmhouse, the last testimony of a burned building. He unfolded it on the windowsill with the care of a man handling scripture.

"Everyone in that ledger signed their name knowing it might cost them," Ivo said. "Every one of them paid something, or stood ready to. And I rode beside them for a year reading their letters and telling myself it was efficiency." He shook his head slowly. "I'm done telling myself things."

From inside his coat he produced a pencil—the stub of one, a typesetter's pencil, and Odile's breath caught, because she recognized it, because it had belonged to a dead man whose daughter was asleep in a chair across the room with lantern-light on her face.

"What are you going to do?" she whispered, though she already knew, though her heart had known since the moment he came running through the door with the telegram, snow in his hair, invisible no longer.

Down the boulevard, the first bell of midnight began to swing. Ivo bent over the windowsill, and in the margin beneath the four hundredth name, in a hand that trembled only a little, he began to write.
