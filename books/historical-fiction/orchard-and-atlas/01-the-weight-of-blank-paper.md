## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The ink froze first.

Beatrix Fairweather noticed it the way he noticed everything in the workshop now that he worked alone — by absence. The small sounds were gone: Master Aldous Marchetti's cough behind the partition, the wet rattle of his breathing as winter closed its fist around his lungs, the scratch of his pen moving through the long afternoons like a slow animal through reeds. In their place there was only the silence of a room built for two, and the sound of the lamp guttering, and now this — the iron gall ink in his dish thickening at the edges, going sluggish and dark as cold syrup, refusing to flow where he asked it to go.

He breathed on it. Held the little dish between his palms the way you might warm a bird. Outside the workshop windows, Grayharbor lay wrapped in its February fog, the kind that came off the water before dawn and did not lift until noon, if it lifted at all. He could not see the harbor from here — the workshop sat three streets inland, above a chandler's shop that smelled perpetually of tallow and hemp — but he could hear it. The fog bells. The muffled cries of dockhands. Somewhere a ship's anchor chain paying out link by link, each one a dull knell against the hull.

The city had been mapped for two hundred years. Every street, every quay, every warehouse with its licensed dimensions recorded in the guild ledgers. And still the fog could make it new, make it strange, erase it all and redraw it in gray.

He warmed the ink until it ran again, and went back to work.

* * *

The coastline was the last thing left.

Not the whole coast — only the final stretch, the eastern shore where the river met the sea and the land broke apart into marsh and mudflat and the drowned forest no surveyor would ever bother walking. Eleven years Aldous Marchetti had spent on the atlas. Eleven years of plate proofs and corrected proofs and proofs corrected again, of letters to the Guild of Licensed Cartographers arguing over a headland's bearing by two minutes of arc, of winters like this one, when the workshop stove ate more coal than their budget allowed and they both wore coats indoors and said nothing about it, because saying nothing about it was how men like them preserved their dignity.

Aldous had died on the fourth of December, sitting up in his chair by the window, pen still in hand. Beatrix had found him in the morning. There had been a half-finished annotation on the desk — *soundings unreliable N of the spit, resurvey in spring* — and Beatrix had looked at those words for a long time before he covered his master's face with a cloth. *Resurvey in spring.* As though there would be a spring. As though the man who had taught him everything, who had hired him at fourteen because he caught an error in a tide table no one else could see, would simply continue past death into another field season, another round of arguments with the guild, another decade bent over copper.

Eleven years. And now the last coastline, and Beatrix Fairweather, twenty-six years old and unlicensed, alone in a freezing room with a dead man's life's work under his hands.

He cut the pen fresh. Tested it on scrap. The nib sang a thin note against the copperplate — too much pressure, ease off — and he eased off, and began.

The line unspooled beneath his hand. This was the part no one understood about mapmaking, the people who bought atlases and hung them in parlors and traced trade routes with a lacquered finger. They thought a coastline was a fact. They thought you walked to the edge of the land and wrote down what you found, the way you'd write down the price of coal. But a coastline was an argument. It was eleven years of soundings taken in different weathers by different men with different instruments, reconciled. It was the question of whether the marsh east of the river was land or sea — it was both, depending on the hour and the moon, and the atlas had to choose, and choosing meant knowing why you chose. It was Aldous Marchetti standing hip-deep in a November tide at fifty-eight years old, swearing at his assistant, because he would not put a line on paper he had not verified with his own body.

Beatrix drew the line Aldous would have drawn. He knew it the way he knew his own signature.

* * *

The light changed around what he guessed was ten o'clock, though he'd stopped trusting the clock weeks ago. Time moved strangely in a room with one occupant. The fog outside thickened until the window became a pane of milk, then gave up entirely and showed him only his own reflection: a thin man with ink-stained fingers and his master's spare spectacles perched on his nose because his own had cracked and replacements cost money the atlas had already consumed. Behind the reflection, the workshop stretched back into shadow. The flat files. The plate cabinet with its brass fittings gone green at the edges. The great press they could not afford to run anymore except for proofs, proofs, always proofs.

On the wall above the files hung the framed license — the master's license, the guild seal stamped in red wax, ALDOUS MARCHETTI, CARTOGRAPHER, LICENSED BY CHARTER OF THE PORT COMMISSIONERS. Underneath it, in a smaller frame Aldous had never gotten around to hanging, Beatrix's own apprenticeship certificate. Not a license. An apprenticeship. In the eyes of the guild he was nothing — a hand, a pair of trained eyes, legally incapable of signing work or selling it or even correcting it without a licensed name to hide behind.

Which was, of course, the next problem. The atlas was finished — nearly finished, finished today — but it belonged to a dead man, and the guild did not recognize dead men, and Beatrix could neither publish it nor sign it nor sell it. He had written to the guild twice since December. Both letters had come back with the same polite formula: the matter of succession would be addressed at the spring session. Spring. Everything was spring with them. Resurvey in spring. Succession in spring. Meanwhile the plates sat in their cabinet and the world went on believing the eastern shore looked the way it had in 1831, which it did not, which mattered to exactly no one with money or power, which was precisely why the guild felt safe making him wait.

Maps record places, his master used to say. Never people. That is why they endure.

Beatrix had thought it wisdom once. Now, warming his hands over the stove that gave heat to the room's center and none to its corners, he heard it differently. Places endure. People do not. The atlas would outlive them both — would carry Aldous's judgment in every line, his stubbornness in every disputed bearing, and no reader would ever know, and that, Aldous had seemed to feel, was proper. A map should be like a window, he'd said. You look through it. You do not look at the glass.

But Beatrix had spent eleven years looking at the glass. He saw his master everywhere in these plates — in the particular confidence of the lettering, the way Aldous rendered sandbanks with a stipple so fine it looked like breath on the copper. The atlas was a portrait of a man disguised as a portrait of a coast, and when it went to print under someone else's name — the guild's name, the commission's name, whoever they decided owned a dead cartographer's labor — the last of Aldous Marchetti would be signed away, and the man himself would be nowhere in the record at all.

Just as the famine dead were nowhere in the record. Just as the thousands who'd crowded the quays four years ago, waiting on grain that the newspapers swore was coming —

He stopped the thought there. It arrived sometimes, unbidden, the way grief arrives, and he had learned to let it pass through without unpacking it. Four years ago he had stood on Fishmarket Quay with everyone else and watched a ship come in carrying not grain but ballast stone, and the crowd's silence afterward had been worse than any riot. He had gone home and inked soundings. What else was there? Maps record places. The places stayed the same. The quay was the quay. The water was the water.

He dipped the pen. The line waited for him, patient as everything else on copper.

* * *

By late afternoon the eastern shore was done.

He sat back and looked at it the way he always looked at finished work: from across the room, squinting, letting it resolve into the thing a stranger would see. A stranger would see competence. A stranger would see a coast, accurate to within a boat's length, beautiful in the way that only truth can be beautiful — all those soundings marching offshore in their careful numerals, the marsh rendered in Aldous's stipple, the drowned forest marked with a symbol Beatrix had invented himself two years ago and Aldous had grudgingly adopted.

*A good map is a promise,* Aldous told him once, drunk on the guild's cheap claret after a licensing dinner. *Every line says: I was here. I measured. You may trust me with your ship, your cargo, your life. Do you understand what a responsibility that is, boy? We are the liars everyone believes.*

We. He had said *we*, that night, with his hand heavy on Beatrix's shoulder. It was the closest either of them had ever come to saying what they were to each other. Master and apprentice. Employer and employee. Neither word held it. Something closer to father, something closer to son, and both of them too proud and too frightened to name it while there was time.

Beatrix cleaned the plate with spirits, working slowly, giving his hands a task so his mind would not have one. Then he oiled it, wrapped it in flannel, and carried it to the plate cabinet — the tall oak cabinet along the north wall, where the atlas lived in twenty-two numbered drawers, each plate sleeping in its bed of felt.

Twenty-two plates. He slid the last one home and stood with his hand on the drawer a moment longer than the task required.

It was done. Eleven years, and it was done, and tomorrow he would begin the proofs — the final set, pulled clean, checked line by line against the surveys, sealed and sent to the guild commission that would decide whether the atlas deserved to exist. And after that, publication, if the commission approved, and after that —

After that, strangers would hold it. Merchants, captains, schoolmasters. They would see the coast of 1847 and never know that the stippled marsh had cost a man his health, wading in cold water season after season because accuracy was a form of honesty and honesty was a form of prayer. They would never know that the assistant who finished the last coastline had eaten bread and dripping for a month to afford the spirits to clean the plate.

There was a comfort in that. He turned to it deliberately, the way you turn toward a fire. Anonymity was a shelter. The unnamed man passed through the world unburdened; no one demanded anything of him; no one blamed him for anything. His master had been named — licensed, accountable, celebrated in a small way among people who cared about such things — and it had consumed him. Eleven years. It had taken everything, and given back a frame on a wall and a grave in the paupers' row of St. Brendan's churchyard, because the atlas had eaten the money too.

No. Beatrix would finish the work, surrender it to the guild, take his position — they would have to give him a junior license, surely, with the atlas complete and his hand all over it — and live quietly somewhere above some other shop, drawing other people's coasts, keeping other people's promises. Invisible. Safe.

He banked the stove. Put on his coat, because the workshop lost heat the moment the fire died, and because the walk home was long and the fog had teeth in it this time of year. At the door he paused and looked back at the room: the files, the cabinet, the framed license catching the last gray light.

*Resurvey in spring,* the dead man had written.

Tomorrow, Beatrix told himself, he would pull the proofs. All of them, every plate, the complete set, so that the atlas existed on paper as well as copper — so that something of it, some record of it, was out in the world and could not be wholly swallowed by the guild's filing system or the commission's delays. It was practical. Proof sets were standard practice. It had nothing to do with wanting to hold the whole atlas in his arms at once, all twenty-two plates, the entire weight of eleven years, before anyone else could tell him what it was worth or whose it was.

The fog took him at the corner. He walked home through a city erased and redrawn, past the harbor he could hear but not see, and did not look back.

* * *

The proofs took three days.

He pulled them on the old proof press, one plate at a time, damping the paper in the felt-lined trough, wiping the ink into the engraved lines with a pad until the copper's valleys were full and its peaks clean, laying the sheet, pulling the bar. Each impression came up ghostly at first and then, as he learned the plate's appetite, black and certain. Twenty-two plates. He printed three sets — one for the guild, one for the commission's file, and one that he did not have a reason for, which he stacked on the master's desk and told himself was simply prudent.

On the third evening, with the last sheet hanging to dry on the lines strung above the stove, he sat down at Aldous's desk to begin checking. This was the discipline: plate against survey, numeral against numeral, every sounding confirmed, every bearing squared. It would take a week, maybe more, and then the sealed set would go to the guild and the long wait would begin.

He started with plate one and worked through the evening, and the workshop grew dark around him, and he lit the lamp without noticing he had done it. The atlas assembled itself in his hands, sheet by sheet, a country made of paper. Coastlines he knew better than his own face. River mouths. The port itself, rendered at large scale, every quay and basin and bonded warehouse drawn with the loving precision Aldous had reserved for things he distrusted — and he had distrusted the port, Beatrix remembered suddenly, smiling at the memory. Distrusted its tidiness. *Too neat,* he'd say, tapping the drawings the harbor board supplied. *Nothing built by committee comes out this straight.*

Near midnight he reached the last sheets — plates twenty-one and twenty-two, the eastern shore and its margins, finished three days ago in a cold room by the last pair of hands Aldous Marchetti ever trained. He checked them quickly; they needed nothing; they were his own work and he knew every line of them the way a man knows his own handwriting.

Then he gathered the full set to bind it loosely for transport — cord through the left margin, the way proof sets were always done — and reached for the awl, and the needle struck resistance.

He frowned. Ran his thumb along the spine of the stack. Plate nineteen's margin was thicker than the others. Thicker by perhaps the width of two coins, along its inner edge, where the paper should have been bare.

He turned the sheet to the lamp.

Someone had sewn something into the binding. Into the plate itself — no, behind it, between the copper impression and the backing board, stitched flat with fine thread in stitches so small and so regular they might have been part of the manufacturing, invisible unless you were driving an awl through them at midnight with a dead man's tools. The thread was silk, old, the color of weak tea. The stitches were Aldous's. Beatrix knew his master's sewing the way he knew his stippling; he had watched those hands work ten thousand hours.

His heart began doing something slow and enormous in his chest.

He fetched the knife. He hesitated — one breath, two, the lamp hissing, the fog bells tolling out on the water, faint as memory — and then he slid the blade under the first stitch and cut.
