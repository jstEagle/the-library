## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The proofs arrived on the third of February, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of the river.

Beatrix Fairweather carried them up the workshop stairs two at a time, which was a mistake, because the cold had settled into his knees overnight and by the time he reached the landing his breath came in short white rags. He set the bundle on the long table beneath the window, where the light was best, and stood a moment with his hands flat on the oilcloth, letting his heart slow, letting the room come back to him: the smell of size and lamp-black, the racks of dried skins, the great press in the corner under its dust sheet like something sleeping. Forty years of Master Aldous Wren's life in this room. Nine months of Beatrix's own.

He unwrapped the proofs.

There were twenty-two plates in the atlas, and each proof was pulled on paper finer than the final edition would carry, because the printer—Odile Ferrant's shop, though Beatrix had never met her; the arrangement had been made before Master Wren died—insisted that errors were cheapest to catch at the first pull. The proofs lay in their stack like pale leaves. *The Harbour Approaches.* *The Grain Quays, East and West.* *The Salt Marsh Reclamation.* Each one a small country, inked and waiting to be true.

He began, as he always began, with the coastline.

* * *

Checking plate proofs was not reading and it was not drawing. It was a third thing, slower than either—a kind of listening. Beatrix would hold the proof beside the original drawing at arm's length, then close, then at arm's length again, and let his eye travel the line the way a pilot travels a shore: looking for what had shifted, what had been lost in translation from vellum to copper to paper. A harbour mouth narrowed by a hair's breadth could drown a ship that trusted it. A sandbar drawn a cable's length east could put a lighterman aground in sight of his own door. Maps lied in small ways, and small ways killed.

Master Wren had taught him that in his first year, when Beatrix was nineteen and certain of everything. *"A map is a promise,"* the old man had said, tapping the proof with a knuckle gone brown with age and tobacco. *"Every line you cut into that copper is a promise made to someone you will never meet, in weather you cannot imagine. Keep your promises, boy, or get out of the trade."*

Beatrix had kept them. For eleven years he had kept every promise, and for nine months since the funeral he had kept the dead man's too, finishing the atlas plate by plate exactly as the working drawings specified, down to the last sounding and the last stipple-shadow on the downs. The guild had been satisfied. The Voss family—the Voss Company, rather, whose money stood behind the atlas as it stood behind half the licensed cartography in the city—had been satisfied. There had even been talk of a second edition, and Beatrix had allowed himself, in the grey hour before sleep some nights, to imagine his name in the colophon beneath his master's. Not as author. As continuator. It was enough. It was more than enough.

It was a safe life, and he had chosen it deliberately, the way other men chose a wife or a faith.

By late afternoon he had cleared fourteen plates. The light through the window had gone the colour of weak tea, and he lit the lamp, trimmed the wick, and drew the fifteenth proof from the stack: *The Granary District and River Frontages.*

This was the plate he himself had finished, in August of last year, three months after the burial. Master Wren's drawing for it had been nearly complete—nearly, but not quite, and Beatrix remembered the strange week he'd spent completing it, the sense of walking through a house in which the owner had stepped out mid-thought. His master's hand had grown erratic near the end. Everyone said so. The tremor, the lapses, the letters begun and abandoned. In the last month of his life Aldous Wren had written almost nothing that anyone could read, and Beatrix had taken over the correspondence entirely, and so there was no one left who knew the master's late hand well enough to say what was illness and what was intention.

He held the proof to the lamp.

The plate was correct. Every line matched. And yet—he tilted it, caught the raking light across the paper—there was a texture to the lower margin he did not remember cutting. Not a line. A stippling, faint, dense, running along the river frontage between the third and fourth granary marks. He fetched his loupe and bent close.

Survey notes. Tiny ones, lettered in a shorthand the guild used for marginal corrections, threaded through the stipple like veins through marble. At normal reading distance they dissolved into texture. Under the loupe they resolved into figures.

*Tonnage,* Beatrix thought, before he understood why the word came. The numbers were tonnages. Displacement figures, cargo capacities, rendered as if they were soundings—as if the granaries along the river were vessels floating on paper. And dates. Small dates, in sequence, running through the winter two years past. The famine winter.

He sat back. The lamp hissed.

It meant nothing, he told himself. Surveyors recorded tonnages; granaries were measured; his master had been thorough to the point of obsession, and the famine winter had seen more official measurement of grain than any year before or since, because everyone had wanted to know how much there was and where. It was data. It was the trade. It was nothing.

But the stippling should not have been on the proof if it was not on his finished drawing, and it *was* on his finished drawing, because he had cut that plate himself, and—

And he had cut it from Master Wren's original without questioning a single mark, because questioning marks was not what apprentices did with dead masters' work, and because the alternative—that the old man had spent his dying autumn hiding arithmetic in a coastline—was a thought that did not bear holding.

Beatrix set down the loupe very carefully, as if it might go off.

* * *

He told himself he would stop after the sixteenth plate. Then the seventeenth. The workshop grew cold enough that his tea went untouched and skinned over, and he did not notice. He worked through the remaining proofs with the loupe, and found the same buried texture on four more: the Customs House approach, the rail spur to the east yards, the bonded warehouses plan, and—most densely of all—the plate titled simply *Voss Wharf*, which the company itself had commissioned and paid for, and which now, under magnification, disclosed a complete hidden ledger of arrivals, departures, and discrepancies threaded through its dockside shading. Ships listed by name. Ships listed by tonnage in, tonnage out. And in one corner, disguised as a compass rose's flourish, a single word in the tiniest lettering Beatrix had ever cut or seen cut:

*DIVERTED.*

His hands were shaking by then. He made himself put the proofs back in order, wrap them, retie the oilcloth—and stopped, because the binding of the atlas mock-up, the stitched dummy that had gone to the printer with the plates and come back with them, was wrong.

He had bound the dummy himself. He knew every stitch of it. The spine had been sewn five-station, kettle stitch at head and tail, linen thread waxed with his own hands. Now, tilting it against the lamplight, he saw a sixth station—a slight swelling near the tail of the spine, a fullness under the leather that had not been there when he sent it away. Or had it? He turned the dummy over twice. Pressed the swelling with a thumbnail. Something inside shifted, flat and firm, like a card.

He took up the bone folder and slit the stitching.

* * *

The letter was sewn flat against the spine's inner curve, folded twice, enclosed in a second fold of plain paper, and sealed—not with wax but with a drop of glue pressed under a scrap of the same paper, the seal of a man who had no time and trusted no postmaster. Beatrix unfolded it with fingers that would not stay steady, and the lamplight fell on eight lines of writing in a hand he knew better than his father's, a hand gone wild at the edges, the letters leaning like drunks, but legible, legible still, because his master had learned to write in an age when a surveyor's field notes had to survive rain and haste and read by strangers.

*If you are reading this, the atlas is printed, and I am past caring who reads it.*

*I have sewn this where only a binder would look, and only a binder who loved the work would look so closely. So it will be you, Bea, or no one.*

*You will have found my little arithmetic in the margins by now, or you will find it tomorrow, and you will be angry with me for not telling you. I could not tell you while I lived. They watch the workshop. You know they do—the "inspectors," the friendly calls, the commissions that arrive with conditions. If I had spoken aloud, the plates would have been recut within the month and I would have died a fool instead of a dead man.*

*So I hid it in the one place they will never burn, because burning it costs them the atlas they paid for. The grain never rotted, Bea. The ships never sank. The Voss Company took the relief cargoes into their own wharves in the famine winter and sold them onward at blockade prices while the parishes buried children, and the manifests that prove it sit in the customs house to this day, misfiled by a friend I trust, against the day someone looks.*

*I am too weak now to finish this. Finish the atlas. Print the atlas. Let them buy their own evidence if they want it buried—they will try, and the price they offer will tell you everything.*

*Do not be brave. Be exact. Exactness is braver, and lives longer.*

*A.W.*

Beatrix read it four times. On the fourth reading the words stopped being words and became weight, and he set the page down on the table because his hands could no longer be trusted with it.

The lamp burned. Somewhere below, the tide turned against the pier pilings, a long slow knocking like something asking to be let in. He became aware of the cold properly for the first time in hours, and of the tea, and of the fact that his whole body ached as though he had carried something heavy a great distance—which, in a way, he had, since morning, without knowing it.

*They watch the workshop.* He looked up, absurdly, at the dark window, at his own reflection ghosted against the glass and the lamplight behind him. The street beyond was empty. It was always empty. That was why he had liked this room.

He thought about the Voss men who had visited in the autumn—courteous, patient, asking mild questions about progress, about delays, about whether he found the master's later drawings difficult to interpret. He had found the questions flattering. Interest, he had thought. Confidence.

He thought about the phrase *misfiled by a friend I trust,* and wondered which drawer in which customs house held those manifests tonight.

He thought about the word *diverted*, cut so small that only a lens could find it, and about the thousands of people two winters dead, whose parish burials he had read about over breakfast while inking coastlines, feeling nothing but a general sorrow, the sorrow of a man reading about weather in a distant province.

And he understood, sitting in the cold with the letter flat before him and the atlas dummy gaping open at its ruined seam, that the safe life was already over—that it had ended not tonight but months ago, in this room, in his master's trembling hand, and that everything since had merely been the delay before he noticed.

The sensible course was obvious. Take the letter to the guild. Take it to the Voss Company itself, even—show them, negotiate, be the careful tradesman he had trained to be. There would be money in it. There might even be gratitude.

Instead he heard himself saying, aloud, to the empty room, in a voice that sounded younger than his thirty years: *"What did you do, old man?"*

The atlas answered from the table, twenty-two plates of promised harbours and honest soundings, every line a pledge to strangers in bad weather—and threaded through five of them, fine as frost, the other kind of promise, the one his master had kept at the cost of everything and left for Beatrix to keep or break.

Outside, far off toward the east yards, a warehouse bell rang the change of watch. Tomorrow, he knew—knew with the certainty of a man watching fog close over a channel marker—the Voss heirs would hear that the proofs were ready. And tomorrow, or the day after, they would come to this workshop with an offer in their mouths and kindness in their voices, and Beatrix Fairweather would have to decide, before they finished speaking, whether he was a man who wrote history or a man it happened to.

He folded the letter along its old creases. He did not resew the binding. Some things, once opened, should stay open.
