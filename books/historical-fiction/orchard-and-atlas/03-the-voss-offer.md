## 3. The Voss Offer

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, no one knocked at Fairweather & Marchetti—no one had ever needed to; the workshop received its commissions by guild courier and its visitors by appointment, both rare as comets. Second, it was a polite knock. Three measured raps, spaced like a man counting coins into a palm, and Beatrix knew before he opened the door that whoever stood behind it had never waited for anything in his life.

He had slept four hours. The rest of the night belonged to the letter—the sealed letter sewn into the binding of the plate proofs, which now lay hidden inside the false bottom of the drying chest where he kept his winter coat. He had read it six times by candlelight until the words stopped reading like words and started reading like weather: *the Voss grain contracts of '46, diverted through Corvane Sound, the relief shipments logged and burned, the manifests altered under seal.* His master's hand, shaking toward the end but unmistakable. Augustin Marchetti had died knowing this, and had died without telling him, and Beatrix still could not decide which of those facts wounded more deeply.

So when he opened the door and saw two men in coats worth more than the workshop's roof, he was already braced, though he did not yet know what for.

"Master Fairweather." The taller one smiled with professional warmth. He was perhaps forty, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome so deliberately for so long that it had forgotten how to be anything else. "Edmund Voss. This is my cousin Aldous. May we come in?"

It was not a question in any sense that mattered. Beatrix stepped aside because the alternative was a scene on his own doorstep, and scenes were things that happened to other people.

*Other people,* he thought, watching them duck beneath the low lintel, their boots leaving damp prints on his floorboards. *That has always been me.*

* * *

The workshop in morning light was not an impressive room, and Beatrix felt its smallness like a stain. The long drafting table scarred with twenty years of compass points. The racks of plates—copper, each one engraved coast by coast, sound by sound, three hundred and eleven of them, the skeleton of the atlas that had consumed the last decade of two men's lives. The smell of ink and tallow and cold stone. Edmund Voss took all of this in with the mild appraisal of a man pricing a horse, and something in Beatrix's chest tightened defensively, though he could not have said why.

"You've done remarkable work," Voss said. "The northern approaches especially. My father followed Master Marchetti's career with great interest."

*Your father.* The name arrived in the room before Beatrix could stop it: **Voss**. It sat there among the plates and the ink pots, enormous, and Beatrix understood in a single cold flash why they had come—not to admire, not to commission, but because of what lay sewn into the proofs, and because they might know it too.

He made himself move. Made himself set the kettle on, because hands that were busy were hands that did not shake, and poured tea into cups that matched neither each other nor the occasion. Aldous Voss, the younger and softer of the two, accepted his cup with genuine courtesy. Edmund did not touch his.

"We'll be brief," Edmund said. "We know your time is precious, and ours is—" a smile, "—well. Ours is money."

Aldous laughed. Beatrix did not.

"The atlas is nearly complete," Edmund continued. "My father's estate has taken the liberty of reviewing the guild filings. Three hundred and eleven plates. A dedication page unfinished. Marginalia throughout in Master Marchetti's hand—survey notes, corrections, observations. A magnificent record." He paused, and the pause was a thing with weight and edges. "We should like to buy it."

"Buy it."

"Outright. The plates, the proofs, the working papers, the marginalia—everything. The atlas would be published under the Voss imprint, naturally, with full credit to yourself as completing cartographer and to Master Marchetti posthumously. You'd be compensated generously. Five hundred crowns. And—" here the smile widened by a degree, a door opening onto a larger room, "—a standing commission. The family maintains vessels across four trade routes, Master Fairweather. There isn't a chartmaker alive who wouldn't cut off a finger for access to our hydrographic surveys."

Five hundred crowns. Beatrix earned ninety in a good year, and last year had not been good. The number sat in the room like a third visitor, patient and heavy, and he hated it for how easily it fit.

"And the marginalia," Beatrix said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "You want my master's notes."

"Scholarly material loses nothing in private hands," Aldous said smoothly. "Frankly, some of it is rather—parochial. Old grievances, commercial complaints, that sort of thing. Father always said Augustin never met a grievance he didn't engrave. A published atlas ought to be about the coastline, don't you think? Not the coastguard."

There it was. Not hidden at all, really—only draped, the way a body is draped. They wanted the maps and not the handwriting on them. They wanted geography without testimony.

Beatrix looked down at his tea. In the surface, dark and still, he could see the upside-down reflection of the window, the fog pressing gray against the glass, the harbor beyond it where three years ago ships had ridden low with grain that was never meant to feed anyone—and he thought, absurdly, of the letter in the drying chest, close enough that if the fire ever took the workshop it would burn with his coat, and he thought: *they do not know I have it. Or they suspect, and this offer is the net thrown before the confirmation.*

Either way. Either way.

"I'll need to consider it," he said, because refusing outright would tell them everything, and caution was the only inheritance his mother had left him.

"Of course." Edmund rose, unhurried, a man closing a transaction already complete in his own ledger. At the door he turned, and for just a moment the polished warmth dropped away like a coat taken off indoors, and what remained underneath was not cruelty exactly—it was smaller and colder than cruelty. It was certainty. "One thing worth knowing, Master Fairweather. The guild's licensing board meets on the first of the month. Publication requires their seal. Families such as mine have friends on that board." A small shrug, almost apologetic. "Whatever you decide, decide knowing the atlas cannot reach print without us. Nothing reaches print in this city without us."

Then the door closed, and the fog swallowed the sound of their carriage, and Beatrix stood alone among his plates with his heart going like a hammer against stone.

* * *

He tried to work. That was the discipline of his life—grief, fear, hunger, all of it set aside while the pen moved—but the pen would not move. Twice he ruled a line and twice the line wandered from true, and the second time he put the pen down before he ruined a plate that had taken four months.

Instead he walked the length of the table, slowly, the way he walked it every evening, and looked at the atlas as he had never quite looked at it before.

Three hundred and eleven copper plates. Coastlines, harbors, soundings, shoals. For ten years he had believed he knew every line of them—had inked half of them himself after Augustin's hands failed, matching his dead master's stroke the way a son matches a father's signature. But now, with the Vosses' words still hanging in the air—*marginalia, old grievances, commercial complaints*—he found himself seeing the margins. Really seeing them.

There were notes everywhere. He had always known this; the atlas was thick with Augustin's cramped, slanted script, and Beatrix had treated it as texture, as atmosphere, the way one treats wallpaper. Survey corrections. Tide anomalies. Remarks on anchorages.

But there were so *many*. More than any working chartmaker would make. Clusters of them along the eastern approaches. Along Corvane Sound. Little dense paragraphs of figures where figures had no business being—a depth sounding recorded to four places, when soundings went to two; a tonnage written in the same shorthand used for tonnage, sitting beside a lighthouse bearing where no lighthouse existed.

Beatrix stood over plate 214—the Corvane approaches—and felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath him.

*A note in the margin of a map is invisible,* Augustin had told him once, early, in one of the master's rare discursive moods. They had been walking the harbor wall in a wind that smelled of tar and herring. *Everyone looks at the lines, boy. The lines are the map. But a margin is where a man can say what the map is forbidden to say. Kings censor coastlines. No king has ever censored a footnote.*

He had thought it a craftsman's joke. A bit of workshop philosophy, the kind old men shed like dandruff.

Now, looking at plate 214, with the Voss carriage still rattling somewhere in the back of his skull, Beatrix wondered if he had spent ten years sleeping in a house whose walls were lined with letters he had never bothered to open.

* * *

He did not go near the drying chest. That frightened him more than anything else—the deliberate way his feet refused its corner of the room, as though the letter were a stove too hot to touch. He had carried it there at three in the morning like a man carrying a live coal, and since then a bargain had formed between him and the coal: leave it be, and it leaves you be.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape. If he gave the Vosses the atlas, the letter and all, five hundred crowns and silence and a comfortable life resumed—if he did that, whatever was in the letter stayed buried, and the buried stayed buried, and Beatrix Fairweather went back to being a man whom history passed on the far side of the window. Safe. Anonymous. The comfort he had built his whole quiet life around, the comfort of being no one in particular.

And if he did not give it to them—

If he did not, then he was at war with the wealthiest family in the port, and wars, he understood dimly, were not survived by the cautious.

The afternoon fog thickened until the window went white. He lit lamps at four o'clock. He ate bread and hard cheese standing up, without tasting either. And through all of it the offer sat in the middle of the workshop like a boulder rolled through the door: *five hundred crowns, the plates, the marginalia included.*

*Included.* As if the marginalia were ballast. As if Augustin Marchetti's whole secret life were packing material around the pretty coastline.

By evening Beatrix had reached no decision, and he recognized with a kind of despairing clarity that this itself was the trap—that hesitation was exactly what the Vosses had priced in, that every day he deliberated was a day the net drew tighter, that men like Edmund Voss did not make offers, they started clocks.

What surprised him—what genuinely astonished him, when he finally let himself see it—was that the clock had already been running. That sometime between the second reading of the letter and the third rap on his door that morning, the decision had been made somewhere below his reason, in some cellar of himself he had never had occasion to visit. Because when he imagined saying yes—imagined it precisely, the handshake, the deed of transfer, the crates going out his door onto the Voss wagon—something in his chest closed like a fist. Not greed. Not even principle, which sounded noble and abstract. Something rawer. The image of strangers' hands on his master's margins, scraping them away, burning them, unmaking the only place in the world where Augustin Marchetti's fury still lived—and Beatrix's answer was simply, physically, *no*.

He said yes to nothing. He said no before he understood it, the way a man flinches before he knows the blow is coming.

And now he had to live in what came after.

* * *

Near midnight he took out the letter after all. Not to read it again—he knew it by heart now, every shaking line—but to hold it, and think.

They would come back. Men like that always came back, and next time warmer, or next time colder, but next time armed with better instruments: the guild license, the debts, perhaps worse. He had tasted power today for the first time in his life—he understood that now, walking home in his memory through the morning's interview. Power was not the offer. Power was the ease with which the offer was made, the absolute assumption that a cartographer's ten years could be purchased before lunch, that refusal was a formality to be managed. He had lived thirty-four years without once feeling that weight press on him, and now that he felt it he understood, with terrible clarity, that it had always been pressing—on everyone else, everywhere, all the time—and that his safety had never been absence of the weight but merely his smallness beneath it.

Invisibility, it turned out, was not free. Someone had paid for his anonymity all along. He was only now being shown the bill.

Beatrix folded the letter along its original creases, sewn paper crackling faintly, and held it over the lamp for a long moment—long enough to feel the heat begin to bite his fingers. Burning it would end everything. Return the world to its proper shape, with him small and safe inside it.

He blew out the lamp instead, and sat in the dark with the letter against his chest like a second heartbeat.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would look at the margins properly. All of them. Every note his master had ever written in the white space of a map, beginning with plate 214, beginning with Corvane Sound.

Somewhere out in the fog a ship's bell rang the hour, and Beatrix realized he was no longer afraid of what the letter said.

He was afraid of what the atlas said—the atlas the Vosses wanted so badly to buy, margins and all, and burn.

And that fear, he found, had teeth.
