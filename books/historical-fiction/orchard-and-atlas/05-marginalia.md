## 5. Marginalia

He did not sleep. That was the first decision of the night, though it hardly felt like a decision—more like a door closing behind him on its own weight. The letter lay open on the workbench where he had left it, its wax seal broken into two halves he could not make whole again, and Beatrix Fairweather sat across from it with a cold lamp and a colder cup of tea and understood that the man who had gone to bed in this workshop yesterday no longer existed.

The fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere below, the harbor bell counted out the hour—three, then four—and each toll landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*They will come for this,* he thought. *The moment they learn it exists.*

And then, because his mind had always worked the way a plow works, turning over whatever ground was put before it: *But how did Aldous know? How did he know enough to write it down?*

The letter named the Voss family. It named the famine year—the winter the granaries stood full and the poor queues stood longer, the winter they called, in the careful language of the gazettes, "the Season of Scarcity," as though hunger had been weather. It named ships. But it did not name warehouses, or tonnages, or routes. It said only: *the proof is in what I have drawn. Look closely at what you are finishing.*

Beatrix set down the cup.

He rose, crossed the workshop to the flat cabinet where the finished plates rested between sheets of felt, and drew out the plate for the harbor district—the one he had inked three nights ago, believing himself to be completing a coastline.

He laid it under the lamp.

* * *

A map is a language, and like any language it has more words than most people ever use. The public reads coastlines and roads and the names of towns; the guild reads soundings and elevations; but there is a third register, the one apprentices are taught last and masters use rarely—a shorthand of survey notation so dense and so old that half the licensed cartographers in the city could not read it without a key. Aldous Marchetti had helped write that key, twenty years ago. He was one of perhaps six men alive who could compose in it fluently.

Beatrix was the seventh. Barely.

His master had drilled him in it during his second year, when Beatrix was nineteen and resentful of every hour spent copying symbols that "no living soul would ever read." Aldous had rapped his knuckles with the brass stylus and said, in his dry way: *No one reads a lock either, boy, until the day they need what's inside it.*

Now Beatrix stood over the plate with a magnifier and read the lock.

The harbor district was drawn as it always was: quays, basins, the customs house rendered in fine hatched stone, the warehouse rows along Salt Quay labeled by their registry letters. But threaded through the labels, in notations small enough to pass for survey corrections—little clusters of figures beside anchor points, elevation marks where no elevation needed marking—ran a second text.

He began at the upper left, because that was where Aldous had always begun anything, and worked symbol by symbol.

The first cluster resolved itself with a jolt that made him grip the bench. It was not an elevation. It was a date and a tonnage. The eleventh of Harvestmonth, the year of the famine's beginning, and beside it a figure in the thousands of bushels—entered, in the grammar of survey shorthand, exactly the way one would enter the measured capacity of a structure. Except the structure was Warehouse V-4, Voss registry, and the figure was not its capacity. It was its *contents*. Beatrix knew the difference because he had surveyed half those buildings himself; the capacities were already printed elsewhere on the plate, honest as daylight. This figure, tucked into a false benchmark, was nearly double.

Full. The warehouse had been full in Harvestmonth, while the relief ledgers recorded it as receiving grain earmarked for the northern parishes.

He turned the page of his master's old field notebook—he had brought it home after the funeral, along with everything else, meaning someday to sort it, meaning never—and found, folded inside the back cover, a slip of paper in Aldous's hand. Not a letter. A legend. A private key to the private language, mapping each disguised notation to its true meaning: *benchmark = quantity held; traverse line = route of transfer; spot height = price paid per quarter; depression contour = shortfall against relief obligation.*

Aldous had built himself a cipher and hidden it in plain sight, in the one document in the city guaranteed to be preserved, catalogued, and admired.

Beatrix laughed once—a single ugly syllable with nothing of humor in it—and then had to sit down on the drafting stool because his legs were not entirely his own.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the fog and found him surrounded by plates. He had pulled them all now: the harbor district, the river districts, the granary quarter, the coastal approaches where foreign grain made landfall. Eleven plates in all carried the marginalia, and once he held the key, he could not stop seeing it. What had looked like meticulousness—the trait everyone praised at Aldous's funeral, *such a thorough man, such precision*—resolved into testimony.

Warehouse by warehouse, week by week, the atlas told the story the letter only sketched. Grain landed at the foreign-quay anchors, entered in the shorthand with ship names abbreviated to their registry flags. Grain moved inland along traverse lines that matched no actual roads Beatrix had ever walked—because they were not roads; they were transfers, each line annotated with a spot height that was not height at all but the price per quarter, and the prices climbed across the famine months like a fever chart. And at the parish marks, where the relief obligation should have been discharged, the depression contours dipped and dipped—shortfalls, shortfalls, shortfalls—while the Voss warehouses along Salt Quay registered fuller and fuller.

He did the arithmetic twice, because the first time he assumed he had erred. He had not erred. In a single season, better than forty thousand quarters of grain designated for relief had been diverted—held back to inflate price, sold to speculators, shipped out again under neutral flags to markets that would pay what starving people could not. Forty thousand quarters. He tried to convert it into bread, into loaves, into the arithmetic of a family's daily ration, and stopped when the number became a street, became faces, became the men who still slept in the churchyard porticoes of the lower town because they had learned, that winter, that hunger does not always end when the harvest returns.

*He knew,* Beatrix thought. *All along, he knew. He was drawing it while it happened.*

That was the part that undid him—not the crime, monstrous as it was, but the patience. Aldous Marchetti had sat at this same bench, in this same cold, month after month, taking the Voss commission money—for the atlas had been commissioned, everyone knew that, the Voss name was embossed in gilt on the prospectus—and saying nothing, objecting never, while he encoded their guilt into the very monument they were paying him to raise. He must have been questioned. He must have dined with them, drunk their wine, accepted their compliments on his fine hand. And all the while every stroke of his pen was a deposition.

*Why didn't he publish? Why the hiding? Why sew a letter into the binding instead of shouting it from the customs house steps?*

Beatrix asked the empty workshop, and the empty workshop answered with the answer he had been avoiding since midnight: because shouting would have accomplished nothing except a dead surveyor. Because a man alone cannot accuse a family that owns the licenses, the newspapers, half the magistracy, and—as of this morning's offer—very nearly the atlas itself. Because the only archive the Vosses could not buy and burn was one they believed was a map.

*Look closely at what you are finishing.* He had finished it. Three nights ago he had inked the final coastline and felt the quiet satisfaction of a completed task, and all the while he had been signing his name to a confession he couldn't read.

His hand shook as he lifted the pen. Not fear—or not only fear. There was something else underneath, something that frightened him more than the Vosses did, because it felt like inheritance.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day making copies. Not of the plates—he could not copy eleven engraved plates in an afternoon—but of the marginalia itself, cluster by cluster, transcribed into plain figures in a cheap notebook he bought from the stationer on Tallow Lane, paying coin rather than credit so the purchase would leave no ledger trail. It was the first deliberately secretive act of his life, and he was surprised by how naturally it came, how quickly the mind adapted to its own concealment. By the fourth hour he had developed a system, by the sixth he had caught himself checking the window out of habit rather than necessity, and by dusk the notebook held the entire skeleton of the scheme in numbers a child could follow: names, dates, quantities, prices, routes.

Forty thousand quarters. Sixteen parishes defrauded. One family.

He ate nothing. He drank the cold tea and then forgot he had drunk it. Once, the harbor bell tolled and he lost count of which hour it was striking, and realized with distant surprise that the world outside the workshop had continued to exist all day—carters in the street, gulls over the basin, the ordinary commerce of a city that had been fed lies for three years and digested them without incident.

Near evening, someone knocked.

Beatrix went absolutely still, notebook under his palm, heart hammering against his ribs. The knock came again—two raps, a pause, one rap. Not the guild's pattern. Not a creditor's. He crossed the room and opened the door on the landlady's girl, holding a parcel wrapped in sacking.

"Left at the door, sir. No name." She shrugged at his expression. "Been sitting there since morning, likely. Fog's thick."

He shut the door and unwrapped it standing, with the caution of a man who has spent one day learning paranoia and applies it with a beginner's excess. Inside, wrapped again in oilcloth, was a book: Aldous's personal copy of the atlas prospectus, the one Beatrix had searched for after the funeral and been told was lost. Tucked into its front cover, a note in a handwriting he did not recognize, a woman's hand, slanted and hard:

*You will have found the letter by now—you were the only one who could have. Before you do anything brave and stupid, come and see me. My father owed me an explanation for twenty years. You owe me one for tonight. — E.M.*

Below it, an address in the river district. Below that, one more line, smaller:

*I have things he never sent you. Ledger pages. Come before someone else remembers I exist.*

Beatrix stood holding the note a long time, fog breathing against the glass, the lamp guttering. A daughter. Of course—a daughter. Aldous had spoken of his estranged girl exactly never, and Beatrix had asked exactly never, and now the silence between them stood revealed as one more thing the old man had engineered, or merely permitted, or simply failed at, the way he had failed at everything except the map.

Ledger pages. If they were what she claimed, they were corroboration—numbers from outside the atlas, numbers no clever enemy could dismiss as a cartographer's conceit. Numbers that would turn a hidden cipher into evidence.

Or a trap. The thought arrived punctually, wearing Mr. Quill's polite face, and Beatrix examined it honestly before setting it aside. A trap would not need to be so well written.

He put on his coat. He took the notebook, and the letter, and the key, and after a moment's pause he took the harbor plate too, wrapped in felt against his chest like a breastplate. Then he blew out the lamp, locked the workshop behind him, and stepped down into the fog toward the river district—toward the master's daughter, and whatever else Aldous Marchetti had left buried in the world for someone brave enough, or stupid enough, to dig.

Behind him the atlas lay closed on the bench, gilt-lettered, magnificent, and full of ghosts.
