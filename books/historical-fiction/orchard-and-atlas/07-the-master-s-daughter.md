## 7. The Master's Daughter

The address Odile had given him was wrong, or the city had rearranged itself in the night. Beatrix stood at the mouth of Cooper's Lane with a scrap of paper going soft in his damp hand and fog pressing on his shoulders like a wet coat. *Salt-houses past the rope-walk, third door with a blue lintel.* There was no blue lintel. There were no salt-houses. There was only the lane narrowing ahead of him into a throat of grey brick, and somewhere down it, he was told, lived Elin Marchetti — the daughter Aldous Marchetti had mentioned exactly twice in eleven years of apprenticeship, both times in the tone of a man touching a bruise to see if it still hurt.

He had rehearsed this on the walk from the workshop. That was the trouble with rehearsing: the words wore smooth, like coins passed too often, and by the time he needed them they had nothing left to say. He had tried *I have something of your father's* and discarded it as a lie dressed as courtesy — what he had was not something, it was everything, it was the letter and the marginalia and the atlas itself, the whole devouring apparatus that had eaten her childhood. He had tried *I am sorry for your loss*, but the loss was eleven years old and she had presumably grieved it without his help, and besides, Beatrix was not at all certain the man was finished being lost.

So he walked into the lane unrehearsed, which was, he would think later, the only way he could have managed it.

The blue lintel turned out to be green, faded almost to grey, on a narrow house wedged between a chandler's store and a derelict counting office. A curtain twitched as he approached. He knocked. Nothing. He knocked again, and heard — distinctly — someone moving inside with the deliberate stillness of a person deciding whether to pretend not to be home.

"Madam Marchetti," he called, and then, because the fog seemed to swallow even his own voice, "My name is Fairweather. I worked for your father."

The silence changed quality. It did not warm. It sharpened, like a blade being turned over.

The door opened on a chain, six inches of lamplit interior and one eye, brown and unimpressed. "Fairweather." The eye took his measure: coat gone shiny at the cuffs, ink-stained fingers, the particular pallor of a man who had spent the winter bent over plates by candlelight. "The apprentice. The quiet one."

"I was the only one," Beatrix said. "For the last four years."

"And you've come now." The eye did not blink. "Eleven days after the funeral you didn't attend."

"I wasn't—"

"You weren't invited. I know. I sent no invitation. I know that too." She closed the door. The chain rattled. And then, to his surprise, it opened fully, and Elin Marchetti stood before him in a working apron over a good dress gone shabby, a woman perhaps forty with her father's heavy brow and none of his softness. "Come in, then, since you're standing there dripping on my step. But understand me, Mr. Fairweather: I have buried that man twice. Once when he chose the atlas over us, and once when the city did it formally. You get nothing from me by knocking on grief's door unless you've brought something heavier than condolences."

* * *

The interior of the house was a rebuke to everything he knew of its owner's father. Marchetti's workshop had been chaos sanctified — charts layered on charts, pens breeding in cups, the sweet rot of old glue — but here every surface was bare, every object aligned, the fire small and economical. On the mantel stood a single portrait in a cheap frame: a woman Beatrix did not recognize, young, squinting against sun. Not Marchetti. Her mother, then. The wife whose death, if the workshop rumors were true, had driven the master deeper into the coastlines than any grief could plausibly reach.

Elin set a kettle on the hob without asking whether he wanted tea, which meant she had decided he did, which meant she was already managing him. Beatrix sat where indicated, on a hard chair, and put his hands around the cup when it came because they needed something honest to do.

"Say it," she said, standing, arms folded. "Whatever it is. You have the face of a man carrying a parcel he regrets accepting."

Beatrix looked into the tea. Then he said the true thing, the thing he had failed to rehearse because he had not known until this moment that it was true:

"Your father didn't die drafting an atlas. He died having finished one — finished it years ago, in everything but the printing. What he was doing all those late nights, what killed him by inches at that desk, was hiding something inside it. In the margins. In survey notes so dry that no one but another cartographer would ever read them twice." He looked up. "Coordinates of warehouses, Mrs. Marchetti. Grain tonnages written as soundings. The famine year, mapped out plain as a coastline, if you know how to read it."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the fog.

"The Vosses," she said.

It was not a question, and Beatrix felt the floor tilt slightly under his certainty. "You knew?"

"I knew my father stopped sleeping in 1843," she said. "I knew he came home from a dinner at the Voss house white as paper and never accepted another. I knew he started locking his proofs, and that when Mother died he didn't even pause the work, and that I hated—" her voice caught, disciplined itself, continued flatly, "—that I hated a set of copper plates more than I have ever hated a living person. So no, Mr. Fairweather. I didn't know about warehouses. But I knew there was something under the atlas. Everyone who loved him knew there was something under the atlas. We just weren't permitted to look."

"He was protecting you."

"Don't." The word came out like a slammed drawer. "Don't you dare launder him for me. Protection is a story men tell after the fact. My father had a choice every morning of his life between his family and his great work, and every morning he chose the work, and now you arrive at my door telling me the work was *noble*, that all those years I sat across a dinner table from a man reading proofs instead of speaking to me, he was secretly a martyr. Do you know what that does? It makes the waste worse. If he'd simply been vain, I could despise him cleanly. Instead I'm asked to despise him *gratefully*."

Beatrix held the cup very still. He understood, dimly and painfully, that she was right, and that his own eleven years of comfortable anonymity had been purchased with the same coin — the master's absence, the master's obsession, the master's slow self-destruction at a desk while an apprentice learned the trade in the space a daughter should have occupied.

"There's a letter," he said quietly. "Sewn into the binding of the plate proofs. In his hand. It names names. It gives dates. It says what the Voss ships carried out of the famine ports, and what they carried back in, and what was sold and where, and it ends with instructions — that whoever found it should finish what he couldn't."

"Finish what?" Her arms were still folded, but her knuckles had whitened. "What couldn't he finish?"

"The truth. In print. Before they buy it or burn it."

Elin Marchetti laughed. It was the worst sound Beatrix had heard in months — worse than Quill's silk courtesies, worse than the guild men murmuring over their ledgers — because it was her father's laugh, the rare dry bark he'd let slip over some elegant piece of draftsmanship, transplanted into a stranger's face in a bare room above the rope-walk.

"They came to me first, you know," she said. "Three weeks ago. Before they went to you. A pleasant man — Quill — with an offer for 'any papers of a professional nature' left in the estate. Generous terms. I told him the papers were yours, that I hadn't spoken to my father in six years, that the atlas could burn for all I cared." She sat down at last, opposite him, and Beatrix saw that her hands, freed from the discipline of the folded arms, were trembling. "And then I lay awake all night wondering if I'd just sold the only part of him that mattered."

* * *

They talked until the tea went cold and the fog outside turned from grey to the color of dirty wool. She wanted the letter read aloud, twice; she wanted the marginalia explained, sounding by sounding, and Beatrix watched her take in the shape of her father's hidden courage the way a diver takes in a wreck — methodically, unsentimentally, cataloguing everything. Twice she stopped him and made him repeat a passage, and once she pressed her fist briefly against her mouth, and Beatrix pretended to be consulting the notes so she could do it unseen.

"Why did you really come?" she asked finally. "Not for corroboration. You have the printer woman and the customs man, whoever they are. Why the estranged daughter?"

Because, he might have said, the letter named her — a line near the end, *to my daughter, if she will read it, tell her the hours were not theft*. Because the atlas needed an heir beyond its tradesmen. But what he said was simpler and truer:

"Because history happens to everyone, Mrs. Marchetti. The famine happened to people who never saw a grain ship. Your father's silence happened to you. The Voss version of events will happen to everyone in this city unless someone writes otherwise. You were written by his choices. I was too — I just didn't feel the pen until recently." He hesitated. "I thought you should choose."

Elin was silent for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost its armor and gained something harder underneath, some bedrock the armor had been guarding.

"When I was nine," she said, "he took me to the survey office and showed me a chart of the eastern coast and asked me what was missing from it. I said, *the towns*. He said, *no, the towns are there, see the little marks*. I said, *no — the people in them*. He laughed and said maps record places, never people, and that was the whole of my inheritance, Mr. Fairweather. A man who drew the world beautifully and left everyone out of it. Including me." She stood, crossed to the mantel, and touched the frame of her mother's portrait without looking at it. "If I help you — and I haven't said I will — it won't be for him. It will be to finish the map properly. With the people in it."

"That's the finest brief any cartographer ever received," Beatrix said.

She almost smiled. It was, he suspected, a dangerous sign — the moment bitterness begins converting itself into purpose, which is the most flammable substance in the world.

Then footsteps sounded on the stair outside — unhurried, deliberate, two sets of them — and Elin's head came up sharply, and Beatrix saw her go in an instant from widow-of-sorts to sentry.

"No one climbs to this floor by accident," she said quietly. "The chandler below has no upstairs customers."

Through the window's warped glass, down in Cooper's Lane, Beatrix could make out two figures standing at the corner in the fog, motionless, positioned with the patience of men being paid by the hour. One of them raised a hand, casually, toward the house with the faded green lintel — not a wave. A signal.

"Elin," Beatrix said, rising, "how attached are you to leaving through the back?"
