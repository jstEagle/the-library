## 8. A Fire in the Archive

Tobias Renner had worked at the Municipal Archive of Records and Seizures for nineteen years, and in all that time the building had never been warm.

It was not meant to be. Damp was the enemy of paper, but so was dryness, and so was light, and so were people—people with their oils and their breath and their careless hands. So the archive stood on Cordage Lane behind the customs house, a long stone shed of a building with windows fogged from the inside, lit by whale-oil lamps turned low, and heated by nothing at all. The clerks wore fingerless gloves eight months of the year. Tobias had worn them for nineteen winters, and his hands had grown so accustomed to cold that warmth felt like an accusation.

He arrived before dawn because he always arrived before dawn, because the manifests had to be collated before the day's seizures came in, because routine was the wall he had built between himself and everything that could hurt him. He unlocked the door with the key on the chain under his shirt. He lit the lamp over Bay Fourteen. He took down his coat—the good wool one, Marta's mending still visible at the cuff if you knew where to look—and hung it on its hook.

And then he stopped, because the air smelled wrong.

Not smoke. Not yet. But underneath the familiar smell of dust and lamp oil and old glue there was something sweeter, something like tallow burning in a room where no candle burned, and when he looked down the central aisle toward Bay One, the far end of the hall, he saw a shape moving in the dark that should not have moved.

"Hello?" His voice came out smaller than he intended. "The archive opens at eight."

The shape resolved into a man carrying a cask on his shoulder, and the man was not one of theirs. Tobias knew every porter, every night watchman, every rat-catcher who held a contract with the city. This man wore a laborer's smock with no guild mark, and he moved without the shuffling economy of a man doing honest work. He set the cask down against the shelving in Bay One with the tenderness of a gardener planting something.

Tobias did what a cautious man does. He stepped backward, quietly, out through the door he had come in by, and pulled it shut, and put the key back around his neck, and ran—not to the watch-house, which was four streets away and staffed by men whose wages he could guess, but to the corner of Cordage Lane where the lamplighter's ladder leaned against the wall, and from there he watched the window of Bay One.

Within ten minutes, the window glowed.

* * *

By the time the fire bells began, half the port was awake and standing in the street in their nightclothes, watching orange light climb the archive's high windows like ivy growing in fast motion. The fire brigade came with their engine and their chains and formed a line to the canal, and they fought it, Tobias would grant them that—they fought it seriously, professionally, for nearly two hours—but everyone in the crowd understood within the first quarter hour what Tobias understood from the moment he'd seen the cask: the fire had been set in the right place, at the right hour, with the patience of people who had done their reading.

Bay One held the grain-year manifests. 1843, 1844, 1845. Every seizure, every bonded warehouse entry, every diversion order signed and countersigned. Three years of paper that said, in the city's own dull bureaucratic hand, exactly what a dead cartographer's letter said the Voss family had done.

Tobias stood in the crowd with his hands in his pockets and let the heat wash over his face, and he thought about his pension.

Nineteen years. He had calculated it a thousand times the way other men calculate a debt or a sin: fourteen more years to full entitlement, a cottage upriver, Clara's schooling paid through her fifteenth year. A cautious life, carefully banked, earning its small interest. And last week he had taken a box of manifests home in his coat—one box, Bay Fourteen, the duplicate seizure records from the autumn of 1844—and now the fire had come, and the fire had come *after* he had taken the box, and either those two facts were strangers to each other or they were not.

A woman beside him crossed herself. Somewhere a child cried and was hushed. The roof beams went with a sound like a ship breaking up, and a column of sparks stood up into the fog and hung there, a red tree in the grey air, and then collapsed.

Tobias turned and walked home. He did not run. Running drew the eye. He walked at the pace of a clerk going late to work, past the chandlery, past the Voss counting-house with its brass fittings catching the firelight, and he did not look at it, and looking away from it told him everything he needed to know about who owned the fire.

Clara met him at the door, already dressed, her dark hair braided wrong the way she did it herself now that she was twelve and too old for help. "Papa, the archive—"

"I know."

"Is it—" She had inherited her mother's habit of finishing other people's dread. "Is it gone?"

"The building is going." He unwound his scarf. His fingers were steady; he made a point of noticing that they were. "I need you to go upstairs and take the loose brick out from under my side of the wardrobe, and bring me what's wrapped in oilcloth, and then forget you ever touched it."

She looked at him for a long moment. Marta's eyes. Marta's way of measuring a man before deciding whether to obey him or argue. "You're frightened," she said.

"Yes."

"But you're going to do it anyway."

"Yes."

She went upstairs. He heard the scrape of the brick. When she brought the bundle down—a box the size of a hymnal, tied in oilcloth and string—he took it and kissed the top of her head and smelled woodsmoke in her hair, and understood that whatever happened next, his daughter already lived in the same country he had just emigrated to: the country of people who had been counted.

* * *

The box contained forty-one duplicate manifest sheets from September to December of 1844. Tobias sat at his kitchen table with the lamp turned up and read them the way he had read manifests for nineteen years—quickly, mechanically, the numbers sliding through him without friction—and for the first time he forced himself to stop and see what the numbers said.

Here: the brig *Anselma*, inbound under bond, listed cargo eleven hundred sacks of wheat, entered at the Grain Bonding Warehouse No. 3. Here, six days later, the same vessel's discharge record: nine hundred sacks, entered at Warehouse No. 7, a warehouse whose lease—Tobias kept a private index of leases the way some men keep racing forms—was held by Voss & Sons Factors. Two hundred sacks of relief grain, vanished between one page and the next, in a season when the price of bread had doubled and the burial registers along the coast road had tripled.

Here again. And here. Forty-one sheets, and on thirty-three of them the same small arithmetic of disappearance, each one signed by a junior examiner whose name Tobias knew—Hollis, a weak-chinned man who now drove a carriage for the family he had once inspected.

It was not proof, quite. Duplicates could be dismissed as clerical error, transcription faults, the ordinary rot of any bureaucracy. But stacked against Elin Marchetti's father's letter—against the coordinates Beatrix Fairweather had shown him disguised as survey notes, warehouse by warehouse, tonnage by tonnage—it was not error. It was pattern. It was the same map drawn twice, once by a dying man in ink and once by the merchants themselves in the city's own hand.

Tobias copied the key figures onto a single sheet in his smallest script, folded it small, and sewed it into the lining of his winter coat, under the collar, where Marta had once hidden coins from him during the bad spring after her mother died. Then he sat with the originals and considered the fire.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. The fire had come *after* the box left the building—or the fire had come regardless, and the box was coincidence, and the sensible thing, the pensioned thing, was to believe in coincidence. He thought of Mr. Quill, whom he had seen once across the customs house floor, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs who asked questions the way a physician asks where it hurts. He thought of Hollis driving his fine carriage. He thought of the night watchman at the archive, old Prewitt, who had let the cask-carrier in or slept through him, and who this morning was being questioned by superiors who would find, in the end, that the failure was Prewitt's and no one else's.

They will burn the record, and then they will burn the witness, and they will do the second more slowly.

Clara appeared in the doorway in her nightgown, though he had told her to sleep. "You've been sitting there for an hour," she said.

"I'm working."

"You're deciding." She came and stood at the table's edge, arms folded, twelve years old and ancient. Her mother used to stand exactly so. "Marta's brother's wife sent word the coastal parishes lost another forty children in the famine winter. Forty. In one parish." She recited it flatly, a child repeating sums. "Nobody wrote it anywhere except the burial book."

Tobias looked down at the forty-one sheets, at the neat columns, at the two hundred sacks that had become nothing between one line and the next, and felt something shift in his chest that he recognized, distantly, from the week Marta died: the moment when fear stops being a wall and becomes a floor you can stand on.

"They'll come asking whether anything left the building before the fire," he said. "There will be an inquiry. There will be a list. My name will be near the top, because I had keys and I was seen leaving early."

"Then we should leave earlier," Clara said. "Permanently."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "If we run, we confirm it, and the record burns twice—once in the building and once in the telling. No. We stay. I go to work tomorrow at the temporary office they'll set up, and I file my reports, and I am the most boring man in the port." He tapped the coat with the sewn lining. "And this stays sewn where it is until it can be useful. Not before. Useful, Clara. Remember that word. Everything we do from today has to be useful, or it's just grief wearing a brave coat."

She considered this with her mother's gravity, and then, being twelve, ruined it entirely: "Grief would look better in a brave coat than your old one."

Despite everything—because of everything—he laughed, and the laugh cracked something open, and for a moment he let himself be afraid properly, all the way through, the way a man is allowed at his own kitchen table with his own child. Then he blew out the lamp.

* * *

The temporary office opened the next morning in two rented rooms above the salt weighhouse, and by noon the story was settled, smooth as river stone: a faulty flue, an unlucky spark, oil stored too near the shelving by a negligent watchman. Prewitt had confessed, they said. Prewitt, who had served thirty-one years, had broken down under questioning and confessed to negligence, and would face the magistrates alone.

Tobias filed his morning report. He sharpened his pens. He answered a supervisor's questions about Bay Fourteen's duplicate policy with the flat thoroughness of a man who has nothing in his pockets, and watched the supervisor's eyes, and saw in them nothing at all—which was worse. Empty eyes are trained eyes.

At midday a runner brought him a note. He expected requisitions. It was a single line, unsigned, written in a fair commercial hand:

*A careful man keeps only what he can account for. The Guild of Cartographers has inquiries of its own. — a friend of accuracy*

He read it three times. Then he fed it to the stove and watched the corner catch and curl, and thought: *the atlas*. Fairweather and his plates and the master's marginalia—they were moving too, whoever else knew or suspected. The fire had been meant to leave the letter orphaned, uncorroborated, a madman's scribble sewn into a dead man's atlas. Instead it had left Tobias Renner holding the only duplicate manifests in existence, and someone—Fairweather, or the printer woman, or the daughter—knew it.

That evening he took the long way home along the quay, coat collar up, the sewn paper riding against his neck like a second pulse. At the mouth of Orchard Road, where the cobblestones gave way to mud and the derelict farmhouses stood black against the dusk, a figure detached itself from a gate and fell into step beside him: Odile Ferrant, the disgraced printer, smelling of ink and woodsmoke, walking with the rolling gait of a woman who had spent her life lifting type.

"You got the note," she said. It was not a question.

"My daughter says grief should wear a brave coat," Tobias answered, because he had decided, somewhere on the quay, that if he was going to be a marked man he might as well be a marked man with allies, and because he found, to his surprise, that he liked this woman's walk.

Odile glanced sideways at him. "Your daughter sounds smarter than most magistrates. Come on, then. There's a farmhouse at the end of the lane with a stove that works and a press that doesn't yet, and people inside who need to see your handwriting." She nodded back toward the dark fields. "Bring the coat. Bring everything the coat knows."

Tobias looked back once, down the long grey length of the harbor, at the smoke still rising faintly from Cordage Lane, at the counting-houses with their lit windows, at the whole orderly machine of the city arranging itself around a lie. Nineteen years he had been a man who kept records. Tonight, walking into the dark at the end of Orchard Road with a dead archive sewn into his collar, he understood that the keeping had only ever been the apprenticeship.

Somewhere ahead, a door opened and lamplight spilled across the mud, and voices—four, maybe five—went quiet as he approached, the way conspirators' voices do, and then, hearing his name spoken by Odile, resumed, warmer.

He stepped through.
