## 9. What the Victorians Will Write

The invitation came on cream cardstock, edged in gilt, smelling faintly of violets—the perfume of money that had never had to apologize for itself.

*Mr. and Mrs. Aldous Voss request the pleasure of the company of Mrs. Elin Marchetti at a supper commemorating the fifth anniversary of the Relief Commission, Graywater House, Thursday evening.*

Elin read it four times. The word *commemorating* sat on the page like a fly in milk. Five years since the famine year—five years since the winter her father had stopped writing to her, stopped answering her letters, stopped being anything but a bent silhouette over his drafting table in her imagination. Five years since the grain ships had sailed past the starving harbor towns with their holds full and their manifests sealed.

They were going to *commemorate* it. The way one commemorates a battle won.

She should have burned the invitation. She knew that even as she turned it over in her hands, feeling the weight of good paper, paper made from rags that someone poor had once worn. But her father was dead now, and the atlas he had died drafting sat half-finished in Beatrix Fairweather's cold workshop, and somewhere in that tangle of coastlines and soundings there might be—if the letter Beatrix described was true—a record of what the Voss family had done while the city ate its candles and boiled its boot leather.

If they wanted her in the room where the story was being shaped, then she wanted to be in it too. Not as a guest. As a witness.

She sent her acceptance by the morning post, and spent the intervening days hating herself for the tremor in her hand when she wrote it.

* * *

Graywater House stood above the harbor like a man who had never once looked down. Its façade was pale limestone quarried from the same hills that had starved the crofters who'd carved it, and its windows were tall enough to admit the light without admitting the view of the fish-quay below, where the smell of tar and offal rose every afternoon like an accusation.

Elin arrived at eight o'clock in a borrowed gown—her landlady's sister's, a decade out of fashion, dyed a deep bottle green that made her feel like a bottle herself: sealed, glassy, full of something volatile. The footman who took her cloak did not quite look at her. She understood why. She was not gentry. She was a curiosity—the dead cartographer's estranged daughter, summoned for reasons no one had explained to her, and the servants had already decided she was either a charity case or a trophy, and were leaning toward trophy because trophies were more interesting to gossip about.

The entrance hall alone could have housed the workshop where her father had spent thirty years bent over copper plates. Marble underfoot, cold as riverbed stone. A chandelier like a frozen explosion. And on the walls—not portraits, as she'd expected, but maps.

She stopped walking.

Maps. Dozens of them. Framed in gilt, hung with museum precision, each one bearing the small printed cartouche she knew better than her own signature: *Surveyed and Engraved by the Office of A. Marchetti.* Her father's work. Every coastline he had ever drawn, purchased by the family whose name he had sewn into the binding of his final atlas like a knife wrapped in linen.

"He was the finest draftsman of his generation."

Aldous Voss had come up behind her without sound, which she would learn was a habit of his—the quietness of a man accustomed to rooms rearranging themselves around him. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft, well-fed face of a portrait sitter and eyes that did all the working. He offered his hand, and she took it, and his grip was dry and brief as a clerk's receipt.

"You have his hands," he observed, tilting his head at hers. "Long fingers. Draftsman's fingers."

"My mother's hands," Elin said. "My father's were stained to the wrist for thirty years. Copper, ink, acid. You could always tell which window was his by the color of the light through it." She smiled, and felt the smile arrive like a weapon being drawn. "You knew him well?"

"I employed him. Which is a kind of knowing." Voss gestured her deeper into the house, toward the rising murmur of the assembly. "He was difficult, you know. Brilliant men usually are. But he drew the coast like no one else—every rock, every shoal, as if he'd been born drowning. We shall miss him."

*We shall miss him.* Spoken the way one misses a good horse.

The supper room opened before her, and Elin stepped into the warm gold light of it and felt her stomach tighten. Forty guests at least, maybe fifty, glittering under the chandelier: aldermen in their chains, naval officers in dress uniform, two magistrates, a bishop's suffragan, a newspaper editor she recognized from his caricature, and scattered among them the wives and daughters whose laughter carried the real business of the room, the way birdsong carries the fact of a forest.

Waiters moved through it all with oysters and champagne. Oysters. In the fifth year after the famine year, they were eating oysters.

"There she is," said a woman beside the fireplace, in the carrying voice of one who has never needed to wonder whether she is overheard. "Mrs. Marchetti. The daughter."

And so the introductions began, and Elin moved through them like a swimmer moving through cold water, smiling, nodding, letting the names break over her without purchase. Lady Voss—Aldous's sister-in-law, a handsome woman built like a figurehead—kissed the air near her cheek and pronounced her "the image of your dear father." The editor, a Mr. Calloway of the *Gazette*, shook her hand with both of his and told her the city owed her family "a debt of gratitude." An elderly admiral told her a long story about sounding the outer channel in '39, in which her father appeared only as "your parent, madam, the little fellow with the squint"—her father had no squint—and required three corrections she was too careful to make aloud.

It was during the soup that the histories began to be written.

* * *

She noticed it first as a rhythm. Aldous Voss moved from table to table between courses—he did not sit at the head; he *circulated*, the spider does not web himself into one corner—and everywhere he paused, the conversation bent toward the same shape. She heard it at the far end of the room first, a fragment carried on the noise:

"...and of course the Commission acted with the information available at the time..."

Then closer, at the admiral's elbow: "...no one could have foreseen the failure of the western harvests, none of the returns suggested..."

Then directly across from her, Voss himself, standing with his back to the great marble mantelpiece, addressing a cluster of six or seven listeners with the modest reluctance of a man asked to repeat a compliment: "I will say only this. When the blight came, my late father did not hesitate. He convened the Commission within the week. Grain was purchased—at ruinous prices, gentlemen, ruinous—warehouses were leased, relief committees formed in every parish. That mistakes occurred—" he lifted his hands, palms open, a gesture of terrible humility— "that some of the machinery of mercy moved slowly, I do not deny. It was a storm, gentlemen. And we bailed water until our hands bled. History will judge us kindly, I believe, because history judges effort, and effort there was, and plenty of it."

Applause. Modest, murmured applause, the kind given between courses.

Elin set down her spoon.

The machinery of mercy. She turned the phrase over in her mind and found it perfectly machined—each word fitted to the next with the same precision her father had brought to a coastline, no gap anywhere for a fact to slip through. *Acted with the information available.* Not a lie, exactly. A frame. Build the frame correctly and whatever truth remained outside it would simply never be seen. The famine became weather. The diverted grain ships became *purchased grain*. The sealed manifests became prudence. The thousands dead in the harbor parishes became—she searched the room, the candlelight, the faces—for the moment they became nothing at all. Absent. Unmapped.

Her father used to say that a map was an argument about the world. She had thought it a craftsman's vanity. Now she watched the argument being drafted in real time, in a supper room, over oysters, and understood that whoever finished the draft owned the coast forever.

"The soup is to your liking, I hope."

Lady Voss again, materializing at her shoulder with a smile of perfect porcelain. "You've hardly touched it."

"It's excellent," Elin said. "I was listening."

"Ah." The woman followed her gaze across the room to where Aldous Voss held court. Something flickered behind the porcelain—amusement, or warning; with these people the two were likely the same organ. "Aldous does love the subject. He's preparing a memoir, you know. Of the Commission years. The publisher is already engaged." She sipped her wine. "It will be the definitive account. All the documents are in the family's possession, naturally. One can't rely on rumor and secondhand recollection—one wants the *papers*, the actual papers. Everything else is just... talk."

"Everything else," Elin repeated.

"Mm. Talk." Lady Voss smiled at her, warmly, and Elin understood that the smile was a door being closed in her face with great courtesy. "Your father's papers too, of course. Such a loss that his last work was left unfinished. Aldous hopes to acquire the plates—you know how he treasures your father's hand. For completeness. A collection wants its whole."

There it was. Delivered between the fish course and the meat, priced at nothing, aimed with care: *we are coming for the atlas.* They had already tried Beatrix with money and failed—or had they? She realized she did not know. She had been in this house for two hours and already the room was doing its work on her, dissolving certainty into etiquette.

"And if Mr. Fairweather refuses?" Elin asked.

The pause was very small. A professional's pause. "Why would he refuse?" said Lady Voss pleasantly, and drifted away toward the bishop's man.

* * *

Later—after the meat, after the sweet, after the toast to the memory of the late patriarch Voss ("a public servant in the truest sense," said the magistrate, and forty glasses rose)—Elin escaped to the gallery of maps.

The crowd's noise fell away behind her. Gaslight ran along the gilt frames in long yellow streaks, and her father's coastlines glowed under it, patient and immaculate. She walked the length of the room slowly. Here was the eastern cape he'd surveyed the summer she turned nine, when he'd been gone eleven weeks and sent her a drawing of a lighthouse with a note: *This is what I am looking at. What are you looking at?* She had written back *nothing*, and meant it as an accusation, and he had framed the letter. Here was the harbor chart, the one that hung in every shipping office in the city, the one on which the grain ships' routes had been planned—routes drawn by her father's hand, she saw now, or at least begun there, and finished by others for other purposes.

A map was an argument about the world. And the Vosses had bought every argument her father had ever made, hung them in gilt, and called it tribute.

"They tell me you are the cartographer's daughter."

The voice came from the shadowed end of the gallery. A thin man detached himself from the dark between two windows—unfashionable, unremarkable, dressed in gray that cost more than her year's rent, with a face like a folded document. He had been watching her for some time. She was almost sure of it.

"I am," she said.

"Elin Marchetti." He said it as though confirming an entry in a ledger. "My name is Quill. I manage certain affairs for the family." He did not offer his hand. "I confess I hoped to speak with you before the evening ended. There is a matter of your father's estate—unfinished business—that concerns the family's interests."

"The atlas," Elin said.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite approval. Recognition, perhaps—the small satisfaction of a man who finds a lock exactly as complicated as advertised. "The atlas," he agreed. "Mr. Fairweather has proven... sentimental. Sentiment is understandable. It is also expensive, for everyone concerned." He glanced up at the harbor chart above them, at the tiny engraved ships riding her father's engraved sea. "I wonder, Mrs. Marchetti, whether you know what your father kept in his workshop. Whether he confided in you. Late in life especially. Men near the end become careless with paper."

The question slid past her like a blade passed flat along a whetstone. She heard what lived underneath it: *We know about the letter. We don't yet know what you know.*

"My father confided in his maps," she said. "That was the whole of him. Ask anyone. Ask the family that employed him for thirty years."

Quill regarded her for a long moment. Then he inclined his head, precisely, a bow calibrated to the inch. "Of course. Forgive me. Grief makes one blunt, and banquets make me worse." He began to move past her, then paused, and spoke without turning, in a tone so mild it took a heartbeat to understand it as anything other than courtesy.

"Do enjoy the rest of the evening, Mrs. Marchetti. And do give Mr. Fairweather my regards. Tell him the guild's licensing session approaches, and that licenses, like memories, can be renewed—or allowed to lapse." He walked on into the dark between the frames. "History is generous to those who help write it. It can afford to be. It has so many pens."

Then he was gone, absorbed into the light and noise of the supper room, and Elin stood alone beneath her father's harbor chart with her heart going hard against the bottle-green silk.

She looked up at the engraved ships, riding their engraved sea toward the engraved harbor, five years gone now, their holds full, their manifests sealed. Somewhere beyond the gallery wall, the definitive history was being toasted in champagne, and the papers that would prove otherwise lay hidden in a binding in a cold workshop across the city—in the keeping of a stubborn young man with her mother's hands, whom a man named Quill had just promised to destroy.

Elin straightened her borrowed gloves.

She would need a hackney. She would need to reach Beatrix Fairweather tonight, before Quill's courtesy reached him first.
