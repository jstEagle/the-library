## 11. Orchard Road

The farmhouse appeared out of the fog the way drowned things appear to sailors: suddenly, and all at once, as if it had been waiting just below the surface of the world.

Odile saw it first because she was the one who knew where to look. She had not been down Orchard Road in eleven years—not since the funeral, not since she had stood in this same drizzle with her hands empty and her brother's coffin between six hired men who had never met him—and yet her feet remembered every rut of it. The cart wheels complained beneath them. Beatrix held the lantern low so its light would not carry past the hedgerows, and by that stingy yellow glow the orchard rose around them, ancient apple trees gone feral, their branches knotted into shapes like hands mid-argument, heavy now with small hard fruit nobody would pick.

"There," she said, though there was nothing to point at but darkness. "Past the third wall. The house sits behind the rise so you can't see the harbor from the porch. Lucien always said that was the point of it."

"Was he a careful man, your brother?" Tobias asked from the back of the cart, with his daughter's blanket-wrapped bundle against his chest. The girl had slept through the whole journey, mercifully, her breath making small clouds in the cold.

"He was a man who kept his accounts honestly," Odile said. "In this city, that made him careful whether he liked it or not."

She did not say the rest: that honesty was what had killed him, eventually, in the slow way honesty kills—by degrees, by debts called in, by the seizure of a press that had printed one pamphlet too many on behalf of dockworkers who could not pay for their own defense. Lucien Ferrant had died owing the guild three hundred crowns and owing Odile everything else. The farmhouse had passed to her the way burdens pass, through probate and grief, and she had let it sit these eleven years the way one lets a wound sit under a bandage one is afraid to change.

Now she climbed down and changed it.

The gate had fallen off its hinges sometime in the last decade; she stepped over it where it lay in the weeds like a felled soldier. The front door was locked still—Lucien had been thorough even in dying—but the key was where he had sworn it would be when they were children playing at smugglers, wedged in the gap between the second chimney stone and the drainpipe, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Her fingers found it before her eyes did. Some inheritances are written into the body.

Inside, the air was cold and sweet with rotting apples and old plaster. She lit two candles, then a lamp, and the room assembled itself out of the dark: a kitchen table scarred white with knife-cuts, four chairs, a dresser with its doors hanging crooked, a hearth swept clean by mice or by some neighbor with a guilty conscience. On the mantel stood a clay pipe and a jar of buttons and nothing else. Lucien had owned almost nothing worth stealing, which meant the house had been left alone, which meant—for tonight—it was safe.

Beatrix came in last, carrying the crate. He set it down on the table with the exaggerated gentleness of a man handling something explosive, which, in a sense, he was. Inside the crate, nested in straw, sat the letter in its broken seal, the plate proofs, and the atlas itself—the masterwork of Aldous Marchetti, the licensed surveyor whose name opened doors and whose marginalia, as of nine days ago, opened graves.

"We shouldn't light the fire until we've checked the flue," Tobias said. He was already moving through the room with a customs clerk's instinct for inspection, pressing his palm to walls, peering into cupboards, testing the shutters. "Smoke visible from the road after dark. Anyone watching would know the house is lived in."

"And here I thought you were the cautious one," Odile said.

"I am being cautious." He did not smile. He was a narrow man, Renner, gray at the temples and gray in the voice, the sort of man who had spent twenty years signing manifests for grain that never reached the mouths it was billed for and telling himself the signature was not the same as the crime. Nine days ago she might have pitied him. Now she watched him check the flue with a taper, watch the smoke draw properly upward, and thought: this is what a man looks like when he has decided to be guilty of something better.

* * *

They ate bread and hard cheese and the last of a jar of pickled onions Tobias had brought from his own pantry, as though fleeing one's pension were an ordinary errand requiring provisions. Mira, waking briefly, ate half a pear from the orchard floor—bruised but sound, like everything in this house—and went back to sleep in the bundle of blankets by the hearth.

It was around the table, by lamplight, that they finally spoke plainly.

"The press," Beatrix said. "That's the question. Everything else we can do by hand—copy the letter, transcribe the marginalia, swear affidavits. But a letter can be called forgery. An affidavit can be bought. A printed page, set in type, run off in hundreds—" He stopped, because Odile was shaking her head.

"No."

"No?" Elin Marchetti looked up sharply. She had been quiet all evening, sitting with her father's atlas open before her like a widow with a portrait, touching the edges of the plates as if checking for wounds. "Odile, if you know where a press is—"

"I know where my press *was*," Odile said. "The guild seized it four years ago. The type cases, the platen machine, the ink stock, all of it. They auctioned the furniture and melted—I was told they melted—the type." She heard her own voice go flat and knew they could hear the old wound in it, and hated them a little for hearing it, and hated herself more for the hatred. "You don't understand what a seizure means. It isn't losing equipment. A printer's type is her vocabulary. They took away my ability to say things."

The silence stretched. Rain began again on the roof, soft as fingertips.

Then Elin turned the atlas around on the table so it faced Odile, and opened it to the middle pages, and tapped one long finger on a margin note Odile had seen a dozen times without ever reading it closely—a column of figures disguised as tidal corrections.

"My father bought type," Elin said. "I didn't understand it until this week. There are entries in his ledgers—entries I always assumed were for engraving tools—payments to a type founder across the harbor, in installments, spread over three years. Small amounts. The kind of amounts a man pays when he is accumulating something slowly enough that no single purchase will alarm anyone." She looked up, and Odile saw in her face the same expression she imagined lived in her own: grief and fury poured into the same vessel until they were indistinguishable. "He was building a second press. Hidden. He must have known what the Voss material would cost him to publish through any legitimate house. So he built the means himself and died before he could use it."

"Where?" Tobias asked quietly.

"That," Elin said, "is what we're going to find out."

* * *

Odile slept—or lay awake pretending to—in the room that had been Lucien's, under a quilt that still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of him: pipe smoke and lye soap and the particular staleness of a man who worked alone. Through the thin wall she could hear the others settling. Beatrix had taken the chair by the hearth rather than any bed, and sat in it still; she could tell by the absence of his breathing among the sleeping sounds. Tobias and Mira shared the back room. Elin had folded herself onto the kitchen bench with the atlas under her head like other women used pillows, and had been asleep within minutes, the sleep of someone who has stopped fighting exhaustion and simply surrendered to it.

Odile got up and went downstairs and stood in the kitchen in the dark.

This was the room where she and Lucien had argued about the pamphlets. He had sat at this table setting type by hand for the dockworkers' fund, and she had told him he was a fool, that honest work printing almanacs and bills of lading paid the rent and kept the guild off their backs, and he had looked at her with those patient Ferrant eyes and said, *"Odile. Someone has to print the true version, or the false one is the only one that exists."*

She had thought him naive. She had thought it right up to the morning the guild officers carried his press out in pieces, and she had thought it right for four more years of printing wedding invitations and shop signs under another man's license, invisible, harmless, safe.

Safe. She turned the word over like a coin she suspected was clipped.

The truth was that safety had been a transaction all along, and she had never examined what she was paying for it. Her brother's ruin had purchased her caution. The famine year itself—nine hundred souls in this city alone, dead of hunger while granaries stood full under Voss seals—had purchased everyone's comfort, everyone's respectable silence, everyone's well-fed ordinary mornings. Invisibility was not a shelter. It was a tax collected from the dead, and she had paid it gladly, and called it prudence.

Well. No longer.

* * *

They found the press on the second day.

Elin had gone back into the city alone—against everyone's advice, over everyone's objections, with a market basket on her arm and the unassailable cover of a woman buying fish—to search her father's townhouse, which had stood sealed since his death, pending settlement of an estate the Voss lawyers had shown sudden, suspicious interest in expediting. She returned at dusk on the ferry from the fish quay, walking the last mile up Orchard Road in a grey drizzle with her hood down and her chin up, daring the fog to try anything.

"It's here," she said, standing dripping in the doorway, holding up a key on a length of twine. "Under the floor of the coal cellar. Under a floor *under* the floor. He cast the frame pieces as ordinary machinery parts—my father, who couldn't drive a nail straight, bribed a foundryman and drew diagrams good enough to fool a mechanic. The type is in two barrels marked 'ballast.' Three hundredweight of it, Odile. Minuscule sizes. Body text." She was laughing now, or something adjacent to laughter, wet-eyed and wild. "He'd have needed a year to set a pamphlet at that rate. He was going to set a *book*."

Odile took the key from her hand and closed her fist around it until the teeth bit her palm, because she needed the pain to believe it.

A press. Not a seized press, not a guild-licensed press, not a press that existed in any ledger anywhere in the city. Her brother had died owning a press he could never use. Marchetti had died owning one he never got to start. And here in this rotten orchard house, at this scarred table, the two legacies had collided and produced—what? Not safety. Never that again. But a chance. A small, dangerous, hand-set chance that the true version might exist.

"We'll need a tympan and frisket cut fresh," she heard herself saying, already working, already pacing the kitchen, measuring the table with her eye. "The platen will want cleaning and the springs inspecting—four years in a cellar, the grease will have gone to resin. Ink we can buy openly, in small lots, different chandlers. Paper is the difficulty; paper leaves trails. Beatrix—"

But Beatrix was not listening. He had taken the atlas from the crate and was turning it toward the lamp, toward page after page of those modest marginal notes, and his face had gone strange.

"Tobias," he said. "Come look at this. All of you. Look at the harbor chart—the 1843 survey, the one we all praised for its precision." His finger moved along the coastline. "Every warehouse along the east quays. Every one. He surveyed them individually, at a level of detail far beyond what a coastal chart requires. Tonnage capacities in the depth soundings. Dates in the tide tables. He wasn't drawing the coast." Beatrix looked up, and the lamplight caught something in his eyes that Odile recognized from her brother's face in the last year of his life—that terrible clarity of a person who has understood exactly how much a thing will cost and has decided to pay it. "He was drawing the crime. The whole atlas is the testimony. It's been sitting in plain sight for four years, licensed by their own guild, sold in their own shops. Nobody burned it because nobody could read it."

"*We* can read it," Tobias said. His daughter stirred in the blankets by the hearth, and he lowered his voice, but the words lost none of their weight for the lowering. "With the ledger numbers I copied before the archive fire—what survived—we can annotate every figure. Name every ship. Date every diversion." He paused, and Odile watched him look at his sleeping child, and watched him choose, again, deliberately, the way a man chooses a road knowing it does not come back. "My daughter will grow up either way. In one version of the world, her father signed manifests for murderers. In the other, he helped print the correction. I know which record I want her to find."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The rain filled the silence, friendly now, a curtain instead of a threat.

It was Elin who broke it, quietly, looking around the kitchen—at Odile, at Tobias, at Beatrix, at the crate with its impossible cargo—as though taking attendance of something newly formed.

"My father spent ten years drawing this alone," she said. "Hiding it from his patrons, hiding it from me, hiding it, God forgive him, inside the very thing they paid him for. He died thinking no one would ever read it." She put her hand flat on the atlas, on her father's coastlines. "He was wrong. That's all any of us can hope for, I think. To be wrong about dying unread."

Odile went to the window then, on some pretext of checking the shutter, and looked out into the dark orchard. Fog lay along the rows of trees like a tide going out. Somewhere beyond the hedge, down at the harbor, the city glowed faintly with its lamps and its lies—the Voss halls, the guild chambers, the archives burning or already burnt, Mr. Quill and men like him moving through it all with their purses and their patience. The victors were writing their histories even now, she had no doubt, in warm rooms, with good wine.

Let them write. Tomorrow she would begin cleaning type.

* * *

On the third night, with the press assembled in the old barn and covered under sacking that smelled of apples, with the type sorted into cases by candlelight and Beatrix sketching diversion-route maps at the kitchen table until his candle drowned, Odile dreamed of Lucien.

Not the funeral dream she usually had, the coffin dream. This time he was sitting at this very table, setting type by hand, and he looked up at her the way he had in the argument years ago and said, *"Someone has to print the true version."*

"I know," she told him. "I'm printing it. We're printing it. There's four of us, and a clerk, and a child, and three hundredweight of your ballast."

Lucien smiled his patient smile and went on setting type, letter by letter, and she woke with dawn coming grey through the shutters and the smell of apples everywhere, and lay still a moment in the cold room, listening to the house: Mira laughing at something in the yard, Tobias's low voice answering, Elin and Beatrix arguing happily about kerning, of all things, two people who had never met a month ago quarreling like siblings over the spacing of letters.

Fragile. All of it, desperately fragile—one informant, one fire, one raid away from ash. She knew that the way she knew the weight of type in her hands.

But she lay there listening anyway, and found to her astonishment that what she felt was not fear.

Downstairs, the kettle was already on. And on the kitchen table, weighted down with the jar of buttons, waiting for her as though the house itself had set it out, was the first composed galley proof—three lines of her brother's salvaged type, spelling out the opening sentence of the thing that would get them all killed:

*In the year 1843, the firm of Voss & Sons received nine hundred thousand pounds of grain earmarked for famine relief.*

Odile came down the stairs and picked it up and read it aloud to the kitchen, to the found family, to the fog-bound orchard beyond the windows, her voice steadier than she had any right to expect.

From the lane outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hooves on the wet road—two horses, moving slowly, stopping at the fallen gate.
