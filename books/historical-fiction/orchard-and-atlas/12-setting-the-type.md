## 12. Setting the Type

The press had come to the orchard in three journeys, disguised as a dead woman's furniture.

That was Odile's joke, and she repeated it every time someone new helped unload a case, because a joke kept fear at arm's length better than a prayer did. The press itself—her press, the one the magistrates' men had hauled out of her shop on Rope Lane with an axe and a writ and no apology—had been sold at auction to a dealer in salvage who owed her brother a debt. Her brother was dead, but debts to dead men have a way of ripening. The salvage dealer had crated it as "upholstered chairs, damaged" and carted it out through the grain gate on a morning so foggy the harbor master couldn't count his own fingers.

Now it stood in the old cider room of the Marchetti farmhouse, under a roof that leaked in two places and a floor that smelled, faintly and forever, of apples gone to vinegar. Tobias had laid oilcloth over the worst of the damp. Elin had swept out six years of swallows' nests. And Odile had spent two nights with her sleeves rolled and her hands black to the wrist, coaxing the screw, checking the platen, remembering—in her wrists before her head—the particular weight and temper of the machine that had cost her everything and was about to be asked to give it all back.

"You're smiling," Beatrix said from the doorway.

"I'm remembering how to hate properly." She ran her palm along the carriage rail. "A press is just a lever, mapmaker. You put truth on one end and enough weight on the other, and something gives."

"And which are we?"

She looked at him—at this careful, ink-stained man who had spent his whole life being nobody, and who had crossed the city carrying a dead man's letter against his chest like contraband because he couldn't bear to be nobody anymore. "We're the weight," she said.

* * *

They worked by lamplight with shutters drawn, because light leaking from a ruin was a confession.

Odile set the type herself for the first pages; there was no one else whose hands knew the work. She'd been eleven when her father first let her compose a line—*set it reading, girl, never mirror-wise, or you'll print your shame backwards*—and thirty years of that discipline lived in her fingertips now, sorting sorts by touch in the near-dark, the brass of the type warm where her thumb had held it. She had lost her shop, her license, her name in the trade registers. She had not lost this. Her hands still knew the way into the case: lower left, e; upper right, t; the a's worn smooth as river stones from a lifetime of use.

The letter came first. Master Aldous Fairweather's dying testimony, copied fair in Beatrix's hand because the original would stay hidden—sewn back into its binding, its hiding place now the safest library in the city—and then broken into paragraphs that could be set, proofed, distributed. Each paragraph was a fact. Odile insisted on facts. No rhetoric, no thunder. *In the months of February through June of 1846, the barque Voss's Fortune discharged 4,100 sacks of wheat at Warehouse Seven, Salt Quay.* Then the next line. Then the next. Let the numbers do the screaming; numbers were harder to hang than adjectives.

Beatrix sat across the cider room at a trestle table with the atlas proofs spread around him like a surgeon's instruments, drafting.

She watched him when he didn't know it. He worked the way her brother used to mend nets—patient, total, his whole mind sunk into the mesh of the thing. Before him lay the great coastal plates of the atlas, and over them he was laying a new sheet, tracing by lamp and ruling pen the routes that Aldous Fairweather had disguised as survey notes. It was beautiful work. That was the thing she hadn't expected, the thing that made her angry in a way she couldn't spend: it was *beautiful*. The diversion routes ran across the chart in fine vermilion—the grain barges leaving the famine districts full-bound and arriving, via three transshipments and one conveniently fogged customs station, at Voss warehouses on the far side of the strait. The tonnages were annotated in a tiny, elegant italic hand. The warehouses were marked with survey symbols any guild examiner would pass without a second look.

"He hid it in plain sight," she said once, standing behind him. "Your master."

"He hid it in the only place they'd never burn," Beatrix said quietly. "An atlas with the family's own name on the subscription plate."

* * *

By the fourth night they had a rhythm, and rhythm was its own kind of shelter.

Tobias Renner arrived after dark each evening, coming the long way through the orchard lanes with his collar up, bringing copies of manifests copied in a clerk's flawless, deniable hand. He never stayed past midnight. He checked every figure against the letter twice, then a third time unasked, because Tobias was a man who believed that if the arithmetic was perfect, the world might be persuaded to behave arithmetically. When he found a discrepancy—a sack count off by twelve between the letter and the seized manifest—he didn't panic. He sat very still, pushed his spectacles up, and said, "Twelve sacks is a village, Mr. Fairweather. We will account for those twelve sacks or we print nothing."

They accounted for them. A re-shipment, logged twice, in Fairweather's marginal note in the atlas's eastern sheet. Tobias wrote the reconciliation into the copy text in his small even figures, and Odile set it, and somewhere in the setting of it she felt something in her chest unknot that had been tied since Rope Lane.

Elin Marchetti cut paper and washed ink balls and learned, with a ferocity that startled Odile, to read composed type upside down and backward. She had her father's hands—long, impatient, precise. The first night she'd stood in the doorway of the cider room with her arms crossed and her jaw set, daring the room to ask her to feel something about the press or the cause or the father whose house this had been. Nobody asked. Odile handed her a composing stick and showed her where the quads lived, and that was that. Grief can't argue with work.

"Lowercase f is gone," Elin announced on the fifth night. "And I'm short on em-quads."

"There's a case in the loft, wrapped in sacking. My brother's." Odile heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used for prices and other hard things. "He bought it the month before they took me. Said a Ferrant should always have a spare alphabet lying about."

Elin went up for it without another word, and brought it down, and blew the dust off the lid, and set it beside hers, and neither woman said anything else about it. But when Odile reached for an f an hour later, it was there, and her brother's spare alphabet was feeding her brother's killers' exposure one letter at a time, and that, she thought, was the best sentence anyone had ever composed in this room.

* * *

The centerpiece took Beatrix nine days.

He called it simply the Map, capital-M, the way you'd say God. One plate, folded into the pamphlet at its heart: the whole diversion shown at once. The famine districts shaded grey—the districts where the relief granaries stood empty while the ships sailed heavy. The routes in vermilion. The warehouses numbered. The tonnages tabulated in a border margin neat as scripture. And in the corner, where a cartographer would normally put his compass rose, Beatrix drew instead a scale unlike anything Odile had seen on a chart: not leagues, but lives. *One sack = forty loaves = four days of bread for one family.* Every route line on the map could be read in either currency.

When he first pulled the proof and pinned it to the beam, nobody spoke for a long time.

Tobias took off his spectacles and polished them, though they were clean. Elin stood with her arms wrapped around herself, looking at the grey districts—looking, Odile guessed, at whatever part of the grey her father had walked through collecting his evidence while she was refusing his letters in another city.

"It reads," Beatrix said at last, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he started again. "It reads like a map. That's the point. They taught us maps don't take sides. Well. Here is a map taking a side so hard it will leave a dent."

"It'll leave more than a dent," Odile said. "It'll leave a crater with their name at the bottom." She touched the wet proof—her proof, pulled on her press, from type she had set—and felt the small damp miracle of it. Six years since her hands had made a true thing. She had printed broadsheets since, smuggled ones, angry ones, the sedition that cost her the shop. But this was different. This was *documented*. This had a customs clerk's arithmetic and a cartographer's plate and a dead man's sworn letter underneath it. You couldn't call this a pamphlet. This was a *record*, wearing a pamphlet's coat.

"We need a title page," Elin said.

They argued titles until the candle drowned. Odile wanted something blunt—*The Grain Accounts*. Tobias wanted something official-sounding, a forgery of respectability—*A True Report to the Commissioners*. It was Beatrix, tired and ink-bitten, who said, "Let it be what it is. An atlas supplement. *Orchard and Atlas: A Supplemental Plate, with Testimony.* Let them come for it with a warrant and find it licensed-looking, respectable-looking, and damning anyway." Elin laughed for the first time in the cider room—a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed, but real.

They set the title page last, and Odile saved the largest type for it, the display fount she'd buried under her brother's floorboards the week of her arrest and dug up again the week of her redemption. As she composed the words—reading, always, mirror-wise—she caught herself doing what compositors do: mouthing each word aloud as it went into the stick, backwards, letter by letter. *S-u-p-p-l-e-m-e-n-t-a-l.* Her breath made little ghosts on the cold type.

Her brother used to stand beside her doing exactly this. She set the final period, locked the chase, and let herself, for exactly three seconds, miss him so badly her knees nearly went. Then she lifted the form onto the bed of the press, and the missing turned into fuel, and she pulled the first proof of the title page into the lantern light.

* * *

It was near dawn on the ninth day, with the pamphlet half-set and the Map drying in stacks, that Tobias came through the door too fast.

Odile knew before he spoke. She'd watched men carry bad news in their shoulders through a printer's door for twenty years. He stood dripping orchard rain on the floorstones, holding his hat in both hands like a schoolboy, and he looked at Beatrix first, then at her, and his mouth opened twice before it worked.

"The archive," he said. "On Quay Street. There were men there tonight—Voss colors on their collars, though they'll swear otherwise. Carting ledgers out. The manifests. The seizure records. Everything that touches '46." His voice dropped. "And a mason was sent for, with lime and brick. They mean to close a section of the stacks, Odile. Wall it up. Officially, for 'damp remediation.'"

Elin said, "Can they do that?"

"They can do anything," Tobias said, "that no one stops and no one writes down." He looked at the half-composed forms on the stone bench, at the Map hanging on its beam, at the little army of type waiting in its cases like soldiers at attention. "Which means we are now racing a wall. Whatever they don't destroy, they'll bury. Our numbers exist in three places now, gentlemen and lady: in my copies, in that letter, and in that metal. Two of the three can be taken from us."

Odile looked around her cider room—at the press her enemies thought was firewood, at the daughter setting type by lamplight, at the mapmaker already reaching for his pen to check a figure—and felt the old fear rise in her throat, and felt something older than the fear rise higher.

"Then we set faster," she said. She picked up her composing stick.
