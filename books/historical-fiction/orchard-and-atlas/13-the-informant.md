## 13. The Informant

Tobias Renner had spent thirty-one years in the Customs House learning one lesson above all others: that trouble never announced itself with trumpets. It arrived the way fog arrived off the harbor—quietly, at an hour when honest men were tired, and by the time you saw it clearly it had already closed around your throat.

He was thinking of this on a gray Thursday morning as he climbed the worn steps of the Customs House, because the fog that day was thick enough to taste, coal-smoke and brine on the tongue, and because for three weeks now he had felt the fog of something worse gathering around the edges of his ordinary life.

The box of manifests sat in his office safe where it had sat since the night of the fire. One box, pulled from a burning archive while men he could not name watched from the shadows across the yard. He had told himself it was an act of clerkish stubbornness—paper was paper, and paper belonged to the record, not to whoever held the torch. But he knew better now. The box was not paper. It was a verdict waiting to be read aloud, and everyone who touched it became a party to the sentence.

He unlocked his office, hung his coat on its nail, and did what he had done every working morning for three decades: he checked the ledger shelf.

The gap was still there.

Three weeks ago, a single volume had gone missing from the 1846 seizure manifests—the year of the diverted grain, the year the Voss ships had sailed light out of the harbor while the granaries along Orchard Road stood empty and the death registers filled. He had noticed the absence the way he noticed everything, silently, cataloguing it against future need. He had assumed carelessness. A volume borrowed by some junior examiner and misfiled, or carried home and forgotten under a bed.

This morning, standing before the shelf with the fog pressing against the window like a hand, Tobias understood that no one had been careless at all.

* * *

His colleague's name was Aldous Wrenn, and Tobias had trusted him longer than he had trusted most members of his own family.

Wrenn occupied the desk opposite Tobias's office door, had occupied it for nineteen years, and in all that time had never once failed to greet him with the same dry joke about the weather or the coffee or the eternal complaints of shipping agents. When Tobias's wife Maren died, it was Wrenn who had sat with him in the mourning room of the Custom House chapel, saying nothing, which was the only thing worth saying. When Tobias's daughter Liesel took her clerk's examination, Wrenn had tutored her in tariff law over three Sundays, refusing payment, refusing thanks, waving both away with the same modest motion of his ringed hand.

Ringed. Tobias thought of the rings now—two of them, plain gold, unremarkable—and of how he had never asked where a senior clerk's salary found the money for gold, even plain gold. You did not ask such questions of friends. That was what friendship meant in a place like the Customs House: an agreement not to look too closely at one another's hands.

He waited until the mid-morning lull, then crossed to Wrenn's desk under the pretense of returning a borrowed magnifier.

"Quiet morning," Tobias said.

"Quiet as the grave." Wrenn looked up, and his smile was exactly the smile it always was, warm and slightly rueful, the smile of a man perpetually amused by the small absurdities of bureaucracy. "Which reminds me—you look terrible, Renner. Sleeping?"

"Badly."

"You want to take something for that." Wrenn tapped his own temple. "A clear conscience is the best physic ever compounded. Cheap, too."

Tobias set the magnifier down. His hands were steady; thirty-one years of signing documents under the eyes of suspicious examiners had taught them steadiness. "You heard anything," he said, lightly, "about the missing volume? Forty-six seizures?"

Something passed behind Wrenn's eyes. It lasted less than a heartbeat—a flicker, a shutter drawn and redrawn—but Tobias had spent his life reading faces across counters, watching smugglers decide whether to lie, and he caught it the way a fisherman catches the tell-tale twitch of a line.

"Not a word," Wrenn said. "Why? Missing something of yours?"

"No. Nothing of mine." Tobias smiled his own smile, the mild, forgettable one, and went back into his office, and closed the door, and stood with his back against it while his heart performed the slow, sick arithmetic of betrayal.

Because he remembered now. He remembered everything, once the shutter-flicker opened the door to remembering.

Two weeks ago, Wrenn had stayed late—unusual, but explicable, there had been a bonded-goods audit. Two weeks ago, Wrenn had asked him, casually, over coffee, whether Tobias was sleeping well, whether he seemed worried lately, whether the fire at the archive had unsettled him. *You've been distracted, old friend,* Wrenn had said. *Since the fire. Anyone would be.* And two weeks ago, a junior porter had mentioned, in passing, that he'd seen Mr. Wrenn speaking in the street with a gentleman in a gray coat—"not from here," the porter said, "some kind of agent, very fine manners"—and Tobias had thought nothing of it, because why would he think anything of it, because Wrenn was Wrenn, because Wrenn had sat with him in the chapel when Maren died.

Tobias sat down at his desk and put his face in his hands.

*Mr. Quill.* He had never met the man, but Odile Ferrant had described him twice, precisely, the way she described everything—gray coat, gray eyes, manners so fine they were a kind of violence. Quill was the Vosses' instrument, their finder, their knife that walked. And Quill had been walking beside Aldous Wrenn on a public street, and within days a manifest volume had vanished, and within days after that Wrenn had begun asking gentle, loving questions about Tobias's sleep.

They were building a file on him. Of course they were. That was how such men worked—not with pistols in alleys but with dossiers, with leverage assembled grain by grain, until a man's whole life lay in someone else's drawer, ready to be poured out wherever it would do the most harm.

And Tobias Renner, cautious man, careful man, man who had spent three decades ensuring he was beneath notice, had handed them everything they needed. He had confirmed the letter's numbers against the seized manifests. He had copied pages. He had given those copies to Beatrix Fairweather and the woman Ferrant and Elin Marchetti, whoever and wherever they were now, hidden somewhere in this city like embers in straw.

There was only one reason Quill would want a dossier on Tobias Renner.

Not to stop him. To find the others through him.

* * *

He left the Customs House at half past four, an hour early, pleading a dental appointment he did not have. The fog had thickened rather than lifted, and the gaslights along Harbor Street burned as pale smudges, each one wrapped in its own small cloud. He walked without hurrying, taking the long way, doubling back twice out of habits he'd learned from watching the watchers, and all the while his mind ran its circuits like a rat in a maze.

Warn them. Don't warn them. Warn them.

If he warned them—if he sent word to the orchard farmhouse outside the walls, or however one reached them now—he exposed himself completely. Wrenn would report the early departure; Wrenn perhaps already had. A message intercepted, a messenger followed, and the whole fragile enterprise would burn down around them, press and plates and people together. And Tobias would lose everything he had: his pension, his post, his name, and—

And Liesel.

That was the blade of it. That was the knife turning slowly between his ribs as he walked. They would not need to threaten him directly. They had something better than threats. They had Liesel—nineteen years old, newly badged, working her first posting in the bonded warehouses, walking every day through doors her father's enemies controlled. A word in the right ear and her career ended before it began. A word in another ear, and a girl who had done nothing, who knew nothing, who had begged him—*Papa, please, stay out of it, please*—would find herself standing in front of an inquiry board answering questions about her father's associations, her father's papers, her father's midnight copying of documents that belonged to the Crown's archive.

He had promised her. He had stood in their narrow kitchen with its blue tiles and promised, hand on her shoulder, that he would be careful, that he would stay invisible, that caution had kept him alive through famine and fever and forty years of paperwork and would keep them both safe now.

*Caution,* said a voice in him that sounded like his dead wife. *Your caution let the Vosses sail their empty ships. Your caution is what Wrenn is counting on.*

Maren had never been cautious. Maren had marched in the bread riots of '46 with Liesel strapped to her chest, and when he'd raged at her she had looked at him with those level dark eyes and said: *Someone has to be seen doing it, Tobias, or next year they'll do it again, and the year after, and they'll do it to our daughter.* She had died three years later of the lung-fever, ordinary and unfair, but her words had lived in him like a splinter working toward the surface ever since.

He stopped at the corner of Chandler's Lane, under a lamp whose flame fought the fog, and made himself look at the choice plainly, the way he looked at a column of figures that refused to balance.

Option the first: silence. Say nothing, do nothing, trust that Wrenn's information was incomplete, that Quill would find nothing, that the farmhouse would hold. And if it didn't—if soldiers came to the orchard because Aldous Wrenn, his friend, his mourner-in-the-chapel, had sold the coordinates for gold rings and a quiet conscience—then Tobias Renner would have chosen his pension over other people's lives, and he would know it, every morning, for the rest of his careful life. He would be a man who balanced his books by writing other people's deaths in the margin.

Option the second: warning. Send word tonight, by whatever channel remained—Odile Ferrant had left him a means, a chandler's shop near the North Gate, a name to ask for, a phrase to say. Risk interception. Risk Wrenn noticing his absence, his agitation, the change in his gait. Risk everything, including Liesel, because if Quill learned that Tobias had broken his pattern, the polite pressure on the daughter might become something less polite.

Both roads led through his child. That was the cruelty of it, the exquisite design of it. Quill had built the trap that way, whether knowingly or not: any move Tobias made endangered Liesel, and standing still endangered everyone else. Loyalty and loyalty, grinding against each other like millstones, and Tobias caught between them with the grain of his whole life about to be crushed.

He thought of the letter then—Fairweather's master's letter, sewn into the atlas binding, written by a dying man who had known exactly what it would cost and had paid it anyway. *I have charted this coast for forty years,* the dead cartographer had written, *and I am ashamed to say I drew every mile of it and named none of the drowned.* Something like that. Elin Marchetti had read it aloud once, in the kitchen of the orchard house, her voice cracking on the last line, and Tobias had looked down at the table and seen not wood grain but water, the harbor water, black and patient, full of names.

Maps record places, Fairweather said. Never people. Well. Someone had to start recording the people.

Tobias turned up his collar and walked north, toward the North Gate, toward the chandler's shop, walking not fast and not slow, the walk of a man going about ordinary business, and inside himself he was screaming.

* * *

The chandler's shop smelled of tallow and rope-end and the particular staleness of places that stored things nobody wanted to think about. The proprietor was a squat woman with forearms like hawsers, and when Tobias said the phrase—"I'm told you stock the long wicks, for the winter voyages"—she studied him for a long moment, then jerked her chin toward the back room.

The boy was maybe sixteen, sharp-faced, quick-eyed, one of Odile Ferrant's printer's apprentices saved from the wreck of her old shop. He listened to Tobias with the stillness of a animal deciding whether to bolt.

"Aldous Wrenn," Tobias repeated. "Senior examiner, Customs House. He's been talking to a man in a gray coat. The manifest volume from '46 is gone from the archive—they didn't burn it, they *took* it, which means they wanted it read, which means they're assembling their own case. And Wrenn's been asking about my sleep, my habits, my moods, for two weeks. They're mapping me, boy, the way your mistress maps type. Letter by letter. And when the map is finished—" He stopped, steadied himself. "Tell Ferrant. Tell them all. Tell them the Customs House knows something, and that the leak runs through Wrenn, and that I don't know how much he has. Maybe he has only me. Maybe he has more. They must assume the worst and move accordingly."

"And you?" the boy said. "Will you come out there? Tonight?"

"I can't." The word came out of him like a tooth. "My daughter works the bonded warehouses. If I vanish the same night my warning arrives, they'll know it was me, and they'll take it out of her. I have to be at my desk tomorrow morning looking exactly as boring as I have looked for thirty-one years."

The boy nodded slowly. "Ferrant says you're the bravest man she knows."

"Ferrant," Tobias said, "has a romantic streak she hides under all that ink." He pulled his hat down. "Go. Now. Before the fog lifts and the streets get eyes."

* * *

He went home the long way again, past the bonded warehouses where Liesel worked, and stood across the street in the fog watching the lit windows, telling himself he was only making sure she'd gone home already, knowing perfectly well he was memorizing her, the way a man memorizes a coastline before a voyage he may not return from.

She was at the third-floor window, actually—her small silhouette bent over late paperwork, lamplight haloing her hair—and the sight of her undid him quietly, thoroughly, the way grief and love undo a man when he has no time scheduled for either. Nineteen years old. Her mother's jaw, her mother's stubbornness. Everything he had, arranged in a lit window across a street, available to any man in a gray coat who cared to reach out and close his hand.

*Choose,* said the millstones, grinding.

But he had already chosen. He had chosen in the chandler's shop, and he knew it, and the strange thing was that the choosing had not felt like courage. It had felt like arithmetic finally balancing—terrible sums, but true ones. A pension could be lost and survived. A daughter could perhaps be protected by a father's careful dullness, for a while, maybe. But the people at the farmhouse—Ferrant with her rage and her ruined hands, Marchetti with her inherited bitterness slowly transmuting into something harder and finer, young Fairweather carrying his dead master's atlas like a lantern—those people were trying to write the true record of the famine year, and if they were destroyed because Tobias Renner valued his own invisibility, then he deserved the historians the Vosses would hire. Deserved to be a footnote in their polished, lying book, if he appeared at all.

History happens to everyone, Fairweather had said once, awkwardly, earnestly, the way young men say true things. The only choice is whether you write it or are written.

Tobias Renner intended, at fifty-eight years of age, to write.

* * *

The next morning he was at his desk by eight o'clock precisely, coat on its nail, inkwell filled, spectacles polished. He greeted Wrenn with the usual nod and received the usual dry remark about the weather, and he laughed at it, the usual amount, and inside himself he observed his own performance with cold professional admiration: flawless. Thirty-one years of practice.

At ten o'clock, a clerk came through with the morning's correspondence, and among the routine circulars Tobias found an envelope of heavy cream paper with no franking mark whatsoever. Inside, a single card, engraved, scented faintly with some expensive spice:

*Mr. Quill presents his compliments to Mr. T. Renner, and requests the pleasure of a private conversation regarding matters of mutual interest. This evening, seven o'clock, the Meridian Rooms. No reply is necessary; attendance will be understood as courtesy, and non-attendance will be understood as well.*

Tobias read it twice. Then he folded it along its crease, slid it into his breast pocket, over his heart, where it sat like a coal.

Across the room, Aldous Wrenn glanced up from his desk and smiled at him—warm, rueful, familiar, the smile of nineteen years—and for the first time in their long acquaintance, Tobias smiled back and felt nothing at all except the precise location of the knife.

Seven o'clock. The Meridian Rooms. Quill wanted to meet him face to face, which meant the dossier was complete, which meant the map of Tobias Renner was finished and the time had come to make the offer or the threat—or both, delivered in that fine gray velvet voice.

They would offer him Liesel's safety. That was certain. They would offer it the way a man offers a drowning sailor a rope attached to an anchor, and Tobias would have to sit across the table and listen, and give nothing away, and buy hours—hours for Ferrant and the press, hours for the plates to move, hours for whatever desperate plan was forming out there beyond the walls—by pretending to consider selling every soul he loved.

He looked out the window at the fog-bound harbor, at the masts rising ghostly above the roofs, at the city that had let thousands die and was now so busy polishing its own innocence.

*Come then, Mr. Quill,* he thought, and pocketed the card, and reached for his pen, and signed his name to the morning's first document in his small, neat, unremarkable hand—the hand of a cautious man, about to do the least cautious thing of his life.
