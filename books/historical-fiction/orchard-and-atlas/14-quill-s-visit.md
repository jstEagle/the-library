## 14. Quill's Visit

The fog came off the harbor that morning in slow grey ropes, and Beatrix Fairweather watched it swallow the orchard road from the farmhouse window while the others slept.

They had been eleven days at Orchard Road. Eleven days of Odile setting type by lamplight with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands black to the second knuckle; eleven days of Tobias arriving after dark with documents sewn into his coat lining; eleven days of Elin learning to hold a composing stick without cursing it, though she still cursed it often enough. The exposé was half-formed now — forty pages set, the diversion-route map drafted in Beatrix's own hand, waiting for the engraver's needle. It had a shape. It had weight. For the first time since he'd found the letter sewn into his master's binding, the thing felt real rather than merely inevitable.

Which meant, Beatrix thought, watching the fog, that they were due.

He had learned this much about power in the weeks since the Voss heirs first sat across his workbench with their generous offer: power did not strike like lightning. It knocked. It arrived on schedule, dressed well, carrying something you wanted. The guild letter revoking his provisional license had come by courier, polite as an invitation. The second letter had not bothered with politeness — only a single line in a clerk's hand: *Consider what your master would have wished.* They were learning him. Testing which hinge of him would give.

So when the knock came — three measured raps at the farmhouse door, at nine in the morning, in open daylight, which was either arrogance or assurance — Beatrix was already dressed, already awake, already standing in the kitchen with his heart going hard and quiet against his ribs.

"I'll take it," Elin said from the stairs, pulling her shawl on.

"No." He kept his voice level. "He came for me."

"You don't know who—"

"A man doesn't knock twice," Beatrix said. "And no one else knows this road yet." *Yet* hung in the air between them, doing its ugly work. "Stay by the window. If I raise my voice, or if I'm not back at the door within the hour, take the type cases out through the north field and don't stop."

Odile appeared in the doorway of the parlor, a quill of her own tucked behind her ear — she'd taken to wearing one there out of some printer's superstition. She said nothing. She only looked at him, and in that look was everything she'd told him once over the kitchen table, the night she'd read the letter and named the famine year that took her brother: *They count on us wanting our old lives back more than we want them answerable.* She turned back to her press without a word, but she turned slowly, and he understood it as a blessing given in the only dialect she trusted.

Beatrix opened the door.

The man on the step was not what he expected, which was itself the first trick of him. Beatrix had imagined Quill — the name had reached them even here, passed along by Tobias's nervous contacts like a fever symptom — as something reptilian, all wet eyes and long patience. Instead he found a broad, comfortable man of perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, dressed in good drab wool that managed to suggest wealth without confessing it. His face was ruddy and open, the face of a man who coached a parish rowing crew. He held a leather satchel in one hand and a walking stick in the other, and he smiled at Beatrix with genuine, unguarded warmth.

"Master Fairweather." He extended his free hand. "Mr. Quill. I expect you know that already, so let me spare us both the theater: yes, that Mr. Quill, and no, I don't have men hiding in your orchard, because if I wanted you dragged anywhere I wouldn't need them. May I come in? It's beastly damp and I've walked from the toll road."

Every instinct Beatrix owned said *no*. But refusal, he reasoned, was also information, and he could not afford to be legible. He stepped aside.

Quill entered the farmhouse the way a man enters an old friend's home — pausing in the kitchen doorway to admire the low beams, setting his stick against the wall precisely, declining the chair Beatrix didn't offer by simply taking it anyway. He looked around at the kitchen table strewn with proofs, and his eyes — quick, blue, entirely unclouded — catalogued everything with a speed that belied his comfortable bulk. Beatrix saw the moment the cataloguing finished. Quill's expression didn't change at all, which was how Beatrix knew it was finished.

"You're further along than my sources suggested," Quill said pleasantly. "Forty pages, is it? And the map. That map will be the death of somebody, Master Fairweather, though perhaps not whose you intend."

"You've seen it?"

"I've seen descriptions of it." Quill unbuckled his satchel and began, unhurried, to lay out its contents on the scrubbed wood of the kitchen table, and Beatrix felt the floor tilt under him, because he knew those pages. He had drawn them. Drafts of the route map, in his own hand — the ones he'd believed burned with the first batch of spoiled proofs, the ones Tobias had carried to a contact in the customs house who had sworn —

"Don't," Quill said gently, watching his face. "Don't try to work out who. You'll torture yourself and get it wrong, and it hardly matters now. What matters—" he tapped the drafts "—is that I know exactly what you have. Not approximately. Exactly. Which means that today, Master Fairweather, we may speak honestly, and honest conversation is such a rare luxury in my line of work that I confess I look forward to it."

* * *

What Quill offered, he offered the way a physician offers medicine: soberly, kindly, with full disclosure of side effects.

"The letter first," he said. "Your master's letter. I want the original and every fair copy. I want the marginalia struck from the plates — I'll pay for the burnishing myself, my own craftsman, you can supervise. In exchange—"

He laid it out in items, ticking them off against the table edge with one blunt finger.

"First: your license. Full guild standing, restored this week, with the revocation expunged. You finish the atlas under your own name — your name alone, Master Fairweather, no taint of association — and it is published by the guild press with every honor the guild can confer. Second: five hundred crowns. Not a bribe; call it a commission, call it damages, spend it however eases your conscience. Third—" and here Quill's voice changed, softened, became almost tender, and this was the part Beatrix had not prepared for "—third, we restore Aldous Marchetti's reputation. Fully. Publicly."

Beatrix went very still.

"You think we want him disgraced?" Quill spread his hands. "Good God, no. A dead man's disgrace is useful to no one, least of all us. Here is what actually happened, as best I can piece it together, and you may believe it or not: Marchetti found the same evidence you found. He was drafting the atlas and he found it, in the manifests, in the tonnages, in the gaps where grain should have been. And being the man you apparently knew him to be, he couldn't decide what to do. So he hid it. Sewed it into his life's great work like a splinter under skin, and told no one — not his daughter, not his apprentice — and then his heart gave out before he chose. That's not cowardice, Master Fairweather. Or it is, but it's the cowardice of a decent man, which is the saddest kind. And it left you holding the decision he couldn't make. Which strikes me as a cruel thing for one friend to do another, whatever else it was."

"He wasn't my friend," Beatrix heard himself say. "He was my master."

"Were you not friends?" Quill asked, with such plain curiosity that Beatrix had no answer ready, and Quill nodded as though the silence were one. "Then consider: surrender the letter, and the atlas goes out clean. *Orchard and Atlas*, the masterpiece completed by the faithful apprentice. Marchetti remembered as the finest cartographer of his generation. No famine inquiry, no scandal, no heirs hounded through the courts — the famine happened eight years ago, young man, everyone who could have been saved is past saving, and all your edition will accomplish is teaching the city that its merchants are monsters and its historians are liars, which it will learn anyway, eventually, without your help. Whereas what I offer—" he leaned forward, and his ruddy face was full of something that looked terribly like compassion "—what I offer is that nobody else has to burn."

"Nobody else," Beatrix repeated.

"Tobias Renner walks free. His pension intact, his daughter's school fees paid — oh yes, we know about Miriam, we know about the manifests he copied, and I promise you the magistrate has better things to pursue than a grieving clerk. Odile Ferrant gets her press back. Her *name*, Master Fairweather. Do you know what sedition charges do to a printer? She could set type in daylight again." Quill ticked the last item. "And Elin Marchetti gets her father back — not the obsessive ghost who chose maps over her, but the man the city will mourn. She hates that atlas. I've made inquiries. Imagine handing her instead a father restored, and a brother-apprentice who chose her father's peace over a crusade."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a rook called from the bare apple trees, and the sound of it seemed to come from a great distance.

Beatrix understood, then, what he was looking at. He had imagined corruption as something monstrous, something with wet eyes. But Quill was a human face — warm, reasonable, tired around the eyes in a way that suggested he had done this many times and had never once enjoyed it. That was the horror of him. Not the threat. The *sincerity*. Somewhere behind that open face was a ledger, and in the ledger the dead of the famine year were entries, and entries could always be balanced against other entries, and Quill genuinely believed he was offering balance.

"You've done this before," Beatrix said. "This speech."

"Variations." Quill didn't flinch. "Most people, given the choice between justice and their friends' lives, choose their friends, and then hate themselves quietly forever, and tell themselves at funerals that they had no choice. I offer them the fiction of a choice because the fiction helps. Would you prefer I threaten you? Some men prefer it. It's cleaner. But you strike me as a man who'd find threats clarifying and kindness corrosive, so let me be kind, and let it corrode."

"It won't."

"No," Quill agreed mildly. "Probably not. Your master couldn't decide either, and you're cut from the same timber — that's not an insult, it's the highest compliment I know how to pay a man. But hear the cost of refusing, plainly, because you deserve plain dealing." He rose, retrieved his stick, and stood by the window where the fog was thinning into pale gold. "If you print it, the Voss family survives. Understand that first. We are too large, too woven-in; an inquiry will scratch us and the historians of thirty years hence will note that the matter was investigated and certain irregularities were acknowledged. What dies is smaller things. Renner will hang or rot — I won't decide that, the magistrate will, but I will make sure the magistrate is annoyed. Ferrant's press was seized once for sedition; a second seizure comes with prison, and she is not a young woman. The girl Marchetti — she'll be ruined by association, unmarriageable, unemployable, the daughter of the traitor cartographer. And you—" he turned, and his blue eyes were kind and terrible "—you will have signed your colophon, Master Fairweather. First time in your life you'll have put your name to anything. Make sure the thing you put it to is worth what it costs, because it will cost everything, and it will save no one. The dead stay dead either way. All you can change is who gets to sleep at night afterward."

He set a card on the table. Heavy stock, unmarked except for an address on the harbor front and tomorrow's date written in pencil.

"I sail on the evening packet the day after tomorrow," Quill said. "After I sail, the offer expires and the other instrument begins — the one that isn't me. I am, you should know, the polite version." He collected his drafts of the map, squared their edges, and left them in a neat stack on the table — deliberately, Beatrix realized; a message: *we have copies, burning yours means nothing.* At the door he paused and looked back, and for a moment the warmth fell away entirely, and Beatrix saw underneath it something older and wearier, a man who had carried ledgers so long they had shaped his shoulders.

"For what it's worth," Quill said, "I hope you refuse. Not for my employers' sake. It's just that I've bought back enough consciences to furnish a cathedral, and every single one of them rattles." He smiled his rowing-crew smile. "Good morning, Master Fairweather."

The door closed. Footsteps crunched down the frost-stiff lane and faded toward the toll road, unhurried, a man with nowhere urgent to be.

* * *

For a long time Beatrix didn't move. He stood in the middle of the kitchen with Quill's card on the table before him and the neat stack of his own stolen drafts beside it, and he let himself feel all of it — the license, the five hundred crowns, his master's name washed clean, Odile's press returned, Tobias safe, Elin's grief answered — he let the whole shining arrangement settle over him like the fog had settled over the harbor, and he was ashamed to discover how warm it was. How easy it would be. He thought of his cold workshop on Cooper's Lane, of the anonymous comfort of coastlines, of the man he had been four months ago who recorded places and never people and slept soundly every night of his invisible life.

Then he thought of the warehouse coordinates disguised as survey notes, and the tonnage figures hidden in soundings, and the years of his master's silent courage stitched into the binding — courage that had failed only at the last inch, the final signature, the act of naming — and he understood suddenly that this was the inheritance. Not the atlas. The unfinished decision. Aldous Marchetti had died leaving his apprentice the exact weight he himself could not carry, and every temptation Quill had just laid on the kitchen table was the same weight, gilded.

Elin came down the stairs. She had clearly heard none of it and divined all of it; she looked at the card, the drafts, his face.

"Well?" she said.

"He wants the letter by tomorrow night. In exchange, everything. Everyone walks away. My father—" she corrected herself, jaw tightening, "—*your* master is remembered as a saint."

"And if we refuse?"

Beatrix picked up Quill's card and held it to the stove until the corner caught, then dropped it hissing onto the iron. "Then the impolite version begins," he said. "Tonight, I think. Possibly sooner. Get Odile — we're moving the type cases to the north field tonight whether or not anyone comes. And send for Tobias." He was already reaching for his coat, his mind running ahead of his fear toward the one door Quill had left unlocked, the one move available to men who cannot fight and cannot flee. "If they can steal our drafts from inside our own circle, then we're finished hiding. So we stop hiding. Tomorrow we go to the guild — all of us — and we ask them, publicly, on the record, to license the atlas as drawn."

Elin stared at him. "The guild is bought. You said so yourself."

"One vote isn't," Beatrix said. "There's always one." He pulled the door open onto the thinning fog, onto the orchard rows standing pale and patient in the cold, twenty-two chapters of ordinary lives arranged like trees waiting for the axe or the spring. "And we have until the packet sails."
