## 15. The Raid on the Orchard

Elin Marchetti woke to the smell of woodsmoke and thought, for one merciful moment, that it was morning—that Odile was already at the stove in the farmhouse kitchen, coaxing heat from damp applewood, and that Tobias had come down from the loft where he slept with his boots beside him like a man who never fully trusted a floor to stay beneath him.

Then she heard the horses.

Not one horse. Many. Coming up the orchard road at a pace no honest rider used in fog this thick, because honest riders slowed for the ruts and the fallen branches and the old stone wall that the Vosses' grandfather had built when the orchard still belonged to working men instead of to memory.

She sat up in the narrow bed in what had been her father's room—the room he had never once brought her to, the room she now slept in because grief is strange and wants proximity—and she counted the hoofbeats the way her father had taught her to count survey chains. *One, two, four, more.* Eight. Perhaps ten.

"Tobias," she said aloud, though he was upstairs, though the walls were thin enough that her voice would find him. "Tobias, they're here."

The floorboards above her answered—his feet, quick but not panicked. She heard the loft hatch open, heard him say something low to Beatrix in the next room, and then the house woke all at once: Odile's door, the scrape of a chair, the muffled clink of type being covered with sacking in the back parlor where the press stood under its tarpaulin like a sleeping animal.

Elin dressed in the dark. She had practiced this without knowing she was practicing—two months of sleeping in her stays half-laced, of keeping her shoes by the bed pointing outward, of folding into one bundle everything she could not bear to lose: her father's last letter, the sealed original, the thin notebook of Tobias's verified figures written in his careful clerk's hand. It sat now in a oilcloth wrap beneath her pillow, and her hands went to it before her mind finished deciding.

Downstairs, the kitchen was already full of people moving quietly, which was worse than shouting. Quiet meant they understood. Shouting might have meant they still believed there was time.

"Out the cider cellar," Odile said. She stood by the cold hearth with an iron poker in her hand and her grey hair loose around her shoulders, and she looked, in that instant, exactly like the woman who had run a print shop through two riots and a sedition charge. "There's a culvert under the north wall. It comes up in the drainage ditch past the third field. You take the letter and the ledger and you don't stop."

"We're not leaving the press." Odile's own voice, from her own mouth—but Elin said it, and Odile turned to look at her with an expression Elin had seen once before, on the night they'd first set type together: recognition, reluctant and fierce, of someone who might be made of the same material.

"The press is iron and beech," Odile said. "You are the only person alive who can read her father's hand well enough to testify to it. Go."

"And you?"

"I'm sixty-one years old, girl, and I've been arrested before. The food's terrible but the company is educational." But her knuckles were white on the poker, and her eyes went to the back parlor door, and Elin understood that Odile Ferrant did not intend to be arrested tonight either. She intended to stand between the soldiers and her press for as long as standing was possible, because a printer who abandoned her press was not a printer at all, only a woman with ink-stained hands.

Hoofbeats stopped at the gate.

Through the frost-furred window Elin saw them dismounting: six soldiers in municipal grey, and behind them, wrapped in a fine coat that cost more than the press, a figure she knew by silhouette alone. Mr. Quill. He carried a lantern and a folded paper, and he held the paper up toward the house as if the house itself were required to read it.

"A warrant," Tobias said at her shoulder. He had come down without his coat, without his boots, and his hands were trembling—not with fear, she realized, but with the specific fury of an accountant watching figures be falsified. "They'll have a warrant. Quill is too careful to burn us without paper. That's the whole horror of him—he'll do it *legally*."

"Then let him read it to the empty house," Elin said. "Cellar. Now."

* * *

They did not all go.

This was decided in eleven seconds of glances and half-sentences, the way such things are always decided among people who have become, without ceremony or consent, a family: Beatrix pressed the oilcloth bundle into Elin's hands and pointed her toward the cellar door; Tobias refused to leave before Odile, and Odile refused to leave before Beatrix, and Beatrix—who had spent forty years learning invisibility as a discipline—said, in a voice Elin had never heard him use, "Someone has to make sure the plates go into the ground where they can't melt them down," and was already dragging the plate chest toward the cold hearth.

So it fell out that Elin went into the cellar with the evidence, and Tobias went with her because his daughter's face rose unbidden behind his eyes—she learned this later, from his own mouth, in a different kind of testimony—and Odile and Beatrix stayed above with a press, a chest of copper, and six soldiers coming through the front door with axes.

The cellar was black and smelled of apples rotting sweet into cider. Elin found the culvert mouth by touch, a brick-lined gap behind the last shelf, breathing cold air that smelled of ditchwater and freedom. She turned back once.

From above came the splinter of the front door giving way, and then Quill's voice, pitched perfectly for carrying, mild as a man reading a menu: "*By authority of the municipal magistrate, this property is seized for the recovery of stolen documents—*" And Odile's voice, cutting across it like a blade through wet paper: "Stolen? Boy, I set every letter of that edition myself. You can't steal what you birthed."

Elin laughed. It was a horrible sound, a laugh wrung out of terror, and she hated herself for it, and she climbed into the culvert because Tobias's hand on her shoulder did not ask, it directed, and because the bundle against her ribs held the only truth left in the world that mattered.

Behind them, the smashing began.

* * *

She heard the press die.

That is the only way she could ever describe it afterward, and she knew how it sounded, the way a musician knows a wrong note: the deep wooden crack of the frame being broken at its joints, the shriek of the screw assembly wrenched apart, the bell-note toll of iron falling on stone flags. Then smaller sounds, methodical and obscene—type being scooped from its cases and hurled into the hearth, she supposed, or into the yard, galley after galley of the exposé that they had set page by patient page over five weeks, fourteen pages complete, the fifteenth half-composed in Odile's composing stick when they'd gone to bed.

Fourteen pages. She had read the proofs by candlelight two nights before, standing at the kitchen table while snow ticked against the glass, and she had felt something she had not felt since her father died: that the world could be argued with. That facts, properly arranged, were a weapon no fortune could buy out of existence.

Now the soldiers were feeding those facts into a fire, and somewhere above her head Mr. Quill was probably apologizing for the mess.

The culvert was low and she crawled it on elbows and knees, the bundle strapped tight to her back, Tobias breathing hard behind her. Water ran in the bottom of it, ankle-deep and bitter cold, and the brick gave way to earth, and the earth gave way to the open cut of the drainage ditch, and the sky overhead was the color of dirty wool with the fire-glow of the farmhouse beginning to bloom against it.

They came up out of the ditch in the third field and turned, both of them, unable not to.

The farmhouse was burning from the north end—the end where the back parlor was, where the press had been. Flames stood up out of the roof like things being born. Sparks went up into the fog and were swallowed. And along the fence line, silhouetted, soldiers were carrying out furniture and breaking it, and one of them was swinging an axe against something long and pale that caught the firelight—applewood, Elin thought. The great workbench. Her father's bench. He had planed it smooth himself thirty years ago, before the atlas, before the obsession, back when he had still come home for supper.

"They got out," Tobias said beside her. His voice was raw. "Odile and Beatrix. I saw—I saw the cellar door swing shut behind me. They were right behind—" He stopped. He did not finish, and the not-finishing was louder than any sentence.

Because they had not come out of the culvert. And the culvert came up fifty yards east of the burning house, in the ditch, in plain view of the fence line, and no figures moved along it.

"Come on," Tobias said, and took her arm, and she shook him off.

"No." Her voice came out flat and strange, belonging to some other woman. "I want to see their faces. I want to know which one swings the axe, so that if I live to be ninety I know whose name goes in the record."

"Elin—"

"*I want to see their faces.*"

He grabbed her then, both arms, a clerk's grip, precise and immovable, and pulled her down into the ditch grass, and held her there while the house burned, and she fought him for perhaps a minute and then simply wept, silently, furiously, hating every tear, because tears were exactly what Quill wanted from people like her—wetness, weakness, women weeping in ditches while the official version of events dried itself crisp and clean by morning.

It was in the middle of this that she saw the wagon.

It came up the orchard road at a walk, unhurried, a farmer's cart drawn by a bony horse, driven by a shapeless figure in a hooded cloak—and as it passed the burning house it slowed, just slightly, just long enough for a second shapeless figure to heave two heavy wooden boxes up onto the cart bed from the shadow of the drainage ditch. Two boxes. Long. Narrow. Heavy in the way that lead type is heavy.

The type cases.

Beatrix must have carried them out through the culvert ahead of her—no. No, she'd been behind him. Unless he'd sent them ahead, unless he'd rolled them into the ditch water to kill the smoke-smell, unless—her mind skittered across the possibilities and landed nowhere, because grief makes a poor cartographer.

The wagon creaked past the soldiers, who glanced at it and dismissed it, because a farmer's cart on a country road at dawn is invisible to men doing God's work with axes, and invisibility, Elin thought with a savage flash of pride, is a craft, and Beatrix Fairweather had been apprenticed to it for forty years.

The cart turned south on the far road and was gone into the fog.

Tobias had seen it too. She felt it in the sudden loosening of his grip—in the way his breath changed, the accountant's breath, the breath of a man whose columns balance.

"The cases," he whispered. "They saved the type."

And then, because the world does not permit even one mercy per hour, the soldiers found Tobias Renner.

* * *

It happened fast, the way disasters are efficient. A soldier walking the fence line, kicking through the ditch grass, checking the culvert mouth—and finding it wet, and fresh-mudded, and following the mud with a tracker's patience to the place where two fugitives lay flattened in winter stubble.

Elin heard the shout and was moving before thought, and Tobias was faster—he shoved her flat again, down into the frozen furrow, and stepped up out of the ditch with his hands raised, alone, a slight grey man in his shirtsleeves with no boots on, presenting himself to six rifles like a man submitting a form to the wrong office.

"He's the customs clerk," a voice said, and she knew it—Quill, close now, his lantern light washing over the ditch grass inches above her face. She pressed her cheek into the frozen mud and did not breathe. "Renner. Tobias Renner, Port Customs, twenty-two years' service. Well." A pause, and she could hear the smile in it, the professional satisfaction. "Twenty-two years. What a waste."

"I'd like my boots," Tobias said.

"You'll get boots where you're going. Search him."

Hands went through Tobias's pockets. She heard the small rain of his belongings on the fence rail: his watch, his pencil, his spectacles case, the folded paper of his pension certificate—which Quill took and opened and read by lantern light, slowly, like a eulogy.

"Seized manifests, forged certifications, conspiracy to publish seditious libel," Quill recited. "You understand none of it will matter. There will be a trial, brief and orderly, and the record will say you acted alone, for money, and the record will be *tidy*, Mr. Renner. Tidiness is the whole art of government."

"My daughter gets my pension," Tobias said. "Whatever else happens. She gets the pension."

"If you cooperate."

"I'm cooperating now. I'm lying in a ditch letting you arrest me. Write it down—it's the first accurate entry anyone's made tonight."

Something struck flesh—she heard it, the flat crack of a baton or a pistol grip, and heard Tobias grunt and go down, and heard Quill say, mildly, "Put him in the wagon," and the wagon wheels ground away northward with a man she had known for three months and would have trusted with her father's grave.

Elin lay in the mud until the fire burned down to a red skeleton and the soldiers mounted and rode out, until the fog closed over the whole ruin like a page turning, and then she crawled east along the ditch, south along the hedgerow, following the route she had already fixed in her mind from the atlas plates—because her father had taught her that you memorize your escape before you need it, and because everything he had taught her had turned out, in the end, to be ammunition.

She found the wheel ruts of the farm cart at the crossroads and followed them into the fog, two heavy boxes riding somewhere ahead of her through the dark, fourteen burned pages and one surviving alphabet, and she walked with her father's letter against her spine and her brother-in-arms in a prison wagon and her fury banked down to a coal so hot and so small that it would burn, she promised herself, for years.

Let them burn the house. Let them smash the press and jail the clerk and write their tidy record by lamplight.

An alphabet cannot be arrested. Somewhere ahead of her, in the fog, a cart full of letters was rolling toward whatever came next—and whatever came next, Elin Marchetti intended to be there when it arrived, with ink on her hands and names in her mouth, ready to set the type again.

She only hoped Beatrix knew where he was going.

Because the cart, she realized, stopping dead in the road as the fog thinned around her—was heading toward the river. Toward the naval yards.

Toward the one print shop in the city that had never yet refused Odile Ferrant work.
