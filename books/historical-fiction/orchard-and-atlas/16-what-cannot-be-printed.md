## 16. What Cannot Be Printed

The ash had not stopped falling by morning.

It came down through the broken roof of the orchard farmhouse in slow grey flakes, settling on the scorched beams, on the blackened wreck of Odile's press, on our shoulders as we picked through the ruin like crows at a battlefield. The soldiers had come at dusk and left before full dark, efficient as butchers. They took Tobias in a cart. They smashed what could be smashed, burned what would burn, and rode off satisfied that they had killed a book.

They were almost right.

I stood over the press with my sleeves rolled and my hands black to the wrist, lifting pieces of it the way one lifts pieces of a body—reverently, hopelessly, knowing no piece will answer. The screw had been bent double with a crowbar. The platen was cracked clean through. Someone had taken particular care with the type cases, or so I thought at first, until I saw Elin Marchetti kneeling at the far wall of what had been the kitchen, hauling a tarpaulin aside.

She looked up at me, and her face was streaked with soot and something else.

"Count them," she said.

I crossed the room. Beneath the tarpaulin, stacked in the cold hearth where no soldier had thought to look because no hearth holds anything worth taking, sat the type cases. Four of them. Chased brass corners, oak frames scarred from thirty years of Odile's trade. She had carried them out herself, I understood then—one at a time, while the hay barn burned and Quill's men shouted in the yard, she had walked past armed men with forty pounds of lead and brass in her arms and her face arranged into the blankness of a woman fetching firewood.

"They're all here?" I asked.

"Every fount we'd cut. Every character we set." She ran her thumb along the edge of the nearest case, along the little compartments of letters lying in their beds like seeds. "The edition is ashes. But the words are here."

"The words are here," I repeated, and heard how hollow it sounded. Words without a press are breath without lungs. We had spent three weeks setting pages by lamplight—Odile composing, Tobias checking figures against his salvaged manifests, Elin reading proof aloud in a voice that grew harder each night—and now the whole of it existed only as memory and a few hundredweight of loose metal.

Odile came in from the yard carrying a bucket of water she did not pour on anything. There was nothing left worth dousing. She set it down beside the ruined press and stood looking at it the way one looks at a grave one dug oneself.

"Thirty years," she said. Not to anyone. To the press. "They gave me a license when I was nineteen. They can take it in an afternoon." She turned to us, and her eyes had the flat brightness I had learned to fear in her, the brightness that preceded either laughter or violence. "So. What do we do now? Because I am not going back to setting wedding invitations in some border town under a false name. I have done that. It is worse than hanging."

No one answered her. Outside, the fog was coming in off the harbor, thickening around the black skeleton of the barn, softening the world into the grey sameness the city wore nine months of the year. Somewhere beyond it, Tobias Renner sat in a cell waiting to learn whether the Vosses' money had already reached the magistrate. Somewhere beyond that, Mr. Quill was drafting whatever report made this morning's work sound like civic hygiene.

Elin stood up. She brushed ash from her skirt with two brisk strokes, the gesture of a woman tidying before important business, and I saw that she had gone somewhere in her head while the rest of us mourned—a cold, high place, a place of calculation.

"Beatrix," she said. "Where is your guild seal?"

I touched my coat pocket without meaning to. "Here. Why?"

"How many plates does the atlas have?"

"You know how many. Forty-two."

"And whose name is on the license petition?"

"Mine. My master's posthumously. It's—" I stopped. Something moved behind my ribs, the first tremor of an idea too large to look at directly, the way you sense a ship before you see its sails.

"It's filed," Elin said. "It's been filed since spring. Approved, sealed, stamped by the Board of Licensing. There is a document in the guildhall vault right now, under law and wax, authorizing the printing and publication of *Orchard and Atlas*, complete in forty-two copper plates, together with such annotations and marginalia as appear upon said plates." Her voice was very even, the voice of someone reciting from a document she had memorized. "Is that not the wording? You read it to us in October. You read it twice."

"Elin—"

"The Vosses bought the guild once," she said. "Chapter seventeen of their plan: buy the vote, bury the marginalia, burn my father's notes. That was their mistake, Beatrix. They thought the atlas was paper. Paper burns." She kicked the wrecked press, gently, almost affectionately. "But a license is not paper. A license is a right. And rights, once granted, are devils to revoke."

We gathered in the kitchen—the half of it that still had a roof—around the table where we had eaten our suppers and planned our small treasons, and Elin laid it out the way a surveyor lays out a route across bad country.

"The guild's charter," she began, "grants every licensed member the exclusive right to publish approved works bearing the guild's mark. Correct?"

"Correct," I said slowly. "Publication is the guild's word for it. Any reproduction of licensed plates within the city requires—"

"Requires what? Say it exactly."

"Requires the consent of the licensee. Which is me." I felt my mouth go dry. "Elin, they'll simply call another vote. They control the board. They bought eleven of fifteen seats last time—we watched it happen."

"They bought a vote on *amendment*. On striking the marginalia from the approved plates." She held up one finger. "That is a different instrument entirely, and it is written differently in the record. Look at me. I have spent six years as a clerk in a shipping house listening to men find loopholes in contracts for fun. The amendment struck the annotations from the *approved text*. It did not strike them from the *plates*. The plates are copper, Beatrix. Copper is not text. The guild approved the plates as drawn—as *physically drawn*—and my father drew those warehouses and tonnages into the copper itself, disguised as survey notes, and no vote of any board can un-engrave metal."

I stared at her. Around the table, Odile had gone very still, the way she went still when a page was coming together in her hands.

"But the marginalia was inked over," I said. "In the approved proofs. The survey figures were printed faint, beneath the coastline hachures—they're nearly invisible unless you know to look—"

"*Nearly* invisible," Elin said. "Not invisible. And who wrote the guild's own standards for legibility of engraved annotation? Who sits on the committee that defines minimum depth of cut for navigational markings?" She let the silence do its work. "Your master did. Twelve years ago. He wrote the rule that says every functional engraving on a guild plate must be cut deep enough to print clearly under standard pressure. He wrote it, and then he obeyed it. Those warehouse figures aren't decoration, Beatrix. They're compliant. By his own regulation, the guild is *obligated* to ensure they print legibly."

The kitchen seemed to tilt. I put my hand flat on the table.

"That's madness," I said. "That's—it's beautiful, and it's madness. The board will never—"

"The board doesn't matter yet. Hear the whole route before you object to the first mile." She was pacing now between the table and the hearth, and I recognized her father in her gait suddenly, painfully—the same economy of movement, the same way of planting each step like a point on a chart. "Step one: as sole licensee, you file a notice of intent to print. Not a petition—a *notice*. The guild cannot refuse a licensee's own plate. Step two: the Vosses move to block you, which forces the question into open session, on the record, with witnesses. Let them argue in public that the atlas must not be allowed to show what everyone can see it shows. Let them explain, before the whole guild, why grain tonnages frighten them."

"Quill will have bought the hall before we finish filing."

"Some of it. Perhaps most of it." She stopped pacing. "And perhaps not all. There was one defector last time. One man who looked at eleven bought votes and said no anyway. Men like that exist, Beatrix. They are rare, and they are expensive, and they are the entire difference between a record and a rumor."

Odile spoke from the corner, where she had been turning a damaged quoins key over in her fingers. "Even if the guild swallows it—even if the notice stands—you still need a press. Yours is kindling. Mine is kindling. The naval print shop is three days' hard bargaining away and Quill knows about it now."

"I know," Elin said.

"And the plates themselves," Odile went on, relentless. "Forty-two copper plates, each the size of a coffin lid. Where are they?"

Silence. We all knew where they were. In the cellar of my old workshop in Chandler's Lane, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden beneath the floorboards under the proofing stone—which meant they might as well be sitting on Quill's desk. He had searched the workshop twice already. He had simply not found them, and men like Quill did not fail twice.

"He'll have them within the week," I said. "He'll have the workshop watched. He may have it watched tonight."

"Then we don't go tonight," Elin said. "We go when he decides we won't. When he relaxes. When the watch on Chandler's Lane rotates to bored men on a cold shift." She looked around the table—at Odile, at me, at the empty chair where Tobias should have been, and something crossed her face like weather. "We have time. Not much. But we have a route, and cartographers work routes. Don't they, Beatrix?"

I looked down at my hands, black with the press's blood, and understood that she was asking me for more than assent. She was asking me to stop being the man who finished other people's maps. My master had drawn this route twenty years ago, in copper, in secret, dying by inches at his desk while I fetched his inks and believed I knew him. Elin had inherited his courage the way I had inherited his burin—without being offered a choice about it.

"Yes," I said. "Cartographers work routes. And we work this one."

*

I did not sleep. I told myself it was strategy—someone must draft the notice of intent, and the language had to be flawless, every clause quoted verbatim from the charter and the licensing standards, leaving no seam for Quill's lawyers to pick open. So I sat in the surviving half of the kitchen with a candle stub and the guild charter copied out in my master's hand, and I drafted, and the fog pressed against the broken window like a listener.

But somewhere past midnight the drafting became something else, and I set down the pen and let myself feel what we had actually decided.

We were going to publish the truth through the murderers' own machinery. We were going to walk into the guildhall—the same hall where the Vosses' agent had strolled the aisles during the last vote, counting heads like a man counting sheep—and file a notice that would force the famine onto the public record in the one form no one could burn without burning the map of the known world. If they struck the marginalia, the copper kept it. If they seized the copper, the guild's own standards demanded it be printed. If they rewrote the standards, they confessed in writing that the standards had been changed to hide something. Every road led somewhere we wanted them to go. My master had built it that way. He had died building it, alone, thinking no one would ever walk it.

I thought about what he must have felt, cutting those warehouse coordinates into the plate by candlelight, knowing that the fainter he cut them the safer they were and the more surely they'd be lost, and finding instead the exact depth where safety and survival met. There is a moment in every engraving when the line becomes irreversible. You can scrape copper back, but never perfectly; there is always a ghost. He had chosen his lines. Now we would choose ours.

Tobias was in a cell because of those lines. Odile's brother was in a pauper's grave because of the grain they measured. Elin had grown up hating the desk that killed her father and had just used its discipline to build us a weapon. And me—I had spent a lifetime believing that maps recorded places and never people, that anonymity was humility, that a man who finished other men's work owed the world nothing but accuracy.

The candle guttered. I dipped the pen again and wrote the final line of the notice: *the undersigned intends to print and publish the said work complete, as approved, without omission.*

Without omission. Two words. They weighed more than the press.

Near dawn, Elin appeared in the doorway with two cups of the barley coffee Odile stretched like a miser. She set one beside me and stood looking at the draft for a long time.

"It's good," she said finally. "Father would have checked it twice and changed one comma."

"One comma?"

"To make sure you'd reread everything after it." Almost a smile. Then it faded, and she said quietly: "If this fails, they don't just take the plates. They take Tobias's pension, Odile's freedom, your guild seat. Me they take last, and worst. You understand that you're signing all of us onto this page."

"I've signed worse lately," I said. "I signed a refusal to the Vosses before I understood why. This time I understand."

She nodded slowly, and outside the first pale light came up over the orchard—the leafless trees standing in rows like witnesses, the fog beginning to lift off the fields, showing, for the first time in days, actual distance between things. A road. A horizon. The harbor beyond it, and somewhere on the harbor, a sympathetic naval printer who did not yet know what we would ask of him.

"There's one thing we haven't counted," Elin said, watching the light. "The defector. The eleventh vote against. If we're going to force this into open session, we need to know who he was—and what it costs a man to say no to the Vosses twice."

I pulled the guild roll toward me, my finger moving down the list of names, and stopped.

Because I knew him. Of course I knew him. Everyone in the trade knew him.

"His name is Aldous Wren," I said. "And he keeps the guildhall vault—where our license is filed, and where Quill will go the moment he hears we've survived the raid."
