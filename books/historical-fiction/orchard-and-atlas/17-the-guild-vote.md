## 17. The Guild Vote

The cell door opened at half past six in the morning, and a turnkey with a face like wet paper said, "Renner. Your papers came through. You're free."

Tobias had spent nine days in that cell learning to sleep sitting up, his back against stone, one ear tuned for boots on stairs. He had learned which guards could be paid in gossip and which wanted coin he didn't have. He had learned that fear, given enough time, stopped feeling like drowning and started feeling like weather—something you dressed for.

"Free," he repeated.

"On a writ of *habeas corpus*," said a voice behind the turnkey, and there was Elin Marchetti, standing in the corridor in a gray traveling coat, holding a document sealed in green wax like it might bite her. "Filed by a solicitor named Aldous Crane, who owes Odile Ferrant a favor from 1839 and has apparently been waiting all these years to pay it. The warrant for your arrest cited the wrong statute. Sedition instead of obstruction. Different courts, different jurisdictions, and the magistrate who signed your commitment had no authority over the second." She handed the papers through the bars as if passing a plate at supper. "They were so busy being thorough that they weren't careful."

Tobias read the writ twice because his hands would not steady enough to read it once. "My daughter—"

"With friends. Fed, asleep, and asking every hour when you'd come home. Come home, then."

He walked out of the Clink Annex into a morning so ordinary it seemed indecent: gulls screaming over the fish market, a boy dragging a barrow of ice, fog burning off the harbor in long gray rags. Nine days ago he had been a customs clerk with twenty-two years of unblemished service and a pension he protected like a candle flame in wind. Now he was a marked man with a burned archive behind him and a daughter who had learned his face could be taken from her by men in Voss livery.

He found he did not want his old life back. That frightened him more than Quill ever had.

"They're moving tonight," Elin said as they walked, low, without looking at him. "Or trying to. Come to the orchard and you'll understand."

"The press is smashed," Tobias said. "I saw what they carried out of that farmhouse. Type cases, forme, the iron bed of the press itself, all of it on a cart like plunder."

"Yes," Elin said. "And Beatrix has thought of something so mad it might work. But first we need you to do the thing you do best."

"Which is?"

"Count," she said. "In front of witnesses."

* * *

The Cartographers' Guild Hall stood on Compassion Street like a fat ledger dropped from heaven—three stories of pale granite, its façade carved with the four winds blowing their trumpets, its doors of black oak banded in brass that had never once needed polishing because a thousand gloved hands kept it bright. Inside, the licensing chamber was a semicircle of walnut benches rising toward a ceiling painted with a map of the known world, though the known world stopped at the edge of the colony charts and left everything inconvenient unpainted, which Tobias had always considered the most honest thing about the building.

For forty years he had walked past this hall on his way to the customs house. He had never once gone in. Guild business was guild business; clerks kept their heads down and their ledgers clean. His whole life had been built on the principle that a man could pass through history untouched if he only kept to his own narrow street.

Now he sat in the public gallery between Odile Ferrant—who had scrubbed the printer's ink from under her nails for the occasion and failed—and a fishmonger's widow named Mrs. Halloway whom none of them had invited but who had heard what they meant to do and planted herself beside Odile with the words, "My husband starved in the winter of '43. I've nowhere better to be."

Below them, on the floor of the chamber, Beatrix Fairweather stood alone at the petitioner's rail in his best coat, which fit him like a borrowed coffin, holding the atlas.

Tobias watched him and marveled, not for the first time, at how unremarkable the young man looked. Medium height, ink-stained fingers, a stoop acquired over years of bending over plates—the sort of man whose name you forgot while shaking his hand. The Vosses had spent a year assuming such men were furniture. The furniture had come to speak.

The Master of the Guild, Alderman Corvus Hale, presided from the high chair with the gilded dividers of office laid before him like a sword. He was a broad, comfortable man with the complexion of someone who dined well, and Tobias had done his homework since dawn: Hale held three shipping shares through his wife's family, and two of those ships had carried grain in the famine year. Not proof. Never proof. Just weight, quietly stacked on one side of the scale.

"The petition before the bench," Hale read, in the tone of a man reading a menu, "requests licensure of an atlas submitted under the title *Orchard and Atlas*, being the final work of the late Master Edmund Marchetti, completed by his journeyman, one B. Fairweather. The petition further requests that the work be licensed *as drawn*, inclusive of marginalia, survey annotations, and all matter presently upon the plates, without alteration or expurgation." He looked up. "The bench notes this request is irregular. Marginalia is working matter. Working matter is struck before licensure. It has always been so."

"With respect," Beatrix said, and his voice carried further than his body suggested it could, "it has always been so because no master ever drew what Master Marchetti drew. The marginalia is not scratch work, Alderman. It is the atlas. Strike it, and you license a beautiful lie."

Murmuring moved along the benches like wind through barley.

Hale lifted a hand. "The bench will hear argument. Then the members vote. As provided by charter, section eleven." A pause, delicately weighted. "A simple majority of members present."

Section eleven. Tobias had read the charter in the customs house library that morning, twice. Section eleven required a majority of *members present*, and the Voss agent Mr. Quill had spent three days making certain the chamber would be full of men who owed the Voss warehouses favors, contracts, credit, and silence. Forty-one members were seated below. By Odile's count, twenty-six of them had eaten from the Voss table in one way or another. The arithmetic was not subtle. It was not meant to be.

Quill himself sat in the third row, hands folded on his knee, gray and neat and patient as a filing cabinet. He caught Tobias's eye in the gallery and inclined his head a quarter inch—a courtesy between professionals—and Tobias felt the old cold slide up his spine. *We know where your daughter sleeps,* that nod said. *We know what you've lost already.*

Tobias made himself nod back.

Beatrix spoke for nearly an hour. He did not perform; Tobias had expected performance and got something rarer. The young man simply stood at the rail and explained maps the way a priest explains sacraments—what a coastline promised, what a tonnage figure concealed inside a survey note, why a warehouse drawn to scale at the mouth of the Marrow Channel mattered more than any speech. He turned the great atlas page by page on its stand and let the members see: depth soundings that were grain tonnages, tide tables that were shipment dates, a compass rose whose thirty-two points each anchored a note naming a ship, a date, a diversion.

"Master Marchetti knew he could not publish this openly," Beatrix said. "So he hid the truth in the one place no one would look—in the work itself, in the margins of a licensed monopoly. He trusted this guild to license the map as drawn. He trusted you to be, someday, the men who let the record stand." He closed the atlas gently, as one closes a coffin. "I am asking you to honor that trust. Nothing more. License the atlas as drawn, and history will do the rest."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the painted winds went on blowing their trumpets at the edges of the world.

Then Master Fenwick rose—one of the senior licensers, a dry stick of a man with a voice like sandpaper on oak. "I have examined the plates," Fenwick said, "at the request of interested parties." Tobias saw Quill's shoulders settle a fraction. "The annotations are indeed unusual. They are also, I am advised, potentially defamatory. To license defamatory matter into the public record exposes this guild to suits it cannot survive and dishonors a dead colleague, who surely never intended—" he gestured vaguely at the atlas as if it were something unpleasant on his shoe, "—whatever feverish construction is now being placed upon his working notes. I move that licensure be granted contingent upon removal of all marginalia."

"Seconded," said four voices at once, rehearsed as a choir.

Hale called the question with visible relief. "All in favor of licensure as amended—marginalia struck?"

Hands rose along the benches. One, three, seven. Tobias counted them the way he counted everything, automatically, hopelessly: twelve, fifteen, nineteen, and still rising, twenty-four, twenty-six—

Twenty-eight.

"All opposed?" Hale began, and his voice had the flatness of a man closing a drawer.

"One point of order."

The voice came from the back bench, and it was old, and cracked, and Tobias knew it before he turned, because everyone in the port knew it—the voice of Master Ambrose Kettleby, who had surveyed half the northern coast before his eyes went, who had taught half the men in this room to hold a graver, who had not attended a guild session in six years and sat now in the last row wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, his milky eyes aimed generally at the ceiling.

Hale's jaw tightened. "Point taken, Master Kettleby, but the question—"

"The question can wait on an old man's memory." Kettleby rose slowly, leaning on a stick, and began to walk down the aisle, each step deliberate as a sounding line dropped into dark water. "Thirty years ago I charted the Marrow Channel with Edmund Marchetti. We worked one summer, he and I, sleeping in a barn and arguing about projection until two in the morning. I have known perhaps four honest men in this trade, and he was the chief of them." He reached the rail and put one spotted hand flat on the closed atlas. "You propose to strike his margins because you are told they are feverish constructions. I say no man in this room knew Marchetti's mind better than I, and I will tell you what his mind was: exact. He did not draw a single figure in sixty years that he had not verified twice. If there are tonnages hidden in his soundings, they are true tonnages. And if they are true—" the old voice gathered force, cracking across the chamber like ice shifting on a lake, "—then striking them is not editing. It is burying."

"Master Kettleby," Hale said heavily, "you are out of order, and I would remind you that the vote—"

"I have not finished." Kettleby turned his ruined eyes toward the benches, toward the raised hands, some of which were now, awkwardly, lowering. "Gentlemen. Some of you took my course in hydrography. Some of you drank my brandy and married my students' sisters. Look at your hands. Look at what they're doing." Silence, terrible and total. "In the winter of '43 I lost my granddaughter to the fever that followed the hunger. They told us it was scarcity sent by God. I am an old man and my eyes are gone, but my ears work, and I have listened to this chamber for an hour, and what I hear is not God. What I hear is arithmetic." He drew breath. "I call for the question to be put again, and I vote *no*. Licensure as drawn. All of it."

"Seconded," said Mrs. Halloway from the gallery—which was improper, which was impossible, which made half the chamber laugh despite itself, because she was not a member—but then a member did second it, a young licenser named Pell, whose father Tobias happened to know had hauled grain sacks for Voss & Sons and died of the lung-rot at thirty-four. Then another hand. Then another.

Tobias counted. He could not stop counting. Fourteen. Seventeen. Nineteen. The chamber had become a tide gauge and the tide was turning, slow and then all at once, hands rising in clusters now, men glancing at their neighbors and finding courage contagious.

Twenty-one against twenty-eight. Twenty-three against twenty-eight. When the count settled it stood at twenty-four to seventeen, and Alderman Hale sat very still at his high chair beneath the painted world, and Tobias understood that the alderman was performing rapid private arithmetic of his own—about ships, about shares, about how a defeat recorded in open session might be survived if it were merely regrettable rather than corrupt.

"The motion fails," Hale said at last. "Licensure as drawn carries." A pause like a man swallowing glass. "The guild will issue its certificate."

The chamber erupted—not cheering, nothing so honest, but a roar of talk, men standing, clustering, the sound of forty-one careers recalculating themselves in real time. Quill rose without hurry, buttoned his coat, and looked up once more at the gallery. This time he did not nod. This time he smiled, small and correct, and mouthed something Tobias could not hear but did not need to.

*Noted.*

Odile's hand closed on Tobias's wrist, hard as a shackle. "Did you feel that? Did you feel it turn?" Her eyes were wet and furious and shining. "One old blind man. One. That's all it took."

"That's always all it takes," Tobias said. "That's the whole secret of every ledger ever kept. The totals only change when somebody changes a line."

Below, Beatrix Fairweather stood at the rail with the atlas in his arms, and Kettleby had reached him somehow, the old man's hand finding the young man's shoulder by touch alone, and whatever passed between them neither spoke aloud.

* * *

They came out of the guild hall into a dusk that smelled of coal smoke and coming rain, and the certificate was real, folded in oilcloth inside Beatrix's coat, and for one hour—Tobias would remember the length of it exactly—they allowed themselves to believe the thing was done. Elin laughed at something, actually laughed, a sound Tobias suspected few men alive had earned. Odile talked type sizes and paper stock the way other women talked of weddings. They ate standing up at a chandlery counter, bread and hard cheese and small sweet apples, and Tobias thought: *this is what winning feels like, and it tastes like lunch.*

Then the boy found them.

A runner, maybe ten years old, dockside quick, sidling up along the chandlery's shadow with a folded paper he pressed into Odile's hand before anyone could stop him. "From the naval yard," the boy said. "From a man with ink on his cuffs. He said tell the printer woman: tonight or never, and bring the plates yourselves, because after midnight the gate watch changes and the new watch isn't his."

Odile unfolded it. Read it once. Her face did something complicated—hope and terror fighting to a draw.

"Well?" Elin said.

"It's Bramwell," Odile said. "The master at the naval print shop. He was apprenticed to me, before. He says the Admiralty presses run at night this week—war charts, convoy routes—and that between the last war-run and the morning shift there is a window of maybe three hours when a second edition could go to paper unnoticed, or noticed too late to stop." She looked up, and the lamplight caught the ink still ghosting the creases of her knuckles. "But the plates are in a warehouse on Tanner's Row under seal of the court, pending the Voss suit. Guarded."

"By how many?" Tobias asked.

"Two at the door, and a clerk who keeps the keys," Odile said. "And by morning, after tonight's vote reaches the right ears, I'd wager by considerably more."

Tobias looked down the street toward the harbor, where the fog was rolling in again off the water, thickening around the mastheads, swallowing the lights one by one. He was a cautious man. He had spent a lifetime being a cautious man, and caution had saved his pension and his neck and nearly cost him everything worth either.

"Three hours," he said. "Two guards and a clerk." He pulled out his old customs watch and checked it against the chandlery clock, out of habit, out of love, out of forty years of knowing exactly what time it was. "It's half past seven. The seals on that warehouse are customs seals, gentlemen—guild writ or no, nobody moves court-seized goods past a customs officer without a clerk's countersignature." He pocketed the watch. "Fortunately, you appear to have one."

Elin stared at him. "You're proposing to walk into a guarded warehouse and countersign the theft of evidence from your own government."

"I'm proposing," Tobias Renner said, "to finally put my twenty-two years of service to honest use. Fetch the atlas. Fetch the plates list. And somebody find out what time the fog comes off the water tonight—because we'll want it thick, and we'll want it soon."

Above them, on Compassion Street, the guild hall windows glowed warm and gold, and somewhere inside, Tobias had no doubt, Alderman Hale was already writing letters. Whatever window the old man Kettleby had opened, it would not stay open long. The record was theirs to carry now—all of it, plates and names and tonnages—across a guarded city, in the dark, before the victors woke.

"Tonight or never," Odile said quietly, folding Bramwell's note away.

"Tonight," said Beatrix, and for once his voice did not shake at all.
