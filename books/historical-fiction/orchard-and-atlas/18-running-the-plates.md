## 18. Running the Plates

The crates smelled of ink and fear.

Odile Ferrant had packed a press twice in her life before this night—once when the authorities seized it from her shop on Cordwainer Lane, the soldiers' boots grinding type into the cobblestones like teeth, and once more in her dreams, every night for six years after. This was the third time, and she swore to God and to her dead brother Anton that it would not be the last time she packed one. Only the first time she would unpack it somewhere better.

"Again," Beatrix said, kneeling beside the third crate with his coat off, sleeves rolled, his long cartographer's fingers wrapping each copper plate in oilcloth as though it were an infant. "Check the corners."

"I checked the corners."

"Check them again."

She checked them again. Eleven plates, each one engraved with the diversion routes—the warehouses on Gull Street, the tonnages disguised as survey notes, the grain that had sat rotting behind Voss padlocks while the famine year ate the city from the inside. Eleven plates. The whole truth of it, cut into metal by her own hand over nine sleepless nights at the orchard farmhouse, working by lamplight while Elin read the master's marginalia aloud like scripture and Tobias counted and recounted his ledger numbers until his voice went hoarse.

Eleven plates. She could carry three at a time without staggering. That meant four trips across the port, or fewer trips with more risk, and either way it meant crossing two miles of fog-bound streets where Mr. Quill's men were already looking for them. Tobias had learned that much before he'd been arrested: Quill knew about the orchard now, knew about the press they'd hidden there, knew everything except where the plates would go tonight.

"They'll watch the harbor road," Tobias said. He stood at the farmhouse window with his daughter Marta's shawl still around his shoulders—he'd come straight from his release, pale as whey, refusing to sit down, refusing to be thanked. "They watched it when I was taken. Two men, oilskin coats, one with a lantern though the fog made it useless. They weren't hiding. They wanted me to see them."

"Then we don't take the harbor road," Odile said.

Elin looked up from the crate she was nailing shut. Her father's daughter, yes—the same stubborn jaw, the same way of going very still when the world demanded movement. "Where then?"

"The naval yard," Odile said, and heard her own voice steady itself the way it did only when her hands were busy. "Commander Ashe's print shop, behind the ropewalk. He owes me." She didn't explain how a disgraced printer came to be owed favors by a naval officer; some debts were private currency, spent best unexamined. "His shop has a license, a guard at the gate, and presses that could run these plates by morning if we get them there."

"And if we don't get them there?"

"If we don't get them there," Odile said, "then the Vosses win, and the famine year stays a footnote, and everyone who died becomes weather instead of murder. So we get them there."

* * *

They moved out at the change of the tide, when the fog came off the water thickest, a grey wool blanket thrown over the whole port. Odile took the first load herself—a fishmonger's barrow borrowed from a woman who owed Anton's memory a kindness, plates buried under herring ice that stank so fiercely no customs man would want to dig through it. Beatrix walked beside her pushing a second barrow of scrap lead, playing the part of a type-founder's drayman because, as he put it with that terrible earnestness of his, "I have always been invisible. Let me use it."

Tobias stayed behind with Elin and Marta, guarding the last crate and the farmhouse, though they all understood the orchard was burned ground now, a place they'd already stayed one night too long. It was agreed: if Odile didn't send word back by dawn, the remaining plates went into the well and the family scattered. Nobody said aloud what else might happen if Odile didn't send word back by dawn. Nobody had to.

The first half mile was nothing. Fog, fish stink, the wet rattle of the barrow wheels, her breath smoking under the lampless dark. She'd been afraid before—in the courtroom when they read the sedition charge, in the cell, on the day they auctioned her type by the pound—but this was a different animal. This fear had exhilaration braided through it, a wild bright thread, and she recognized it with something like shame: she was alive tonight in a way she hadn't been since Cordwainer Lane. Six years of safe small work, jobbing bills and wedding invitations, printing other people's happy lies—and here, at last, hauling the truth through the fog on a stinking barrow, hunted, her heart hammering like a compositor's mallet.

*This is what it feels like,* she thought, *to matter.*

Then the lantern swung out of the alley ahead of her, and the exhilaration curdled straight back into terror.

Two men. Oilskins. One carrying the lantern high, sweeping its beam across the street in slow, patient arcs—not searching, patrolling. Watching. The same pair Tobias had described, or their brothers. They stood at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, which was the only way through to the ropewalk unless she doubled back half a mile and came around by the salt works, and doubling back meant passing the customs house, and the customs house meant Quill's clerks, and—

"Hold on there," the lantern man called. "What's in the barrow?"

Odile kept walking. That was the trick, she told herself, the trick she'd learned in six years of being nobody: keep walking. Guilty people stop. Guilty people run. Innocent tired women with fish carts never stop for anyone.

"Herring," she said, in the flat harbor accent she hadn't used since her shop fell. "And you're welcome to smell it."

The second man laughed—one bark, quickly smothered—but the first didn't move. He stepped closer, lantern up, and the light found her face, and she saw his eyes narrow with the slow arithmetic of a man trying to place a face against a description. Quill would have circulated descriptions. Quill struck Odile as exactly the sort of man who circulated descriptions.

"You're Ferrant," he said suddenly. "The printer. The seditious one."

Every plan she'd built in the fog collapsed at once, and in the wreckage of it she did the only thing left: she shoved the barrow hard at him and ran.

The barrow caught his knees. He went down in a clatter of ice and cursing and the lantern burst on the cobbles in a bloom of burning oil, and Odile ran—not toward Cooper's Alley, which was finished now, but sideways, down the fish-gutted passage between the smokehouses where she'd played as a girl, where the walls leaned close enough to touch on both sides. Behind her shouting, boots, the second man's whistle cutting the fog in three sharp notes that meant more men would come.

*Three loads,* she thought wildly, running. *Four trips planned. One trip made, and they know my face, they know my name, and Beatrix is somewhere behind me with a barrow full of lead thinking himself invisible—*

She skidded around the smokehouse corner and collided with a figure, and her scream died in her throat because the figure caught her arms and said, "What happened?" in Beatrix Fairweather's precise, frightened voice.

"They know me," she gasped. "They know my face. I can't go back for the rest—you can't go back—"

Beatrix was silent for one heartbeat. In that heartbeat she watched him do the arithmetic she couldn't: eleven plates, three delivered nowhere yet, two barrows abandoned or burning, one route closed, one night shrinking. Then he said, in a voice she'd never heard him use, low and absolutely certain:

"The salt works. There's a boat."

"There is no boat."

"Tobias told me. His wife's cousin moored a pilot boat there—the *Kestrel*—for just such nights as this. Water route. We can come along the quay inside the naval yard itself." He was already moving, pulling her by the wrist, and she let herself be pulled because terror, she was learning, made strange captains of quiet mapmakers. "You go to the boat. I'll go back for the plates."

"You'll be taken."

"Nobody knows my face," he said, and there it was again—that terrible gift of his, invisibility, the thing she'd cursed him for and envied him for, turned at last into a weapon. "That has been true my whole life, Odile. Tonight it's useful."

He pressed something into her hand as they parted in the dark: the smallest plate, the eleventh one, the colophon plate with the diversion routes' key engraved around its rim. "If I'm taken," he said, "this still reaches Ashe. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, and meant it the way people mean things when promises are knives.

* * *

The *Kestrel* rode low at the salt works jetty, a black shape among black shapes, and the woman aboard—Tobias's cousin by marriage, weathered as a bollard—took one look at Odile's face and the wrapped plate in her arms and said, "How many more?"

"Eight. Maybe ten minutes. Maybe never."

"Then get in and pray," the woman said, which was, Odile reflected, approximately the whole of theology.

The wait on the water was the worst of it. Fog closed them in a grey room thirty feet wide; sounds arrived from everywhere and nowhere—the clank of harbor bells, a drunk singing somewhere near the tanneries, once, horribly, a whistle: three notes, the patrol signal, close enough that the boatwoman's hand went to her tiller and froze there. Odile sat with the colophon plate against her chest like a breastplate and counted seconds, and discovered that terror and hope, given time alone together in the dark, become indistinguishable.

Ten minutes. Twelve. Fifteen.

At nineteen minutes a barrow rattled onto the jetty through the fog, pushed by a figure running bent double, and Odile's heart performed a maneuver she felt certain was medically inadvisable. Beatrix threw himself aboard with the last crates, gasping, his collar torn, a smear of someone else's blood on his knuckles.

"One of Quill's men," he managed. "By the salt works gate. He grabbed the crate and I—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. Looked slightly ill. "I hit him with the lead barrow. I've never hit anyone. I'm going to be sick."

"Do it over the side," the boatwoman said pragmatically, and pushed off from the jetty.

They came down the black channel between the moored hulls with the oars muffled in rags, and the naval yard rose out of the fog on their left like a cliff of rigging and lantern-glow. Twice a sentry's challenge drifted across the water and twice the boatwoman answered with a countersign that must have cost Commander Ashe a favor of its own, and then they were bumping against the yard's inner stairs, and a lieutenant with a shaded lantern was hissing "Quickly, quickly, for God's sake," and Odile was climbing iron steps with eight hundred pounds of engraved truth in her arms while her legs shook so badly she nearly went down twice.

The print shop behind the ropewalk was warm, lamplit, and smelled like her entire lost life: ink and oil and hot metal. Commander Ashe himself stood among his presses, a heavy grey-whiskered man in shirtsleeves, and he looked at the crates, and then at Odile, and said only, "Cordwainer Lane, was it? I read your pamphlets. The good ones, before they banned them." He turned to his men. "Clear the stone table. We run these plates tonight."

And that should have been the end of it—the end of the fear, the end of the running—but Odile stood in the doorway with the colophon plate still hugged to her chest and felt the exhilaration drain out of her all at once, leaving something colder underneath. Because the plates were safe. Because the atlas could print at dawn. And because Beatrix was standing beside her, flexing his bruised knuckles, saying quietly, "We should send word to the orchard. Elin and Tobias will be waiting for dawn."

But Odile was looking past him, at the lieutenant, whose face had gone carefully blank in the way faces do when they are carrying bad news, and she heard him say:

"There was word, ma'am. An hour ago. A rider came through the yard gate asking after the Voss agent's men." He hesitated. "The orchard road's alight. The farmhouse is burning."

* * *

Outside, far off across the water, through the fog, the glow of it was just visible—a dull orange stain on the underside of the clouds, exactly where a derelict orchard farmhouse would be. Odile stared at it and felt her brother's name rise in her throat and stay there, unspoken, the way it always did.

"They got out," Beatrix said beside her. Not a question. A prayer said in the tone of a fact. "They're careful. Tobias is careful. Elin—"

"Elin saved the type cases," Odile heard herself say. "Whatever happens, that woman saves the type cases. I'd stake my life on it." She realized she already had, several times over, and that the stake had somehow stopped frightening her.

Behind them, inside the warm shop, the first plate met the first sheet of paper, and the press began to turn with a sound like a great patient heart.
