## 21. What It Cost

The ground had not yet softened from the frost when we began to dig.

There were six of us with spades—Beatrix, Odile, Elin, two of Odile's printer friends whose names I would learn only later, and myself—and the earth of the orchard fought us the way the whole year had fought us: stubbornly, grudgingly, giving an inch for every yard of effort. The farmhouse was a charred skeleton behind us, its roof beams jutting into the grey morning like ribs. The press was ash. But the grave we were digging was not for any of that.

It was for Aldous Marchetti, who had been dead eleven months already.

His body had lain in the guild crypt all this winter, in a lead-lined drawer among the other licensed cartographers, waiting for a plot his daughter could afford and a name his daughter was willing to speak over him. Elin had refused both, through the long months of our hiding—the plot because it sat in the shadow of the Voss-funded chapel annex, the name because she had not yet decided whether her father deserved it. *Aldous Marchetti, Master of the Guild* was one man. *Aldous Marchetti, who saw what was done at Hallowmere and wrote it into the margins of an atlas* was another. She had needed the year to decide which one she was burying.

She had decided on the night of the raid, she told me later, when she pulled the type cases from the burning farmhouse with her sleeves on fire. Not in triumph. In fury. Fury, she said, was just grief that had found somewhere to put itself.

So we dug beside the oldest apple tree in the orchard, the one Odile's brother had planted the spring before the famine took him, and we did it without a priest, because no priest in this city would come to ground belonging to a convicted seditious printer, and none of us wanted one anyway.

I dug because my hands needed work. Grief sits strangely in a clerk's body. For thirty years I had sat at a high desk in the customs house with a ledger before me and my back straight as a ruled line, and every sorrow of my life—my wife's wasting, slow as ink drying; the stillborn years between; the small humiliations of a careful man—I had entered into that body somewhere, in the shoulders, in the wrists, in the ache behind the eyes that comes from reading fine print by bad light. Sorrow wants a task. Digging was a task. The spade went in, the earth came up, and for whole minutes at a time I did not have to think about the cell.

But thinking finds you anyway. It found me now.

They had held me nine days. Nine days in a room below the harbor barracks with a stool, a bucket, and a lamp they never let go out, and Mr. Quill coming down twice a day with his soft voice and his clean cuffs to ask me questions I did not answer. What I had taken from the archive. Where the boxes were. Who else had seen the manifests. He never raised his voice. That was the worst of it—he never raised his voice, and by the fourth day I understood that he didn't need to, because he believed, quite sincerely, that I would break on my own schedule, the way a ledger balances eventually if you keep at the arithmetic.

He was nearly right. On the sixth night I had composed, in my head, the whole confession—the names, the addresses, the orchard—and I had gotten as far as rehearsing how I would say Elin's name so it would sound like a stranger's. And then I had thought of Marta.

My daughter is nineteen. She has her mother's chin and my caution, God help her, and when I came out of that cell—released on a technicality, Quill's own warrant signed against the wrong statute, the kind of error a careful man notices even when he is half-starved—when I came out and found her waiting at the barracks gate in the rain, having stood there, the guard told me, since dawn, she looked at me and said nothing. Just took my arm. And walking away from that gate, leaning on my girl's thin arm, I understood something that no amount of fear in the dark had taught me: that Quill had never once threatened her. He hadn't needed to. My imagination had done his work for him, free of charge, and that is what fear is—it is labor performed on behalf of powerful men by people like me, unpaid, unthanked, and endless.

I had nearly lost everything to protect the record. Standing there in the rain I had also understood, for the first time, what the record was for. It was so that Marta, at nineteen, at forty, at seventy, could stand at a gate or a graveside or a ballot table and know what had been done in her city and by whom. A record is a weapon that only works if someone is alive to pick it up. I had kept myself alive to be picked up.

Now I drove the spade into the frozen ground and tried to be grateful, and mostly failed, and kept digging anyway.

* * *

We lowered him at noon.

Odile had built the box herself, in the navy print shop's back room where the plates had spent their final night, using offcuts of pine and nails she paid for from her own purse, and she had planed it smooth and oiled it dark, and Beatrix had said it was the finest piece of joinery he'd ever seen and meant it. Inside, wrapped in linen, lay a small collection that Elin had chosen: her father's dividers, worn to fit his fingers; the original draft plate of the Hallowmere coastline, the one with the survey notes that were not survey notes; and a single printed sheet from the atlas—the true atlas, the one we had run three hundred copies of at dawn while Quill's men pounded on the naval yard gates—open to the page bearing the colophon.

Beatrix had signed that colophon. His own name, in his own hand, under his master's title: *completed and set forth by Beatrix Fairweather.* When I asked him once why it mattered so much, signing it, he said, "Because a map without a named maker is just weather." I think about that more than I should.

Elin stood at the head of the grave. She wore black, but not mourning black—working black, the smocked coat she'd worn setting type, and I noticed that detail and understood it. She buried her father as a tradesman, not a corpse. Her hands were steady until the first shovelful rang on the lid, and then they were not, and Beatrix moved to take her arm and she let him, which from Elin Marchetti was roughly equivalent to a public embrace.

She spoke briefly. "He drew coastlines," she said. "That's what I hated about it, when I was young. He drew coastlines while Mother coughed her lungs out, and he drew coastlines the year the grain ships sat in the roads at Hallowmere doing nothing, and he drew coastlines while people ate bark, and I thought: there is a man drawing pictures while the world burns. I was right," she said, and her voice cracked on it, "and I was wrong. Because underneath the pictures he was writing the only thing anyone has ever written about that year that was true. He couldn't say it aloud. So he hid it in the margins, in numbers, in coordinates, where no one looks. He spent his whole life being invisible so that the truth could be visible later. Later is now. Rest, Papa. You're in the record."

Then the earth went in, and the wind came off the water smelling of tar and thaw, and the two printer men began, quietly, badly, to sing—an old typesetters' song about the letter A, of all things, about how the A stands alone at the top of the case and all the other letters lean on it—and nobody corrected them, because it was as good a hymn as any for a man who had hidden truth in the spaces between letters.

* * *

Afterward we stood around the new grave in the way people do, not ready to leave, and the orchard did what orchards do in early spring: it insisted on continuing. Buds on the blackened trees. Grass pushing through the scorch marks where the farmhouse had burned. There was something obscene in it and something consoling, and I could not decide which, and I have since decided it was both, which is the honest answer to most questions.

Marta found me at the fence line.

She had not come to the burial—or rather, she had come and stood apart, near the road, the way she had learned to stand during the nine days, keeping herself out of the frame of other people's danger. Now she came to me and stood looking out at the orchard with her hands folded, and I knew the posture. It was mine. I had invented it across thirty years of ledgers. Seeing it on my daughter was like seeing my own handwriting on a stranger's page.

"You almost died," she said.

"Yes."

"And you'd do it again."

I considered lying to her. I am ashamed to write that I considered it; I am more ashamed to admit that lying to her has always been easy, because she trusts me the way I trusted the archive—with my whole weight, without checking the foundations. But I had watched a family spend a generation not speaking to each other because one man chose silence over truth, and I had watched what it cost Elin to dig him up out of that silence. I would not build the same thing in my own house.

"Yes," I said. "Not gladly. Not the same way. If I could do it again I'd do some parts differently—I'd get the manifests copied sooner, I'd never trust Halloran, I'd have sent you to your aunt in Corvess before the raid instead of after. But yes. I'd be counted again."

"Why?" she asked, and it wasn't defiance in her voice. It was hunger. Nineteen years old and hungry for a reason, the way I had been hungry at her age for a rulebook, and I understood suddenly that I had given her rules all her life and never once given her reasons, because giving reasons means admitting the rules might be broken.

"Because of what Quill said to me," I told her, "on the last day. He said—very reasonably, the way he says everything—that history is written by whoever can afford the ink, and that I was a small man interfering with people who could buy all the ink in the world. And I lay on that stool and I thought: yes. All true. Every word. They can buy the ink. They bought the archive and burned it. They bought the guild vote and very nearly bought the atlas. They will buy the historians, and the schoolbooks, and the sermons, and in twenty years the famine will be a sad story about weather." I looked at her. "And then I thought: but ink isn't the only thing that gets written. Ledgers get written too. Manifests. Tonnages. Dates. I spent my life keeping those records, and I never once thought of them as anything but drudgery—but a record doesn't care who buys the ink. A number is a number. Eleven thousand bushels diverted is eleven thousand bushels, whether the Vosses like it or not, whether the historians mention it or not. Somebody kept that number. Somebody kept it honestly. And because somebody did, we could prove it. That's why, Marta. Because the truth survives only if ordinary people keep ordinary records honestly, and then, once in a generation, refuse to hand them over."

She was quiet a long time. Down the slope, Beatrix and Elin were filling in the grave's edges, and Odile was talking to her printer friends with her sleeves rolled up, already, I suspected, planning something—there was talk of a second edition, of plates recut, of a cheap pamphlet version that could sell for a penny.

"Mr. Quill was released yesterday," Marta said at last. "The inquiry gave him leniency for testimony. It's in the paper this morning."

"I know."

"Does that make you angry?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually managed. "Yes," I said. "But it makes the record angry louder. That's what testimony is—Quill's confession is now in the record next to ours, and he can't take it back, and neither can we. We're bound together in it now, the guilty and the honest. That's what a record is. Nobody gets to leave it."

Marta looked at me for a moment longer, and then she did something she had not done since she was small: she leaned her head against my shoulder, briefly, and stepped back. "Then teach me the trade," she said. "The records. Before I'm too old to want reasons."

I could not speak for a moment. The frost was going out of the ground; the orchard smelled of wet earth and old apples; my dead wife's daughter stood before me asking to be counted, and I found that the grief I had carried all day had shifted its weight, the way cargo shifts in a hold—not lighter, but balanced, rideable.

"Yes," I said. "First lesson: never trust a colleague named Halloran."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and it carried down the slope, and Elin looked up from the grave with red eyes and, for the first time since I had known her, smiled.

* * *

We stayed until dusk. Beatrix built a small fire against the ruined wall of the farmhouse, and we drank cider from a barrel Odile swore had survived the fire in the cellar, though I privately doubted it and publicly did not care. Talk turned, as it inevitably does among survivors, to what comes next. The inquiry would sit through the summer. The Voss heirs had retained four lawyers and a bishop. The guild was in uproar, half its members furious at the defector who had swung the vote, half furious at everyone else. And there was talk—Elin's voice, low and certain, over the fire—of the next thing. Of a printing house rebuilt. Of a second atlas, with a new final plate Beatrix had begun sketching, though he would show no one.

I walked the fence line before we left, alone, as the light went blue. Beside the old apple tree the fresh earth showed dark against the grass, and I stood over it and did the thing I do, the clerk's thing: I made the entry. In my head, in the ledger I keep behind my eyes, where my wife lies filed and my stillborn sons lie filed and now this too:

*Aldous Marchetti, master cartographer. Interred beside the orchard he never owned, in ground purchased with a printer's savings. Survived by a daughter who resented him and honored him in the same breath, by an apprentice who finished his work, and by a record that will outlive everyone who read it.*

*Cost of entry: nine days in a cell, one burned farmhouse, one smashed press, one arrested clerk, one nearly corrupted soul, and the peace of mind of every person standing at this fire.*

*Value: beyond entry. See marginalia.*

I smiled at my own joke, alone in the dark, and then I stopped smiling, because down the road a lantern was coming toward the orchard—moving fast, swinging with the gait of someone walking hard, and behind it, faint on the wind, a voice calling a name.

It called mine.

Marta heard it too. She came to stand beside me at the fence, and together we watched the lantern grow, and I felt the old cold settle back into my shoulders, the clerk's cold, the cold of a man who knows that a record is never finished, that there is always one more entry, and that the next one may cost more than the last.

The lantern reached the gate. A boy stood there, breathless, holding out a folded paper stamped with the seal of the inquiry commission.

"They said to find the customs clerk," he panted. "They said to tell you—they've subpoenaed the manifests. Yours. All of them. And Mr. Fairweather—" he swallowed, glancing past me toward the fire, toward the man who had signed his name in history at dawn—"they've subpoenaed the atlas. The whole edition. Every copy."
