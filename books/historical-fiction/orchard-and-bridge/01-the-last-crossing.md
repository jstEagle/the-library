## 1. The Last Crossing

The bridge was still open when Mara reached it, but only just — the way a door is open after the latch has begun to fall.

She had walked since dawn from the inland road, following the river as it widened toward the sea, and all morning the bridge had been growing out of the mist ahead of her: first a gray suggestion, then a spine, then a long iron sentence written across the water in rivets and girders. Now she stood at its near end with her breath smoking in the cold and her bag strap biting into her shoulder, and she could see, on the far side, the roofs of Vesterhavn stacked up the hillside like dishes left to dry — red tile, slate, turf — and above them the pale smear of the sky where the mountains would be if the weather allowed.

A soldier stood at the bridge's midpoint. Not German. Norwegian, one of the new customs men the occupation had installed in October, a boy really, younger than she was, stamping papers with an importance he did not yet own. Mara watched him for a long moment before she stepped onto the planking. She let herself feel the fear fully, once, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape — and then she folded it small and put it away beneath her ribs, where it could ride without showing.

The coat was her aunt's gift across four hundred kilometers of silence: heavy wool, brown as wet bark, cut in the fashion of five years ago, mended at both elbows by hands that were not hers. The dispatches lay against her left side, sewn into the lining between the second and third panels, flat as pressed flowers. Forty-one pages of a government that no longer existed, reduced to tissue and ink and thread. When she walked she could not feel them. When she stood still she felt them the way you feel a splinter too deep to draw — not pain exactly, but knowledge.

Halfway across, the wind came up the fjord and tried to take her hat. She held it with one gloved hand and looked down through a gap between planks at the black water sliding beneath, cold enough to stop a heart in ninety seconds, and thought: *if they stop me here, I am to say I am going to my aunt's to help with sewing for the winter.* She had said it so many times on the road behind her that it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket, and she no longer heard the falseness in it. That frightened her more than the soldier did.

"Name?"

"Iversen. Mara Iversen."

"Purpose of travel?"

"My aunt lives in Vesterhavn. Solveig Iversen, on Bakkegata. I've come to keep house and do sewing through the winter."

He looked at her papers, then at her face, then at the papers again, and stamped them with a thump that shuddered through the little table. "You crossed today," he said, almost kindly. "Good. Tomorrow the bridge closes for the season. Ice in the span, they say." He jerked his chin toward the far tower, where men were already uncoiling cable. "After tomorrow there's the ferry only, and the ferry is theirs now." He said *theirs* the way people had begun saying it all along the road — flatly, eyes lowered, a word handled with tongs.

Mara thanked him and walked on, and behind her she heard the wind moving through the girders, a low organ note, like the bridge clearing its throat before a long silence.

* * *

Vesterhavn received her the way a sickroom receives a visitor: politely, quietly, with everyone keeping their voices down.

Bakkegata climbed from the harbor in a series of stone steps worn hollow at their centers by a hundred years of fishwives' boots. The houses leaned companionably toward each other, their eaves nearly touching overhead, hung with icicles like old lace gone gray. There were people about — a man scraping ice from a boat trailer, two women pausing mid-conversation outside the baker's — and every one of them turned to look at her, and every one of them smiled, and none of the smiles reached anywhere near the eyes. It was not unfriendliness. It was arithmetic. In a town this size, a stranger was either a curiosity or a danger, and no one yet knew which, so everyone was careful with their faces.

The baker's window held three loaves. Three. Mara counted them twice before she understood what she was seeing, and remembered the ration cards the stationmaster's wife had whispered about in Trondheim, and understood that the town was already hungry and being polite about it, the way Norwegians seemed to be polite about everything now — hunger, grief, the sound of German boots on their streets.

There were boots on the harbor road. A pair of officers in gray-green walked past the fish sheds unhurried, hands clasped behind them, looking at the boats moored along the quay with the mild proprietary interest of men browsing a shop whose owner had died. One of them stopped at the Gull — though Mara did not know its name yet — a fishing boat with high bowed shoulders and paint gone chalky at the waterline, and made a note in a small book. Something in the way he touched the rail, briefly, as if testing a horse's teeth, made her look away.

Solveig Iversen's house sat halfway up Bakkegata, blue once, weathered now to the color of a winter sea, with a small orchard behind it gone dormant and bristling, apple trees standing in rows like patients waiting. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, disciplined line. Mara stood at the gate a moment longer than she needed to. Somewhere behind that door lived the last two people in the world who shared her blood, and she had rehearsed this meeting on buses and in barn lofts all the way from Oslo, and none of the rehearsals survived contact with the actual blue door.

Astrid opened it before she knocked — a girl of eleven or twelve, all elbows and braids, with Mara's own gray eyes set in a thinner, sharper face. They stared at each other across the threshold like two animals meeting at a fence line.

"You're the cousin," Astrid said.

"I'm the cousin."

"Mama said you'd come today or not at all." The girl's gaze went past Mara's shoulder, down the hill, assessing the road behind her with a frankness that made Mara want to laugh and did make something in her chest loosen half a turn. "Because of the bridge."

"Because of the bridge."

Astrid nodded, satisfied, and shouted over her shoulder into the dim interior: "*Mor! Hun er her!*"

* * *

Solveig Iversen was smaller than Mara remembered, and slower, and her hair had gone entirely white since the funeral in '37, but her hands were the same — broad-knuckled, precise, a seamstress's hands even now, always moving, always finishing some small invisible task. She took Mara's face in those hands and studied it for a long moment in the lamplight of the front room.

"You have your father's chin," she said at last. "And his habit of arriving in winter." Then she pulled her close, and Mara smelled lanolin and woodsmoke and something faintly medicinal, and felt how thin her aunt had become — the shoulders like a bird's frame under the good wool dress — and had to close her eyes against the sudden sting of it.

"I couldn't write ahead," Mara said into her collar. "I'm sorry. It wasn't safe to—"

"Hush." Solveig held her at arm's length again. "You're here. The rest is talk, and talk is cheap this year." But her eyes moved over Mara's face with a thoroughness that was not merely affectionate, cataloguing, weighing — the pallor, the cracked lips, the calluses on fingers that claimed to be a city girl's — and Mara understood that her aunt had already begun reading her the way she read cloth, by touch, by weight, by what the weave concealed. "You'll take the back room. Astrid will show you. Supper is at six; we eat early now because lamp oil costs."

The house was warm and smelled of boiled fish and beeswax. On the wall of the front room hung a photograph of Mara's father as a young man, squinting into a sun that had been dead for years, beside a boat she didn't recognize. Below it, on the shelf, a tin cup, a spool of black thread, and a folded flag. Mara looked at these things only briefly. She had learned, in the last eight months, not to look long at photographs of the dead in houses she might be searched in.

Supper was cod, potatoes, and a spoonful of lingonberry preserve apportioned with surgical fairness among three plates. Astrid talked the way rivers run — about school, about the German lorry that had gotten stuck on the coast road and how the whole town had pretended not to know how to push it free for two days, about the harbor master's dog who bit only Germans and was therefore the most beloved animal in Vesterhavn. Solveig said little, ate slowly, and watched Mara over the rim of her cup with those reading eyes.

"And your sewing," Solveig said at last, into a pause. "You learned in Oslo? Properly?"

"Well enough to be useful."

"We'll see. Hands tell the truth even when mouths don't." She said it lightly, a domestic proverb, nothing more. But she said it while looking directly at Mara's hands, which lay very still on the tablecloth, on either side of her plate, like two small animals playing dead.

Later, Astrid showed her the back room — a narrow bed, a washstand, a window looking down the hill at the bridge, which was strung now with work lamps, men crawling along its span like lice on a great recumbent animal. The closing had begun. By morning the road behind her would exist only as memory and rumor.

"You can see everything from here," Astrid said. "In summer I watch the boats. Mama says I have salt in me instead of blood." She paused at the door, then added, with the devastating casualness children deploy: "You slept on the floor last night, didn't you. You've got the lines on your cheek."

"Yes," Mara said.

"From walking?"

"From walking."

Astrid considered this, decided it was acceptable, and went to bed.

* * *

Alone, Mara unpacked by feel, in the dark, without striking a match. Spare stockings. A Bible with a hollowed margin. Her mother's thimble, worn to fit another woman's finger. And the coat, which she hung on the back of the door, where it swung gently, gently, its hidden weight pressing against the lining like a second heartbeat.

Through the frost-feathered glass she watched the bridge until well past midnight. The workmen finished near one o'clock. One by one the lamps along the span went dark, and then a chain rattled somewhere in the middle distance, and a barrier swung down across the roadway — she could not see it clearly, but she heard it, the clank of it carrying up the hill through the cold air, unmistakable, final, like the lid of a box coming down.

So. The road behind her was closed. Whatever happened now, happened here.

Mara lay down on the narrow bed in her coat, because it was warmer that way, and because — she admitted it to the ceiling, to the dark, to no one — she could not yet bear to have it farther from her body than an arm's reach. Down the hill, the tide was turning. Somewhere in the harbor the Gull rocked at her mooring, unaware of what she would soon be asked to carry. And in the morning Aunt Solveig would hand her needle and thread and say *show me your stitches*, and Mara would have to begin the longest performance of her life: hemming dresses, mending nets, memorizing forty-one pages by lamplight, being nobody, being ordinary, being safe.

Outside, snow began to fall on the closed bridge, filling the wheel ruts, softening the barrier, erasing, flake by flake, the evidence that anyone had ever crossed at all.
