## 2. A Seamstress's Hands

The needle was the first thing to betray her.

Mara had told them she was a seamstress, and so a seamstress she became, but her hands had not been trained for it. They were a courier's hands — quick, sure, made for knots and locks and the flat sliding weight of papers passed palm to palm beneath a coat. Now they fumbled at Solveig's mending basket, pricking themselves on pins, tangling thread, and each small failure felt like a confession. Aunt Solveig watched from her chair by the stove and said nothing, which was worse than correction.

"Thimble," Solveig said at last, on the second morning, holding one out. It was brass, worn smooth as a river stone by some earlier woman's decades of work. "You hold it like an egg you're afraid will hatch."

Mara took it. "I learned in Oslo," she said. "Fine work. Hems, mostly."

"I know where you learned." Solveig's voice was mild as milk. She turned back to her newspaper, the *Vesterhavn Blad*, four pages thin now under the censor's knife, and said no more. That was the arrangement between them: Mara performed the niece, and Solveig permitted herself to be convinced, and both of them knew exactly how much of it was theater.

* * *

The house sat at the top of Strandgata with its gable turned toward the water like a listening ear. From the kitchen window Mara could see the whole sweep of the harbor: the Gull riding at her mooring, tar-black and patient; the fish sheds with their doors padlocked for winter; the bridge itself, that long iron spine across the fjord, already growing quiet as the season died. Each day fewer carts crossed. Each day the wind off the ice carried more of a smell she couldn't name until Astrid named it for her.

"That's the smell of things stopping," the girl said, matter-of-fact, peeling potatoes at the sink. "Papa used to say the fjord smells like money in summer and like waiting in winter. Now it only smells like waiting."

"And what does waiting smell like?" Mara asked, threading a needle for the third time.

Astrid considered. "Cold iron. And paraffin. And other people's bread."

Mara laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, unguarded, the first since Oslo — and immediately hated how loud it sounded in the small warm room. But Astrid grinned, gap-toothed at eleven, triumphant at having produced it, and something in Mara's chest shifted half a degree out of true. She had built herself into a corridor with no furniture, a person through whom information moved without snagging. Affection snagged. Affection was a hook the enemy could pull. And yet here was this child with potato starch to her elbows, looking at her as if she were interesting, as if she were someone, and the corridor grew a chair in the corner, a lamp, a window with a view.

She would have to be careful. She wrote it in the ledger of her mind, under the column headed *Risks*: *the girl*.

* * *

The days took on a shape that was almost merciful in its dullness. Mornings, nets. The men of Vesterhavn brought their gear to be patched because Solveig's household had always done such work and habits outlast wars. Mara learned to sit among the women in the fish shed with her fingers stiffening in the cold, working twine through mesh, and to listen. Listening was the one skill she had in abundance. The women talked of herring prices and ration cards and whose son had written from the camp at Falstad, and they talked around the Germans the way water talks around a stone — never touching it, never ceasing to flow because of it. Mara filed everything. Names, grievances, the fact that the ferry office still employed two men who drank too much and one man who drank nothing at all.

That last one was Halvard Brandt, though she didn't yet know his name.

He came to the house on the fourth afternoon, stamped snow off his boots with unnecessary thoroughness, and filled the doorway like weather. He was broad, gray at the temples, with a face arranged for friendliness the way shop windows are arranged for Christmas — everything visible, nothing real. He carried a parcel of wool.

"For the girl's mittens," he said. "Solveig, I heard you'd family come for the winter. Good. Houses shouldn't echo."

"So kind of you," Solveig said, in the tone of a woman accepting a loan she intended never to repay.

His eyes found Mara and stayed there, pleasant as a hand on the shoulder. "And this is the niece. From Oslo."

"From Lillehammer originally," Mara said. The lie was prepared, smooth as a hem. "But yes, I've lived in Oslo these six years. Sewing."

"Sewing." He nodded slowly, as if the word deserved respect. "It's a hard trade now. Cloth being what it is." He unwrapped the wool himself, laying it out with strange delicacy for such big hands. "You'll find us quiet after Oslo. Though we get our excitement. The bridge closing, for one. First year I can remember they've closed it early. Ice, they say."

"Ice," Mara agreed.

"You crossed it the last day, did they tell me? The twenty-second. Late crossing, too — near dusk. You must have hurried."

The kitchen went very slightly colder. Mara felt Solveig go still beside the stove, a stillness like a held breath, and understood that this man collected comings and goings the way other men collected stamps.

"The roads were empty," Mara said lightly. "Everyone sensible was already across. My aunt needed help with the winter mending, and my own situation in the city had become — difficult. Rents. You know how it is in wartime."

"Wartime," Halvard repeated, tasting it. "Yes." He smiled at Astrid, who had stopped peeling potatoes and stood watching him with open dislike. "And how is school? Still learning your English songs?"

"We don't sing much now," Astrid said. "The radio's broken."

"Broken radios are very common these winters." He gathered his wrapping paper, folded it into precise quarters, tucked it inside his coat. At the door he paused, and the friendliness drained out of his face for just a moment, like water finding a crack, leaving something older and wearier beneath. "Welcome to Vesterhavn, Miss Iversen. Small town. We notice things. It's not unfriendliness — it's just that noticing is all the entertainment we have left."

Then he was gone down Strandgata, his footprints filling with new snow.

Solveig let three full minutes pass before she spoke. "Halvard Brandt is not your enemy," she said. "That is what makes him dangerous."

"What is he, then?"

"He is a man with a son in Germany." Solveig picked up her mending. "Hostage work goes both ways, niece. Some men are leashes. Remember which end of him is held."

* * *

Night was hers alone, or nearly. Solveig retired at nine with liniment and a cough that had begun to worry the edges of itself. Astrid slept in the alcove bed, breathing in the deep tidal rhythm of childhood. And Mara sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low, shade tilted toward the wall, and worked.

The dispatches were gone — surrendered to the fire in Oslo months ago, their contents pressed into the only vault the Gestapo could not search. Sixty-four groups of five figures, keyed to a shipping table, memorized in chains of association she had spent a whole autumn building: numbers disguised as grocery lists, tide tables, hymn verses, her mother's birthday, the price of herring. Every night she recited a portion, and every night she tested herself against the little exercise book where she copied them from memory, then burned the page in the stove and stirred the ash until it was indistinguishable from any winter's ordinary grey.

Tonight the figures came easily, and that frightened her more than difficulty would have. Ease bred carelessness. She made herself slow, made herself suffer through the third chain twice, and when she finished she sat looking at the flame through the stove grate and thought about Halvard Brandt saying *we notice things*.

Her cover was good. Her accent was the problem. Six years in Oslo had sanded the Lillehammer off her vowels, and to a town's ear Oslo speech was city speech, and city speech in wartime meant government, and government meant either collaboration or resistance, and neither kind of stranger was comfortable. She began, methodically, to roughen herself. She practiced the flattened vowels of the coast while washing dishes, letting the words come up through her chest instead of her head. She mispronounced words on purpose in front of Astrid and let the girl correct her, laughing, correcting her again, until her tongue learned the local grain. It was exhausting in a way that had nothing to do with muscles — this constant double life, this woman walking around inside a woman, each one keeping the other's books.

By the eighth night she was so tired she caught herself reciting cipher figures aloud in a whisper, and clapped a hand over her mouth as if she'd spilled scalding water.

"Mara?" Astrid's voice, sleep-thick, from the alcove.

"Just counting stitches," Mara said. "Go back to sleep."

"You count funny."

"I count the way Mama taught me. Go to sleep."

Silence. Then, drowsily, dangerously: "Will you teach me to sew properly tomorrow? Proper seams, not net knots. I want to make something. A dress, maybe. For after the war. I'm going to have a red dress with white buttons, and I'm going to wear it to the bridge opening, and everyone will say, look, there's Astrid in her red dress, and nobody will remember there was ever a war at all."

Mara sat in the lamplight with sixty-four chains of figures locked behind her ribs and felt the strangest sensation: grief and tenderness arriving together, inseparable, like twins. A red dress. After the war. As if after were a place you could simply walk to, over the frozen hills, through occupied country, past every checkpoint between here and freedom.

"I'll teach you," she said. "Proper seams. French seams, even. They're the strongest kind — the raw edge gets folded away inside, where nobody sees, and the seam holds anyway."

"Like people," Astrid murmured, already sinking. "Folded away inside."

Mara turned down the lamp. In the dark she listened to the child breathe and the stove tick and, far below, the ice shifting in the fjord with a sound like slow applause. She thought: *this is the danger. Not Halvard Brandt and his careful eyes. This.* The performance of ordinariness was supposed to protect her, but ordinariness, performed well enough, became real, and a real aunt, a real cousin, a real kitchen — those could be taken hostage faster than any secret.

She lay down and set her mind, as she did every night, to the discipline of forgetting how much she loved them.

* * *

On the ninth morning, frost ferned the windows and the world outside had gone silent and white. Mara was at the table reheeling a fisherman's boot — another domestic art acquired overnight — when Astrid burst in from the woodshed with her cheeks blazing.

"There's a man at the ferry office asking about boats! Old Sverre saw him — he's got papers, official ones, and he's writing names. He asked about the *Gull*, Mara. Why would anyone ask about the *Gull*?"

Mara kept her eyes on the boot, on the waxed thread, on her hands doing the work of a woman with nothing to hide. Through the window, far down at the harbor, she could see a figure in a long coat standing on the quay beside the frozen slipway, making notes in a book, unhurried, thorough.

"Probably inventory," she said. "They count things now. Boats, barrels, people."

Astrid shivered. "Whose side is counting for?"

"That," Mara said quietly, threading her needle, "is the whole question of the war."

Down at the harbor, the man in the long coat finished his page, closed his book, and turned to look up Strandgata — straight up, it seemed to her, toward the house with the listening gable.
