## 6. Three Berths, One Boat

The news came with the fish.

Old Torvald brought it up from the harbor on the fourth morning of December, stamping snow off his boots in the entryway and holding out a cod so stiff with frost it might have been carved from driftwood. He set it on Solveig's kitchen table as though laying down a card he had been waiting all winter to play.

"Gull can run," he said.

Mara's needle stopped mid-stitch. She was mending one of Astrid's stockings by the stove, and for a moment she did not trust herself to look up. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves of the house the way it always did in Vesterhavn — patiently, like a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Solveig wiped her hands on her apron. "Run where?"

"South, along the coast, then west past the light. There's weather coming off the sea Thursday night — fog thick enough to pour into a glass. The patrol boats won't see their own bows." Torvald lowered his voice, though the kitchen was empty but for the three of them and the ticking of the clock above the dresser. "One crossing. One tide. After that, they'll be watching every hull that floats."

Mara kept her eyes on the stocking. Her heart was behaving disgracefully — hammering, leaping — and she made herself count the stitches instead: four up, four down, the way she had counted everything since Oslo fell. Four stitches to an inch. Four days to the tide. She had learned early that a courier's pulse could betray her faster than any document, and so she sat very still and let the arithmetic do its quiet work beneath her calm.

"How many berths?" Solveig asked.

"Three. The Gull's a small boat — you know her. Three souls besides the skipper, no more, or she rides too low if the swell picks up." Torvald rubbed his jaw, gray stubble rasping under his palm. "But there's the other thing. The harbor master. Bergstrom."

"What about him?"

"He'll release papers for one passenger only. One name, stamped, official-looking enough to satisfy a patrol boat at dawn." Torvald shrugged, a small bitter movement. "He says it's all he dares. Maybe it is. Maybe he's right. But it means somebody has to choose."

The word *somebody* hung in the steam rising off the kettle. Mara threaded her needle through the wool and pulled it tight, and thought: *not somebody. Me. It will have to be me who chooses, because I am the one carrying the weight.*

She had known this was coming. Not this precisely — not the number three, not the particular cruelty of papers for one — but some version of it, ever since she'd sewn the dispatches into her coat lining in a dark room in Oslo while the radio read out names of the arrested. Every plan has a hinge, and every hinge eventually swings. She had simply hoped, in the coward's way one hopes, that the swinging would happen somewhere far from this kitchen, far from Astrid's stockings drying by the stove, far from Solveig's hands kneading bread dough with the steady rhythm of a woman who had outlived her husband and most of her friends and refused to be surprised by anything else the world did.

"I'll need to think," Solveig said.

"You'll need to think fast," said Torvald. "Names to Bergstrom by Wednesday night."

* * *

After Torvald left, the day went on in the terrible ordinary way days went on. Solveig salted the cod. Astrid came home from helping at the schoolhouse with red cheeks and snow in her braids, and announced that Fru Dahl had given them dried apples and that she'd saved hers, and produced two wrinkled rings of fruit from her pocket with the solemnity of a diplomat presenting credentials. One for Aunt Solveig. One for Mara.

Mara ate hers slowly. Across the table, Astrid chewed hers in quick birdlike bites and talked about whether there would be a Christmas this year, really, with things the way they were, and whether the Germans would let the church bells ring, and whether Mara had ever seen a real Christmas tree in Oslo, one of the big ones, taller than a door.

"Taller than two doors," Mara said.

"Were you happy? At Christmas, I mean. Before."

The question landed strangely, the way children's questions do — aimed straight through all the armor an adult wears without even knowing it's there. Mara looked at her cousin: twelve years old, gap between her front teeth just closing, a smudge of ink on her wrist where she'd been practicing sums. Twelve years old, which meant she had been eight when the war began, which meant half her childhood was already something the occupation had taken and would never give back.

"I was happy," Mara said. "We had a tree my father cut himself, from up past the summer pasture. My mother strung it with candles, real ones, and we weren't allowed in the room alone until they were lit because — " she smiled despite herself, "— because my mother believed the whole house could burn down from one careless elbow."

Astrid laughed. And Mara felt the thing she had been refusing to feel since the bridge, since the first night in this house when Astrid had shyly set an extra blanket on the guest bed and said goodnight in a whisper, as if strangers deserved quiet. She felt affection, plain and enormous, sitting down inside her chest like a guest who intended to stay.

It was the most dangerous thing that had happened to her yet.

That night, after the lamp was turned low, Mara lay awake doing the inventory. She was good at inventories; the work demanded them. Weight, bulk, value, risk — she could reckon a cargo in her sleep. So she reckoned.

*Astrid.* Twelve years old. Whole life ahead of her — sixty, seventy years of it, Christmases and summers and whatever she would grow into, and she was already brave, already quick with a lie under pressure, as the inspection had proved. If Astrid crossed, the family's future crossed with her. If Astrid stayed, everything ahead of her stayed hostage to the war's slow teeth.

*Solveig.* Fifty-eight years old, though she carried them like seventy. The doctor in September had used careful words about her heart — *she must not exert herself, she must avoid distress* — words that in wartime were a kind of joke, since distress was now the daily ration. Solveig would never survive the open water in winter, and everyone knew it, including Solveig. And Solveig would refuse anyway. Mara had watched her these weeks: the woman moved stores to the boathouse in the dark, shifted flour and tinned fish and lamp oil with the methodical silence of someone building an escape she would never speak aloud, and Mara understood that the escape Solveig was building was not for herself. It had her brother's stubbornness in it. It had grief converted, gram by gram, into preparation.

*Herself.* Twenty-six years old, healthy, trained, and carrying a cipher sewn against her ribs worth more than every life in this town stacked together. That was the doctrine — she had been taught it plainly in Oslo: *the dispatch outranks the courier.* A courier's job was delivery, not survival. If the code reached England, it could shorten the war. Shorten it by how much? Nobody knew. Months, perhaps. Perhaps only weeks. But men died in weeks. Men died in days.

Three berths. Papers for one.

The mathematics was simple and it was monstrous. Send Astrid — spend the single stamped name on the child, keep the future moving west across the water. Then walk the cipher south over the frozen hills herself, alone, through occupied territory, with a route she half knew and papers she didn't have. Or take the berth herself, deliver the code safely by sea, and leave Astrid and Solveig to the occupation's mercy — mercy being a word the occupiers kept in their vocabulary the way a fisherman keeps a hook in his tackle box: present, sharp, rarely offered.

Or third: give the berth to Solveig, let the old woman have her one impossible chance at escape, and watch pride and love argue until neither won.

There was no version of the sum that came out clean. Every column ended in blood or grief; the only question was whose.

* * *

Wednesday morning Mara walked down to the harbor, ostensibly to buy oil, actually to see the Gull.

She found her moored behind the breakwater, small and black-bowed, nets piled on her deck like sleeping animals. The skipper — Eirik Lund, a broad silent man who had fished these waters thirty years — was checking his lines, and he acknowledged her with a nod that contained an entire conversation. He knew what she was. He had known since the bridge closed, probably; fishermen knew everything the water carried. He didn't say a word about it. He just tapped the deck twice with his knuckle, the way a man taps a barrel to hear how full it is, and went back to his work, and Mara understood that he had already decided he would take whoever was put aboard and ask nothing, and that this was its own kind of courage, quieter than hers.

Bergstrom the harbor master stood in the doorway of his office, a thin man blowing on his hands, and when Mara passed he gave her the look officials give — flat, weighing, already rehearsing his denials. *Papers for one,* Torvald had said. One name on a form. As if a life could be reduced to a line in a ledger, and perhaps it could, perhaps that was what ledgers were for.

On the walk back up the hill, the wind came hard off the fjord and drove the snow sideways, and Mara stood a moment at the bend where the path overlooked the water, letting it push against her like a hand testing her balance. Below, the Gull rocked at her mooring. Beyond the point, the bridge stood closed for the season, its span furred white, going nowhere.

She thought about her father cutting the Christmas tree above the summer pasture, and about her mother's rule about candles and careless elbows. One flame, tended properly, lights a whole room. One flame, handled badly, burns a house down.

By Wednesday night, someone's name had to go on that form.

When Mara came through the gate, she saw Halvard standing at the fence line next door, watching her over the top of the snow, and he did not look away quickly enough.
