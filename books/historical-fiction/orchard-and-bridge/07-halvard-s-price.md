## 7. Halvard's Price

The ledger did not lie, but it did not tell the truth either. Halvard Ness had learned the difference years ago, in the first winter of the occupation, when he'd sat across a desk from a German clerk with damp hands and a polite smile and understood that everything he said from that moment on would be written down somewhere, weighed, and someday — perhaps — used to hang him or save him. He had chosen his words carefully ever since. He kept two ledgers now: the one the occupation office saw, neat and compliant, recording fish landings and ferry schedules and the comings and goings of a town that had learned to be boring; and the one he kept behind his eyes, where nothing was written but nothing was forgotten.

It was the second ledger that troubled him tonight.

He sat in the back room of his boathouse below the bridge road, a single lamp turned low, a cup of coffee gone cold at his elbow. On the table before him lay three items he had assembled over eleven days of watching, each one small enough to dismiss alone, each one heavier when set beside the others.

The first was a thread. Literally — a length of grey wool he'd found snagged on the fence rail outside Solveig Iversen's house, the kind of hard-wearing weave that came from Oslo shops, not Vesterhavn ones. A seamstress's scrap. Except that Mara Iversen, the niece, had been in town eleven days and had bought no wool at the Sundt store, because Halvard had asked the Sundts, casually, over herring, the way he asked about everything.

* * *

The second was a name. He had gone to the occupation office on Tuesday with the weekly landing reports, as he always did, and while the clerk stamped pages he had let his eyes drift — just drift — to the transit notices pinned behind the desk. Notices about persons of interest moving north along the coastal roads before the bridge closed. One description in particular had stayed with him the way a splinter stays under skin: *female courier, approximately twenty-five years, believed traveling under family cover, western fjord districts.* No name. Just the shape of a person, described by someone who had never seen her.

And the third was her hands.

He had watched her at the dock on Thursday, helping Astrid coil a net, and there was nothing wrong with how she handled the mesh — clumsy enough, even, for a girl raised in town. But when the wind gusted and took the net's edge toward a stack of gutting knives, her arm had come out fast, flat, economical, pinning the whole weight against her body without a wasted inch of motion. That was not a seamstress's arm. That was an arm trained to move quickly in places where quickness mattered.

Halvard turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. Through the wall he could hear the water slapping the pilings, the sound that had been his whole life until the Germans came, and would be again, if his son lived to see it.

His son. Eirik, nineteen years old, held since October in a camp near Trondheim, hostage to Halvard's usefulness. The arrangement had never been stated so baldly, but baldness was unnecessary; the major who managed him had a way of saying *your boy writes good letters, doesn't he, Herr Ness?* that made the arithmetic clear without any figures being shown. Cooperate, and Eirik ate. Falter, and Eirik became a number transferred eastward, into paperwork too thick for letters to cross.

So far, cooperation had meant small things. Names of men who grumbled. Times of fishing runs. The location of a radio Halvard had pretended not to know about and then, weeping inside, had given up. Each small thing had felt survivable at the time, the way frostbite feels survivable right up until the fingers blacken.

Now the small things were converging on a girl of twenty-five who mended nets badly and moved like a soldier, and Halvard understood that the season of small things was over. If he reported what he suspected — and he could report it, tomorrow morning, and be thanked, and maybe even win Eirik a transfer home — then the net around the Iversen house would close within a day. He knew how they worked now. He had watched them work on other houses.

But if he said nothing, and they found her anyway — and they would, eventually; the major missed nothing, least of all a man's silences — then the silence itself would be his confession. And Eirik would pay for it.

He put his head in his hands. Outside, the last light went off the water, and the bridge stood dark and closed against the winter, its iron spine holding nothing anymore.

* * *

He told himself he was only walking past. It was a lie he permitted himself, the way a starving man permits himself to smell bread.

The Iversen house sat above the harbor road with its back to the orchard, smoke rising thin and straight from the chimney, windows lit amber against the early dusk. Halvard came up the path slowly, boots crunching on frozen ruts, rehearsing sentences and discarding them. What do you say to a woman you may destroy? What do you say to one you might still save?

Solveig opened the door before he knocked. She had always been like that — his late wife's cousin, keeper of other people's silences for forty years. She looked at him a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face, she stepped aside and let him in without a word, which was either kindness or a trap, and Halvard had stopped being able to tell the difference between those things long ago.

The kitchen was warm. A lamp burned on the table beside a half-mended dress, blue wool, child-sized. Astrid was nowhere in sight; he heard her upstairs, singing something under her breath — a skipping song, nonsense syllables, the kind children use to count things out.

Mara stood at the stove with her back half-turned, and when she looked at him, Halvard felt the strange vertigo of recognition. Not recognition of her — he had never known her — but recognition of the thing in her face. He had seen it in his own mirror every morning for three years. It was the look of a person carrying a weight whose exact size must remain secret even from the people feeding you. The look of a survivor doing sums.

"You're Halvard Ness," she said. Not a question. "Astrid says you know everyone's business."

"Astrid is generous." He took the chair Solveig indicated, feeling the absurd politeness of it, sitting down to tea with people he might hand to the Gestapo before the week was out. "I've come about the boat."

"The Gull," Solveig said. Her voice gave away nothing, but her hands, folded on the table, had gone very still.

"There's talk she'll run once more before the ice. Morning tide, day after tomorrow." He kept his eyes on the lamp flame. "I know because I'm expected to know such things. I report them." He said it plainly, laying his own corruption on the table between them like a fish too rotten to sell. "I thought you should hear it from me rather than learn it later."

Silence. The fire ticked in the stove. Upstairs, Astrid's song trailed off mid-phrase.

"And why," Mara said quietly, "would you warn us?"

Because my son is in Trondheim and your face is in a file and I cannot decide which of you I am going to betray. Because I have done small evil for so long I no longer know if large good is still possible for me. Because you look at me the way I look at myself and I cannot bear it.

What he said was: "Because once, a long time ago, before any of this, Solveig's brother carried dispatches through these hills in weather worse than this, and I ferried him across the sound twice and asked nothing. I've been asking for things ever since. I wanted one hour of being the man I was."

Mara studied him, and he endured it, and he saw the moment the sum changed in her eyes — not trust, nothing so clean as trust, but the grim fellowship of two people who both understood exactly what occupation costs, down to the coin.

"Your son," she said. "Eirik. Astrid mentions him."

"He's alive because I'm useful. I'm useful because I say things. There are things I could say about this house." His voice cracked and he hated it. "I haven't said them. I don't know yet whether I can keep not saying them. That's the truth of why I came. Not to threaten. To beg."

"To beg?" Solveig's eyebrows rose.

"To beg you to decide quickly. Whatever you're going to do — sail, stay, burn, run — do it before I'm asked directly. Because I don't know what I'll answer when they ask me straight, Mrs. Iversen. I'd like to believe I'd hold my tongue. But I've believed that before, about other things, and I was wrong every time." He stood, knees stiff, an old man suddenly at fifty-one. "Decide faster than my courage fails. That's all I ask."

At the door, Mara touched his sleeve — lightly, the way you touch something you aren't sure is solid. "The man you were ferried him twice," she said. "Maybe the man you are gets a third crossing."

He walked home in the dark with the words turning over in him like stones in a tide. Behind him the house glowed warm against the hillside, and inside it, he knew, three women were already counting berths.

In his back room, the telephone sat black and patient on its shelf. Tomorrow the office would call, as it called every morning, and a man with damp hands would ask his questions.

Halvard looked at the instrument for a long time. Then he blew out the lamp, so that whichever choice he made at dawn, he would make it in the same darkness as everyone else.
