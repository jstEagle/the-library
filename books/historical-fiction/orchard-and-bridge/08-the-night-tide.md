## 8. The Night Tide

The decision came to Mara the way ice comes to a fjord — not all at once, but in slow, silent sheets that thickened until nothing could move beneath them.

She had spent the day telling herself she would decide at night. That was a lie she had permitted herself, because the daylight hours demanded hands: hands for mending, hands for stirring barley into the pot, hands for the small domestic deceptions that kept a household from noticing it was drowning. But when Solveig banked the stove and Astrid went up to the cold loft with her candle, and the house settled into its creaking winter quiet, Mara sat at the kitchen table with her aunt's ledger open before her and knew that the arithmetic had finished itself without her permission.

Three berths on the *Gull*. One set of papers.

She turned the pages without reading them. Solveig's hand marched down the columns in ink gone brown with age — herring, salt, lamp oil, flour — a whole life measured out in weights and sums. Her brother Eirik's name appeared in the early years, before the war, before the boat that had carried him out one morning and returned without him. Mara traced the faded figures and understood, finally, why Solveig never spoke of him: grief was not something her aunt carried. It was something she *did*, daily, like chores, like prayer, like the quiet rearrangement of stores in a boathouse that no one was supposed to notice.

Mara had noticed.

* * *

Solveig was in the parlor, where she always sat after dark now, wrapped in two shawls because the wood ration would not stretch to warming rooms no one slept in. Her heart made itself known these days in small betrayals — the pause at the top of the stairs, the blue tinge at her lips on cold mornings — and she spoke of it the way she spoke of weather, as an inconvenience belonging to the sky rather than to herself.

"Astrid should take the berth."

Mara said it standing in the doorway, because sitting down beside her aunt would have made it a conversation, and conversations could be argued with. She had rehearsed the sentence all evening and it still came out wrong — flat, abrupt, like a dropped plate.

Solveig did not look up from her knitting. The needles clicked twice, three times, in the silence. "The girl is twelve years old," she said. "And you are twenty-three, and I am fifty-eight, and we have all just discovered arithmetic." She set the needles down at last and folded her hands over the unfinished sock. "Say the rest of it, Mara."

"The harbor master releases papers for one passenger only. If we put Astrid's name on them, no one will question it. A child going to her mother's people in Bergen — no officer looks twice at that." Mara's voice held steady through sheer force of practice; she had learned in Oslo that steadiness was not a feeling but a skill, like sewing. "You know it's right."

"I know nothing of the kind." Solveig's eyes came up then, gray as the sea off Vesterhavn in November, and they did not soften. "I know that a child alone on a boat in wartime is not safer than a child here. I know that if the *Gull* is stopped, a girl carrying papers with our name on them becomes evidence. And I know that you have been walking around this house for ten days like a woman carrying a stone in her chest, waiting for permission to drop it. So say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"You are staying."

It was not a question, and Mara did not answer it, which was answer enough. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the bare orchard with a sound like low voices.

"My brother crossed those hills in '41," Solveig said quietly. "Winter route, over the ice, down toward Åndalsnes. He was twenty-five." She looked at her hands, at the knuckles swollen with decades of rope and needle. "I have already given this family one courier to the mountains. I had hoped God would consider the account settled."

"Then let me give you something back instead."

"No." Solveig rose, slowly, one hand braced on the arm of the chair, and stood looking down at her niece with an expression Mara had never seen on her face before — not anger, but something older and more terrible: recognition. "You want me to stay because of my heart, so let us speak plainly about hearts. Mine is failing, yes. It has perhaps a winter left in it, or two, and I intend to spend them in my own house, in my own bed, with my own apples going soft in my own orchard. I will not die in some strange port with my hat pinned on, waiting for news of a niece who chose a cipher over her family." She picked up her knitting again, and her hands trembled only a little. "Astrid goes. You go over the hills or you go nowhere. And I stay, because someone must be here when the soldiers come asking questions, and an old woman with a bad heart makes a very uninteresting prisoner."

"Solveig—"

"Go to bed, Mara. The tide won't wait for your courage, and neither will the child."

* * *

But Astrid would wait. Astrid was waiting now, and had been since supper, and Mara knew it the way one knows weather coming — in the quality of the silence above stairs, in the too-careful way the girl had said goodnight.

Mara climbed to the loft. Astrid sat cross-legged on her cot in the candlelight, fully dressed, her schoolbooks stacked neatly beside her as though she had packed them and unpacked them a dozen times. She was thin in the way of children that winter, wrists like bird bones, but her eyes were Solveig's eyes, gray and unfooled.

"Is it me?" Astrid asked. No preamble. Children of occupation learned to skip preambles; hope wasted time.

Mara sat down on the edge of the cot. The floorboards were cold through her stockings. "It's you."

"And you're staying."

"Yes."

"Because of the thing sewn in the coat."

Mara went very still. Ten days of performance — the hems and the nets and the careful Oslo-blunted vowels — and a twelve-year-old had simply watched and known. Perhaps that was what childhood became in wartime: not innocence lost, but innocence sharpened into something like sight.

"It doesn't matter what's in the coat," Mara said carefully. "What matters is that there are words you need to carry, and no paper can carry them safely. Only memory. Only you."

Astrid lifted her chin, the way she did when the teacher called on her and she intended to be right. "Teach me."

So Mara taught her. She had prepared for this without admitting it, the way Solveig had stocked the boathouse without naming the escape — the burned half of the cipher reduced, over three nights by lamplight, to numbers that could be sung. She had hidden them inside a skipping song, an old nonsense rhyme every child between here and Trondheim knew, so ordinary that even a patrol whistling past would hear nothing but a girl amusing herself. Mara sang it through once, slowly, watching Astrid's lips move, mapping each altered count onto the sequence she had carved into her own memory and then into flame.

"Now you."

Astrid sang it back. Perfect. Then again, faster, with the swinging rhythm children gave such songs on playgrounds, and it was done — the key to everything Mara had risked her life to carry, lodged now in a child's mouth disguised as a game.

"If anyone asks," Mara said, "it's a counting song. Sing it badly. Sing it bored. And sing it to yourself every night, because forgetting is the one thing that cannot be undone."

"What happens to you?" Astrid asked. The question came out small, and Mara understood it was not really about the mission.

"I walk east over the hills while you sail west. In the spring, when this is over—" *if*, said a cold voice inside her, and she stepped on it— "I'll find you. Or you'll find me. Families do that."

Astrid considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she reached under her pillow and produced a knitted mitten, lumpy, one thumb longer than the other, dyed an ambitious and uneven red. "I made it in secret. For the crossing. But your hands get colder than mine — you stand at the water." She pushed it into Mara's palm and closed Mara's fingers over it with both of her own small hands. "That way we're both wearing it. Sort of."

Mara could not speak. She pulled the girl against her and held her and felt the thin shoulder blades, the heartbeat quick as a bird's, and thought: *this is what inheritance is. Not what you keep. What you put into someone else's keeping and trust the world to deliver.*

"Sleep now," she whispered. "Tomorrow the tide comes early."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the banked stove with the mitten in her lap and let the house tell her its last quiet hours: the settling beams, the wind worrying the shutters, Solveig's slow tread overhead as her aunt moved through her rooms touching, Mara imagined, every object she had decided to stay and die among.

Near three o'clock, Solveig came down. She did not ask why Mara was awake. She set water on to heat, made coffee stretched with roasted acorn, and put a cup in Mara's hands as though it were any ordinary morning, and perhaps — Mara thought — that was the truest kindness available to either of them.

"Eirik's boots are in the boathouse," Solveig said, staring into the middle distance. "Third shelf, behind the tar pot. They'll fit you. He had big feet." A pause. "He also had a fool's courage, and look where it got him. So be smarter than my brother, Mara. Be boring. Be slow. Be the most tedious traveler those mountains have ever failed to notice."

"I'll try."

"Don't try. Arrive." Solveig wrapped her shawls tighter and stood at the window, where the first gray suggestion of dawn was bleeding into the black of the fjord. "The tide turns at six-forty. Halvard will expect us at the dock by six. Wear the coat with the lining torn — a seamstress with a perfect coat draws eyes. And Mara." She turned, and in the faint light her face was unreadable, except that her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "When you plant yourself somewhere safe next spring and think of this house — don't grieve us. Grief is for things that are finished. We are not finished. We are sent."

Outside, far down the hill, the harbor bell began to ring the tide. Somewhere below, the *Gull* knocked gently at her mooring like a patient horse. And along the quay, Mara could see — small and unmistakable in the lamplight — the long dark shapes of German greatcoats, more of them than yesterday, moving unhurried among the fishing boats.

One of the figures stopped. Turned. Looked up the hill, toward the house.

Mara stepped back from the window, her heart slamming, Astrid's song running through her head like a pulse.
