## 9. Morning Tide

The tide would turn at 6:14. Mara knew it the way she knew her own pulse now — by lamplight, by repetition, by the small arithmetic of survival she had been keeping since Oslo. At 5:40 she stood in the boathouse with the smell of tar and old herring in her throat, the cipher flat against her ribs where Solveig had sewn it into a canvas bodice that morning with hands that did not shake.

"You'll bind them too tight," Solveig had said, pulling the last stitch through. "A body needs to breathe, even a body carrying secrets."

Mara had almost laughed. Almost.

Through the boathouse's cracked window she could see the dock, silvered with frost, and beyond it the Gull riding low at her moorings, her paint gone dull as an old coin. Astrid was already aboard. Of course she was. The girl had not slept — none of them had — but she had come down before dawn with her hair braided and her one good coat buttoned to the chin, and she had said only, "I'm ready," in the voice of someone reciting a lesson they intended to pass.

Mara pressed her forehead to the cold glass. Down at the harbor mouth, two figures moved in the grey: German soldiers, their greatcoats too heavy for them, their breath rising in little white flags of surrender to the cold. They were not supposed to be there. Halvard had sworn the patrol kept to the bridge road until seven.

They were lingering.

* * *

Astrid sat on a crate of salt fish with her mittened hands folded in her lap and counted her breathing the way Aunt Mara — no, Cousin Mara, though it had never once felt like cousinship — had taught her on the second night, when the search had come to the house and Astrid had lied about love letters with her heart hammering so hard she was certain the officers could hear it in her teeth.

In, four counts. Out, four counts.

She was carrying a song. That was what Mara had told her, kneeling beside her bed in the dark, holding Astrid's face in both cold hands. *Not a secret. A song. If anyone asks, you are a girl going to her grandmother in Bergen, and the song is just a song.* But Astrid was eleven, not stupid, and she understood that the notes her uncle Erik had set to a skipping rhyme about goats were also numbers, and that the numbers were worth more than the boat, maybe more than all of them, and that this was why she was going and Mara was staying.

She had cried then. She was done crying now. Crying took breath, and breath was for the counting.

Uncle Henrik — not her uncle either, but the Gull's skipper, a broad silent man who smelled of diesel and prayer — moved along the deck checking lines he had checked twice already. His eyes kept going to the harbor mouth, and each time they came back narrower.

"They're still there," he said quietly, not to Astrid, or so she pretended.

"Then we wait," said the other man, the one from the resistance whose name Astrid had never been given. "Tide holds till half past."

Astrid looked back at the town. Vesterhavn in winter was a handful of lights drowning in blue snow, church spire first to catch the coming dawn, then the red wall of the fish warehouse, then — smallest, furthest up the hill, one lamp burning in an upstairs window — home. Aunt Solveig had lit it on purpose. *So you can find us again,* she'd said, which was a lie wrapped in kindness, because the light would burn whether Astrid ever found them or not.

Astrid counted her breathing and watched the soldiers on the dock and thought: *please, please, please,* without any word attached to the prayer, just the raw wish itself, aimed like a stone at the sky.

* * *

Mara saw Halvard before the soldiers did.

He came down the dock road at twenty to six with his collar up and his gait deliberately uneven, the walk of a man with errands, and Mara's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the boathouse. Of all the mornings. Of all the men. Halvard, who asked questions with soft eyes; Halvard, whose name Solveig spoke only with her lips pressed flat; Halvard, who had stood in their kitchen three nights ago and begged without begging, and left with nothing decided.

He stopped near the soldiers. He did not look at the Gull. That was the first mercy — a man looking for trouble looks straight at it.

Mara eased open the boathouse door a hand's width. Cold poured in. Sound carried strangely over frozen water; she caught fragments.

"—fish quota inspection," Halvard was saying, in the bored tone of a man who had made this complaint a hundred times. "You're standing on my weighing shed. And the harbor master wants the north pier cleared — ice damage, a beam came loose in the night. Whole thing's unsafe. You want a beam on your head, stand there another hour."

One soldier laughed. The other said something Mara couldn't catch, but the tone was the tone of young men being managed by an older man's competence, and competence, Mara knew, was the most convincing costume in occupied Norway.

Halvard produced papers — of course he had papers; he lived on papers, on names, on the small currency of information — and the soldiers bent over them, and while their heads were down he pointed north, away from the Gull, away from everything, and his pointing hand did not tremble.

*Why?*

The question rose in Mara like water into a crack. He had found out who she was. He had come to the house. He had begged — for his son, held somewhere in a camp outside Trondheim, his life balanced against Halvard's usefulness. And now, unasked, unpaid, he was spending that usefulness on a fishing boat he had every reason to sink.

Unless this was the price. Unless selling the Gull's cargo was the payment he'd extract. Mara's hand went to the canvas at her ribs.

But no. If he meant to sell them, he would simply step aside and let the soldiers do it. Misdirection cost him something. Misdirection was a withdrawal from an account that could not be refilled.

She watched him herd the soldiers north along the pier, talking all the while about ice damage and quotas and the harbor master's temper, and when they rounded the warehouse corner and vanished, Halvard did not look back at the boat. He walked on, alone, toward his own long day, and Mara understood that she would probably never know what it had cost him, and that this — the unknowing, the unwitnessedness of it — was perhaps the whole nature of courage in an occupied country.

She turned to the skiff tied inside the boathouse. It was time.

* * *

From the deck of the Gull, Astrid saw the boathouse door open and her whole body went tight as wire.

Cousin Mara came down the steps quickly, head bare, frost in her dark hair, and Astrid's chest did something painful and complicated, because Mara was supposed to be on this boat. There had been a night — several nights — when Astrid had built arguments in her head like little houses: *I'm strong, I can row, I can memorize anything, take Aunt Solveig instead, take Mara instead.* Every house had collapsed under its own weight. Eleven-year-olds did not run blockades better than grown women. And Aunt Solveig had said, in the voice that ended discussions, *My heart will decide for me soon enough; I won't waste a berth on a decision already made.*

Still. Watching Mara walk the dock and not board felt like watching someone choose to drown slowly.

Mara reached the gangplank and stopped. She didn't come aboard. She crouched instead, so her face was level with Astrid's, and Astrid saw that her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"Sing me your song," Mara said.

"Here?"

"Here. Quietly. Once."

Astrid sang it. The goat rhyme, silly and skipping, five verses, her voice cracking only a little on the third verse where the numbers hid thickest. Around them the harbor breathed — rope creak, water slap, Henrik's boots overhead. When she finished, Mara nodded as though Astrid had passed an examination that determined the fate of nations, which, Astrid understood, she had.

"Once more on the boat whenever you're afraid," Mara said. "It works both ways. The counting."

"I know."

"And when you get to Bergen—"

"I know. Grandmother Ragna. The fish seller with the cart. I say the rhyme to her and nobody else." Astrid swallowed. "I'm not going to forget."

"No," Mara said softly. "You're not. That's rather the point of you."

Then she did the thing Astrid would carry across the sea and across the years: she took off her own scarf, deep green wool, the one she'd worn every day since arriving, and wound it around Astrid's neck over the coat collar, tucking the ends in with quick fingers, the way a mother does, the way Aunt Solveig does, and she said, "There. Now you can't get lost. Green finds green," which made no sense at all and made perfect sense, and Astrid threw her arms around her and held on and did not cry, did not cry, did not—

"All aboard!" Henrik called, and the engine coughed alive beneath them like something waking from a long sleep, and Mara stood and stepped back, and her face was doing the thing adults' faces do where the top half says one thing and the mouth insists on another.

"Go," Mara said.

The Gull pulled away from the dock.

* * *

Mara stood on the planks and did not wave. Waving was for people parting; this was not a parting, she told herself, this was a relay, the baton passing out of her hand into smaller, stronger ones. The lie sustained her for approximately nine seconds.

Then the Gull cleared the breakwater and Astrid turned at the stern — Mara saw it clearly, the small figure in the green scarf, one mitten raised, not waving either, just raised, an answer to a question neither of them had spoken — and Mara's composure went through her like a blade and out the other side, leaving her standing, upright, functional, hollowed.

*She's away. She's away, she's away.* Mara repeated it like a psalm. Behind her ribs the cipher lay flat and warm from her own body heat, and she thought: *now it matters. Now every step north matters, because the song has sailed and the paper stays.*

Henrik's voice carried faintly over the water — "keep her steady, keep her low" — and the Gull ran for the open sound, small and black against the brightening east.

And then Mara heard the boots.

Soldiers, rounding the warehouse at a jog — the patrol, back from Halvard's phantom ice damage, too early, too fast, one of them shouting something about papers, about a vessel. Mara's blood went to ice. The Gull was fifty meters out, thirty seconds from the harbor mouth, too slow, too visible, a sitting bird on glass-still water.

She could not run to the boat. Running would name her. She could only stand, a seamstress out walking at dawn, and watch, and the helplessness of it was a physical weight, pressing her down through the frost-slick planks.

The lead soldier raised a hand. Shouted. The sound cracked across the harbor.

And the Gull — Henrik's Gull, patient old Gull — answered with a belch of black smoke and a sudden, reckless burst of speed that no fishing boat should have possessed, surging for the gap in the harbor netting as the second soldier fumbled his rifle sling free. Mara's heart stopped on the beat between one moment and the next.

The rifle did not fire. Whether the soldier hesitated, or jammed, or simply could not bring himself to shoot a child's silhouette into the water — Mara never learned. The Gull slipped through the netting gap, threaded the narrows, and was gone behind the headland, and the soldiers stood cursing on the dock in the rising light, and Mara Iversen stood among them unnoticed, a woman in a plain coat with a nation's secrets sewn against her heart, watching the sea close over the wake like a page turning.

She stayed until the last shimmer faded. Then she turned uphill, toward the one lamp still burning in the window, toward Solveig, toward the frozen hills and the long road north she had chosen in the dark hours of the night.

Behind her, at the harbor mouth, the tide began to turn.

Ahead of her lay two hundred kilometers of occupied country, and somewhere on it, waiting, whatever remained of the government that had trusted her with its last words.

Mara pulled her collar up and started walking.
