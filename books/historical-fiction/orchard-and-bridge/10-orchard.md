## 10. Orchard

The bridge stood where she had left it, though it was not the same bridge, and neither, Mara supposed, was she.

Five years had passed since the morning tide that carried the *Gull* out past the breakwater with Astrid aboard and the patrol's boots ringing hollow on the dock stones behind her. Five years of frozen hills and borrowed names, of a cipher sewn into a different coat and delivered at last into hands in Stockholm that shook when they read it — not from cold, Mara had decided afterward, but from what the numbers meant. The dispatches had helped sink three convoys. They had saved, someone told her once, an estimated eleven thousand lives, though no one could say which eleven thousand, and Mara had learned that arithmetic of that kind did not balance so much as it simply accumulated, weight upon weight, until a person learned to walk bent under it or else set some of it down.

She came back in September, when the light over Vesterhavn went long and gold and lay across the water like something spilled from a generous hand.

The bus from the south let her off at the near end of the bridge, because the far end was still being rebuilt — the Germans had blown its middle span in their retreat, as if destruction were a kind of farewell they couldn't resist leaving behind. A temporary trestle carried foot traffic and bicycles now, wooden planks still pale and new-smelling, and Mara walked it slowly, one hand on the rail, letting the wind come up the fjord the way it always had, smelling of salt and diesel and, faintly, of fish.

The town looked smaller. That was the first thing. Towns do that when you carry them inside you for five years; you build them larger than they are, out of longing, and then you arrive and must forgive them for their ordinary size. But the church steeple was the same, and the harbor master's office with its green door, and the row of boathouses along the quay with their doors painted colors that had faded to suggestions of themselves. Nets hung drying on racks. Children ran shrieking along the seawall after a gull, and one of them — a girl of six or seven, dark-haired, quick — turned to look at Mara as she passed, and for one unsteady moment Mara saw Astrid at nine years old, solemn-eyed, memorizing a song.

*Row, row, the herring home,*

*nine knots north and never alone—*

Astrid had sung it for the soldiers on the dock, loud and tuneless, while Halvard pointed his thick finger down the coast road and swore he'd seen a stranger running toward Skarvik with a bundle under his arm. The patrol had believed him. Or perhaps they hadn't believed him but had chosen, in that gray dawn hour, to believe him, because believing him required nothing of them and doubting him required everything. Mara had never asked. Some mercies are better left unexamined.

Halvard himself had not survived to see the liberation. He had been shot in March of '45, two months before the end, by men from the resistance who had finally tallied the ledger of names he'd sold to the occupation office — and among them, in those last weeks, names he'd sold falsely, names he'd invented whole to buy time for people he would never meet. The tribunal after the war had weighed both columns. It hardly mattered. His son had come home from the camp in Germany weighing less than forty kilos, and had lived long enough to stand at his father's grave for exactly as long as it took to put flowers there, and then had emigrated to Canada, where, the town said, he wrote letters home every Christmas and received none.

Mara stopped at the harbor master's office. The green door was freshly painted. Inside, a young man she didn't know sat behind a desk stacked with ration ledgers, and when she gave her name he frowned, then brightened.

"Iversen — the Iversen boat? The *Gull*?"

"My aunt's boat."

"Then you'll want the papers." He rummaged beneath the ledgers and produced a folder tied with string, and inside it, brittle now and browned at the edges, the release papers for one passenger, stamped in 1940, never used. "We kept everything," he said, almost apologetically. "After. In case anyone came back to ask."

Mara untied the string. Her own name was written there in Solveig's careful hand — *Mara Iversen*, the ink gone sepia — with the date of the morning tide beside it and a blank space where a signature should have been. Solveig had prepared papers for all three of them. Of course she had. She had moved the winter stores, mended the sails, counted the berths, and filled out the forms, all without ever saying aloud what she was doing, because saying it aloud might have made it a hope, and hope was a thing Solveig had learned to handle only with gloves.

"She knew," the young man said quietly. "Your aunt. Everyone knew about the boat. Nobody said anything." He smiled, a little helplessly. "That's how we got through it, I think. All the things nobody said."

* * *

Solveig's house stood above the town on the hill road, whitewashed, shuttered, its garden run to grass and rosebay willowherb. The orchard behind it — her late husband's pride, twelve apple trees in rows — had gone properly wild, the grass waist-high, two of the trees dead and silver-limbed, the rest heavy this year with small hard apples no one had picked.

Mara had written ahead, but she had not known if anyone would answer, and so she stood at the gate for a long moment before opening it, listening to the house the way she had once listened to occupied streets: for what it held.

The front door opened before she reached it.

Astrid was nineteen now, tall, broad-shouldered like her mother had been, with their shared stubborn jaw and hair the color of wet sand. She wore a man's cardigan over a print dress and flour on her forearms, and she looked at Mara across the overgrown yard without moving, the way one looks at a figure walked out of a dream — checking, first, whether the dream will permit it.

"You're shorter than in my head," she said at last.

"You're taller."

"I'm taller than everyone." And then Astrid came down the steps fast, taking the last three in a single stride, and caught her in an embrace that smelled of bread and woodsmoke and the particular soap of the house, unchanged after five years, and Mara felt something in her chest unlock that she had not known was locked — some bolt thrown shut on a frozen hillside in February 1941, when she had watched from the tree line as the *Gull* cleared the point and became, in the haze, indistinguishable from weather.

Inside, the house was warm. Astrid had kept it — had refused the relocation offers, the city jobs, had stayed and worked the boat herself, because the *Gull*, she said, was the only conversation left between her and everyone she'd lost, and she meant to keep talking. On the kitchen table, under glass, lay the song sheet. *Nine Knots North*, written out in a child's careful hand, the cipher key threaded through its verses, preserved now like a relic — which, Mara thought, was precisely what it was.

"They teach it in the school," Astrid said, watching her look at it. "Not the key part. Just the song. Old Fru Dahl has them sing it in the spring assembly, and none of them know why the teachers cry." She poured coffee, strong and black, and pushed the cup across. "Tell me the rest. You've never told me the rest. The hills, after. What happened to the code."

So Mara told her. Not all of it — there were nights she would never give to anyone — but more of it than she had ever given. The frozen passes. The farmer in Røldal who hid her in a root cellar with his daughters and taught her to knit so her hands would have alibis. The border crossing at night, the river, the Swedish officer who read the reconstructed cipher twice and then sat down heavily, as if the numbers had physical weight, and said, *Do you understand what you've been carrying?* And Mara had said no. She had carried it for four hundred kilometers and she had never once understood it, and she understood now that this was the mercy: that the code had never been hers to comprehend, only hers to deliver.

"It wasn't mine," she finished. "None of it. Your mother gave me the boat. Halvard gave me the dawn. You gave me the song. I just — walked. Carrying what everyone else had handed me."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup.

"That's what inheritance is, then," she said finally. "Not what you keep. What gets handed forward." She said it plainly, without drama, the way she had said everything since she was nine years old — a child who had learned early that plainness was its own kind of armor. Then she stood, and took a spade down from the wall, and nodded toward the orchard. "Come on. There's something we should do before dark."

* * *

They dug the hole together in the west corner of the orchard, where Solveig's husband had always said the soil was deepest, and Mara knelt in the turned earth and set the roots of a young apple tree — a seedling Astrid had raised in a bucket on the porch for two years, waiting, she admitted, without quite knowing for what — and they packed the soil around it with their hands, and Astrid fetched water from the rain barrel, and the tree stood crooked and small against the enormous evening sky, holding its few leaves up like small flags of a country not yet recognized.

"For her," Astrid said.

"For all of them," Mara said. "For everyone who stayed and everyone who left. For the ones who got a berth and the ones who gave theirs away." She pressed her palm flat against the young trunk, feeling its thin life pulse under the bark. "Trees don't keep their fruit. Did you know that? Everything a tree makes, it makes to be taken. That's the whole design. An orchard isn't a storehouse. It's a promise that keeps renewing itself."

Beyond the orchard wall, down the hill, the temporary bridge crossed the fjord in the last light, and a boy on a bicycle rattled across it, bell ringing, heading home. Somewhere below, the *Gull* knocked gently against her mooring. Tomorrow Astrid would sail her out with the morning tide, as she did most days now — not fleeing anything, just going out to where the water deepened, because going out and coming back had become its own meaning, its own quiet argument that the world could be trusted again.

When they came inside, there was a letter on the mat — foreign stamps, forwarded twice, creased soft from the journey. Mara held it out, but Astrid shook her head.

"It's addressed to you."

Mara opened it with fingers that remembered other sealed things. The handwriting inside was round and confident, grown-up now. *Dear Mara,* it began. *I hope this finds you, and I hope you have gone home, because I am writing to ask something large. There is a girl here — sixteen, stateless, sharp as a tack — who reminds me of someone I used to be. She needs a place. I thought perhaps the house on the hill, and the boat, and the town where nobody says the important things but everybody does them anyway. I know it's much. But you taught me, or you and Mamma together taught me, that a berth is only worth having if you leave room in it for the next person.*

Mara read it twice. Outside, the wind had come around into the west, and it moved through the young tree in the darkened orchard with a sound like the sea deciding, patiently, to begin again.

"Astrid," she said, and found she was smiling, and that her eyes were doing what the teachers' eyes did at the spring assembly. "How soon can we air out a bedroom?"
