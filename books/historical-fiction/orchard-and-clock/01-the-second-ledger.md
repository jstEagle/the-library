## 1. The Second Ledger

The dead are tidy. That is the first thing they never tell you about war—the living bleed everywhere, but the dead fold their hands and wait to be counted, as though they understand that somewhere a clerk is waiting with a form.

I was the one who counted them back.

Not officially. Officially I was Sister Emrys Vane of the Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service, third from the bottom of the hierarchy at No. 4 Casualty Clearing Station, which meant I held the rank of approximately furniture. Officially, my duties were dressings, morphine doses, bedpans, and the delicate art of telling men who had lost half themselves that they would be going home soon, when "soon" was a word I'd stopped believing in around the second week.

Unofficially, I kept a ledger the Army did not know existed, and if you're reading this—if anyone ever reads this—then it outlived me, which was rather the point.

* * *

It began, as most bad habits do, with a Tuesday.

The ward that day smelled of carbolic and gangrene in roughly equal parts, which meant we were winning against neither. Forty beds, thirty-eight occupied, and the two empty ones filling faster than we could strip the sheets. Matron called it "a busy morning." The men called it nothing at all; they were mostly past speech, lying in their rows like books someone had shelved carelessly, spines cracked.

I was changing a dressing on a gunner named Hobbes—both hands gone, wrapped in white so thick he looked as though he were praying into pillows—when Private Ellery died in Bed 14.

He didn't die dramatically. They rarely do. There was no last whisper, no clutched photograph, none of the theatre the letters home would later invent. He simply stopped doing the work of staying alive, quietly, between one breath and the next, while I was three beds away up to my elbows in someone else's salvation. Sister Marsh certified him. A stretcher party took him to the mortuary tent. And by evening he had become a number on a casualty return, typed by an orderly whose handwriting suggested he found death tedious.

Except he hadn't become a number. Not quite.

Because when I went off duty at ten o'clock that night, exhausted past the point where exhaustion becomes a kind of drunkenness, I sat on my bunk in the nurses' quarters with a candle stub and a schoolgirl's notebook—fivepence, marbled cover, bought in Amiens before I understood what I would use it for—and wrote:

*Pte. Thomas Ellery, 2/7th Btn., died 14 March 1917, approx. 0940 hrs. Wounds, abdomen. Age unknown. He liked to talk about his mother's plum trees.*

I don't know where the plum trees came from. He'd said it once, days earlier, drifting on morphia: *My mum's plums come in early, Sister. First in the village.* I had carried it around for a week like a stone in my shoe, and when he died it fell out onto the page.

The official record said: *Died of wounds.* Four words. The Army's arithmetic of a human life.

Mine said he had a mother who grew early plums.

* * *

You will want to know why. Everyone does, eventually, in the particular tone people reserve for questions about other people's obsessions. The honest answer is that I am not sure I ever chose it. It chose me, the way a fever chooses you—suddenly, and then permanently.

But there is a less honest answer that is also more true, and it concerns my brother.

Later. Not now. Even now, eight years on, I ration that story like sugar in a blockade. Let us stay in 1917, where everything hurt equally and therefore nothing needed explaining.

The routine settled over me within the month, the way routines do even in hell—hell being, after all, chiefly a matter of schedule. By day I was Sister Vane, competent, unremarkable, praised once by a visiting colonel for her "steady hands," which I accepted graciously while thinking that my hands were not steady at all, merely occupied. By night, when the ward lamp was turned down to its blue whisper and the moaning had worn itself into sleep, I lit my candle behind the screen of my bunk and opened the marble notebook.

The rules I set myself were strict, because rules are how cowards pretend to be brave:

One. I recorded only what I witnessed or could verify—name, regiment, date, hour, wound. No guesses dressed as facts. The Army lied by omission; I would not lie by addition.

Two. I recorded only names the official ledger missed. And oh, they missed so many. Men who died in transit, logged nowhere. Men who arrived unconscious and left as *Unknown, British*, their identities dissolving somewhere between the ambulance and the grave registration detail. Men killed in the clearing station itself during shelling, when the returns got hurried and hurried ledgers eat names the way fire eats paper. And—this was the part I did not yet understand, the part that would later turn my notebook from a memorial into a weapon—men who died of things that should not have killed them. Wounds that should have healed. Fevers that should have broken. Hunger in a hospital, which is an obscenity so complete that at first I mistook it for clerical error.

Three. I gave each man one true thing. A sentence. His plum trees, his laugh, the song he hummed, the way he apologized for bleeding on my apron. Because a name alone is just a label on a box, and I refused to let them be boxed twice.

Four—and this rule I broke exactly once, and the breaking of it is another chapter—I told no one.

* * *

By autumn the notebook had a companion volume, and by winter a third. I kept them in the false bottom of my instrument case, beneath the scissors and the artery forceps, because no inspector in the world searches a nurse's instruments twice. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed more than the entire hospital.

I want to be clear about what those pages were, because history, if it ever gets hold of this, will want to make them either sentimental or seditious, and they were neither. They were not sentiment. Sentiment is crying at funerals. This was bookkeeping. Somebody had to keep the accounts, and God knows the Army's accountants were either incompetent or complicit, and I genuinely could not decide which possibility disgusted me more.

And they were not sedition. I loved the Army the way you love a large stupid dog that keeps biting its own leg—grief and loyalty tangled past separating. I believed in what we were doing about as much as anyone over there believed anything, which is to say: I believed in the men. The cause was above my pay grade. The men were below my hands, every night, and I knew them.

No. Those notebooks were something older than both. They were witness. The oldest profession there is—not the one the joke tells you about, but the one before it, the watcher at the edge of the field who says: *this happened, I saw it, it will not be unwound.*

Every civilization dies twice. Once when the last breath goes, and again when the last name is spoken. I was determined, in my small furious way, to postpone the second death as long as I possibly could.

* * *

There were nights, I'll admit, when the covenant felt less like a sacred trust and more like madness.

Nights when the shells came close enough to rattle the candle flame, and I lay flat on the floor of the quarters with my notebook under me like a pregnant woman guards her belly, and thought: *Emrys Vane, you are risking a court-martial to preserve the memory of a private who once complained about the tea.* Nights when the list grew by six, by nine, by twelve, and my hand cramped, and the candle burned down toward my fingers, and the arithmetic of it all pressed on my chest like a boot: *you cannot save them, you can only write them, and writing is not saving, writing is not saving, writing is—*

Writing is.

That was all. Writing is. It has to be enough, because it is what we have.

Besides, there were compensations. There is a particular pleasure—a shameful, secret, delicious pleasure—in knowing something the institution doesn't. Every night I committed my small treason of ink, and every morning I reported for duty with a face like boiled linen, and some part of me, deep under the fatigue, purred. Major Halloway himself could have marched through the ward, and I would have stood straight as a rifle barrel while my instrument case sat there holding forty-one dead men that the War Office had never heard of. It made me feel, absurdly, rich.

I mention Halloway because he was already there by then, though I did not yet know what he was. Everyone knew *of* him—Major Corven Halloway, Deputy Assistant Quartermaster-General, supply lines for our entire sector, a man whose reputation arrived in rooms several seconds before he did. Efficient. Charming. Decorated. The sort of officer who remembered your name, which in my experience is either a virtue or a hunting technique, depending on the man.

He toured our clearing station twice that spring. Both times he shook hands with the walking wounded, complimented Matron on her wards' cleanliness, and made some light remark to me about the state of my boots—"Sister, your footwear has seen more of France than most divisions"—and both times I laughed politely and forgot him before supper.

Remember that. Remember that I forgot him. When the reckoning comes, everyone always says they *knew*, they *sensed*, they saw the devil looking out of the man's eyes. I didn't. I thought he was a pleasant, competent staff officer with good teeth and a joke about boots. Evil, in my experience, rarely announces itself. It signs the correct forms, compliments your hygiene, and moves on.

* * *

The night the notebook reached its hundredth name, I did something I had never done before. I read them aloud.

Whispered, mind you—barely voiced, a breath with consonants—behind the screen, candle guttering, ward breathing its long ragged breath around me. One hundred names, and the true things attached to them. The boy who whistled music-hall songs off key. The sergeant who'd been a schoolmaster in Sheffield and corrected the grammar of his own delirium. The Canadian who asked, over and over, whether anyone had fed his horse.

When I finished, I sat in the dark and felt neither holy nor comforted. I felt like a woman standing in a very large field with a very small lantern, aware of all the field still unlit.

Then I blew out the candle, put the notebook away beneath the forceps, and slept the dreamless sleep of the resolved, which is the only luxury war grants its professionals.

* * *

March of 1918. That is where this properly begins, though beginnings are a courtesy we impose on chaos afterward.

On the evening of the fourteenth, the ambulances came in a convoy of eleven, and we worked until the lamps burned low and the orderlies' arms shook, and by midnight the ward was full and quiet in the particular way full wards are quiet—breathing, groaning softly, alive. I made my rounds. I checked the morphine charts. I noted, with the dull professional grief that had long since replaced weeping, that Bed 22 would not see morning, and that Bed 9 might, if the fever turned.

At half past midnight I took out the notebook and began the night's accounting. Two names. A lance-corporal from the mortar battery, and a gunner who had lasted nine days and talked about a girl in Cardiff the whole time.

I had just written *Cardiff* when I heard footsteps in the corridor.

Wrong footsteps. Not the shuffle of an orderly, not Matron's decisive clip. Heavy, hesitant, mud-weighted steps, the walk of a man who has been traveling a long time and is afraid of what happens when he stops. They came along the row of sleeping men, unhurried, and stopped at the foot of my screen.

I closed the notebook and slid it under the blanket and looked up.

He was tall, or had been before whatever had bent him—mud to the knees, a civilian coat gone green at the seams, a face three days past shaving and a week past eating. He looked at me the way men look at chaplains: hopefully, and apologizing for the hope.

"Sister Vane," he said. My name. Not *Sister*. Not *Nurse*. My name, spoken soft and certain, in a ward where perhaps four people knew it.

"I'm sorry to wake you."

"You haven't." My voice came out level, which surprised me. Underneath the levelness, something cold and quick had begun to swim. "Who told you my name?"

"No one living," he said, "told me anything."

And then he drew from inside his coat a letter, creased and rain-stained, and held it out to me, and even across the dimness of that blue-lit ward I recognized the hand on it before I recognized anything else about the situation—

Because I had spent eight months believing its owner was dead.

* * *

There. You see how it starts? A stranger, a letter, a name spoken in the dark. But I'm ahead of myself, and the dead hate it when the living rush.

Let me say only this, before the stranger and the letter take over the story entirely: I want you to understand what that notebook was to me on the night of the fourteenth of March. It was not yet evidence. It was not yet dangerous. It was a hundred names and a candle stub and a coward's rules, and I loved it the way you love anything you've built alone in the dark without permission.

In twenty-four hours it would be worth killing for.

I didn't know that yet. I only knew that a mud-caked man stood at the foot of my screen holding handwriting eight months buried, and that every instinct I owned—the nursing ones, the sisterly ones, the stubborn Vane ones—rose up together and said, in one voice:

*Take the letter. Take it now, and run.*
