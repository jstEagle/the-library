## 2. A Stranger at the Ward Door

There are two kinds of quiet in a field hospital, and I have learned to distrust them both.

The first is the quiet of a ward at three in the morning, when the morphine has done its work and the men breathe in ragged unison like a tide going out. That quiet is honest. It earns its keep.

The second is the quiet that falls over Ward Four at half past four on a grey Tuesday in March, when the shelling has paused and the orderlies have gone for tea and Sister Idris has stepped out to argue with the quartermaster about bandage allocations — an argument she loses weekly with the enthusiasm of a woman who considers losing her civic duty. That quiet is not honest. That quiet is the held breath between blows.

I was re-splinting Private Callahan's forearm when the second kind of quiet walked through the door.

He came in sideways, the way strangers do when they haven't decided whether they're allowed to be here. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, mud in the creases of his face as though he'd been assembled from the Somme itself and never quite cleaned up after. A civilian's coat cut for a bigger man, buttoned wrong. Eyes that did the arithmetic of every exit before they did anything else.

"Miss Vane?" he said.

Now. You will have to take my word for what happened inside me in the space of that single syllable, because I have spent eight months learning to keep my face like a locked drawer, and I am rather proud of it.

Nobody calls me Miss Vane.

The nurses call me Emrys or, less charitably, Vane-the-Vane when I refuse to laugh at their jokes. The doctors call me Nurse. The officers call me *you there*. The men in the cots call me miss or sister or, in their fevered hours, names belonging to mothers and wives I have no right to answer to. My surname exists on paperwork — pay ledgers, duty rosters, the casualty lists I am not supposed to read as closely as I do. It does not exist on the tongue of a mud-caked stranger who has no business knowing it.

"I'm afraid you've got the wrong ward," I said, tying off the splint. "And the wrong hospital. And quite possibly the wrong war."

Private Callahan, whose arm was broken in two places and whose sense of humor had survived intact, made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the morphine wearing off early.

The stranger didn't move. "You're Emrys Vane," he said, quieter now, the way a man says a thing he's rehearsed and still hates saying. "Late of the Queen Alexandra's. Transferred here in November. You write letters for the men who can't hold a pencil."

That last detail was true, and it was the true one that frightened me most, because it was specific in the way only two kinds of people are specific: people who love you, and people who have been watching you.

"And who's been writing letters about me, then?" I asked. "Because if it's my aunt in Kent, she knows perfectly well I'm alive and owes me six months of correspondence besides."

He crossed the ward then, unhurried, giving the cots the wide berth of a man who has seen enough hospitals to know the etiquette of the dying — don't loom, don't stare, don't let your shadow fall across a face that might mistake it for something else. He stopped an arm's length from me. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, something chemical and sharp I couldn't place. His hands were steady. That unsettled me more than the mud. Men who arrive looking like that and keep steady hands have either practiced very hard or have nothing left to lose, and both varieties end badly.

"I'm to give you this," he said, and produced from inside his coat a letter.

Not an envelope. A letter — folded paper, soft at the creases from handling, stained along one edge where rain had gotten ambitious. He held it out flat on his palm, the way you'd offer bread to a horse you suspected might bite.

I looked at it. I looked at him. I did not take it.

"You'll forgive my caution," I said, "but I've been offered things by strange men before, and it always turns out to be either propaganda or lice."

"It's not from me," he said.

"No," I agreed. "Clearly it's from whoever sent you, which is worse."

"Captain Wren," he said.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Captain Aldous Wren, and why the name went through me like cold water through a torn coat.

Eight months ago — the seventeenth of July, if you want the precision, and I always want the precision; precision is the only religion I've kept — I was on night duty, transcribing casualty returns because the clerk had gone down with dysentery and someone had to feed the beast of military paperwork before it starved and died all over the adjutant's desk. I sat under a lamp with carbon paper and a list of names, and somewhere past midnight my eye found a line that stopped my pen mid-stroke.

WREN, A., Capt., 4th Btn. Killed in action.

I knew the name the way you know a name you've heard spoken in a warm room. Aldous Wren had passed through our hospital the previous spring — not as a patient, or barely one; a graze along the ribs he'd tried to have treated without anyone filing a report, because reports meant evacuation and evacuation meant leaving his company short an officer. I'd stitched him myself, three sutures, while he talked the entire time in that dry, precise way of his, apologizing for the inconvenience of his own blood. He'd signed a requisition form against my desk with a hand so elegant it looked like forgery — loops and spurs and a long decisive crossbar on the *t*, ink applied with the confidence of a man who believed the written word was a contract with the future.

He'd said something I remembered longer than was sensible. I'd asked him, in the way nurses ask, whether he wasn't afraid, and he'd considered the question with genuine seriousness, the way other men consider a chess problem.

"Fear's just attention pointed the wrong direction," he'd said. "Point it at what needs doing and it becomes useful."

Then he'd walked back out toward whatever needed doing, and I'd thought: that man will die out there someday, and it will be nobody's fault but the century's.

And then, eight months later, under a bad lamp, I read that he had.

I remember thinking — this is the shameful part, the part I keep in the drawer with my locked face — I remember thinking, *at least it was quick on paper.* One line. No suffering recorded. The army's mercy being that it kills men in a single stroke of a clerk's pen, tidily, between a case of trench foot and a sergeant's pneumonia.

I copied the line into the ledger I was making anyway. Because that is what I do. What I have done since a night I'll tell you about some other time, or possibly never: I keep a second accounting. The official lists say who the army admits to losing. My notebook says who actually went. The difference between those two columns is wider than the Channel, and I have been filling it, name by name, date by date, in a hand far less elegant than Aldous Wren's, because I made a covenant with myself in the dark of a dressing station once, and I have not yet been forgiven by anyone, including myself, for the terms of it.

So when a stranger said *Captain Wren* in the quiet of Ward Four, eight months after I'd watched the man die in ink, you will understand that my body did several things at once and none of them were useful. My hands went still. My pulse set up a drumming in my ears loud enough to wake the dead, who were, after all, the majority population of my acquaintance.

"That's impossible," I said.

"Yes," he agreed. "It is. And yet."

* * *

Sister Idris returned mid-crisis, as Sisters do, trailing the smell of the quartermaster's defeat and carrying a bundle of bandages she had clearly liberated under duress. She took one look at the stranger — mud, wrong buttons, the general atmosphere of a man who had traveled too far to be turned around — and fixed him with the look she reserves for visiting chaplains and other natural disasters.

"If you're selling anything," she said, "we've no money and no use for hope. If you're clergy, the Church of England sent its representative last week and we buried him in the east row. If you're wounded, sit down before you fall down. And if you're none of those—"

"He's asking for me, Sister," I said.

Her eyebrows performed a maneuver I have never successfully replicated. "Asking for you. By name?"

"By full name," I said, "and service history."

Idris looked at the stranger the way she looks at gangrene arriving in a clean ward: not personally offended, exactly, but deeply disappointed in the universe's housekeeping. Then she looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her decision for her, because Idris reads faces the way I read casualty lists — completely, quickly, and against orders.

"Ten minutes," she said. "The supply tent. And Vane—" she lowered her voice to a register intended for the dead and nearly deaf, "—if this is trouble, I want to know before it knows I know."

It was, I reflected, watching her go, a remarkably sensible request, and one I would have been entirely unable to fulfill.

* * *

The supply tent smelled of carbolic and canvas and the particular despair of army logistics. I chose it because it had one entrance, one lamp, and no patients to overhear things that might get them transferred somewhere worse than the front. I stood with my arms folded, which is my posture for interrogations, though I confess I have never once been the interrogator.

"Tomas Bell," he said, offering a hand I did not take. He withdrew it without offense, wiping it on his coat as if to make it more presentable for next time. "I work — worked — transport. Convoys. The kind nobody thanks because nobody's supposed to notice them." He said *worked* with a small hitch, the verbal equivalent of a man stepping on ice and hearing it crack.

"And you've come forty miles of mud to deliver a letter from a dead captain to a nurse he barely knew. Forgive me, Mr. Bell, but in my experience men don't cross a war for sentiment. Sentiment doesn't pay for boots."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt exactly, guilt's cousin, the one who shows up at funerals and won't give his name. "No," he said. "It doesn't." He held out the letter again. This time I took it.

The paper was cheap army stock, the sort requisitioned by the ream and hoarded by the sheet. But the handwriting —

I have a memory for handwriting the way some people have a memory for music. It's not a skill I cultivated; it simply arrived, fully formed, like a curse. I can look at a signature once and find it again in a thousand documents, which sounds like a useless gift until you spend your days among forms and lists and learn how much of war is paperwork pretending to be weather.

Loops and spurs. The long decisive crossbar. The *W* built like a bird in flight, the final letter landing soft instead of stamped. I had watched that hand sign a requisition eleven months ago and I could draw it from memory on a dark night, and here it was, alive again on cheap paper, addressed to me by name.

*Miss Vane,* it began, *by the time you read this you will have been told I am dead, and I am sorry for it, because I wrote the report myself.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing under the lamp while Bell watched me with the patience of a man who has already waited through worse.

The letter was short. It said, in substance: I am alive, which is a longer story than either of us has hours for. It said: the men on your lists — and yes, I know about your lists, which should frighten you, and does frighten me — died of arithmetic, not artillery. It said: there is a number, a manifest number, that I need you to recognize, and when you do, you will understand why I cannot come to you and why you must decide, quickly, whether you are the sort of person who keeps accounts or settles them. It ended: *Forgive the handwriting. It's the only thing about me the army hasn't managed to kill.*

And below it, the manifest number.

Four digits and a prefix. I knew it. God help me, I knew it the way you know a hymn you grew up hating — instantly, involuntarily, in your bones before your brain. It appeared on my pages eleven times. Eleven names. Eleven dates between August and January. Eleven men entered in the official record as lost to enemy action, and entered in my book with a small notation beside each: *supplies short. rations misrouted. morphine absent at dressing station.*

Eleven times I had written that notation and told myself it was chaos, the ordinary chaos of a war that loses things the way rivers lose banks. Eleven times I had chosen the comfortable explanation, because the alternative was a shape I did not want to look at directly.

"What is this number," I said. It came out flat. Not a question. Questions invite answers you can survive.

Bell looked at the tent flap, at the lamp, at his own ruined boots. When he spoke, it was the voice of a man reciting something he'd promised himself he'd never recite aloud.

"It's a convoy manifest," he said. "One of thirty-one. Rations, dressings, morphine, coal oil — medical stores, marked for forward stations. Signed out at the railhead, signed for at the depots, every stamp correct, every signature genuine." He swallowed. "Except the wagons never arrived where the paper says they went. They went south. Across the lines of account into private hands, and from there into ports I'm not supposed to know the names of, sold at prices that would make a bishop blush. And the stations they were marked for—" He stopped. Started again. "The stations got water and willpower instead. And the men at those stations got—"

"Gangrene," I said. "Septicemia. Amputations without anaesthetic. Shock." My voice sounded like someone else's. "I've charted all of it. I've *written it down.*"

"Yes," Bell said quietly.

"How many?"

He met my eyes for the first time since the ward. Whatever he saw there made him flinch, which I noted with a distant, cold satisfaction, because flinching was the least he owed anybody.

"On the manifests I carried? Three hundred and change. On the ones I only heard about—" He shook his head. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the design of it. The whole point of good paperwork is that the crime leaves no bodies."

"Three hundred," I repeated. Three hundred men. Three hundred entries I would need to add to a book that already weighed too heavy in my coat pocket. Three hundred families receiving tidy telegrams composed by clerks who genuinely believed them.

Outside, the light was going. Somewhere eastward the guns resumed their argument, patient and enormous, and the ground under the tent floor carried their opinion up through my boots. Bell shifted his weight, watching me the way you watch a fuse you've lit.

"There's more," he said. "There's a great deal more, and none of it improves. But first I need to know — and I'll beg your pardon for the question, because I already know the shape of the answer — why does a field nurse keep a private ledger of the dead? Why you? Of everyone in this entire bleeding army, why is it you who writes down the names they leave off?"

I put my hand flat against my coat, over the pocket where the notebook lived, where it always lived, where it had slept against my ribs through raids and retreats and one memorable Christmas. I thought about lying to him. I found, examining the impulse honestly, that I didn't have the energy, and that lying to a man with guilt's cousin standing behind his eyes seemed a waste of a scarce resource.

"Because," I said, "someone erased a name once that shouldn't have been erased. And I've been keeping the count ever since."

Bell nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a wager he'd placed with himself at the railhead. Then he glanced at the tent flap, and the lamplight caught his face, and I saw that underneath the steadiness he was afraid — properly afraid, the deep-water kind — and had been the entire time.

"Good," he said. "Then you'd better hear the rest. And Miss Vane — when I tell you the name of the officer who owns those manifests, you're going to wish I'd stayed a stranger."
