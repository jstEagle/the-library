## 3. Ink That Should Be Dry

I did not sleep that night. I want to be honest about that from the start, because later accounts—Quill's dispatches, the tribunal transcript, even Wren's own sworn statement—made it sound as though I had behaved like a soldier: coolly, methodically, with a cup of tea and a steady hand. The truth is I sat on my cot in the linen store with a dead man's letter in my lap and shook like a woman holding a live shell.

The letter said eleven words.

*Emrys — if you are reading this, burn nothing. Trust Bell. — A.W.*

Eleven words in a hand I would have known blindfolded, drunk, or dead myself. The *E* with its long tail, like a man signing his own escape. The *W* crossed twice because Aldous Wren never trusted himself to make an X only once. The ink brown where it pooled at the downstrokes, the way iron-gall always does when you write fast and the pen is cheap.

Dead men do not buy cheap pens.

* * *

Let me tell you how I knew his hand so well, since the tribunal asked me to establish this formally and I have never yet managed to say it without sounding either sentimental or suspicious.

In the autumn of 1916, before the Somme finished with everyone, Captain Aldous Wren was stationed three miles from our clearing station and came through twice a week with requisitions for bandages, carbolic, morphia, splints. He was not a dramatic man. He was tall and stooped slightly, as though apologizing for his height, and he wore his cap pushed back in a way that would have earned any other officer a dressing-down. He signed everything himself—he told me once that he'd seen too many quartermasters sign things they'd never read, and he refused to be one of them.

"You count these when they arrive?" he asked me the third time he came, watching me tally a crate of field dressings against his list.

"Every one," I said. "And I count what's missing, too."

His eyebrows went up—not offended, interested. "Missing?"

"Cotton wool, mostly. Two crates short last month. The requisition says sixty pounds; we received forty-eight." I held out the ledger. "You can check my arithmetic if you like."

He checked my arithmetic. He was the only officer who ever had. And then he wrote in the margin of his own copy—*short 12 lbs, verified E.V.*—and signed beneath it, and said, "There. Now two people are lying about it instead of one."

I laughed. It startled us both. Officers were not generally funny in those months; they were generally dead or trying not to be.

After that he lingered. Not improperly—there was never anything improper in it, whatever the tribunal implied with its careful phrasing—but humanly. He sat on the edge of the supply room while I inventoried, and talked about his orchard in Kent, the apples his grandfather had grafted, a tree called the Costard that nobody grew anymore. He said a man should know the names of things he intended to keep alive. Trees, patients, regiments, it made no difference to him; a name was a name was a name.

I thought about telling him then. About the second ledger, the hidden one. I almost did, twice. But he was an officer and I was a nurse and the army has opinions about which of us is permitted to notice arithmetic, so I kept my covenant folded up inside my apron like everything else.

The last requisition he ever signed was dated the eleventh of March, 1917. Morphia, grain alcohol, sulphonamide powder. His signature at the bottom, that double-crossed W, the ink browning already.

Six weeks later his name appeared on the casualty lists. Killed in action, they said, near Arras, in an action so small it didn't have a name of its own. No body returned, no effects beyond a cigarette case and a wristwatch, both forwarded to a sister in Tunbridge Wells. I copied him into my private book at midnight, by candle-stub, and wrote the date, and sat with my hand flat on the page until the wax cooled.

I had watched him die on paper. Now the paper was sitting in my lap asking me to trust a stranger.

* * *

Bell found me again before dawn, which meant either he hadn't slept either or he had slept exactly as much as a guilty man sleeps, which is to say in shallow installments with one ear open.

"You read it," he said. Not a question. He stood just inside the linen-store doorway with his cap in his hands, turning it like a man wringing out laundry.

"I read it." I held the letter up between two fingers. "Now explain to me why a corpse writes better prose than half the living chaplaincy."

Something went out of his shoulders—the tension of expecting disbelief, perhaps. "It's his hand, then? For certain?"

"For certain certain. I spent eight months checking requisitions against deliveries. I know that W better than I know my own signature." I set the letter down on the folded sheets between us. "Which means one of three things. Either Captain Wren is alive—which is impossible—or someone has learned to forge a hand I've studied for a year, well enough to fool me, for reasons I cannot begin to imagine—or I've finally gone round the bend, and frankly, Sister Okafor has been predicting it since Christmas."

"It's him." Bell said it quietly, the way men say the worst thing they know. "He's alive. He's hiding. And Miss Vane—" he glanced at the door, then lowered his voice further, though there was no one within thirty yards but the sleeping wounded and one cat with a missing ear, "—he sent me to you specifically. Your name. He wouldn't give me anyone else."

"He shouldn't have known my name at all."

"He knew more than your name." Bell hesitated. This was the moment I understood, looking back, that the whole shape of my life turned on a hinge, and the hinge was a mud-caked courier deciding how much truth a stranger could bear before sunrise. "He knew about the notebook."

My hand moved before my mind did—toward my apron pocket, where the current ledger lay flat against my hip, warm as a bird. Bell watched the motion and nodded slowly, and I could have struck him for nodding.

"Who told you?" I said. "Who told him?"

"He didn't say who told him. He said—" Bell closed his eyes, reciting. "He said, 'Tell her the names she keeps are worth more than she knows, and that some of them died of paper, not shot.' Those were his words. Died of paper."

The linen store was cold. It was always cold; we kept the morphine there and morphine likes the cold, and so, apparently, did the truth, because I felt it settle into my bones the way the chill did, gradual and total.

"Died of paper," I repeated.

"Short deliveries. Misrouted convoys. Requisitions signed and countersigned and never filled." Bell's voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading a charge sheet. "Miss Vane, how many entries are in that book?"

"That's my business."

"How many?"

I looked at him for a long moment. He was maybe twenty-five, with a face built for honesty that life had been teaching to lie, and his hands would not stay still around that cap. Everything in me said don't—a year of instinct, a covenant kept secret through inspections and raids and Sister Okafor's needle-sharp curiosity—and then underneath the don't, quieter, the other voice, the one that had started all of it: *someone has to.*

"Ninety-one," I said. "Ninety-one names in fourteen months."

Bell flinched as if I'd shown him the book itself. "Men who died of wounds they shouldn't have survived. Men who bled out waiting for morphine that never came. Men who starved on rations sold off the convoy somewhere between the coast and the front." He swallowed. "Your ninety-one names, Miss Vane—they're evidence."

"Evidence of incompetence," I said. "The war is incompetent at scale. It's practically its defining feature."

"No." He stepped closer, and now I saw that under the mud and the fear he was furious, and the fury was older than tonight. "Not incompetence. *Arithmetic.* Somebody counted, and somebody decided, and somebody profited. Your captain tried to put numbers in a margin twelve pounds short and got told to mind his own ledger. When he wouldn't mind it—when he started writing reports up the chain—somebody arranged for him to die on paper." He spread his hands. "They killed him in ink first, Miss Vane. The shooting part was optional."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of completeness, what I did next, because the tribunal found it significant and I still find it embarrassing.

I laughed.

Not much. A single unlovely bark of a laugh, the kind that escapes when the alternative is screaming, and I clapped my hand over my mouth and stood there shaking while Bell watched me with the expression of a man wondering whether he'd pinned his hopes to a lunatic.

"Forgive me," I managed. "It's only—I spent a year being angry about twelve pounds of cotton wool. I wrote *short 12 lbs* in my own book and felt righteous about it. And the whole time—" I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes. "How many, Bell? If my ninety-one are evidence, how many are there really?"

He was quiet long enough that the cat came in, considered us both, and left again, having concluded correctly that neither of us would feed it.

"Hundreds," he said at last. "Maybe more. Nobody's counted, because counting was the crime. But the manifests I've seen—the ones that passed through my hands—" He stopped, jaw working. "I carried some of them. That's the other thing I've come to say. The false ones. I carried them for eighteen months and took my cut and told myself the war eats everyone's conscience eventually, so mine might as well be eaten profitably."

I stared at him. Outside, somewhere in the ward, a man called out in his sleep and another voice murmured to him until he quieted, the small nightly liturgy of the dying and the nearly-dying, and I thought: *this is what the money bought. This murmur. These ninety-one names.*

"And now?" I said. "What changed?"

"A boy in my village." Bell said it simply, no performance in it. "Same age as my brother. Came through a station that had run dry the week his lot arrived. I saw the manifest afterward. Full allocation, signed, delivered, complete. Paper said he had everything. He had nothing." He met my eyes. "Paper lied and the paper won, Miss Vane. I've been walking toward this errand for four months. Wren gave me the address of my own soul."

I turned away from him, ostensibly to check the morphine stores, actually because I needed a moment in which my face belonged to me alone. This is what I understood, standing in the cold with my hand on a locked box: that the personal thing I had guarded like a jewel—the little notebook, my private grief, my brother's erased name and my quiet midnight covenants—was not private at all. It had never been private. It was a thread, and threads, pulled, unravel other people's weaving. Somewhere behind the front, a man had built a machine out of signatures and shortages, and my ninety-one names were not a memorial.

They were a map.

"If I give you this book," I said, without turning, "people will come for me."

"People may already be coming for you. Why do you think Wren sent me ahead of the news?" Bell's reflection watched me from the dark glass of the storeroom window. "Halloway learns Wren lives, he'll ask who Wren trusts. There aren't many answers to that question. You're one of perhaps three."

"Halloway."

"Major Corven Halloway. Deputy Quartermaster, Fourth Army Lines of Communication." Bell's mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Charming man. You'll meet him. He visits hospitals. Shakes hands. Remembers names."

The cold went through me again, deeper. An officer who remembers nurses' names. An officer who lingers.

"The letter says trust you," I said. "It doesn't say why."

"No. It wouldn't. Wren doesn't waste ink." Bell finally stopped turning the cap. "But I'll tell you why, and you can decide for yourself. Because tomorrow night, if you're willing, I'll take you to him. To Wren. And you can look in his face and ask whether a dead man's word is good collateral."

I took the ledger out of my apron then—not to give it to him, I want that clear; I did not hand it to a stranger in a linen store at four in the morning, whatever the tribunal chose to imply. I simply held it, feeling the weight of ninety-one names through the oilcloth wrapping, and thought of Aldous Wren writing *verified* in a margin because two liars were better than one, and thought of my brother's name vanishing from a list as neatly as chalk from a wet slate.

"All right," I said. "Tomorrow night. But Bell—if this is a trap, if you deliver me to anyone wearing major's pips—"

"You'll do worse than shoot me," he said. "You'll write my name in that book, and I'll deserve it."

It was, I would learn, the most honest sentence he spoke that entire week.

He slipped out the way he'd come, into the grey beginning of the morning, and I sat down among the clean sheets with the ledger open on my knees and read the first entry over and over—*Pvt. Daniel Ashe, 2nd Devons, died 14 Jan, wound to thigh, no morphia recorded*—until the words blurred, and somewhere in the blurring I understood that the quiet part of my life was over, that the covenant had grown teeth overnight, and that the first thing the teeth would meet was a smiling officer due to tour our ward within the fortnight.

I did not yet know his face. I would remember it forever.
