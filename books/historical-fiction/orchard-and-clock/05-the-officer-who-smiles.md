## 5. The Officer Who Smiles

The morning Major Corven Halloway came to Ward Six, the light through the canvas windows was the color of weak tea, and I remember thinking—before I knew who he was—that he had the posture of a man who had never once had to duck.

That was my first mistake, of course: thinking. The second was smiling back.

Sister Ainsley had announced his inspection at breakfast with the particular flatness she reserved for weather and bad news. "Major Halloway, Quartermaster's office, touring the base hospitals. He'll want everything squared away." She said *squared away* the way a woman says *weevils in the flour*, and then she looked at me, just a half-second too long, and I understood that I had been included in the category of things needing squaring.

I had, by that point, been keeping my second ledger for four months. It lived under the loose board beneath my cot, wrapped in an oilcloth that had once protected someone's boots and now protected something considerably more flammable to certain careers. Forty-one names. Forty-one men who had died in wards like this one while the official returns recorded them as *transferred*, *recovering*, or—in the case of poor Lieutenant Sassoon-with-no-relation-*discharged to duty*. The army's arithmetic had a generous imagination. Mine did not.

So when the major walked in at half past nine, all polished boots and unbothered collar, I did what any sensible liar does: I became extremely busy with a bandage.

* * *

He made the tour the way a man walks through a garden he owns but rarely visits—pleased, proprietary, faintly bored. He shook hands with Sister Ainsley. He complimented the cleanliness of the floor, which was a compliment to the orderlies and one he delivered without looking at them. He stopped at each cot, asked each man where he'd served, nodded sagely at answers he clearly wasn't hearing, and distributed charm the way other officers distributed rations—carefully portioned so it would last the whole route.

And God help me, it worked. Even on me, for the first thirty seconds. Charm is a drug like any other; the ward felt lighter where he passed, men sitting up straighter, some of them almost glad of the interruption. Private Ollerman, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since Arras, told him about his mother's bakery in Norwich and actually laughed at the major's reply. I didn't catch what the joke was. I was watching the major's hands.

A man's face can be trained. Hands are honest. Halloway's were clasped behind his back for most of the tour, which is either military habit or a way of keeping them from touching anything—and when they came forward, they moved precisely, economically, never lingering. Except once.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

Bell's letter—the impossible letter, Wren's living hand on dead man's paper—had arrived only two days before, and I had spent those two days in the peculiar hell of performing normalcy. I changed dressings. I wrote letters home for boys whose hands couldn't manage a pen. I smiled at Sister Idris over the tea urn and discussed the scandalous price of soap. And underneath all of it, a voice kept up its quiet commentary: *he knows your name. Bell knew your name. Nobody should know your name.*

Because that was the thing that had kept me awake since the stranger at the ward door. Not the handwriting—I'd verified that against old requisitions until my eyes ached, and there was no mistaking that particular downward hook of the W. Not even the fraud itself, terrible as Bell had described it. What kept me awake was simpler and worse: Tomas Bell had walked into a crowded field hospital and asked for Nurse Emrys Vane by name, and I still did not know how he'd gotten it.

There were perhaps six people alive who knew I kept the second ledger. I could count them without taking off my shoes. Which meant either Bell had learned it from one of those six, or from Wren himself—which raised the question of how a dead man had come to know my habits—or from somewhere else entirely. From paperwork. From a list. From a man whose business was lists, and who counted things for a living.

Quartermasters count things. That is the whole of their profession.

I thought about this, uselessly, while I folded bandages into rectangles so crisp they could have stood at attention.

* * *

"Ah. The famous Nurse Vane."

He was suddenly beside my cot, and I confess he startled me—not because I hadn't seen him coming, but because I had watched him work down the row and calculated, wrongly, that he would pass me by. Nurses are furniture to most inspecting officers. Furniture with opinions about morphine dosages, but furniture nonetheless.

"Famous," I repeated, keeping my eyes on the dressing I was rolling. "I'll want that in writing, sir. My mother doesn't believe a word I send home."

He laughed. It was a good laugh—warm, unhurried, pitched exactly right for a hospital ward, soft enough not to wake the sleeping men two cots down. Everything about the man was pitched exactly right. That was the first thing that frightened me properly: not his rank, not the two service chevrons and the ribbon I didn't recognize, but the effortlessness. Men who work at being charming are merely tedious. Men who don't appear to work at it at all are dangerous.

"I'm told you've been here since the spring," he said. "That's devotion. Most of the sisters rotate out after a few months. The work wears on them."

"The work wears on everyone, sir. Some of us simply don't have anywhere better to be worn."

Another laugh, softer. "And yet you keep records, I understand. Meticulous ones. Colonel Marsh speaks very highly of your returns."

*Returns.* The word went through me like cold water through cloth. My official returns were dull as ditchwater and accurate as far as they went—that was the entire art of them. But *meticulous* was a strange word for a colonel to use, and a stranger word for a quartermaster to repeat. Quartermasters care about supplies. Bandages out, morphine out, blankets requisitioned. They do not care, as a rule, whether Nurse Vane keeps meticulous records of her patients.

Unless they care about what else she might keep records of.

"They'd be more meticulous if the supply chain would send us gauze that isn't older than the patients," I said, and heard my own voice do something reckless—it lightened, teased, matched him. Two liars, fencing politely. "Half of what arrives has gone yellow. I've taken to dating every package. The men deserve to know what year their dressings were milled."

Something shifted in his face. Nothing you'd notice unless you'd spent four months watching faces for the small tectonic movements that preceded death or dishonesty. His smile stayed exactly where it was, but the eyes above it went still, the way a pond goes still when a fish turns underneath.

"Do you date the packages," he said. It wasn't quite a question. It also wasn't quite not one.

"When I have time between saving lives, sir."

"Of course." He glanced down the ward, unhurried, a man admiring the view. "You know, I find nurses are the true historians of this war. Doctors write papers; chaplains write sermons. But a nurse writes down what actually happened." He turned back to me and the smile returned to full wattage. "Everything that happened. Isn't that right?"

And there it was. Laid on the table between us like a card turned face-up, disguised as a pleasantry, deniable in any court in the land. *Everything that happened.* Four words, and my mouth went dry enough to store grain.

"Whatever fits on the form, sir," I said. "The forms are quite small."

"The forms are indeed quite small." He held my gaze for three seconds—I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming—and then he inclined his head, wished me good morning in a voice like warm oil, and moved on down the row toward Private Ollerman, whom he charmed all over again, fresh batch, no visible effort.

My hands, when I finally let myself look at them, were steady. I made a note to be proud of that later, if later arrived.

* * *

He came back at the end of the tour. That was the part I hadn't expected, and it took me years off my life in a single afternoon.

The ward had settled. He'd shaken Sister Ainsley's hand, thanked the matron, done whatever ritual officers perform when they conclude an inspection, and I had allowed myself exactly one long breath of relief before I saw his boots stop at the foot of my cot again.

"Nurse Vane. One more thing." He produced, from inside his coat, a small leather notebook—not unlike mine, though his lived openly in a breast pocket where mine lived under a floorboard—and made a show of consulting it. "We're reviewing documentation practices across the sector. Standardizing forms. I wonder—if a man died in your ward, walk me through it. Where does his name go?"

I could feel Sister Idris at the tea table ten feet away, feeling the temperature change the way animals feel weather. I kept my voice pleasant and my pulse less so.

"To the casualty return, sir. Daily. Then the register, then the chaplain's log, then home to his people if we have an address."

"All of those?"

"All of those. We're thorough with our dead. They're the easiest patients—we always know what's wrong with them."

The joke landed badly and I knew it the moment it left me, but Halloway didn't flinch. If anything he seemed pleased, as though I'd confirmed something on a checklist.

"And the army copy goes to—" he paused delicately, "—the proper channels."

"The proper channels, sir. I stamp what I'm given and post what I'm told. Beyond that, the army's geography is a mystery to me. I'm a nurse, not a mapmaker."

"No," he agreed softly. "You're a keeper of names. There aren't many professions more important." He closed the little notebook without ever having appeared to write in it. "Thank you, Nurse Vane. This has been most illuminating."

He left. The ward exhaled. Somewhere outside, lorries ground past in the mud, and the ordinary machinery of the war resumed, indifferent.

Idris came over on the pretext of restocking my dressing tray. "What did he want?" she murmured, not looking at me, sorting gauze with fierce concentration.

"He wanted to know where names go when men die."

Her hands stopped moving. Just for a moment. Her son's name had gone wherever such names go, eleven months ago—*died of wounds received*, the telegram said, though the wounds had been simple dysentery aggravated by rations that arrived rotten and medicine that arrived never. Idris didn't know the half of it. She knew only that the arithmetic had never come out right for her boy, and mothers carry abacuses in their chests.

"And what did you tell him?" she asked.

"That names go where they're sent."

She looked at me then, directly, and I saw that she understood more than I'd ever told her, maybe more than I'd ever understood myself. "Emrys," she said quietly, "that man asked you that question the way a customs officer asks a smuggler to open her trunk."

"Yes," I said. "I know."

* * *

That evening I sat on my cot and did the mathematics of fear, which is the only branch of sums I've ever been fluent in.

Item: a stranger knows my name. Item: a dead captain's handwriting is warm in my pocket. Item: forty-one names under my floorboard, each one a small act of treason against somebody's tidy books. Item: a charming major who tours hospitals asking where the dead are written down, who lingers at my cot twice in one morning, who uses the phrase *everything that happened* with the precision of a surgeon placing a probe.

Individually, innocent. Together, a net. And nets are woven precisely so that each knot looks harmless on its own.

Here is the worst of it—the thing I set down now because setting it down is what I do instead of running: part of me liked him. That is the obscenity of men like Halloway. He was genuinely funny. He remembered Ollerman's bakery and asked a follow-up question about the man's sister, and Ollerman glowed for an hour afterward. He thanked the orderlies. He had kind eyes, or eyes arranged kindly, and when he laughed at my joke about the forms, some traitorous chamber of my heart wanted to be worthy of the laugh.

Fear wearing a polite face is still fear. But politeness wearing a frightened woman's cooperation—that is a weapon, and I had very nearly handed it over with both hands and a curtsy.

I pried up the board. The oilcloth was dry; the ledger was safe; forty-one names slept in their careful columns. I read them once through by candle stub, lips moving, the way other women said their rosaries. Whitcombe. Ferro. The boy called Duckworth who was nineteen and lied about seventeen. All present. All accounted for, if by no one else, then by me.

Then I dressed in my oldest coat, waited for the change of watch, and slipped out past the latrines into the dark, toward the coastal road, toward the barn Bell had described, toward a man who was supposed to be eight months dead and who owed me an explanation and, God willing, a signature.

Because I had understood something in that ward, standing in the wake of Major Corven Halloway's charm, and the understanding sat in me now like swallowed ice:

He suspects. Perhaps he doesn't know—but suspicion, in a man with his resources and his patience, is only knowledge running late. Whatever I carried, whatever Wren carried, whatever truth remained in this whole filthy enterprise, it had to reach the light before his suspicion finished becoming certainty.

The tide wouldn't wait. Neither, I suspected, would he.
