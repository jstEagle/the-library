## 6. Wren, Alive and Unwilling

Tomas Bell came for me at the hour when the hospital was at its most honest—half past two in the morning, when the morphine had done what it could and the dying did their work quietly, and the living lay awake listening to the building breathe. He stood at the foot of my cot with a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light and said, "You'll want your boots."

I want you to understand something about me: I am not brave. Brave women run toward gunfire. I run toward paperwork. I have spent three years of this war committing a crime so quiet that even I sometimes forget it's a crime—writing down names the army has decided not to keep—and I have done it because it is the only kind of courage I own. So when a mud-caked stranger told me Captain Aldous Wren was alive and wanted to meet me in a barn four miles down the coast road, my first instinct was not heroism.

My first instinct was to check his arithmetic.

"Four miles," I said. "At night. Past the sentry post at Mill Lane."

"There's no sentry at Mill Lane anymore. There's a corporal there who owes me eleven francs and a debt of that size buys anything." Bell shifted his weight, and the floorboard under him groaned like a wounded man trying not to. "You can say no, Sister Vane. But you looked at that letter the way a woman looks at a door she thought was painted on."

He was right, which was irritating. I had read Wren's letter nine times by lantern light behind a screen of bandage crates, and each reading made the world less sensible. *Tell her I am sorry for the inconvenience of being dead,* it began, which was such an Aldous Wren sentence that I nearly wept over its grammar. Eight months ago I had watched him die on paper—a neat typed line, *Wren, A., Capt., killed in action*—and I had written his name in my second ledger with the particular care I reserved for men whose deaths were administered rather than suffered. And now he was signing letters with a hand I'd have known blindfolded, because I had spent one entire winter filing his requisitions and noticing, absurdly, that he crossed his sevens like a Frenchman.

"I'll come," I said. "But if this is a trap, I want you to know I bite."

"Noted," said Tomas Bell, without a flicker of humor, and that absence of flicker frightened me more than anything else he'd done.

* * *

The coast road at night is a lesson in humility. The war has taught everyone within fifty miles of the front to move quietly, but it has not taught anyone to see, and so Bell and I went forward in the manner of two people feeling their way through a house where all the furniture has been rearranged by God out of spite. My boots were army-issue and too large—I had never been issued anything that fit; the quartermaster's office seemed to believe nurses were simply short soldiers—and they filled with cold at every puddle. Somewhere behind us a gun spoke once, twice, and stopped, the way distant guns do, politely, as if clearing its throat before a speech.

"Tell me about him," I said, when the silence got heavy enough to carry.

Bell walked a while before answering. "I only met him twice. First time, he caught an error in a manifest I was carrying—caught it in about four seconds, mind you, while I stood there sweating through my shirt. Second time, he asked me if I knew what happened to a consignment of morphine that left Rouen in March and arrived nowhere at all." He paused. "I lied to him. He knew I lied. He let me walk away anyway, and I've been trying to work out why ever since."

"And now?"

"Now I think he let me go because he wanted one man left standing who could tell the story from inside. Or because he was a decent man in an indecent year. Both, probably. Decent men are always making strategic errors like that."

We came off the road at a gap in a hedgerow that Bell found by touch, and crossed a field where the stubble grabbed at my ankles like small accusing hands, and there was the barn—low, black against a sky the color of wet slate, smelling of straw and old timber and, beneath it, the sea.

The door was already open a hand's width. Inside, one candle.

"You go first," Bell said. "This part isn't mine."

* * *

I have imagined a hundred times since how I expected Aldous Wren to look. Alive, yes—but alive in the way of men who come back from leave: same face, new creases, a story ready. What I found instead was a man who had been dismantled and reassembled by someone in a hurry, using the wrong instructions.

He was sitting on an upturned crate with his elbows on his knees, and when the candlelight reached him I saw that the captain's bearing was gone—not relaxed, *removed*, like a coat taken off and burned. His hair had gone grey at the temples in eight months, which struck me as geometrically unfair. There was a scar along his jaw I didn't recognize, healing badly, and his hands—those precise hands that crossed their sevens like a Frenchman—were shaking slightly around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from.

"Sister Vane," he said. His voice was hoarse, as though it had been packed away in a crate with the rest of him. "Thank you for coming. I'm sorry about the hour. I'm sorry about the barn. I'm sorry about—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at all of it. "The inconvenience of being dead."

"Is that apology going somewhere, Captain, or are you just warming up?"

Something moved across his face that might once have been a smile. "You're angrier than your file suggested."

"My *file*?"

"Bell showed me your letter. The one you wrote to the records office in November, asking after my effects." He set down the cup. "You wrote them four times. They stonewalled you four times. Most people stop after two."

"Most people don't watch a man die on paper and then get handed his handwriting eight months later like a conjuring trick." I sat down on a hay bale opposite him, because my legs had opinions of their own. "Where were you, Captain? Where have you *been*? There were witnesses at Bois L'Hermite. There was a report."

"There was a report," he agreed softly. "Written by Major Corven Halloway. Which should tell you everything, and tells you nothing until I explain it properly, and I find I don't know how to explain it properly anymore. Words have gotten away from me. I used to be quite good with words." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old plaster. "That's what I was, you know. The good-with-words one. Halloway counted figures, I wrote the letters home. Between us we could make anything sound true."

"And then?"

"And then I found a manifest that wasn't true, that I myself had helped make sound true, and I couldn't sleep, and I started counting things Halloway preferred uncounted, and I discovered that a captain who counts is worth more dead than alive—to someone." He looked up. "So I died. It was remarkably easy. One shell crater, one set of identification, one report filed by the man who wanted me dead. The army is very efficient about death, Sister. It's the one supply line that never gets misrouted."

The candle guttered. Outside, the sea worked at the shore with the patience of a clerk.

"The ledger names them," he said. "Your little book. Bell told me—the ones who died of the misrouted supplies. Rations cut, morphine diverted, dressings sold north. Two hundred and some."

"Two hundred and thirty-one," I said. "As of Tuesday."

He flinched. Actually flinched, like a man touched with a live wire. "You count them."

"Somebody has to."

"*Somebody has to.*" He repeated it as though tasting something spoiled. "Do you know what I did when I realized the scale of it? I ran. That's my confession, Sister Vane, delivered in a barn by candlelight: I ran. Not cleverly, not strategically—I ran like an animal, and I lived in ditches, and I told myself I was staying alive to testify, and month after month I stayed alive and testified to nobody. Because testifying means walking back into the machine that chewed me once, and I know exactly what its teeth feel like." He held up his shaking hands, almost formally, an exhibit in evidence against himself. "There he is. Captain Aldous Wren, the army's official corpse. Ready to vanish properly this time. I sent for you to give you the letter, the names I remember, the numbers—it's enough, it has to be enough—so that whatever happens next doesn't depend on me."

"And what happens next depends on whom, exactly? Bell? He's one guilty man with a good memory. Me? I'm a nurse with a notebook and no rank. You're the only witness Halloway fears, and you're asking me to help you be *less* feared."

"Yes," he said simply. "Yes, I am."

I want to record, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because later—in the tribunal, in the newspapers, in the version of events that got printed and prettified—everyone decided I had been magnificent, and I would hate to die with that lie standing. What I felt was fury. White, exhausted, entirely personal fury at this man for being alive and wasted, for carrying the truth around like luggage he kept setting down, for daring to be *broken* when I had spent months breaking myself quietly on his behalf, writing his name into a ledger alongside brothers and sons and boys who called me Sister as though I were family. I had mourned him. Do you understand? I had given him a paragraph of grief, in ink, by candlelight, and grief is not refundable.

"You watched them die," I said. My voice came out low, which surprised me; I'd aimed for a shout. "Or if you didn't watch, you counted. Two hundred and thirty-one men who bled or starved or shook themselves to pieces because the medicine that would have saved them was in a warehouse with Halloway's signature on the outbound slip. And you have their deaths in your head, and you want to *post them to me* and disappear."

"I want the truth to survive me."

"The truth isn't a parcel, Captain. It doesn't travel well. It needs hands." I leaned forward. "You think your name is a liability. Your name is the only currency in this whole rotten country that Halloway cannot counterfeit. Every number in my ledger is deniable—clerical error, fog of war, misfiled. But put *Captain Aldous Wren, sworn, in person* against those numbers, and suddenly the fog lifts. Suddenly it's fraud, and murder, and a major in handcuffs. You aren't afraid of dying. You died eight months ago and found it dull. You're afraid of mattering again, because mattering is what got you killed the first time."

The silence after that was long. The candle burned with a small industrious sound, like someone sewing.

"My brother," I said, before I could stop myself. "Daniel. Killed at Loos in '15. Except the records office says there's no Daniel Vane in the battalion rolls—clerical error, they call it, as though he were a mislaid glove. Somewhere between the trench and the typewriter, my brother stopped existing on paper. My mother writes to them every Christmas. They send back a form." I heard my own voice doing something dangerous, going flat and steady the way it goes when I am about to cry and refuse. "So I keep a second ledger. Every name the army drops, I pick up. It's not courage. It's compulsion. It's the only monument I know how to build. And you—you are sitting on the biggest stone of the lot, and you're asking permission to leave it uncarved."

"Sister—"

"Don't answer tonight. Answer by morning tide, when Bell comes back with the route." I stood, because sitting near him any longer felt like drowning politely. "But hear this much. Loyalty told you to run—loyalty to yourself, to the men who served under you, to whoever it is you're protecting by staying dead. Loyalty is a fine instinct and it lies beautifully. Truth is uglier. Truth makes you walk into harbor offices and sign your name in front of men who want it erased. I've carried both for years, Captain, and I will tell you which one lets me sleep."

He looked at his hands a long moment. When he spoke, it was barely above the sound of the sea.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my father kept bees. And once a hive went wrong—went what he called *quiet*, which is worse than angry. And he said the only cure was to put the hive out in full sun, open the whole thing up to daylight, even though half of it would die of the exposure. Better an honest half, he said, than a whole that rots in the dark." He looked up at me, and for one second, underneath the ruin, I saw the officer who had caught a false manifest in four seconds flat. "I've been rotting in the dark for eight months, Sister Vane."

"Then morning tide," I said, "is your full sun."

* * *

Bell was waiting outside with the lantern shuttered, and he fell into step beside me without questions, which was its own kind of eloquence. We walked back through the stubble field under a sky that had begun, very faintly, to consider dawn.

"He won't do it," Bell said at last. "I've asked him eleven times. I've begged, I've shamed him, I've quoted scripture at him, badly. He'll hand us the papers and vanish."

"Eleven times," I said. "By whom were you sent the twelfth?"

He didn't answer immediately. Ahead of us the road ran pale toward the hospital, toward the ward, toward whatever Halloway's polite grey eyes had seen when they rested on my cot—and I felt, for the first time, the shape of the days ahead pressing close: checkpoints, carts, a mail ship riding at anchor like a promise.

"No one sent me," Bell said finally. "I sent myself. That's the trouble with buying back your soul, Sister—the price keeps changing." He glanced sideways at me. "And there's something else you ought to know before the tide turns. Someone else knows about the barn. I don't know who, and I don't know how much. But this afternoon a dispatch rider passed me on the coast road going the other way, and he was riding like a man carrying bad news at a gallop."

Behind us, faint in the dark, the barn door closed—or the wind closed it; I chose to believe the wind.
