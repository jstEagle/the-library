## 7. Bell's Price

There is a particular kind of silence that falls between two people when one of them has just admitted to something unforgivable. I have heard it in confessionals, though I was never the one kneeling. I have heard it across card tables when a man turns over his hand and finds he's been played all night by a friend. It is the sound of arithmetic being done—of a person weighing exactly what you are worth now that they know what you've cost.

Nurse Emrys Vane was doing that arithmetic about me, and I could not blame her a farthing for the sum she arrived at.

We were sitting in the back room of the canteen at the clearing station, which is to say we were sitting among crates of condensed milk and a smell of wet wool that no amount of carbolic had ever conquered. She had asked me—no, demanded of me, in the voice of a woman who has spent three years asking politely and is finished with it—why a stranger should be trusted with her ledger and a dead man's letter. And I had told her the truth, because lying to her had begun to feel like throwing stones into a well you might someday need to drink from.

"I carried the manifests," I said again, because the first time hadn't seemed to land. "Halloway's manifests. For eleven months."

"You said that."

"And you're waiting for me to explain what that means, so you can decide whether to spit on me or shake my hand."

"I wasn't going to shake your hand," she said.

Fair enough. I poured tea from the canteen urn—it was the color of ditchwater and tasted like an apology—and gave myself a moment to arrange the story into some order that might make me look less monstrous. There isn't one, as it happens. There are only orders of monstrosity, and I had spent eleven months standing fairly high on the ladder.

* * *

You should understand what a manifest carrier was, because the army never put the job in any manual. Officially I was a transport clerk, third class, attached to the supply lines running from the coastal depot up to the forward stations. Unofficially I was the man who made paper agree with itself. When four hundred tins of beef left the depot and two hundred arrived, someone had to make the numbers kiss. When a crate marked *surgical cotton* arrived containing boot heels, someone had to write *surgical cotton* on the receipt anyway, in a hand resembling the depot officer's, with the right smudge in the right corner.

That someone was me.

I was good at it. That's the part I can't sand off, no matter how I tell the story. I wasn't coerced—I mean, yes, there was a pistol somewhere behind it all, the way there always is, but Halloway never once had to show it to me. He showed me a promotion instead. He showed me dry socks and a billet with a roof and extra pay routed through channels so obscure that even the auditors' auditors couldn't have traced it without a week and a candle. He showed me a war where I didn't have to go over the top, and all he asked in return was that I keep the ink honest while the men went hungry.

"Do you know what misrouted morphine looks like?" Emrys asked me. Her voice was flat, which was worse than angry. "From the receiving end?"

"No," I said. "I know what it looks like from the paperwork end. It looks like nothing. That's rather the genius of it."

"It looks like a man biting down on a leather strap at three in the morning because the morphine went to Marseille instead of here. It looks like a surgeon choosing which of two screaming men gets the last ampoule. It looks like—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "You knew."

I had known in the way clerks know things: abstractly, numerically, at arm's length. Four hundred doses short this quarter. Six hundred the next. Numbers on a page, and pages don't scream. I told her that. I told her that the whole filthy architecture of it depended on men like me keeping everything at arm's length, and that Halloway understood that better than any general understood artillery.

"He never once asked me to kill anybody," I said. "He just asked me to make sure nobody could prove who had. There's a difference. It took me a year and a half to learn how small the difference is."

"And Wren?"

"Wren was the fly in the ointment." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "Captain Aldous Wren, quartermaster-adjutant, conscientious to the point of being a public nuisance. He counted things. Actually counted them—the way you're not supposed to, the way the whole system depends on nobody doing. He kept noticing that the depot figures and the delivery figures disagreed, and unlike me, he wrote his disagreements down and signed them. Three reports, formal, through channels. By the third one, Halloway had a choice: ruin Wren or remove him."

"Remove him how?"

"The elegant way." I smiled, and hated myself for smiling; it's a habit from those years, smiling at the wrong moments. "Wren was due to inspect a forward ammunition dump near the canal. The dump took a shell the night before he was scheduled to arrive—or rather, the report says it did. Wren was listed killed in the secondary detonation. Body unrecoverable, which is a very convenient phrase, if you've ever wondered. No body, no grave, no questions from a grieving family that might notice a discrepancy. Just a name moved onto the casualty list, and a clerk—me—entering the inventory adjustment that followed."

Emrys had gone very still. It was the stillness of a woman watching a wound she'd dressed begin to bleed again.

"Which adjustment?" she said quietly.

"Ammunition destroyed in action. Two hundred cases." I met her eyes. "Except the dump wasn't hit. The dump was emptied. Sold through a broker in Rouen, and the loss written off against a German shell that never fell. And Captain Aldous Wren, who would have noticed, became a name on a list."

"His list," she said. "My list. I copied him into my book eight months ago. *Wren, A., Capt., killed in action.*" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I gave him a line in a ledger because the army wouldn't give him a prayer."

"Well," I said, "he'd have been embarrassed by the prayer and flattered by the ledger. He was that sort."

* * *

She asked the question then, the one I'd been dreading, and she asked it without cruelty, which somehow made it harder.

"Why did you stop? What changed?"

And here is where I ought to say something noble—that I saw the light, that my conscience woke like a trumpet. But you've come this far with me, and a man buying back his soul shouldn't inflate the currency.

What changed was a boy called Pincher.

He wasn't a soldier, not properly—he was fourteen and had lied his way into a labor battalion driving mules up the communication trenches. I knew him because he used to hang about the depot office stealing biscuits and telling me lies about his exploits, and I liked him, in the way you like a stray dog that follows you. In November he came down with a gut wound—not fighting, just unlucky, a stray fragment—and they got him back to a station hospital that had run out of morphine two days earlier. Misrouted, the telegram said. Lost in transit.

I later learned that the same consignment, reborn under new papers, had sold in Marseille for eleven thousand francs.

Pincher died asking whether anyone had sent for his mother, and I sat in my warm billet that night with my honest ink and my dry socks, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do for eighteen months. Eleven thousand francs. One boy. I could multiply. We all could, in those days; it was the only sums anyone did quickly.

So I started keeping copies. Little ones, hidden ones—carbon slips tucked inside the covers of my Bible, which amused me, God forgive me. Manifests, dates, routing codes, the names of the brokers' agents. Enough to hang a man, if hanging men were still fashionable. And then Halloway's people found out—I never learned how; suspicion moves faster than evidence in an army—and I ran. Ran ahead of a court-martial, ran with my Bible full of dead boys' prices, and eventually ran to ground in a fish cellar on the coast where a certain captain with no official existence had been hiding for eight months.

"We found each other," I said to Emrys. "Two ghosts in a herring cellar. He wanted to stay a ghost. I told him about you."

"You what?" The teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"I told him there was a nurse keeping names. His name among them. I told him a woman had watched him die on paper and refused to let it stand, and that if he could find the spine to testify, her ledger and my carbons together were enough to bury Halloway so deep they'd need miners to reach him." I set down my cup. "He said no. He said it several times, in several registers. But he wrote the letter, didn't he? Ghosts are sentimental. It's their one weakness."

* * *

Emrys stood then and walked to the window, which looked out on the ward yard, where orderlies were carrying a stretcher through the rain with the unhurried gait of men for whom stretchers had lost their drama. She watched them a long time.

"If I give you the ledger," she said finally, "and it's taken—if you're searched, if you break, if you're simply unlucky—every name in it dies twice. Once in fact and once in hope. Do you understand that? Those aren't your lines in my book. They're mine. I made a promise over each one."

"I understand better than you think. I broke a promise over each one too. Mine was just written in a different hand."

"That's not the same."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Yours was a covenant. Mine was a crime. I'm not asking you to confuse them. I'm asking you to decide whether a guilty man can carry water without drinking it."

She turned around. In the grey light from the window her face had that quality I'd seen in chaplains and surgeons both—a kind of terrible fairness, the willingness to weigh a man honestly even when honesty hurt.

"How do I know you won't trade the ledger for yourself, if they catch you? Amnesty for naming the courier. Men have sold more for less."

"They have. I nearly did, once—there's a version of this errand where I walk into Halloway's office with everything I know and buy my life back at retail." I shrugged. "The reason I didn't is not edifying. It's that I already tried living with it, the other way, and it turns out I'm bad at it. Some men can carry a thing like that their whole lives. I discovered I'm not built for it. Every number I falsified had a face behind it, and after Pincher, the faces stopped queuing politely and started shoving."

"That's remorse, not trustworthiness."

"They travel together more often than you'd think," I said. "Remorse is just trustworthiness that arrived late. You can either accept the late arrival or send it away, but don't pretend it isn't at the door."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was gone before it committed itself, like a man testing ice.

* * *

We talked terms, because she was that kind of woman—she'd negotiate with Death himself, and probably win a concession. The ledger would stay on her body, not mine; she'd seen what happened to couriers, and she trusted her own apron pockets more than my coat. I would carry only Wren's letter and the route plan. If we were separated, she'd continue alone. If I were taken, she was to assume I'd talk—assume the worst, spend no grief on me—and get the documents through regardless.

"You assume I'll betray you," I noted.

"I assume you're human," she said. "It's not an insult. It's the only safe assumption in wartime. Sister Idris taught me that—trust people absolutely, but never trust their circumstances."

"And do you? Trust me?"

She considered me for a long moment, and I want to be accurate here, because accuracy is the one penance I can still perform. She did not trust me. I watched the decision move through her face like weather. But she decided something else instead: that she needed me, and that need could stand where trust couldn't, propping up the same arch.

"No," she said. "But I believe you hate him more than you love yourself, and that's rarer than trust and worth nearly as much. We go together. Forgiveness comes later, if it comes at all."

"Withheld forgiveness," I said. "I've worked under worse management."

"Don't be glib."

"It's how I pray," I said, and this time she did smile—one second, no more, and immediately disowned—but it was there, and I counted it, the way I used to count cases of ammunition. Small honest inventories.

* * *

We were interrupted by the thing I'd been dreading since I walked through her ward door with mud to my knees: footsteps in the corridor outside, measured and unhurried, and a voice giving cheerful instructions to somebody about the placement of a screen. I knew the voice. You spend eleven months listening to a man dictate lies and you develop an ear for him the way a wife develops an ear for a husband's tread.

Major Corven Halloway was touring the ward.

I saw Emrys hear it too. Every drop of color left her face and then, with visible discipline, marched back in again. She straightened her apron. She became, in the space of four seconds, exactly what she appeared to be: a tired nurse in a back room, fetching stores.

"He can't know I'm here," I whispered.

"Then don't be," she whispered back, and nodded at the loading door behind the milk crates, the one that opened on the yard and the rain and the long muddy walk to anywhere else.

I gathered my coat. At the door I paused, because there was one thing left unsaid, the thing that mattered more than the manifests and the money and even Pincher, and I said it badly, because important things always come out of my mouth bent.

"When I carried those papers," I said, "men died whose names I never learned. You learned them. Whatever else happens—whatever you decide I am—you did that. Somebody kept the count. Don't let him take it from you. Not the book, and not the knowing. He can burn paper. He can't burn you."

Through the wall, the cheerful voice was drawing nearer, saying something about how fine the ward looked, how devoted the staff were, how much he admired nurses—Halloway laying down charm the way sappers lay pontoons, something to cross on later.

Emrys looked at me once, level and unsoftened.

"Go," she said. "And Bell—"

"Yes?"

"When this is over, whatever verdict I reach about you—I'll write it down. I keep records."

It was, I decided, trudging out into the rain toward the fish cellars and a ghost who didn't yet know he'd agreed to testify, the fairest sentence anyone had passed on me in years. Somewhere behind me, the major's voice reached the doorway I'd just vacated, warm as brandy, asking Nurse Vane whether she was quite alone.

She would answer him. She'd answer him beautifully.

I only hoped that between us, we'd both live to read the record.
