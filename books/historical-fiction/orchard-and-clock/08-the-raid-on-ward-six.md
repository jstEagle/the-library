## 8. The Raid on Ward Six

The trouble with hiding a crime is that you must go on living around it. You must change its dressings, so to speak. You must smile at it across the ward and ask after its appetite.

For three days after the night at the coastal barn, I carried the ledger against my body the way other nurses carried rosaries—tucked into the band of my uniform, beneath the apron, over the left ribs where a man might take a bayonet. I had sewn a pocket there myself, badly, with thread meant for sutures. Sister Prydderch would have had opinions about my stitchwork. Sister Pryderch had been sent to Rouen in March, which was one fewer opinion in the world.

The ledger itself was not impressive to look at. A quarter-bound notebook, water-stained, its cover soft as an old glove. Inside: one hundred and forty-one names that did not appear on any official list. One hundred and forty-one men who had died of things they should not have died of—gangrene for want of carbolic, wounds gone septic for want of morphine, simple hunger wearing the costume of fever. The army called their deaths "misadventure" or "disease" or nothing at all. Major Corven Halloway's quartermaster clerks called them balanced accounts.

I called them by name, every night, in the linen store, like a woman saying her prayers backwards.

* * *

It was Tuesday when the lorries came.

I knew something was wrong before I knew what, because the orderlies stopped joking. In a field hospital you learn to read the weather of a room the way farmers read sky, and the weather that morning was the particular flat grey of men who have been told something and forbidden to say it. Orderlies know everything. They are the telegraph system of any hospital, faster than signals sections, more reliable than chaplains.

At half past nine, Matron's bell rang twice—the signal for officers on the ward—and then rang a third time, which no one could remember ever hearing.

"Visitors," said Sister Idris at my elbow, folding a blanket with the violence of a woman who has never once in her life folded anything carelessly. She was fifty if she was a day, built like a chapel door, with forearms that could turn a struggling patient as gently as a page. Her son had gone west in '16. She did not speak of him. She spoke of him constantly, in the way she straightened every bed in Ward Six to an exactness that had nothing to do with beds.

"What kind of visitors?" I asked.

"The kind that don't wipe their boots."

They came through the double doors at ten o'clock precisely: six military policemen under a captain whose name I later learned was Fenn, and behind them, unhurried as a man touring his own gardens, Major Corven Halloway.

I had seen him twice before, both times at inspections, both times performing charm for the benefit of the matrons. He was handsome in the way that portraits are handsome—a good jaw, a good moustache, eyes that gave the impression of warmth without ever actually generating any. He wore his major's pips the way some men wear religion: correctly, and for effect.

"Ladies," he said, and the ward went quiet enough to hear the rain starting on the canvas roof. "I regret the intrusion. There has been a regrettable irregularity in this sector—certain documents have gone astray. Stolen, we believe. Papers relating to supply matters." He smiled at us all, including the dying. "Nothing that need concern your work. We'll be brief."

Stolen documents. Supply matters.

My heart performed a manoeuvre I can only describe as a forced march.

* * *

Here is what I did not do: I did not look at my apron. I am proud of this. Every instinct in my body screamed *look down, adjust, confirm*, and instead I looked directly at Major Halloway and said, "Will you be wanting tea, sir? The men are due their feeds at half past."

He turned those portrait-eyes on me, and for one long moment I understood exactly how a rabbit feels about stoats—not afraid, precisely, but *appraised*. Then he smiled wider. "Nurse Vane, isn't it? I'm told you keep excellent records."

"I keep whatever records Matron tells me to keep, sir."

"A model answer." Something moved behind his face, quick as a fish under ice. "We'll manage without tea."

They began at the far end of the ward, at Private Callaghan's bed, and worked toward us with the methodical patience of men being paid by the hour. They searched footlockers first. Then kit bags. Then, with gloves on now, the personal effects of the dead.

And that—that was the moment I understood where the ledger had to go, and hated myself for understanding it.

Ward Six held four men who would not see Friday. At the near end, in the corner bed by the stove pipe, lay Corporal Enoch Maddox, who had come in eleven days ago with a shattered thigh and an infection that laughed at antiseptics. He was twenty-three. He had a photograph of a girl named Winnie tucked into his paybook, and he had made me promise—jokingly, the way young men make promises they mean absolutely—to write her and tell her he'd fought the good fight and complained about the food throughout.

His belongings sat in a canvas holdall under his cot: spare shirt, letters, a harmonica, a tin of fudge gone hard as mortar.

The MPs were two beds away and moving closer.

* * *

You will want to know how a nurse hides a notebook inside a dead-dying man's effects while six soldiers search the room, and the honest answer is: badly, quickly, and with God's assistance, in roughly equal parts.

I did it during a dressing change. This is the great gift of my profession—no one looks twice at a nurse bent over a wound. Blood is the best curtain ever invented. I knelt at Maddox's side, lifted the dressing on his thigh, made the small sounds of professional sympathy, and with my free hand—my left, the sutured pocket gaping open beneath my apron like a little mouth—I drew out the ledger and let it drop into the gap between the cot frame and the canvas holdall beneath.

Then I pushed the holdall six inches with my knee, opened it with two fingers, and slid the notebook down between a spare shirt and a bundle of unopened letters, into the warm dark where a dead man's life was kept.

Ten seconds. Perhaps eight. My hands did not shake until afterward, which I have always considered a mark of good breeding.

Maddox's eyes found mine. He was too far gone to speak much anymore, but his eyes were still working, sharp brown and amused, and they watched me tuck the holdall back under his cot with the expression of a man watching a conjuror and declining, politely, to spoil the trick.

"You're a terrible nurse," he whispered. "That dressing was fine."

"Hold still," I said, and pressed his hand, and he pressed back, and that was the whole of our conspiracy. Some oaths are sworn in churches. Ours was sworn over a tin of fudge.

* * *

Captain Fenn reached Maddox's bed at noon.

By then I had retreated to the dispensary counter, where I was grinding something that did not need grinding, and Sister Idris stood at the foot of the bed with her arms crossed, occupying space the way a mountain occupies a valley.

"Open it," said Fenn, nodding at the holdall.

"It belongs to a dying man," said Idris.

"That's not what I asked, Sister."

"And I'm not answering what you asked. I'm telling you what it is." She did not raise her voice. Idris never raised her voice; she simply became more solid. "Corporal Maddox has perhaps two days. His mother is in Carmarthen. When he goes—and he will go, Captain, I've buried forty of these boys since spring—his things go home to her. Every button. Every letter. If you put your government hands in that bag and something of his goes missing, I will personally make it my life's work to see that Matron, the chaplain, and the Red Cross hear about it, and I am a very tedious correspondent."

Fenn looked at her. I watched him weigh it—the regulation against the sister, the search warrant against the chapel door of a woman who had lost a son to this war and had nothing left to lose except everyone else's sons.

"Step aside, Sister," he said, but his voice had lost its footing.

She stepped aside. Of course she did; she wasn't a fool. But as the MP knelt and began lifting out the shirt, the letters, the harmonica, she talked. That was the thing about Idris—she covered the search the way a pianist covers a wrong note, with volume and confidence and relentless forward motion.

"Maddox, Enoch, corporal, eleventh of September admission, thigh wound, gas gangrene, listed grave—*gravely* ill, Captain, do write that word correctly—personal effects inventoried by me on the twelfth, one shirt, one harmonica, one photograph of a young lady, correspondence sealed, next of kin Mrs. Bronwen Maddox, Carmarthen, and if you crease that photograph I shall want your name and number for the record—"

The ledger sat between the shirt folds, and the MP's gloved hand passed within a finger's width of it, and Idris's voice rolled over the moment like a tide over a rock, and he drew out the shirt alone, shook it, dropped it, and moved on to the letters.

He read three of Maddox's letters. Winnie's letters. The boy was too weak to protest, but his eyes followed, and I saw Idris see it too, and something in her jaw set like concrete setting.

"Finished?" she said, when the MP finally repacked the bag.

"For now." Fenn closed the holdall and stood. Behind him, at the far end of the ward, Major Halloway was watching me again. Not searching. Watching. He touched two fingers to his cap brim—an ironic little salute, courteous as a knife between the ribs—and turned back to his tour.

* * *

They were gone by one o'clock. They took nothing from Ward Six. From the records office they took a crate of files, and from the laundry they took a laundress's statement, and from the kitchen, inexplicably, a sack of onions, because military police are military police the world over.

At two o'clock, in the linen store, with the door barred and the smell of soap and carbolic thick enough to chew, I retrieved the ledger from under Maddox's cot and held it against my sternum and shook at last, properly, like a wet dog.

Idris locked the door behind her and leaned against it.

"You put it in his bag," she said. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"Nurse, I've changed that boy's dressings for eleven days. I know what's under his cot the way you know what's under your pillow." She came forward and took the ledger from me—actually took it, turned it over in her strong hands, read the cover as though it were a parish register. "One hundred and forty-one names," she said quietly. "Bell told me."

"Bell told—you've spoken to Tomas Bell?"

"He came to the laundry gate yesterday, looking like a scarecrow that had lost an argument with a crow. He told me what you're carrying and what it costs." She set the ledger down on the folding table with a click, precise as a surgical instrument. "My son Thomas—" her voice did something complicated and recovered— "went missing at Passchendaele in October. The army lists him as killed. There is no grave. There is no date, even, only a month. For eighteen months I have known exactly one fact about my son's death, and it is that the paperwork was wrong."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere outside, an ambulance engine coughed and caught.

"So when you ask yourself whether you can trust me, Emrys Vane," Idris said, "don't ask. I'm not doing this for you. I'm doing it because somewhere in that book there may be a boy who died of a wound he should have lived through, and his mother deserves a date. Even a wrong one. Even a small one. Dates are all we get."

I had prepared arguments. I had prepared, in the sleepless hours, a whole parliament of reasons why she must not be involved, why the risk was mine alone, why a woman of her age and grief owed this war nothing further. She dismantled the entire parliament with one sentence and a locked door.

"They'll come back," I said. "Halloway knows the ledger exists. He looked at me today like a man checking his watch."

"Then it doesn't stay here, and it doesn't stay on you—they'll strip-search you next time, and we both know it." She was already thinking aloud, already planning, her hands smoothing the table as though ironing the future flat. "It has to move. Out of the hospital, tonight, before they seal the roads. Bell said there's a way south—to the coast, to the mail boat. He said you'd know."

"I know."

"Then tonight. And Emrys—" she paused at the door, and for a moment the chapel-door solidity cracked, and I saw the mother underneath, plain as a scar. "If they stop me, if they question me, I'll lie. I want you to understand that. I have spent fifty years not lying to anyone, and I have decided I'm finished with it. Whatever they ask me about you, I'll lie like a Sunday sermon."

She unlocked the door and was gone, back to her wards, back to her dying boys, leaving me alone in the linen store with a stolen book and the extraordinary, terrifying discovery that loyalty does not always wait to be asked. Sometimes it simply arrives, arms crossed, daring you to refuse it.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and down the road toward the rail depot, I could just see the lorries turning—three of them now, where this morning there had been none—and I understood that the countdown had started whether we liked it or not.

Tonight, the coast. Tomorrow, the tide.

And somewhere between here and the water, Major Halloway was setting his watch by the same clock.
