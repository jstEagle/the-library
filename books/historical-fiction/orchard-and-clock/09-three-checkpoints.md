## 9. Three Checkpoints

I have died twice now. Once on paper in the autumn of 1916, when some clerk with a steady hand and no imagination entered my name into a ledger of the fallen, and once more slowly, over eight months of barns and cellars and the particular silence of a man trying not to exist. You would think the practice would make me calm about danger. It does not. It has only taught me that dying is administrative, and it is the paperwork that kills you.

So when Emrys Vane spread her notebook on an upturned crate in Bell's coastal barn and said, in the tone of a matron announcing visiting hours, "We need to talk about logistics," I confess I laughed. It was not a brave laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has been hiding in hay for half a year and has just been handed a battle plan by a nurse.

"Something amusing, Captain?" she asked.

"You're drawing me a map," I said. "In pencil. Of three military checkpoints."

"I'm drawing *us* a map," she corrected, and there it was — the *us*, dropped into the sentence as casually as a coin into a collection plate, so that refusing it would look like greed. She had learned that trick somewhere. I suspected she had invented it. "Unless you'd prefer to stay here and be found in a week by men who don't knock."

The barn smelled of oilcloth and old apples. Rain worked at the roof in patient handfuls. Tomas Bell sat on a feed sack near the door with his cap in his hands, turning it like a man praying without the courage to kneel, and Sister Idris — who had arrived an hour behind us smelling of carbolic and defiance — stood with her arms folded before the map as though it were an altar she disapproved of but would defend anyway.

And I looked at the four of them, this absurd congregation, and understood that my long apprenticeship in cowardice had just been terminated without notice.

"All right," I said. "Show me how we die."

* * *

Emrys tapped the first mark on her pencilled coast. "Rail depot. Dusk tomorrow. There's an inspection at the platform — they've had one every evening since Tuesday, which means someone above us is nervous, which means Halloway, because nothing makes that man nervous except paper moving toward water." She drew a line eastward. "From the depot we take the coast road to the causeway gate. That's the second. They check papers there against the garrison lists, and the gate sergeant has been promised a commission if he finds anything interesting, so he will be thorough the way only ambitious men are thorough."

"And the third?" Bell asked, though his voice suggested he already knew and hoped the saying of it might unmake it.

"The harbor quay." Emrys's pencil came to rest on the final mark, and for a moment she didn't lift it, as if the dot itself might hear us breathing. "The mail ship sails on the morning tide. Six-forty. After that, whatever we carry goes where Halloway can't burn it."

"Assuming it gets aboard," said Sister Idris.

"Assuming," Emrys agreed, "it gets aboard."

I studied the map the way I used to study trench diagrams, back when I still believed maps described the world honestly. Three checkpoints. Twelve miles. One tide that would wait for no one, least of all for a dead man walking.

"It's not the checkpoints that worry me," I said, and they turned to me with the particular attention people give a dead man offering tactical advice. "Checkpoints are theatre. Soldiers, questions, stamps — all of it designed to make you feel caught before you're caught. What worries me is that Halloway doesn't need the checkpoints. He built them. He knows their rhythms better than the men who work them. He isn't going to search our bags." I touched the edge of the map. "He's going to watch our faces. He'll have men at each gate doing nothing but looking. And I—" I stopped, because the next part required honesty, and honesty was a muscle I'd let atrophy. "I have a face he knows. Eight months ago I sat across a desk from him and told him his supply figures were lies to his teeth. He smiled at me then. He'll smile at me now."

"Then you won't be at the gates," Emrys said simply.

"That's rather the problem, Nurse. If I'm not at the gates, I'm not testifying. And if I'm not testifying, this entire expedition is three civilians smuggling stationery."

Silence took the barn. The rain filled it.

It was Bell who broke it, in the voice of a man reading out his own sentence. "There's another way through. Not past the gates — *through* them, but wearing their own colours." He looked up, and I saw what it cost him. "Stretcher-bearers move through checkpoints without inspection. Standing order. Nobody searches a man carrying a wounded soldier; it's bad luck, and worse manners. We could—"

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard. A stretcher-bearer requires a stretcher. A stretcher requires a wounded man. And a wounded man at a checkpoint requires the kind of performance that gets real wounded men turned away while actors take their places." I stood, because sitting made the argument feel negotiable. "I've spent eight months being nobody. I'm very good at it. But I am not going to dress myself in the war's pity to slip past its police."

Sister Idris snorted — an aristocratic sound, entirely at odds with her apron. "Captain Wren, I have buried one son and forty-one boys who were not mine, and I will tell you what I told the chaplain: sentiment is what people call pride when it costs other people something." She nodded at Emrys's ledger, wrapped now in oilcloth like a body. "That book is two hundred and six men who died of arithmetic. If dressing you in canvas gets it onto that ship, you'll wear canvas and thank us for it."

I opened my mouth to explain, with dignity, why I would do no such thing.

What emerged instead was: "Two hundred and six?"

"Two hundred and eleven," Emrys said quietly. "Three more since Tuesday."

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low, and I will say this for plans made by nurses and penitents: they lack the elegance of staff-college schemes, but they possess a certain terrible durability, the way field dressings are uglier than hospital bandages and hold better under fire.

Bell would take the rail depot with Emrys. His face was unknown to the depot garrison; hers, apparently, was known to everyone and suspected by no one, which is the finest camouflage in any army. The letter — my letter, the sworn account I had written three times and burned twice before letting it stand — would travel sewn into the spine of a hymnal, because Bell argued, with a straight face, that no sergeant in the British Army has ever voluntarily opened a hymnal, fearing it might obligate him to sing.

Sister Idris would carry the ledger through the first gate in the morning, in a laundry cart, among sheets that had earned their stains honestly. She refused to discuss it beyond stating the arrangement as fact, the way one states the weather.

And the causeway gate — the second, the ambitious sergeant, the faces — that was mine. Stretcher-bearer's coat, borrowed from a convalescent orderly who owed Bell a debt and asked no questions, which in my experience is the most expensive currency in France. I would walk a stretcher through at dusk with a "wounded" man under the canvas — Bell's suggestion again, a genuine case of fever from the aid post, genuinely sedated, so that the lie would contain at least one true thing — and past the sergeant whose promotion depended on finding nothing, because finding nothing meant the war was working, and ambitious men believe in the war.

"The third gate," Emrys said, and here her voice changed, went flat and careful, the way voices go over ground that's known to be mined. "The quay is different. Halloway will be there himself or he'll have someone there who reports to him alone. Papers won't matter by then — anyone boarding a mail ship gets searched properly. Which is why nothing we carry needs to reach the ship."

I frowned. "Then what does?"

"*Someone*." She met my eyes, and I saw that she had been carrying this piece of the plan alone, the way one carries a wound below the waterline. "The ledger can be copied, Captain. Names, dates, manifest numbers. I know them. All of them." She said it without drama, and somehow that made it worse. "If the worst happens at that quay, the evidence isn't in the book. It's in me. I recite, your statement stands beside me, and a witness writes it down — there's an American correspondent on the passenger list, Quill, Bell confirmed it — and then it's published, printed, past burning. Halloway can seize paper. He cannot seize a woman's voice in front of thirty witnesses and a pressman."

"And if he decides he can seize the woman instead?" I asked.

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about Emrys Vane that unsettled me more than any danger: she never flinched, she *recounted*, as though fear were simply another entry requiring accurate transcription. "Then he'll have to do it in front of the same witnesses," she said. "Which is exactly what I'm counting on him not daring to do."

* * *

They slept — Bell curled by the door like a dog expecting to be kicked, Idris upright in a chair with the serenity of stone — and I stood at the barn door and watched the dark coast and did the arithmetic that dead men do.

Eight months ago I had signed requisitions with clean ink and believed that signatures were enough. When my figures showed rations arriving that never reached the front lines, morphine entering the ledgers and leaving them before it ever dulled a single wound, I wrote my report and delivered it to Major Corven Halloway with the confidence of a man handing a lit match to a fire brigade. He thanked me. He smiled — he had a beautiful smile, Halloway, the sort that made you feel flattered to be deceived by it — and within a fortnight I was listed among the dead at a sector I had never visited, and the report was ash, and I learned that in a well-run army the truth is not suppressed. It is *processed*.

Since then I had perfected disappearance. I could tell you the exact weight of silence a barn requires. I knew which conscienceless hours of night were safe for movement and which ditches drain and which hold water like a grudge. I had become, I thought, a specialist in survival.

But standing in that doorway with the tide twelve hours off and a nurse asleep on straw who had memorized two hundred and eleven names because no one else would, I understood that everything I called survival had merely been a slower form of the same desertion Halloway had accused me of, that night, with his lovely smile. I hadn't been silenced. I had *stayed* silent. There was a difference, and the difference had a body count.

Morning came grey and salt-smelling. Emrys woke without transition, as soldiers do, instantly and completely, and began checking straps and seams and hymnal spines with the unhurried competence of a woman packing a wound. At the door she paused beside me.

"You'll be at the quay," she said. It was not a question, and it was not quite an instruction either. It was something rarer — a prophecy issued by permission.

"I haven't decided," I said.

"Yes, you have," she said. "You decided the moment you read your own death notice. Everything since has just been you catching up to it." She settled her satchel on her hip. "The tide doesn't wait, Captain. Neither do I."

And she walked out into the grey light toward the rail road, and Bell fell in behind her, and Sister Idris took up her cart handle like a lance, and I stood alone in the doorway of the barn with a stretcher-bearer's coat over my arm and Halloway's old smile burned into my memory like a brand.

Down the coast road, faint on the wind, I heard the first train of the day come into the depot and stop — stop longer than trains stop — and I thought: *there. That's the first tick.*

Twelve miles. Three gates. One tide.

Somewhere ahead of us, a man who smiled beautifully was setting his watch.
