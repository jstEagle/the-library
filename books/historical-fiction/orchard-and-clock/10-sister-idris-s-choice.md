## 10. Sister Idris's Choice

They say grief makes a woman quiet. Whoever they are, they never met me.

My name is Bronwen Idris, and for eleven years I have been a nurse in His Majesty's hospitals, which is to say I have spent a decade learning that the army's arithmetic has no column for sorrow. The army counts men the way a grocer counts tins — so many in, so many out, and if a tin arrives dented or empty, well, the paperwork says full, so who are you to argue?

I argued. Once. In 1915, when the morphine ran out during the push at Loos and we did what had to be done with brandy and prayer and our own two hands. I wrote a letter about it. A very polite letter, because I am Welsh and politeness is our weapon of choice. The reply thanked me for my vigilance and reassured me that supply lines were functioning within acceptable parameters.

Acceptable parameters. My son Rhys died within those parameters too, though I didn't know it yet then. He was at the far end of one of those beautifully functioning supply lines, a stretcher-bearer, twenty-two years old, with his father's chin and my temper, and when the shrapnel took his leg the field station had bandages but no morphia, and he held on for nine hours while a young doctor did arithmetic of his own — deciding which men could be saved with what little there was. Rhys wasn't chosen. That's not a complaint against the doctor. It's a complaint against the ledgers that said the morphia was there when it wasn't, the crates that said FULL when they were straw and sawdust, the signatures that moved bottles from one column to another column and men from living to dead without ever touching either.

So when Nurse Vane came to me three days ago — pale as candle wax, clutching a notebook wrapped in a spare shift like it was an infant — and told me what was written inside it, I did not need convincing. I needed only a moment alone with my hands, which had begun shaking in a way I found undignified, and then I said yes.

She hadn't even finished asking.

* * *

"You can't carry it yourself," she'd said. We were in the linen room, between the shelves of folded sheets, in the particular hush that linen rooms have — the closest thing a field hospital offers to a confessional. "Halloway's people will be watching every face they've seen near Ward Six. They've seen mine. They've seen Bell's, God knows how. But you—" She stopped, and I watched her arrive at the thought the way one watches someone step into cold water. "You're nobody to them."

"Nobody," I repeated, and smiled, because at forty-nine years of age I have made peace with being nobody. Nobody gets through gates. Nobody gets searched twice. Nobody carries a nation's worth of dead men in a laundry cart and is waved along with a bored nod.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I didn't mean—"

"You meant exactly right, and don't apologize for strategy. My mother always said a compliment is just the truth wearing its good coat." I took the notebook from her. It was lighter than I expected. So much weight in so little paper — that's the trick of it, isn't it? The dead weigh nothing at all once they're buried, and everything once they're written down.

"There's a cart," I said. "Thursday mornings the washing goes down to the depot cisterns — the army likes its bandages boiled and its officers' shirts pressed, even at the edge of hell. There's an inspection at the hospital gate now, since the raid. Every cart opened, every basket poked. But they poke the top layer, dear, and the top layer is going to be filthy."

Emrys looked at me. "Filthy?"

"Sick wards make sick linen. Blood, gangrene, worse. No sergeant alive wants to put his hands past the first layer of that, and the ones who do want to prove something will wish they hadn't." I tucked the notebook into my apron pocket, where it sat over my heart like a small hard bird. "The army's great weakness has never been lack of vigilance. It's squeamishness."

* * *

Thursday morning arrived grey and smelling of coal smoke, as all our mornings do. I rose at four, as I always do, and dressed, and looked at myself in the cracked mirror — a broad woman in a grey uniform, hair pinned so tight it ached, face like a Sunday roast left out overnight. Somewhere behind that face was a boy called Rhys who used to steal apples from Mr. Pritchard's orchard and swear he'd share them and never once did.

"Not today either," I told the mirror. "Today you keep them all."

The cart was waiting in the yard, heaped with the morning's wash. I had spent Wednesday evening building it like a mason builds a wall. Bottom layer: sheets, innocent ones. Then the notebook, wrapped in oilcloth, flat as a communion wafer, laid in a shallow tray I'd fashioned from a broken instrument case. Then more linen over it — and here is where craft matters — linen of graduated horror. Clean-ish sheets near the top, then the stained, then the truly unforgivable: dressings from the gas ward that I had been instructed to burn but had saved instead, because I am a great believer in waste not, want not, especially want not regarding a major's sensibilities.

I pushed the cart out into the yard at half past five. The gate was maybe two hundred yards off. Between here and there stood Sergeant Doyle, whom I knew slightly — a Dublin man, decent enough in the way of men who follow orders and call it conscience — and beside him, new since Tuesday, a captain I did not know, with a clipboard and the eyes of a man counting sheep who suspects some of the sheep are wolves.

My heart behaved itself. I was almost disappointed in it.

* * *

"Sister Idris." Doyle touched his cap. "Laundry run?"

"The army does not fight on an unboiled shirt, Sergeant. You know this."

He grinned. The captain did not. He stepped forward, clipboard first, the way some men lead with a handshake. "Captain Merrick. Quartermaster's audit detail. All carts are subject to inspection, Sister."

"Of course they are, Captain. Inspect away." I stepped back and folded my hands, the picture of cooperative invisibility.

He went through the top layer with a stick, poking. The gas dressings turned over and exhaled their particular perfume, and I watched a man's discipline wage war with his stomach. Discipline won, barely. He prodded deeper. Second layer. Third. His stick struck the tray beneath and made a sound — a small wooden knock, nothing, a cart settling — but I felt the sound go through me like a rifle shot.

"That's the bottom boards," I said pleasantly. "Warped since the winter. Everything under there is wet linen, Captain, unless you fancy wringing out sheets infected with gas gangrene. I'd recommend gloves. And a strong constitution. And possibly a change of career."

Something in my voice must have carried conviction, or perhaps it carried the particular authority of a woman who has seen more dying than any three captains combined, because he hesitated. And then — God bless the class system — he glanced at his gloves, fine leather, officer's gloves, and visibly declined to ruin them.

"Proceed," he said, and stepped back, and made a mark on his clipboard.

Doyle waved me through. I pushed the cart between them, nodding to each, serene as a church procession, and did not let my knees inform my face of their opinion until I was fifty yards clear and around the corner of the motor pool shed.

There, in the shadow, I stopped and breathed for a full minute, hand pressed flat over the cart's rim, feeling the oilcloth package beneath its shroud of linen. Still there. Still whole.

"Well done, Rhys," I whispered, meaning myself, meaning him, meaning whatever it is that passes strength down the generations of a family like water through chalk.

* * *

The handoff was to be at the cisterns by the depot, where the washing was unloaded. Emrys would be there — she'd arranged it with a subtlety I admired, trading shifts, appearing where nurses appear, unremarkable. Bell would take the notebook onward. From there it was trains and causeways and a ship, none of which were my affair. My affair ended at the cistern wall.

It should have ended there.

But the world had other ideas, and its ideas had a name, and the name was Halloway.

I saw him before he saw me — or rather, I saw the effect of him: orderlies straightening, a matron's smile assembling itself like troops on parade. Major Corven Halloway walked the depot yard the way a landlord walks his fields, and I understood at once why Emrys spoke of him the way one speaks of weather that kills. He was handsome in the way of portraits, silver-templed, charming, and he shook hands with the depot sergeant as though granting it an honor.

And then his gaze crossed the laundry carts, and slowed, and came back.

I kept folding. There is a way a woman folds linen when she has nothing to hide and a way when she has everything, and I had practiced the former for three days until my hands forgot the latter.

"You're the sister from the base hospital," he said, arriving at my elbow. Not a question. "Idris, isn't it? I believe we met at the dedication service in the spring. You read the lesson."

"We did meet, Major." I had no memory of it whatsoever, but I had learned long ago that powerful men remember faces the way creditors remember debts, and the safest course is agreement. "Though I fear my reading was better received than deserved."

"On the contrary. 'Greater love hath no man.'" He smiled. It reached his eyes, which is what makes such men dangerous — they've learned to route charm through the eyes, where most men only manage the mouth. "You lost a son, I understand. Stretcher company?"

The yard went quiet around me, or I went quiet inside it; it amounts to the same thing. "Rhys. Yes."

"A tragedy. The war eats the best of us first." He said it with real warmth, and that was the worst part — I believed he felt something, some thin sincere regret, the way a man regrets a fence post that broke in a storm. Useful things, sons. Regrettable losses. "If there's ever anything the quartermaster's office can do for you, Sister — anything at all — you have only to ask."

There was a sentence underneath that sentence. I heard it plainly. It said: *we know who grieves, and grieving women can be bought with kindness, or at least quieted with it.* And beneath that, fainter, another: *and we are checking, this morning, who moves what, and whose carts go where.*

"Thank you, Major," I said. "The army gives me everything I need. Bandages, mostly. When they arrive."

His smile held a half-second longer than comfort allows. "When they arrive. Quite." He moved on down the line of carts, and I resumed folding, and only then did I discover what my hands had done — the sheet I'd been holding had been twisted into a rope without my permission. I smoothed it. I folded it properly. I put it in the pile.

* * *

The unload went as planned. Emrys was there, arms full of clean dressings for return transport, and in the shuffle of baskets — one moment of overlapping bodies, a dropped bundle, laughter, apology — the oilcloth package passed from my cart to her apron in less time than a sergeant needs to sneeze. I never saw her hands do it. That's how it should be done; that's how I taught it to her, in the linen room, in the hour we had: *the transfer happens inside the ordinary, dear. Miracles draw attention.*

Then it was done, and she was walking toward the depot steps, and the notebook — Rhys's vindication, and ninety-one other mothers' vindications, because I had counted the names, of course I had counted them, I count everything now, it's what grief does to a bookkeeper's daughter — was leaving my keeping forever.

I should have felt lighter. I felt emptied, like a shell casing.

Which is why I was still standing at the cistern wall, not quite ready to walk back up the hill to a hospital where I would spend the rest of the day boiling other people's horrors out of other people's linen, when Captain Merrick returned with two military policemen and a piece of paper.

"Sister Idris." He did not touch his cap this time. "You're relieved of duty, pending inquiry. You'll come with us."

"Inquiry into what?" I asked, and I confess my voice was not entirely steady, because the arithmetic was doing itself in my head at terrible speed: *he marked his clipboard at the gate. He marked it twice.*

"Discrepancies in laundry requisitions," Merrick said, in the tone of a man reciting words he knows are a costume. "Several items unaccounted for over the past month. Missing dressing packs. You'll be questioned at the provost's office."

Missing dressing packs. Dressing packs I had hoarded, yes — hoarded honestly, hoarded against the day the supply columns lied again, because they lie again, they always lie again, and I'd rather answer to a tribunal than watch a boy die on a table with nothing to stopper his wounds. But that wasn't the charge, and we both knew it. The charge was proximity. The charge was that Halloway had looked at a laundry cart and seen the shape of a thing moving through his beautiful fraudulent world, and he intended to pull threads until the weaving came apart, starting with the oldest thread in reach.

A grieving mother. Who would miss her?

I set down the sheet I was holding. I squared the corner of the fold, because standards matter even at the end of the world, and I looked at Merrick — a boy really, thirty at most, with a wife's photograph somewhere in his pocket and no idea what he was carrying.

"Lead on, Captain," I said. "Though I warn you, I've been questioned by better men than anyone in your office, and I buried the last of them in 1916."

* * *

They took me past the depot steps as we crossed the yard. I did not look toward them. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of this sort: Bronwen Idris did not look. Looking is a confession with the eyes, and my eyes had work left to do — they had to stay dull, tired, offended, a woman inconvenienced, nothing more.

But hearing costs nothing, and so I heard it, as the MPs marched me toward the provost's office: the depot bell ringing the quarter hour, the mail train whistling in from the junction, and above it all, faint and unmistakable, the voice of Nurse Vane calling some ordinary instruction to some ordinary orderly, steady as a metronome.

She had the notebook. The boy-captain at my elbow had nothing but a clipboard. And somewhere across that yard, Major Halloway was smiling at someone else, believing his net was closing on the wrong fish.

Let him believe it. Let him waste his morning on a woman who folds linen and buries sons.

They sat me in a bare room with a table and a lamp and gave me tea I didn't drink, and a lieutenant asked me about dressing packs for two hours, and I answered him with the truth, the whole boring truth, itemized — because the truth is a marvelous weapon if you have enough of it, and I have been accumulating truth for eleven years.

Near the end, as the lamp burned low, the lieutenant leaned back and asked, almost kindly, almost idly: "Sister — do you know a Nurse Vane?"

And I looked at him with eleven years of dead men behind my eyes, and I said, "Vane. Vane." As though tasting the name. "Should I?"
