## 11. The Rail Depot at Dusk

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives on schedule. It is worse than the kind that ambushes you. The ambushed sort at least has the decency to be over quickly, like a shell burst. But a fear with a timetable—a train departing at 6:40, an inspection beginning at 6:15—you carry it around like a stone in your apron pocket, and every minute it grows heavier by exactly one minute's worth.

The rail depot at Harrow's Cross was built by men who believed the war would end in a fortnight and that trains were a temporary embarrassment. It had a corrugated roof that sang when it rained and dripped when it didn't, and a clock above the ticket office that had been stuck at quarter past five since before I arrived in France, which meant that for once I was grateful to it. Somewhere in the world, apparently, it was eternally quarter past five, and nobody had died yet.

Bell stood beside me under the dripping eaves, wearing the uniform of a corporal in the Army Service Corps, which fit him the way a borrowed coat fits—correctly enough to pass at ten paces, wrongly enough to betray him at two. He had acquired it from somewhere he declined to describe, and I had decided not to ask. There is a whole taxonomy of questions one learns not to ask in wartime, and *where did you get that tunic* sits comfortably near the top, just beneath *what happened to the man who wore it*.

"The hymnal," Bell said, for the fourth time since the causeway road.

"In my left pocket."

"Which hymn is it open to?"

"Bell. If they open it, the number of the hymn will not save us."

"It might. Men are lazy. They check the middle pages where the good ones live. 'Abide With Me,' 'Rock of Ages.' Nobody inspects page 311."

"And what holy comfort lives on page 311?"

He glanced at me sidelong. "A letter signed by a dead captain."

Right. Yes. That.

I had spent the afternoon folding Captain Aldous Wren's letter into a rectangle small enough to shame a love note, sliding it between the pages of a pocket hymnal that Sister Idris had pressed into my hands at the gate with a look I am still deciding how to interpret. She knew what we carried. She had not asked. Some women give you their bread; Idris gave you her silences, which were worth more and cost her more.

The depot was filling with the evening shuffle of men going up the line and men coming down it, and the two streams looked identical from a distance—the same mud, the same eyes—but up close you could tell them apart by how they held their heads. The ones going up still looked forward. The ones coming back had learned to look slightly past you, at some point six feet behind your shoulder where safety theoretically lived.

A military policeman worked the queue with a list and a pencil, checking papers without reading them, reading faces without seeing them. Routine. That was the word everyone kept using. *Routine inspection.* As though routine were a comfort rather than the thing that kills you, because it is precisely during routine that men stop looking.

And then I saw the man who was looking.

* * *

He stood apart from the MP's queue, near the lamp standard by the parcels office, in a major's greatcoat cut well enough that even the mud respected it. Tall, fair, with the kind of face that would have been called handsome in peacetime and was now merely called *careful*. He held no list. He held no pencil. He held a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, and he watched the queue the way a fisherman watches water—not the surface, but the places where the surface moves wrong.

"That's him," I said, barely moving my lips. "Halloway's adjutant. Lieutenant—"

"Cairncross," Bell said. "I know. He was at the manifests office. He counted better than Halloway did." A pause. "Don't look at him again."

"I wasn't looking at him."

"You were looking at him the way people look at him right before he looks back."

We joined the queue. There is no art to joining a queue; there is only the terrible democracy of it, standing shoulder to shoulder with men who wanted nothing but a seat and a stove, while somewhere behind your ribs a piece of paper addressed to a dead man rehearsed its own discovery. I ran through my story again. Nurse, Vane, No. 14 Stationary, travel authority stamped twice, returning to duty via coastal hospital, personal effects: one satchel, medical; one hymnal, spiritual. All true except the parts that mattered.

The line moved. A boy ahead of us—eighteen if he was seventeen—had his pack opened and shaken out with a rough tenderness, the way you handle a man's things when you've already decided he's harmless. Cigarettes. Photographs. A tin whistle. The MP grunted, waved him through, and the boy gathered his life off the table with shaking hands and went on toward the platform, whistling tunelessly, God help him, as though the war were a thing that happened to other people.

Then our turn.

"Names."

"Vane," I said. "Nurse, Queen Alexandra's. And Corporal Bell, ASC."

The MP took our papers. He read mine properly—I watched his lips move over the stamps—and something in me unclenched half a notch, because a man who reads your papers carefully is a man who has already decided you're probably what you say you are. Carelessness is reserved for the guilty-looking.

"Corporal." He looked at Bell's tunic. Looked at Bell's face. Looked at the tunic again, doing arithmetic. Bell smiled the smile of a man who has been cold and wet and underfed for so long that smiling is simply the last free thing available to him.

"Requisitioned this morning, Sergeant. Mine's drying after the causeway crossing. You want the wet one instead? Fair warning, it'll walk off on its own."

The MP almost laughed. Almost. He jerked his chin toward the table. "Both packs."

This was the part we had not fully planned for, because planning for it meant admitting it could happen, and neither of us had possessed the courage for that particular honesty. I set my satchel on the boards and opened it as instructed: bandages, scissors, morphine tablets in their brown glass bottle, a spare apron folded to regulation thickness. The MP went through it with the bored efficiency of a man who had done four hundred of these and found nothing in three hundred and ninety-nine of them.

Bell's pack produced socks, rations, a razor, and a copy of *Pearson's* with the cover torn off, and the MP read three lines of an advertisement for boot polish with more attention than he'd given my travel authority.

"Hymnal," Cairncross said.

Not loudly. Not even particularly to anyone. Just a word set down on the table between us, the way a chess player sets down a piece he has been holding for several moves.

I turned. He had come closer without seeming to cross any distance at all, the way some men do, and he was looking not at our bags—he hadn't glanced at them—but at Bell. At the tunic. At Bell's hands, specifically, which were large and scarred and entirely wrong for a corporal who supposedly spent his war pushing paperwork at a supply depot.

"You'll want to check the book too, Sergeant," Cairncross said pleasantly. "Paper travels. It's remarkable, paper. It gets everywhere. Like water finding a crack."

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as dropping through a floor that then dropped through another floor.

But here is the thing about terror disguised as routine: it must be answered with routine, or the disguise fails for both parties. So I said, with the mild irritation of every nurse who has ever had a man second-guess her kit, "It's a hymnal, sir. Page 311 if you're fond of 'Just As I Am.' Though I'd warn you the print's dreadful."

Cairncross's eyes came to me for the first time. Grey, unhurried, the eyes of a man taking inventory.

"Religious, Sister?"

"Nurse. And no. It was my mother's. I keep it for the same reason soldiers keep letters—they make the mud feel temporary." I heard my own voice do something I didn't authorize: it softened, just slightly, on *temporary*, and I saw the word land. Because it was true, and truth is the only material strong enough to build a bluff out of. Lies sag. Truth holds weight it was never asked to hold.

The MP had the hymnal in his hand by then. I watched his thumb move along the page edges the way thumbs do when they riffle books, and the letter sat inside it thin as a wafer, folded eight times, and I thought with strange clarity: *this is the moment. This flat, stupid moment, under a dripping roof, next to a tea urn.* Not dramatic. Not cinematic. Just a thumb, and a crack in the world.

"Page 311," the MP said, finding it. His brow furrowed at the print. He turned a page. Another.

Somewhere behind us the stuck clock insisted it was quarter past five forever.

"Give it here," Cairncross said.

And then the whistle blew.

Not an alarm—a train, the 6:40, arriving in a shriek of steam and brake-iron, and the whole depot shifted toward the sound the way grass shifts toward wind. Porters surged. Soldiers surged. The MP, conditioned by months of timetables overriding everything else including sense, glanced toward the platform, and in that glance—half a second, no more—Cairncross's attention broke too, pulled toward the carriage doors where officers were stepping down into the lamplight.

I took the hymnal from the MP's hand.

I did not run. Running is a confession. I closed it, tucked it into my apron pocket, and said, "If we're finished, Sergeant, I'd like to catch my connection before it catches someone else's," and the MP, whose hand had already moved on to the next man's pack in his mind, waved us through with a jerk of his chin.

We walked. We did not hurry. Hurrying is also a confession. We walked the length of the platform at the pace of two tired people with nowhere interesting to be, past the tea urn, past the parcels office, past Cairncross—who did not stop us, who only watched, and whose watching I felt on my neck like weather changing.

At the far end of the platform, Bell exhaled through his teeth. "Page 311," he murmured. "Told you nobody checks 311."

"Nobody checked 311. He checked *you*."

"He checked the tunic. Tunics can be explained." Bell found our compartment, held the door. "Faces are harder."

Inside, the carriage smelled of pipe smoke and wet wool and the particular boiled-cabbage melancholy of all troop trains. Two other passengers: a chaplain asleep upright with his collar askew, and a young subaltern reading by the guttering lamp, his book propped against his knee, his boots—good boots, officer's boots—resting on the opposite seat with the entitlement of the very exhausted.

I took the corner seat. Put the satchel at my feet. Put my hands in my lap like a woman with nothing in her pockets, and felt the hymnal's small hard rectangle against my hip like a second heartbeat.

Bell leaned close, under the cover of checking the window strap. "Quay gate tomorrow, low tide, seven o'clock. Then the harbor. One more day of being ordinary."

"I don't know if I remember how," I said. It came out more honest than I intended. "Being ordinary. I've been extraordinary for so long—extraordinary in the way of keeping my head down and counting names in a notebook—that ordinary feels like a costume now."

Bell studied me. In the bad light his face was all angles and apology. "You could still put it down," he said quietly. "Hand it to me at the quay, let me take it the rest. If they take a nurse, it's a scandal they have to explain. If they take me—" he shrugged, and the shrug contained prisons, possibly firing squads, "—well. I'm already a paragraph in someone's file."

"No."

"Emrys—"

"You carried false manifests for that man for eleven months. You will not carry the truth for me for one." I said it without heat, which surprised me. Somewhere between the ward door and this train, my anger at Bell had cooled into something harder and more useful: certainty. "Besides. Idris took the ledger through the laundry cart today. She lost her post for it, maybe worse. Wren came out of hiding to stand in a queue dressed as a stretcher-bearer. Everyone's carrying something now. That's how it stops being one person's sin."

The train lurched. Began to move. The depot slid backward past the window—the lamp, the clock frozen at quarter past five, the tea urn steaming like a small domestic volcano—and for one moment I saw Cairncross still standing under the eaves, motionless among the moving crowd, watching the carriage go. He raised one hand. Not a wave. Something more like a salute, or a promise, or a man marking a card.

Then the dark took the platform, and there was only the window reflecting our own faces back at us: a nurse, a corporal in a borrowed coat, and between us, in a hymnal, a dead man's handwriting traveling third class.

The subaltern across the aisle glanced up from his book. "Rotten evening for traveling," he offered, politely, to no one and everyone.

"Dreadful," I agreed.

And we rode north through the dark, ordinary as rain, while somewhere behind us a careful man made his telephone calls.
