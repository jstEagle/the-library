## 12. What Emrys Owes

The night train to the coast smelled of coal smoke, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who had been given three days' leave and intended to spend it unconscious. I had a corner seat in a carriage meant for six, wedged between a snoring sergeant of artillery and a window that rattled like a nervous chaperone. Across from me, Tomas Bell slept sitting up with his cap over his eyes, which struck me as either great confidence or great guilt. With Bell, I was learning, the two were often the same thing.

I did not sleep. Sleep is for people whose consciences fit inside them without folding.

Instead, with the carriage swaying and the dark fields unspooling past the glass like film run through a projector too fast, I took out my ledger.

*Ledger.* The army would have called it contraband. Halloway's people, if they knew of it—and by then I suspected they might—would have called it evidence. Sister Idris, who had seen me writing in it by candlelight more nights than she could count, called it *that blessed book* in the tone other women reserved for gin or gambling. But it was none of those things, really. It was a debt book. And I have always paid what I owe.

There were forty-one names in it now. Forty-one men the official lists had declined to mention—not because they hadn't died, but because their deaths were inconvenient, misfiled, or attached to paperwork someone preferred not to exist. Men who had died of wounds that should have been treated with morphine that never arrived. Men who had starved on rations diverted to some quartermaster's private arithmetic. Men whose last letters home said *I am well* because even they didn't know what had killed them.

I read them the way you'd read a rosary. Name, rank, date, ward, cause as best I could determine it. Private Ellis Mowbray, 14 March, Ward Two, sepsis following a wound that should have been cleaned within hours and wasn't, because there was no carbolic and no time and no one whose job it was to care. Corporal Denzil Ffoulkes—yes, really, Ffoulkes, he'd been teased about it his whole life and bore it with the dignity of a man who could spell it faster than anyone mocking him—19 April, dysentery compounded by malnutrition, which is the army's polite way of saying he starved while stores sat in a warehouse somewhere growing fat for somebody else's profit.

I had watched most of them die. That was the part no tribunal would ever quite grasp, I thought—the clerical horror of it. On paper, each death was a line. In the ward, each one was a sound. A particular rattle in the throat. A hand that went from gripping yours to merely resting in it to weighing nothing at all. The paper said *deceased*. The ward said *stop breathing, please, please stop*, and then, an hour later, when he had: *thank you*, and then the terrible quiet where a man used to be, and the orderly whistling as he changed the sheets because if he didn't whistle he would scream.

The sergeant beside me shifted and muttered something about a horse named Boudicca. The window rattled on.

Here is the thing I had never told anyone, not Bell, not Wren, not Idris, though Idris guessed at it the way women who have buried sons guess at everything. Here is why the ledger exists at all.

My brother's name was Rhys Vane. He died eleven years ago, before this war, before anyone had thought to industrialize grief on quite such a scale. He was nineteen. He drowned in the reservoir above our village—or that is what the coroner wrote, in a round schoolmaster's hand, on a form that cost our mother two shillings to obtain and told her nothing except that her son had stopped being alive on a Tuesday.

But here is what the form did not say. It did not say that Rhys had been on the reservoir works that summer, one of thirty boys hired cheap because boys are cheap. It did not say that the scaffolding on the eastern face had been reported unsafe twice, or that the report had gone into a drawer, or that the drawer belonged to a man who was also the cousin of the contractor who had underbid everyone else on the strength of cutting precisely such corners. It did not say any of that, because the inquest heard from three witnesses, all of them employed by the same contractor, all of whom agreed that Rhys had been reckless, had climbed where he shouldn't, had—in essence—drowned himself through his own foolishness, and wasn't it a tragedy, and here's your form, madam, two shillings.

My mother believed it. That was the cruelest part. She spent the rest of her life believing her boy had been careless, and she grieved him with a small private shame, as though he'd let the family down twice—once by dying and once by deserving it. She died four years ago still believing it. I found the truth afterward, in a bundle of papers a retired foreman sold me for a sovereign and a bottle of whisky, papers that proved Rhys had been sent to the eastern face that morning precisely because he was the newest boy and the newest boys got the worst work, and that the foreman himself had signed the safety complaint that vanished into the drawer.

Eleven years too late, I held proof that my brother had been killed by arithmetic. And there was nothing—nothing—I could do with it. The contractor was dead. The foreman was dying. The company had dissolved and reformed twice under new names, the way companies do, shedding liabilities like old coats. The truth sat in my lap like a stone, and every person I might have shown it to was beyond the reach of justice, and so the stone just stayed there, and I carried it, and I carry it still.

That is the inheritance. Not the house—we never had one worth the name. Not the silver—our family silver was a teaspoon with the monogram worn off. The inheritance was this: a debt unpaid, a name wrongly written, and a daughter who learned that the people who erase names count on exactly this—that by the time you find the proof, everyone who could act on it will be dead, and grief will be too tired to fight.

Well. I decided, somewhere between my mother's funeral and the first winter of this war, that I would not be too tired. I would be the one who acted *in time*. If names were going to be erased, I would write them back. If lists lied, I would keep a list that didn't. I could not save Rhys. But I could stand at the door of every ward in France, metaphorically speaking, with a pencil behind my ear, and say: *not this one. This one gets remembered. You'll have to go through me.*

It is not heroism. I want to be clear about that, because I've caught myself preening sometimes, late at night, and preening over a notebook while men suffocate on their own fluid is obscene. It is closer to superstition. A covenant with the dead, or perhaps with myself—a bargain struck in the churchyard the day we buried Mother, standing there in the rain thinking, *someone has to pay this back, and everyone who owes it is gone, so I suppose it's me.*

Debts don't dissolve with the debtor. They pass down. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never inherited one.

* * *

The train stopped at a junction for twenty minutes that felt geological. A girl came through selling coffee that tasted of hot pennies, and Bell woke long enough to buy us both cups without asking whether I wanted one, which was either courtesy or a test, and I drank mine because refusing felt like showing him something.

"You read it every night," he said, nodding at the ledger, which lay open on my knee. "Like scripture."

"Scripture asks things of you. This only remembers."

"That's harder." He turned his cap in his hands. He had a way of looking at his hands when he spoke that made everything sound like a confession; possibly everything was. "Most people find remembering harder than praying."

"And which are you, Mr. Bell? Most people?"

He smiled without much humor. "I'm the man who carried the false manifests," he said, quietly, for the third time since the barn. "You keep forgetting that."

"I keep *declining* to forget it, actually. There's a difference." I closed the ledger. "A man who carries false manifests for money and then spends his own money and risk to undo it is either a saint or a very sophisticated liar, and saints are rare enough that I'm hedging my bets."

"And if I'm the liar?"

"Then I'll know eventually. Liars get tired. Truth-tellers only get tired of liars." I looked out the window at the junction lamps, orange smudges in the fog. "Besides, you asked for me by name. Nobody sends a liar after a nurse with a notebook. Liars go after generals."

He didn't answer that, and the whistle blew, and the train hauled itself onward toward the sea.

* * *

I reread the ledger again after that, but differently this time—not as a rosary but as a reckoning. Because Bell had told me things on the platform at the barn, and in the lane, and once in a whisper while pretending to adjust my kit strap, and those things had rearranged the book in my hands. Before Bell, my forty-one names had been tragedies. Now they were *counts*. Charges, almost. Manifest numbers. Dates on which certain supply convoys had been signed through certain depots by a certain elegant major with a smile like a polished boot.

Private Mowbray's sepsis: traced to a convoy of antiseptics marked delivered on 11 March and actually sold, per Bell, to a civilian merchant in Amiens. Corporal Ffoulkes's starvation: rations drawn and receipted for a battalion that had been disbanded six weeks prior, the ghost-battalion trick, paper soldiers eating paper meals while real men went hungry. And so on, and so on, forty-one times, each name now wearing its number like a wound dressing.

And threaded through all of it, the name Aldous Wren, who had counted what Halloway counted and reached a different total, and had written reports saying so, and had then—officially—died. Eight months officially dead. Eight months of his mother presumably mourning a son who was alive in a barn smelling of pigs, and I do not know which crime made me angrier, the thefts or that.

I thought about what I owed each of them. It is a strange arithmetic, grief. You owe the dead remembrance, obviously, but remembrance is the easy part; any fool can feel sad in the correct direction. The harder question—the one the train's rhythm seemed to be asking me, *what do you owe, what do you owe, what do you owe*—was what I owed the living. Wren, who needed his name back and couldn't fetch it himself. Idris, who had lost a son to these same misrouted supplies and didn't yet know it, though I suspected she'd worked it out around the edges, the way mothers do. Bell, who owed a soul and was paying installments. Even Halloway, in a sense—owed a trial, which is more than he gave any of them.

And I owed my brother. Eleven years of interest on a name written wrong.

Somewhere past midnight the carriage lights failed and the sergeant's snores filled the dark like weather. I sat with the ledger shut on my knees and did the thing I never do in daylight, which is admit that I am afraid. Not of dying—nurses develop a professional indifference to dying, it's practically a uniform accessory. I am afraid of failing. Of getting to the harbor and finding the ship sailed, or the gate shut, or my memory—forty-one names, dates, manifest numbers, all of it crammed into a skull that has taken more than one blow from a collapsing stretcher rack—not equal to the task. Because if the ledger burns and I fail, the debt rolls forward again. Another generation inherits it. Some other nurse, years hence, finds her brother's name erased and starts a notebook in the dark, and thinks, as I think tonight: *why did no one finish this?*

I won't be the reason someone asks that.

The train began its long descent toward the coast. I could smell salt through the rattling window, faint under the coal, the way you smell the sea before you see it—like a promise the world makes and mostly keeps. Dawn was still hours off. The tide, Bell said, waited for no one, and the mail ship rode it out at first light, and everything I owed had to be aboard her when she went.

I tucked the ledger inside my coat, against my heart, which is where you carry debts.

Bell stirred across from me and lifted his cap a fraction, and in the gray light coming up off the water I saw that his eyes were open and fixed on the dark window—and reflected in that window, faint as breath on glass, I saw the station platform sliding past behind us, and on the platform, under a single lamp, a figure in a major's greatcoat, watching the train go by.

He wasn't looking at the train.

He was smiling at the carriage window. At me.

The lamp slid away, the platform ended, and we ran on into the dark toward the sea, and I understood that whatever I owed, Major Corven Halloway had decided it was time to send me an invoice.
