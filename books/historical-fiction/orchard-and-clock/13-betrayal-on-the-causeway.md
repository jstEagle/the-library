## 13. Betrayal on the Causeway

There is a particular smell to a military checkpoint at low tide: salt, creosote, wet rope, and the nervous sweat of men who would rather be anywhere else. I have stood in that smell before, though never on this side of the ledger. For two years I stood behind the table with a stamp in my hand and a lie in my pocket, waving through crates whose manifests were works of fiction—flour that was never milled, morphine that went south to a buyer in Marseille, boots that existed only on paper and therefore kept no soldier's feet dry. I stamped those lies with a steady hand and told myself it was arithmetic, not murder.

Arithmetic has a way of becoming murder when you divide by the wrong number.

Now I stood on the wrong side of the table, in a private's greatcoat two sizes too big for me, holding a canvas satchel that contained a hymnal, a letter written by a dead man, and enough consequence to hang us all twice over. The causeway gate at Marren Point was a wooden barrier across a road barely wide enough for one cart, flanked by sandbags and a hut with a stove pipe leaning like a drunk against a wall. Beyond it, the flats stretched out grey and glistening, exposing everything the tide had hidden—old shells, a drowned cart, the ribs of something that had once been a boat. The sea does that here. It gives back what it takes, eventually, and doesn't care who's embarrassed by the return.

Emrys Vane walked ahead of me with her medical satchel and her nurse's stride, that flat, unhurried walk women of her profession learn because running suggests panic and panic suggests guilt. She did not look back at me. We had agreed she wouldn't. In the plan, we were strangers—a nurse going where nurses go, a stretcher-bearer's relief lagging behind with orders he'd lost. It was good cover. It was excellent cover. It would have been perfect cover if the universe had not decided, somewhere around the rail depot, that Tomas Bell had suffered too little for his sins.

"Name and business," said the corporal at the barrier, a boy of perhaps nineteen with a chilblain on his ear the size of a shilling.

"Bell. Tomas Bell. Relief detail, Fourth Field Ambulance." I produced papers Bell had no right to carry and Wren had somehow obtained, and I watched the boy read them with the slow, lip-moving concentration of a man defusing something.

He was still reading when the voice came from the hut behind him, warm as butter left out in the sun.

"Well," said the voice. "Well, well, well. If it isn't the Counting House itself."

I knew the voice before I turned around. You know certain voices the way you know certain pains—the ones you gave yourself.

* * *

His name was Ferro Dannick, and in another life—one I try not to visit often—he had been my partner in the trade. Not Halloway's partner; Halloway didn't have partners, he had instruments. But Dannick and I had been the hands of the instrument together: he wrote the false tallies, I carried them, and between us we moved enough phantom supplies to buy a house in Kent that neither of us ever saw, because Halloway bought the house instead and called it his commission. Dannick had a face like a friendly ferret and a laugh that made strangers want to confide in him, which is how he'd talked three quartermaster sergeants into falsifying receipts they'd swear to their graves they'd never touched. He was wearing a captain's pips now. Of course he was. Rats climb.

"Tomas Bell," he said again, strolling up to the barrier like a man greeting an old friend at a funeral, which, in a sense, he was. "They told me you'd gone to ground. They told me you'd gone soft. And here you are, walking through my checkpoint in a borrowed coat."

"It's not borrowed," I said. "It's issued. There's a difference, and a man with your eye for paperwork should appreciate it."

The corporal looked between us like a spectator at a tennis match played entirely over his head.

"Dannick," I said. "Captain now. Congratulations. Did you earn them or inherit them from a man who died of misrouted quinine?"

Something flickered behind the friendliness—not conscience; Dannick's conscience had been amputated years ago and the stump healed clean—but calculation. He hooked a finger toward the hut. "Corporal, take the lady's particulars. Captain Dannick will interview this gentleman privately. Standard procedure."

"There is no standard procedure," Emrys said, without turning around, "that requires separating a relief man from his orders."

"Standard procedure," Dannick said pleasantly, "is whatever I say it is, Sister. Go on. The corporal will stamp you through."

And there it was—the hinge of the whole rotten morning. Every instinct I had screamed to stay near her, because the satchel she carried held the letter and mine held only a hymnal and my own worthless history, but plans are built for the world as it should be, and the world as it was had just handed me to Ferro Dannick behind a closed door.

She looked at me then, just once. Her eyes are the colour of the sea off this coast, which is to say they look gentle until you notice nothing gets past them. She gave me nothing—no signal, no fear—and walked through the barrier. The corporal bent over her papers. The stove-pipe hut swallowed me.

* * *

Inside, Dannick poured tea from a dented kettle into two enamel mugs and pushed one across the table like we were going to reminisce.

"You look terrible, Tom."

"I've been told grief becomes me."

"Grief." He laughed, that easy laugh, and sat down across from me, cradling his mug. "Is that what we're calling it? Because from where I sit, it looks like a man who ran away carrying something valuable, and has now walked directly into the one checkpoint on this coast staffed by someone who knows exactly what he used to carry."

I drank the tea. It was terrible. Army tea always is—it tastes the way the army wants you to feel, which is grateful for very little. "If you know what I used to carry," I said, "then you know I can't be threatened with it. You can't blackmail a man with his own confession, Ferro. I've already given it. To people who write things down properly."

That landed harder than I expected. His knuckles whitened on the mug. Good. Let him wonder which people. Let him spend a sleepless night imagining tribunals.

"Here's what's actually going to happen," he said, recovering, leaning forward, dropping the buttered warmth down to something more honest underneath. "Major Halloway knows a courier came through the depot last night. He knows the courier is moving evidence north—to the mail ship, to Quill, to London, to all the tedious machinery of justice that men like us spent two years making sure stayed broken. What the Major doesn't know is names. Give me the name, Tom. Give me the route. And you walk out of here with amnesty in writing. Full amnesty. Your part in the manifests, buried. Your name, clean. You could be in Kent by Christmas, drinking tea that doesn't taste like punishment."

"And the courier?"

"The courier is a detail."

"A detail," I repeated. "That's what we called the flour, once. A detail. That's what we called the morphine. Do you know what a detail adds up to, Ferro? Four hundred men at Vellin Ridge waited nine hours for dressings that were sitting in a warehouse in Rouen with our signatures on the manifest. Four hundred details. Some of them bled slowly enough to think about it."

"They were going to bleed anyway," he said. "It's a war. Everyone bleeds. All we did was choose the order."

I want to tell you I felt rage. Rage would have been cleaner. What I actually felt was recognition—that cold, sick familiarity of hearing your own old sentences come out of another man's mouth, polished and ready, the exact arithmetic I'd done behind the stamp table. *Everyone bleeds. All we did was choose.* I had said those words. I had believed them for eleven months, which is eleven months longer than any decent man should believe anything so convenient.

"No," I said.

Dannick sighed, genuinely regretful, which was the most frightening thing about him. He could be sorry for you the way weather is sorry for a field. "Tom. Think about it. Amnesty. You're a dead man walking otherwise. Halloway's already signed the warrant—he keeps it in his breast pocket like a love letter. One name and it goes back in the drawer."

"I don't want it in the drawer. I want it read aloud."

"You always did have a flair for the dramatic. It's why your manifests were so convincing—all that narrative energy." He stood, and the friendliness drained out of his face like water out of the flats outside, leaving the mud underneath. "Last chance. Name."

And here is the thing I want recorded, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does: I did not swallow poison. I did not clench my jaw and endure heroically. I did none of the things a brave man does in stories. Instead I did the one thing I have always been good at, the one talent my whole crooked career was built on.

I lied beautifully.

I let my shoulders drop. I let my eyes go to the floor. I let two years of genuine shame rise up into my face, because shame, unlike courage, I keep in reliable supply. And I said, quietly, brokenly:

"It's the sister."

Dannick went very still. "Which sister?"

"The nurse. Vane. She's not a nurse—or she is, but that's not the point. The point is her brother died at Vellin, and she found the manifest discrepancy herself, months ago, and she's been building a case on her own. Halloway's people missed it because nobody suspects a woman who changes bandages. I'm not the courier, Ferro. I'm the fool she recruited because I owed her brother money. She's using me the way she'll use anyone. Check her satchel. Check her satchel and you'll find whatever she wants you to find, and you'll arrest her, and she'll give you the name of some other poor devil, because that's how she's built. I've watched her do it for six weeks." I looked up at him with wet, ruined eyes. "You're not catching a courier. You're being caught."

Now, understand: every word of that was false except the parts about her brother and the manifest, which were true, and true things are the load-bearing walls of any good lie. I handed him Emrys Vane on a plate—her motive, her method, her cunning—and I did it knowing, praying, trusting that she was already through the barrier and moving, that the corporal's stamp had come down, that by the time Dannick finished savouring his triumph she would be a hundred yards down the causeway road and gaining.

It was the filthiest thing I have ever done. I took the one person in this whole affair who has behaved honourably since the first page, and I painted her as the villain, to her enemy, with her enemy's own tea cooling between us. If she ever learns exactly what I said in that hut, she is entitled to hate me for it, and I will accept the sentence without appeal. Loyalty is not clean. Nobody tells you that. They tell you loyalty is a banner, bright and upright. Mostly it's mud. Mostly it's saying ugly things in a hot little room so that someone else can walk free down a cold road.

Dannick smiled. It was the smile of a man who has just been handed the answer to an exam he was sure he'd fail. "The sister," he repeated, savouring it. "Halloway will love that. He's been trying to work out her angle for days." He moved to the door and barked for the corporal, and for one glorious moment I thought it had worked completely—

—and then he stopped, hand on the latch, and turned back, and the ferret-face was thoughtful.

"One problem, Tom. If she recruited you six weeks ago, why are you coming through my checkpoint *with* her? Why would she bring the loose end along?"

Because the plan required two sets of hands. Because a woman alone draws eyes. Because I insisted, God help me, on not letting her walk into this alone. None of those answers would survive ten seconds of scrutiny.

So I lied again, second course, and this time I made it humble. "Because she doesn't trust me. She wanted me where she could see me. That's the kind of operator she is—you're not a colleague, you're luggage. Ask yourself why a trained nurse needs a relief man with bad knees on a supply run. Ask yourself what I'm carrying that she couldn't risk leaving behind." I tapped the satchel. Inside it lay a hymnal, a spare shirt, and a letter I had already copied twice and hidden in places Dannick would need a week and a crowbar to find. "Open it if you like. I'd rather you opened it than her."

He hesitated. And in that hesitation I saw the flaw in him that Halloway had built on purpose and never repaired: Dannick wanted the credit. If he opened the satchel and found nothing, he'd wasted an hour and looked a fool. If he took my word and caught the sister red-handed further up the road, the triumph was his entire. Greed doesn't just mean wanting money. It means wanting the story to end the flattering way.

"Keep the satchel," he said finally. "Corporal! Detain this man. Log it as assisting inquiries." Then, to me, almost gently: "Don't go anywhere, Tom."

"I'm under arrest," I said. "Where would I go?"

* * *

They put me in the hut's back room, which had once been a net store and smelled of tar and herring, with a chair, a table, and a window overlooking the causeway. Through the glass, streaked with salt, I watched the road.

The corporal came in twice to check on me and both times I asked him about his chilblain and both times he told me his mother's remedy, which involved goose fat and a prayer, and both times I listened as though it were scripture, because a nineteen-year-old with chilblains is a human being and I have spent enough of my life treating human beings as line items. He loosened after that. By the second visit he mentioned, unprompted, that the sister had gone through fine, half an hour ago, walking toward the harbour road, and wasn't it strange, the captain ordering him to note her description anyway.

Half an hour. She was half an hour gone. Whatever Dannick sent up that road would find empty air and a widening lead.

I sat in the herring-smelling dark and did the arithmetic I do now instead of the old arithmetic. Cost of the morning: my freedom, certainly; probably my neck, once Halloway learned his adjutant had held the courier and let the courier's companion walk through on a forged recommendation. Gain of the morning: roughly thirty minutes of distance, one letter safe in its hiding places, and one nurse still walking. On balance, the best trade I'd ever made, and the first honest one.

Toward dusk, boots in the passage. Not the corporal's—officer's heels, unhurried. The door opened and Dannick stood there with a lantern, and behind him, filling the frame, two military policemen with the flat patience of men who drive cattle.

"Change of plan, Tom," Dannick said, and there was something new in his voice—not triumph anymore. Something tighter. "Your sister, as it turns out, never came through the barrier at all. No nurse matching her description passed this gate today. The corporal's log says she turned back at the flats." He lifted the lantern so the light found my face. "Which means either my corporal can't write, or you can't stop lying even when it costs you. So we're going to drive you to the harbour ourselves, nice and slow, and you're going to point her out to Major Halloway personally. And Tom—if you've sent her ahead alone, into what's waiting at that quay—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. Outside, far down the darkening causeway, I could see the lamps of the harbour town coming on one by one, small and yellow against the black water, and somewhere among them, a mail ship loading for the morning tide, and somewhere nearer than that, a woman with a satchel full of the dead walking straight toward the one man in France who wanted her stopped.

"Get your coat," Dannick said.
