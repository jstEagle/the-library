## 14. The Gate at Low Tide

Dannick drove us the last four miles in a cart that smelled of turnips and old rain, and I spent every one of those miles learning a new discipline: how to sit still while your heart does all the moving.

The satchel sat on my knees. Inside it, beneath a folded apron, two rolls of bandage, and a tin of carbolic that had long since given up pretending to be antiseptic, lay the ledger. Two years of names. My brother's name, in its way, though he'd died before the war and never appeared in any of my columns. The dead of Ward Six. The men Halloway's arithmetic had starved and bled to death between one false manifest and the next.

I had carried it through a rail depot with an adjutant's eyes crawling over my face like flies. I could carry it through a gate.

That was what I told myself. It is remarkable, the lies a person can make comfortable if she fluffs the pillow often enough.

* * *

The causeway gate was nothing like the depot. The depot had been chaos—crowds, steam, the blessed anonymity of a hundred people all trying not to be noticed. This was a single road across a mile of tidal flats, pinched down at its narrowest point to a wooden barrier, a sandbagged post, and six soldiers with rifles who had clearly been told to stop being polite sometime around noon.

The tide was out. That was the trouble. At high water the causeway flooded and the boats came close; you could hear the gulls arguing over the harbour. At low water there was nothing between the barrier and the sea but mud shining grey as pewter, flat as a ledger page, and nowhere at all for a guilty woman to hide.

"Names," said the sergeant at the barrier. He was young, with the kind of moustache boys grow when they want very badly to be taken seriously, and he held Dannick's papers at arm's length as though they might go off.

"Dr. Dannick, casualty evacuation," Dannick said. "Nurse Vane. Stretcher-bearer's pass for the man behind."

I did not look behind. Behind was Wren, wrapped in a stretcher-bearer's coat two sizes too big, a canvas mask over the lower half of his face—the official sort, the influenza sort—and his hands, those careful officer's hands, gloved against scrutiny and gripping the handles of an empty stretcher as if it were the only thing keeping him upright. Which, in a sense, it was.

We had argued about it in the barn until the candles burned down to their ears.

"I'm a dead man," he'd said. "Dead men travel light. Nobody searches a corpse's escort."

"You're a *recognisable* man," I'd answered. "There are posters in three towns with your face on them, and the reward printed underneath in letters a child could read."

"And if they read my face anyway?" He'd almost smiled. "Then at least I'll be arrested doing something instead of hiding from everything. There's a difference. I've sampled both."

I hadn't liked it. I still didn't. But the causeway was closed to anyone without papers, Bell was in custody at the second checkpoint—we hoped, we prayed, we told ourselves the story of Tomas lying beautifully to men with notebooks—and the letter and the ledger had to reach the quay before dawn, and there were exactly three of us, and so Aldous Wren put on a borrowed coat and became a stretcher-bearer named Private Hobbes, and I stopped arguing because I had run out of arguments and was running low on time.

"Satchel," said the sergeant.

* * *

Here is a thing nobody tells you about fear: it makes you precise. In the hospital, when a wound went bad, my hands went steady—not calm, never calm, but exact, because panic wastes seconds and seconds are blood. So when the sergeant held out his hand for my medical satchel, my hands were steady, and some distant clinical part of me observed this with interest, the way one observes a patient's pulse.

He set the satchel on the barrier table—a plank laid across two barrels, which was the entire administrative infrastructure of His Majesty's checkpoint on the Flats—and began going through it with a soldier's thoroughness, which is to say no system whatsoever but great energy.

Apron. Bandages. The carbolic tin—he shook it beside his ear like a Christmas present, which nearly undid me; if the lid had come loose I'd have been standing in a puddle of useless disinfectant explaining myself to six rifles. Scissors. A spare torch. The folded papers of my nursing orders, stamped and countersigned by Sister Idris, God keep her, wherever they've posted her now.

And under all of it, wrapped in a spare shirt, square and hard-cornered and heavy as a sin: the ledger.

"Long shift?" the sergeant asked, not looking up.

"Since Tuesday," I said. It was even true.

"War's full of Tuesdays." He lifted out the shirt.

Time did something odd then. It thickened. I watched his hands gather the fabric by its corners, watched the shape inside sag and swing, and I heard myself saying, "Careful, that's got a dressing soaked through, you'll want gloves"—and he paused, disgusted, holding the bundle at arm's length, and I understood that I was about to lose everything on the strength of one boy's reluctance to touch a stranger's bloody laundry—

—which was when the shouting started.

* * *

It came from the seaward end of the causeway. A voice, cracked and carrying, and then another answering it, and then a third, and the sergeant's head came up like a dog's.

On the flats below the causeway, halfway between the gate and the receding waterline, a man was down. A stretcher-bearer—I knew the coat, I'd helped button it an hour ago—was kneeling in the mud beside him, and the man on the ground was convulsing, or performing convulsions, and either way he was loud about it.

"Gas case!" the kneeling figure shouted. "Off the flats—get him off the flats—" And then, to the soldiers, with a desperation so perfectly pitched it broke my heart: "You! You lot! Help me lift him or help me hold him, but *do something!*"

They did something. Soldiers always do; it is what they're for, and a crisis is a comfort to them after hours of checking papers. Three of them went over the sandbags at a clatter, rifles slung, boots sucking in the mud, and the sergeant hesitated exactly one second—one second in which his hand rested on the shirt-wrapped ledger, one second in which the whole future balanced on a boy's boredom—before duty yanked him over the barrier too.

"Stay here," he told me, meaning don't move, meaning don't follow, and then he was gone down onto the flats, shouting about a stretcher, about the aid post, about whether the man had a mask on under there.

I stayed. I am proud of that, in a way that has curdled over the years into something closer to shame. I stayed exactly where I was told, hands folded, while four soldiers and one disguised captain wrestled a faking dying man up the slippery slope of the causeway, and I did not run, and I did not shout, and I did not go to him, because the satchel was back in my hands and the letter was sewn into Wren's coat lining and the tide was turning and there was a ship, there was *one ship*, and I had learned by then what my covenant cost, and it cost this: standing still.

Wren passed within six feet of me.

They carried him—Hobbes the stretcher-bearer staggering under his own captain, which must have been the strangest labour of his life—past the barrier, past the barrel table, past me, and for half a step our eyes met above the canvas mask.

He couldn't smile. The mask hid whatever his mouth did. But his eyes did something—they crinkled, they steadied, they said what he'd said in the barn when I'd begged him not to do anything stupid: *Someone has to be the noise.*

Then they hauled him around the sandbag emplacement toward the aid post tent, six men and one miracle of theatrical collapse, and I picked up my satchel, and I walked through the open gate.

Nobody stopped me. The remaining sentry was watching the mud. Everyone was watching the mud.

* * *

Dannick caught up with me a quarter mile along, driving slow so as not to look like flight. I climbed up beside him and we rolled on through the dark with the lamps of the harbour town growing ahead of us, and neither of us spoke for a long time.

"He bought it," Dannick said finally. "The fit. Old trick—saw it done at Ypres once. Man fakes a shell-shock seizure in front of a staff colonel and walks out of a court-martial while everyone's fussing over him." He glanced at me. "It doesn't always end well for the man on the ground."

"No," I said.

"They'll want to examine him at the aid post. Strip the coat to listen to his chest. It's sewn into the lining, you said?"

"It's sewn into the lining."

"Then he's got perhaps ten minutes of being a hero left in him." Dannick flicked the reins. "After that he'll need to be somewhere else entirely, and so will we, and so will that book of yours, and the tide comes back in at half five."

I looked back once, at the little knot of lantern-light around the aid post, at the flats going silver as the moon found them, and I thought about what it costs a man to lie in the mud and shake and scream when he has already done his shaking and screaming for one lifetime, quietly, in a barn, alone. Courage isn't always charging. Sometimes it's lying still and letting strangers' hands search you while your whole testimony sits an inch from their fingers.

"He'll get clear," I said, because someone had to say it aloud to make it true. "He's too stubborn to be captured twice."

* * *

The harbour town received us the way towns receive night travellers: badly, and with dogs. We left the cart in a yard behind a chandlery whose owner owed Dannick money, and walked the last stretch to the quay on foot, two medical people out late, nothing to see here, move along.

The mail ship sat where the plan said she would, low and black against the darker water, her funnel breathing a thin smoke that meant *soon*. A gangway. A purser with a list. A handful of passengers' trunks going aboard under one swinging electric lamp, and beyond the lamp, the dark, and beyond the dark, England, tribunals, newspapers, the whole machinery of truth waiting to be fed.

One more checkpoint. The quay itself. And Halloway would be there—I knew it the way you know weather coming in your bones. A man does not build a machine that kills hundreds and then let the last pieces sail away unwatched.

At the mouth of the quay street, Dannick stopped.

"Lanterns," he said.

There were more than there should have been. Military lanterns, spaced along the quay rail at tidy intervals, and figures beside them, and at the far end, where the gangway met the stones, a small cluster of officers with the particular stillness of men who have been told exactly whom to wait for.

And walking up the middle of the quay, unhurried, elegant even at midnight, his greatcoat collar turned just so against the sea air, came Major Corven Halloway.

Behind us, back along the dark causeway road, faint but distinct, came the sound of a whistle. Then another. Then a bell at the aid post, ringing the way bells ring for emergencies.

They'd found him. Or lost him. Either way, the noise had started, and there was no version of the next hour in which Aldous Wren stayed quiet.

Dannick looked at me. I looked at the satchel on my shoulder, at the ledger sleeping inside it with all its dead, at the ship breathing smoke thirty yards and one major away.

"Well," I said. "No time like low tide."

We stepped out onto the quay, straight into the light.
