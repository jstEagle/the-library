## 15. Halloway Closes the Net

I have always believed that a man's desk tells you everything the man himself will conceal. Mine is walnut, imported before the war made importation vulgar, and its surface holds exactly three things: a blotter, a telephone I rarely use because conversations can be overheard, and a small brass clock that runs four minutes fast. The clock is not an affectation. It is a discipline. If I believe it is later than it is, I am never late; if everyone else believes it is earlier than it is, they are always behind me.

Major Corven Halloway does not run behind.

The morning the net began to close—though I confess at the time it felt less like closing a net and more like mending one that some careless fisherman had left in the shallows—I was drinking tea that tasted of tin and victory. The tin because the war had eaten all the good china along with everything else. The victory because my adjutant, Captain Pryce, had come in at six with the particular stiffness of a man carrying news he believes will displease me.

Pryce displeases easily. He thinks bad news is a personal failing of whoever brings it. I have tried to cure him of this. It is, after all, my war too.

"Out, sir," he said. "At the causeway gate. Last night."

"The nurse."

"The nurse, sir. And the messenger—Bell. We have Bell."

I set down my cup. "And the ledger?"

"Not recovered, sir."

"Then we have half a fish," I said, "and the half that matters is still swimming."

Pryce looked at his boots. Pryce's boots were always immaculate, which told you most of what you needed to know about how Pryce fought his wars—from a chair, with paperwork, terrified of mud. I do not say this with contempt. Some men are hammers and some are files, and a file in the right hands is more dangerous than any hammer. But a file should know what it is.

"Tighten it," I said. "The harbor quay is sealed from midnight. No civilian passes without my countersignature. No mail ship takes a parcel unopened. And no woman named Vane boards anything that floats."

"And if she's already aboard?"

"She isn't." I returned to my tea. "People like Nurse Vane are constitutionally incapable of leaving early. They believe the work is the point. It is their great virtue and their fatal arithmetic."

He went. I sat for a while with the brass clock ticking its lie at me, four minutes ahead of truth, and considered the shape of the thing I had built and the small, stubborn instrument now rattling around inside it.

* * *

You will want to know how a man comes to divert rations and morphine from soldiers dying in the field, and I will tell you honestly, because dishonesty is for amateurs who fear being caught. I never feared being caught. I feared only incompetence, which is the same crime committed slowly.

The army is a machine that eats numbers and excretes corpses, and somewhere in that metabolism there is slack. Slack in the manifests, slack in the convoys, slack in the ledgers of dead men who cannot audit anything. I found the slack the way water finds it. I did not invent hunger; I merely redirected where the bread went, and the bread arrived somewhere, and someone paid for it, and the payment came back to me in paper that could be burned. Morphine was simply the profitable end of the same pipe. Pain is constant at the front; supply need not be.

Did men die of it? Of course men died of it. Men die of everything here—the weather, the mud, a sergeant's bad map, a general's vanity. My contribution to the mortality tables was a rounding error dressed as a tragedy. I have made peace with the arithmetic. What I have never made peace with—and this is the truth I keep beneath the blotter, so to speak—is being *counted*. A fraud survives on fog. One nurse with a notebook is not a danger to me. One nurse with a notebook who has met a second witness is a *fog dispersal system*, and fogs, once dispersed, do not return on command.

Which is why Aldous Wren still breathing is the single greatest inconvenience of my professional life. A dead captain who reports nothing threatens no one. A living captain, hiding, holding his tongue out of shame or injury—but holding it loosely, the way a tired man holds a railing—is a catastrophe with a pulse. Bell was supposed to be watching him. Bell, it developed, had watched him straight into treason. There is a lesson there about hiring guilty men: they betray whoever is nearest, and proximity is a matter of chance.

So: Wren must be found or rendered irrelevant. The letter must be recovered. The nurse must be turned, frightened, or stopped. In that order of preference, though I would accept them in any order at all. I am flexible in methods and inflexible in outcomes. It is the only reliable philosophy I know.

* * *

The intermediary question required thought, because intermediaries are like gloves: they protect your hands and they remember every grip.

I settled on Mrs. Ada Fenwick, who ran the convalescent canteen two wards down and whose brother-in-law owed me a debt of the sort that cannot be repaid in currency. She was respectable, plausible, and possessed of the particular gravity that makes women in grey uniforms invisible to officers, who see uniforms and stop seeing persons. She would carry an offer. Not a threat—an offer. Threats harden people. Offers soften them, especially offers aimed at the exact place where a person keeps their oldest wound.

To find that place, I had done my reading. I read everyone. It is astonishing what a man learns when he reads personnel files the way other men read novels—for pleasure, late at night, by lamplight.

Emrys Vane. Welsh, hence the name that sounds like a psalm with a fever. Entered service 1915. Steady record, commendation for calm under bombardment, noted twice for "insubordinate thoroughness"—which is the army's way of saying she wrote things down that she'd been asked not to. And there, in the family annex, the entry I wanted:

Brother. Gwilym Vane. Private, 14th Battalion. Killed in action, listed. Then—this was the beautiful part, the part that had taken an afternoon and two telegrams to excavate—a discrepancy. The battalion's own casualty return had him wounded and evacuated. The official roll, the one sent home, the one the mother would have read with her hand over her mouth, had him dead. Somewhere between the trench and the War Office, a clerk had killed a man who was merely broken, and no one had ever corrected it. No death record had reached the family in proper form. The true papers existed—in a registry box in a depot in Rouen, misfiled, gathering damp.

Gwilym Vane had died in the spring of '16, of wounds, in a hospital not unlike the one his sister now kept clean. But the *record* of it—the true date, the true place, the certificate that would let a mother in Carmarthenshire stop setting a place at the table for a son whose death she had never once been permitted to properly mourn—that record was sitting in a box like a lamp waiting for someone to light it.

I could light it.

That is the thing about temptation, you understand. Done properly, it should arrive wearing a human face, carrying something you have wanted so long you've stopped admitting you want it. Not money. Money insults people of principle. Principle is only another currency, and I am fluent in all of them.

* * *

Mrs. Fenwick came to my office at four o'clock, smelling faintly of cocoa and disinfectant, and stood where I indicated, which was nowhere near the desk. I find it puts people at ease when you don't loom. Looming is for men who lack arguments.

"You'll go to Ward Six," I told her, "and you'll ask for Nurse Vane. You will not mention me. You will not mention the army. You will say—" and here I gave her the words whole, because I write my own dialogue; leaving lines to amateurs is how scenes go wrong—"you will say that you know a clerk in Rouen who handles old registries, and that certain records can be retrieved for families who apply through proper channels. That the Vane family's application has been delayed, but that delays can be lifted. By tonight. If a certain notebook finds its way back to the army's custody."

She repeated it back to me, flat and exact. Good. I don't require understanding from messengers, only accuracy.

"And if she asks who's offering?"

"Tell her a friend of correct procedure." I allowed myself the smile I keep for these occasions—it reaches the eyes, which is why it works. "Everyone trusts a friend of correct procedure. It's the one confidence man nobody checks."

She went. I stood at the window and watched the harbor below my office—the gray water, the gray boats, the mail ship riding at anchor with its lights beginning to come up like a slow argument for morning. Somewhere down there was a woman with a satchel and a memory, and somewhere inland was a dead-alive captain, and somewhere in a registry box in Rouen was a dead man's honest paperwork.

Four minutes fast, my clock said. Time to make it true.

* * *

I will record, because a memoirist owes even himself honesty: part of me hoped she would take it.

Not because the trade would have saved me—though it would have, cleanly, the ledger back in my stove and the letter presumably still folded inside some hymnal, ash by dawn. I hoped it because there is a rare pleasure in watching a person choose the wound they know over the truth they don't. It confirms my entire theory of mankind. People do not want justice. They want their grief *certified*. They want the paper. Give a mourner the document and they will forgive you almost anything, because the alternative—living with an uncertified loss, an unwitnessed death—is unbearable, and I am, whatever else, in the business of relieving unbearable things. Ask anyone. I relieve officers of surplus supplies. I relieve families of uncertainty. I am practically a charity.

But I did not build my position on hope. Hope is what other men feel while I make arrangements.

If she took the offer: fine. The stove, the ash, the tide going out without her.

If she refused: also fine, in the way that winter is fine. You dress for it. The quay would be sealed. Her name would be on every gate list from here to the breakwater. Wren would surface eventually—guilty men always drift toward confession the way drowned things drift toward light—and when he surfaced, my people would be standing on the light.

There remained only the variable I genuinely could not price, and I sat with it while the harbor darkened and the brass clock lied at me: the possibility that the nurse was not bargaining at all. That she had never been keeping that ledger to trade it. That some people write names down not as currency but as covenant, and a covenant is the one instrument I have never learned to forge.

It was an uncomfortable thought. I poured a whisky against it and it mostly worked.

* * *

Mrs. Fenwick returned at nine.

I knew before she spoke. That is not mysticism; it is simply that messengers who have succeeded walk differently than messengers who have failed, and hers had the gait of a woman who had been made to feel small by someone smaller than herself.

"Well?" I said.

"She listened," Fenwick said. "All of it. The clerk in Rouen, the record, the brother." She paused, and I gave her time, because extracting information from the reluctant is a patience business. "Then she thanked me. Politely. Said she'd pray for the clerk's diligence." Her eyes came up. "And she said—she said to tell whoever sent me that Gwilym Vane doesn't need a paper to have died well. He needs the truth about why the morphine didn't reach him. She said the registry box and the manifest book come from the same drawer."

The ward went very quiet, except for the clock, lying away to itself.

I had made an error, and it took me a long moment to name it, because my errors are rare enough to be unfamiliar guests. I had assumed her brother's erasure was a wound I could dress. It was not a wound. It was the reason for the ledger. I had offered a woman her inheritance in exchange for the house it was built on.

"Thank you, Mrs. Fenwick," I said pleasantly. "You've been precise and you've been discreet, and both are noted."

She hesitated at the door—women always hesitate at doors when leaving my office, as though crossing the threshold might count as testimony—and then she was gone.

I stood alone with my whisky and my fast clock and the harbor lights, and I felt the thing I imagine a chess player feels when the opponent declines a draw: not anger. Respect, cold and clarifying, followed immediately by the understanding that the game ends tonight whether the board likes it or not.

Because here is what she had understood in that ward, standing among her bandages and her dying men, that I had priced wrong: I had offered to certify a death. She was in the business of witnessing them. Those are opposite trades, and no amount of elegant brokerage closes the gap.

Very well. No more offers. Offers are for the persuadable, and I had spent a day learning that Emrys Vane was not persuadable, only delayable.

"Pryce," I said into the telephone, and heard him come awake on the third ring. "Double the watch on the quay. Every gangway, every crane, every crate of post. And find out which correspondent boarded that ship's passenger list today. Writers, Pryce. Nothing is real until a writer says it is, and I intend to be present when the saying happens—or absent from it entirely."

I hung up. Outside, the tide had begun to turn, dragging the harbor's dark water toward the sea, and the mail ship rode at anchor with her lights burning, patient as a promise.

Somewhere between my office and that water was a nurse carrying a hundred names in a head I could not search.

I put on my coat. If the net would not close by morning, it would close at the dock—with me standing on it, smiling, four minutes early.
