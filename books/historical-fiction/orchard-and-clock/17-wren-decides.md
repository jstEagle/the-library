## 17. Wren Decides

The fishing boat smelled of herring and old lies, which suited me fine, because I had been living on both for eight months.

Tomas Bell had gone to fetch Emrys and the ledger, and I had been left alone with the boatman, a taciturn Breton named Yves who spoke no English and pretended, with great conviction, not to speak French either. He mended nets in the corner of the hold while I sat on a crate of salt fish and rehearsed the only two futures I had left.

In the first, Yves rowed me out past the breakwater at slack water tonight, and a Dutch coaster picked me up off the light, and I landed in Rotterdam under a name I'd been practicing in the mirror—Alden Voss, a clerk, a dull man, a man whose hands had never signed a requisition that got anyone killed. From Rotterdam, anywhere. Argentina, if the money held. Somewhere with orchards, even. I had developed a sentimental weakness for orchards.

In the second future, I stayed.

I had been rehearsing the first future for eight months and the second for about four days, which told you something about the relative weight of my arguments. Cowardice, given a long enough head start, begins to feel like prudence. It had a whole stable of horses by now. *You're no use to anyone dead*, said one. *The tribunal can convict Halloway on the manifests alone*, said another, which was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, because paper without a living witness is just paper, and Halloway had spent two years teaching paper to say whatever he wanted.

The third voice—the one that had been getting louder since Emrys Vane stood in the doorway of this barn with rain in her hair and called me a coward in the politest possible terms—said nothing at all. It just held up the faces. Corporal Finch, who died of a gut wound that two tablets of morphine might have let him survive, while the morphine sat in a warehouse in Rouen with Halloway's initials chalked on the crate. Private Osei, who went into the line unfed because his company's rations had been "misrouted" for eleven straight days. Lieutenant Marsh, who was shot by his own men, and whose death was entered as *enemy action*, because the truth would have required someone to ask why his men were starving.

I had written all of their names in reports. I had written them in a neat, upright hand that I was rather proud of, once. And I had watched those reports go into Halloway's office like letters into a furnace, and I had said nothing, and then the shell came, and the war very kindly gave me a way to say nothing forever.

Dead men make excellent witnesses. They can't be cross-examined, and they can't be bribed, and best of all, they can't be ashamed.

I was not dead. That was the whole trouble.

* * *

Emrys came back an hour before dawn with Bell, and the three of us sat in the hold among the fish crates while the boat rocked and Yves mended his nets and pretended, with tremendous discipline, to be deaf.

She had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart. I watched her hold it and understood, in a way I hadn't in the barn, that this was not a document to her. It was a congregation. She had stood over dying men for two years and written down the names the army declined to write, and she had done it knowing that if anyone found the book, it would be her neck, and she had done it anyway, night after night, by candle stub, in a hospital that smelled of carbolic and the sea.

I had written reports. She had written scripture. The difference between us was that she had never once considered stopping.

"The mail ship sails on the morning tide," Bell said. He was working his hands together the way he did when the guilt got loud—rubbing the knuckles like he was trying to rub something off them. "Quay gate opens at five. Halloway will have men there. He'll have men at the causeway and the rail depot too, but the quay is where he'll be, because the quay is where it ends, and Corven Halloway likes to be present at endings. He thinks of them as his signature."

"Then we go around him," I said. "There are other boats. Yves here—"

"Yves is taking you to Rotterdam tonight, if that's what you still want," Bell said, not unkindly. "That was always the arrangement. That's the arrangement now."

And there it was, laid out on a fish crate between us like a hand of cards. The boat. The tide. The name I'd practiced.

Emrys said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing. She looked at me with those grey eyes that seemed to have been issued by the same quartermaster as her spine—regulation, unbreakable, slightly the worse for wear—and waited for me to be the man she'd already decided I was. I hated her a little for that. I hated her more for being right.

"If I get on that boat," I said, "Halloway wins."

"Halloway wins if you're dead, too," Bell said. "He wins either way, most likely. He has the army, the provosts, the whole machinery. You'd be walking into the harbor office of a port he controls and signing your name to a statement that names him as a war profiteer who murdered several hundred of His Majesty's soldiers. They don't hang officers for that. They hang officers for *saying* it."

"I know how the arithmetic runs, Tomas."

"Then say it plain," Emrys said. Her voice was low and even. "Say what you're choosing."

I looked at the boat. I looked at her.

Eight months ago I had lain in a ditch outside Passchendaele with my tunic full of shell fragments and my lungs full of mud, and the first coherent thought I had—the thought I built my whole new life on, if you could call it a life—was *this is the exit*. God, or the artillery, or simple administrative error had opened a door marked ALDOUS WREN: DECEASED, and I had crawled through it and shut it behind me and called it survival. I had let her believe I was dead. I had let *everyone* believe it. It had been, I told myself, the safest thing for the testimony.

It had also been the easiest thing for Aldous Wren, and I had learned in those eight months that I was very good at confusing the two.

"The manifests prove supplies were diverted," I said. "They don't prove who diverted them. Halloway's initials on a chalk mark is nothing; chalk washes off. The requisitions in my name that I never signed—those prove forgery, but forgery needs a forger, and the forger will swear it was me, and I'll be a dead man who can't swear back. The only thing that breaks him is a living officer, under oath, who was in the room. Who saw the ledgers. Who told him to his face that men were dying of it and watched him smile." I heard my own voice go dry and strange. "There is exactly one of those, and he's sitting on a herring crate deciding whether to run."

Emrys nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a figure she'd already carried.

"You could still get on the boat," she said. "I want you to understand that I know that. Whatever you choose, I know what it costs, and I know you've already paid eight months of it."

"That's the thing," I said. "I've paid eight months of it, and it turns out the interest is worse than the principal."

* * *

We spent the last dark hours preparing, and I will record honestly that my hands shook through all of it.

Bell wrote out the statement's shape from memory—the dates, the warehouse numbers, the convoy schedules, the names of the two clerks who had done the forging and could be found if anyone looked. Emrys cross-checked it against her ledger by candlelight, name by name, and I sat and watched a dead man's war get rebuilt out of a woman's notebook and a smuggler's conscience.

There was a moment—I keep it now the way other men keep a medal—when she read out a name and a date and I said, "He was there. He was standing at my elbow when I told Halloway the Third Battalion hadn't eaten in four days." She wrote it in the margin. She wrote it as if it mattered, as if the fact that a man had been *witnessed* was itself a kind of provision, issued to the dead like a blanket or a ration.

Maybe it is. I had stopped believing in most provisions by then, but I found I wanted to believe in that one.

Yves finished his nets and looked at me once, and I understood the question. I gave him the answer in the only French I had that mattered: "Pas ce soir." Not tonight. He shrugged with the whole of his body, spat over the side with great philosophy, and began unloading the salt fish as though the whole affair had been a disappointing business transaction, which I suppose it had.

"You understand they may not even let you testify," Bell said. "They may just shoot you for desertion and call it tidy. Eight months absent, Captain. You're not a witness to them, you're a problem with a commission."

"I'm aware."

"And Halloway—"

"Is a man who smiles," I said. "I've seen the smile. I signed things under it for two years." I stood, and my knees complained, and I discovered I didn't care. "Tomas. The night I decided to die, I mean properly die, be dead on paper, never come back—do you know what I told myself? I told myself the truth needed me alive. That a witness in hiding was worth more than a corpse in a courtroom." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It was a lie. It was the most comfortable lie I ever told, and I told it in my own handwriting. If I run now, I'm just the same man I was in Halloway's office, saying nothing, with better weather."

Emrys put the oilcloth bundle in my hands—the statement, the copied pages, the names—and folded my fingers over it.

"Then don't say nothing," she said.

* * *

The harbor at first light was the color of pewter, and it smelled of tar and rope and the particular cold sweetness of a tide about to turn. The mail ship stood out at her moorings with steam up, a grey lady impatient to be gone, and between us and the harbor office lay two hundred yards of open quay with Halloway's men arranged along it like furniture someone had chosen deliberately.

I had worn a stevedore's jersey and a cap borrowed from Yves, and I had shaved off eight months of beard that morning, and the face in the cracked mirror of the boathouse had startled me—a thinner face, an older one, but underneath it, unmistakably, the face from the casualty lists. There was no disguising it. There never had been. That had been the flaw in my whole architecture of survival: I had hidden a man whose name was already carved in stone, and stone is patient.

Emrys walked beside me. Bell hung back by the fish sheds, doing his guilty-messenger's arithmetic, watching the roofs.

"You could hang back too," I said.

"I could," she agreed, and didn't.

At the door of the harbor office, a naval clerk looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man who had already been disappointed twice that morning, and I took off the cap.

"I am Captain Aldous Wren," I said, and my voice did not shake, which surprised me, because everything else in me was shaking. "Eleventh Field Company, Royal Engineers. I am reported dead. I am not. I have come to make a sworn statement concerning the diversion of army supplies, and I am asking you, sir, to write down the hour and the minute, because I suspect I will not be alive to insist upon it later."

The clerk looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at Emrys, mud-stained and grey-eyed and utterly unafraid, standing at my shoulder like a verdict.

"Names," he said, and pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and dipped his pen.

I began with the date of the first diverted convoy, and I spoke clearly, the way I had once spoken in Halloway's office, except that this time the words were going somewhere they could not be burned. Through the office window I could see the quay, and on the quay, unhurried, elegant, a figure in a staff officer's greatcoat had stopped walking and turned his head toward the harbor office—and even at two hundred yards, even across cold morning air, I could see that Corven Halloway was smiling.

"Write faster," Emrys said quietly, to the clerk, to all of us. "He's seen us."
