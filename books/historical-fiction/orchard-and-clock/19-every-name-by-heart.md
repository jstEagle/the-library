## 19. Every Name by Heart

They let me go the way you release a bird with a broken wing—not out of mercy, but because a thing that cannot fly is no longer worth holding.

I walked out of Halloway's presence on legs that belonged to somebody else. Behind me, in the iron brazier at the quay office, my ledger was still burning. I had watched it go—watched the cover curl first, then the pages, then the names, each one lifting off the paper as ash before collapsing into itself. Private Alfred Dunn, 14 March. Sergeant Milo Ketch, 2 April. On and on, page after page, four years of nights spent by candlelight while men snored and moaned around me, my pen scratching in the dark like some small stubborn insect that refused to die.

The smoke had smelled of ink and tallow and something else underneath, something sweeter. I chose not to identify it.

Halloway had been courteous throughout. That was the worst of it, or so I believed at the time. He had searched my satchel himself, declining his adjutant's offer with a little wave, as though he wanted the pleasure to be unshared. He had drawn the ledger out from beneath the bandages and the morphine ampoules as if he were delivering a child. And when he'd held it up to the lamplight and read the spine—the plain oilcloth cover, the string binding—he had smiled the smile of a man confirming a suspicion he has nursed for months the way other men nurse a flask.

"Sister Vane," he said. "You have been busy."

I said nothing. There seemed little point. A woman caught red-handed might as well save her hands for other work.

"Four years," he said, turning the pages with two fingers, delicately, the way one handles something unclean. "Do you know what I admire about this? The discipline. Most people can't keep a diary past Lent." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were not cruel—they were worse than cruel, they were *interested*. "You understand this proves nothing. A nurse's grievances, scribbled in the dark. Half these spellings would embarrass a tribunal."

"They're spelled correctly," I heard myself say, and hated how thin it sounded.

"Perhaps." He closed the book. "It doesn't matter. Because it's going to burn, and you're going to stand there and watch, and tomorrow morning you'll be on a hospital ship home, and in six months nobody will remember there ever was an Emrys Vane." He paused, and here he did me the courtesy of dropping the charm entirely, letting me see the machinery behind the face. "That is not a threat. It is an inventory of your options."

Then he burned it, and I stood there, and I watched, and I did not cry, because crying would have meant the ledger mattered more than the names, and the names were never made of paper.

But walking away from that quay office into the dark of the harbor town, with the tide hours off and Wren's letter pressed flat against my ribs where I had hidden it inside my corsetry—a decision that had seemed absurdly melodramatic at the time and now seemed like the single competent act of my life—I felt something come apart in my chest. Not grief exactly. Grief I knew; grief was an old lodger who paid his rent in silence. This was more like a wall coming down in a house I had thought empty, revealing a room I had forgotten I owned.

* * *

I found shelter the way rats do: low, dark, and by instinct. A net shed down by the eastern slips, its door hanging on one hinge, its interior smelling of tar and dead fish and old rope. I sat down on an upturned crate among the coiled lines and put my head in my hands, and for perhaps ten minutes I allowed myself the luxury of being finished.

Finished. What a comfortable word. *It's over, Emrys. You lost. Go home. Plant something.*

And then—because I am apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone even when well enough alone is all that remains—I began, without meaning to, to recite.

"Private Alfred Dunn, Fourteenth Northumberland Fusiliers. Died the fourteenth of March, nineteen-seventeen. Gas gangrene following shell wound to the left thigh. Requisition manifest 4471-C, marked received, signed Corven Halloway. No morphine arrived. No carbolic. No clean dressings."

I stopped. My own voice sounded strange in the dark of the shed, echoing off the nets.

I said it again.

Because it was all still there. Every word. Not approximately, not in outline, but whole—the dates, the regiments, the wounds, the causes, and beneath all of it the manifest numbers, those lovely damning columns of figures I had copied out night after night from requisition chits and supply returns that crossed the ward desk, because some part of me, some part I hadn't consulted, had understood even then that a name alone is an accusation but a name *with paperwork* is a verdict.

Sergeant Milo Ketch, Second Royal West Kents. Second of April. Septicemia from a wound that should have been dressed within hours and wasn't, because the field station three miles back had no antiseptic, though manifest 4489-D recorded forty tins of it delivered and receipted. Signed C. Halloway.

Corporal Teodor Vasch, Serbian liaison attached to the dressing station at Ypres salient. Eleventh of May. Died of a wound in the abdomen that a man with proper supplies might have survived, though I confess I wrote him down mostly because the army had written him down nowhere at all—not as wounded, not as present, not as anything. He existed only because I said he did.

Private William Oakes. Private Henry Fenn. Private Thomas Rhydderch, whom we called Riddle because he could never answer a question straight. Lance-Corporal James Mowbray. Private Eamon Sutcliffe, who was seventeen and lied about it so badly that everyone pretended otherwise, and who died asking me to write to his mother and tell her the lie had worked, that he had been brave, which was true, and that he had been nineteen, which was not.

All of it. All of it was still in my head, arranged in order, dated and cross-referenced, as accessible as my own name.

I sat very still on the crate in the net shed and understood, with a sensation not unlike vertigo, what had actually happened at the quay.

Halloway had burned a book.

He had burned a book, and thought himself clever, and gone to bed—or wherever elegant men go—with his accounts balanced. But a book was never the evidence. A book is just a container, and containers can be seized. The evidence had walked out of his office on two legs, wearing a nurse's cape, carrying nothing but a hymnal and a letter sewn against her heart.

*I am the ledger.*

The thought arrived without drama. It didn't blaze; it simply settled, the way frost settles on a window overnight—one moment glass, the next a garden of white ferns that will not be wiped away.

* * *

I got up then, and my legs were mine again, and I began to pace the length of the shed, because the body needs to move when the mind changes gear. Out loud, in the dark, I tested myself. Names forward. Dates backward. Manifest numbers in their sequences—4471 through 4516, the spring convoy; 4603 through 4688, the summer scandal; every gap accounted for, every gap itself a kind of testimony, because a missing number means a destroyed document and a destroyed document means someone had something to destroy.

Two hundred and eleven names.

I had never counted them before. Isn't that strange? Four years of keeping the record and I had deliberately never totaled it, because a total is a weight and I wasn't sure my back could take it. Two hundred and eleven men whose deaths the official record had misplaced, misfiled, mislaid, or murdered outright—and every one of them was currently residing, rent-free and immortal, in the skull of a twenty-six-year-old woman sitting on a fish crate smelling of tar.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I let it, because there was nobody there to see a field nurse lose her composure, and composure, I have since decided, is overrated.

Here is what I understood in that shed, and I understood it completely, the way you understand a thing after a long illness finally breaks: they had taken the paper because paper was the only part of me they could reach. They could burn the pages. They could arrest Bell, search Idris's laundry carts, seal the harbor, bribe me with my brother's ghost. But they could not burn what I had done with four years of candlelit nights, which was to move the dead out of the archive and into the witness.

My brother's name had once been erased from a list. I had built my whole stubborn life on refusing to let that happen again, and I had built it so thoroughly, so obsessively, so *stupidly well*, that the army could confiscate my satchel and find nothing but lint and morphine, because everything that mattered was filed somewhere they had no jurisdiction over.

Grief, it turns out, makes an excellent clerk.

* * *

There was a knock at the shed door—the loose one, the hanging one—and I nearly went through the far wall. Then came the signal we had agreed on, or rather the signal Bell had invented and Idris had improved upon, which was two knocks, a pause, and then a tuneless hum of the first bar of "Abide With Me," delivered with the musicality of a foghorn.

Idris slipped inside. In the darkness her face was a pale smudge above her collar, and there was a mark along her jaw that hadn't been there yesterday—the shape of a question asked too hard by the wrong man—but she stood as straight as a lance.

"They've posted the harbor sealed until further orders," she said. "Quay office is manned double. Halloway's adjutant is checking papers against a list." She paused. "Your name is on the list, Emrys. Not for arrest. For *detainment pending transport*. Which sounds gentler and isn't."

"Then I'll need a different name."

"You'll need more than that." She lowered herself onto the crate opposite mine, and in the dim light leaking under the door I saw her hands, folded in her lap, steady as surgical instruments. "The mail ship sails at dawn. Quill is aboard already—came aboard tonight, posing as a late passenger, and he's asking questions in the smoking room that are making the purser sweat. He says whatever you have, he'll take it in shorthand, and he says—" she hesitated, and I realized Sister Idris did not hesitate often, and that when she did it was because the words mattered, "—he says shorthand travels faster than fire."

I almost told her then. It rose up in me like water finding a crack: *Idris, I don't need shorthand, I don't need paper, I need a dock and a voice and two hundred and eleven reasons.* But I held it, because a plan spoken twice is a plan halved, and because I wanted to be sure—truly sure—before I asked anyone else to stand near me when it happened.

Instead I said, "Where is Captain Wren?"

"That's the other thing." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register nurses use for news that must travel fast and quiet. "He wasn't at the barn. Bell went at midnight—got free of them somehow, God knows what it cost him—and the barn was empty. Cold ashes, bedroll gone. But there was a note pinned to the post, and it's addressed to you."

She passed it over. A scrap torn from a requisition pad, folded once. Even by feel I knew the hand before I opened it—that upright, disciplined script I had spent an evening verifying against old supply forms, ink from a man officially eight months dead.

I struck a match and read:

*Sister Vane — I have thought all night about courage and concluded I have been practicing cowardice in the name of prudence. You carried the names alone long enough. If the tide is truly morning, meet me at the harbor office steps at first light. I intend to sign my name where they can see it. I am told I remember things too. — A.W.*

Below, smaller, almost sheepish:

*Also I have your hymnal. I took it by mistake. It may prove useful.*

I read it twice. The match burned down to my fingers and I let it.

"Well?" Idris said.

I folded the note and tucked it beside the letter over my heart, where the paper evidence lived, though by then I understood the paper hardly mattered. Outside, far off across the black water, a buoy bell clanked once against the turning tide. Hours left. One dock. Two witnesses who remembered. A correspondent with a notebook. And a major who believed, with the serene confidence of a man who has never once been contradicted successfully, that the story died in a brazier tonight.

"He thinks he burned it," I said, standing, brushing tar and rope-fiber from my skirt. "He thinks what he burned was the evidence."

Idris studied me, and slowly, in the gloom, I saw her arrive at the same frost-fern understanding, because she had lost a son to those falsified manifests and she knew precisely what kind of arithmetic a grieving woman keeps in her head.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

"Two hundred and eleven," I said. "Every name. Every date. Every manifest number. In order. Backward if they ask."

Something moved through her face—grief and pride and a fierce, terrible gladness sharing the same small space—and she rose to stand beside me, and together we looked out through the gap in the shed door at the harbor, where the mail ship rode at anchor with her riding lamps lit, waiting for the tide like a held breath.

"Then we had better get you to that dock," Sister Idris said, "before the sun gives you away."

We went out into the dark together, and behind us the net shed kept its silence, and ahead the first gray suggestion of dawn was already gathering at the edge of the sea—as patient, and as inevitable, as a name that refuses to stay erased.
