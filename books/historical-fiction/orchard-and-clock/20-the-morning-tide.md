## 20. The Morning Tide

*Emrys*

The tide came in grey, the way most honest things do.

I stood on the quay at half past four in the morning with salt wind pulling my hair loose from its pins and nothing in my hands at all. No ledger. No letter. No satchel, no hymnal, no clever hiding places left to me. Halloway had taken everything an hour ago with the courtesy of a man returning a borrowed umbrella, and he had burned it all in a brazier while I watched, and he had smiled while it curled, and then he had let me go because he believed—oh, he believed with his whole elegant heart—that a woman without paper was a woman without proof.

He was wrong by exactly one memory and one stubborn man.

The mail ship sat low against the water like a patient animal, funnels breathing, lamps lit along her rail. Passengers were already boarding in ones and twos: civilians wrapped in coats too fine for this coast, soldiers with travel warrants, and one man standing apart from all of them near the gangway, hatless despite the cold, a leather writing-board under his arm and ink-stained fingers drumming against it.

Nathaniel Quill. Bell's last gift to us before they took him off the causeway—a name whispered through a cell door, a man who "prints things that make colonels weep."

Wren stood beside me, disguised until this moment as a stretcher-bearer and now disguised only as himself, which was the braver costume. He had shaved aboard a fishing boat somewhere in the dark hours. Without the stubble he looked younger and worse, if such a thing were possible—the face of a man who has been dead for eight months and has decided to stop pretending otherwise.

"They'll come," he said quietly. His eyes were on the harbor road, where lanterns were beginning to move. Not yet close. Coming.

"I know."

"When you start, don't stop. Whatever happens. Don't stop until he's written every name."

"Wren." I looked at him properly. "When did you decide?"

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked down the middle, but it was his. "Somewhere between the barn and here. I kept waiting for courage to arrive like reinforcements. It doesn't. You just run out of retreat." He flexed his right hand, the one that would sign. "You told me once that names deserve survival even when men don't. I've been dead eight months, Sister Vane. Time I found out what survives."

The lanterns on the harbor road were closer now. Six of them. Perhaps eight. Behind them, further back, the grey suggestion of mounted men.

I walked toward the gangway, and Wren walked beside me, and behind us the whole sleeping war began to stir.

* * *

*Wren*

I have faced artillery. I have led men over parapets into wire and machine fire. None of it—not one hour of it—required what this required: to stand still in the open, on a public quay, in full light of the ship's lamps, and wait to be seen.

Quill met us halfway. He was shorter than I expected and quicker in the eyes, the sort of man who reads a room the way other men read telegrams. He looked at me perhaps two seconds longer than politeness allowed.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said.

"I'm told the paperwork is excellent."

"Paperwork usually is. That's rather the point, isn't it?" He flipped open his writing-board, licked the tip of a pencil out of habit, produced a proper pen instead. "Bell's message said names, dates, manifest numbers, sworn statement. In front of witnesses. Before the ship sails."

"In front of as many witnesses as we can gather," Emrys said. She turned to the small crowd lingering at the foot of the gangway—stevedores, two naval petty officers, a customs clerk, a woman with a birdcage—and raised her voice in the flat carrying tone I remembered from ward tents, the voice that made orderlies move. "Gentlemen. If you can spare five minutes from your cargo, there is something here that ought to be heard. It concerns the supply lines at the northern sector. It concerns rations and morphine. It concerns how several hundred men died."

The birdcage woman stayed. The stevedores stayed. The customs clerk glanced nervously up the harbor road, saw the lanterns, and stayed anyway—which I noted, and have never forgotten. Courage is often just curiosity that refuses to leave.

Emrys opened her mouth and began to recite.

* * *

*Emrys*

I had feared I would need notes. I had dreamed, these past three nights, of standing here and finding the ledger gone from inside me the way it had gone from my hands—burned, scattered, blank.

But grief is a strange archivist. It files everything.

"Private Daniel Oakes, Second Wessex," I said, and my voice did not shake, which surprised me, because everything else did. "Died the fourteenth of March, of wounds untreated for want of morphia. Manifest 4-4-1-7, signed Corven Halloway, quartermaster division. The manifest records forty crates of medical stores delivered to the advanced dressing station. The dressing station received eleven."

Quill wrote. His pen moved fast and never stopped, and watching him write was like watching rain fall on a fire.

"Lance-Corporal Thomas Merrick, died the second of April, enteric fever aggravated by malnutrition. Ration returns for his company record full issues for February and March. The company ate half-rations for six weeks. Manifest 4-5-0-2."

"Sergeant Elias Brand. Private Wilfred Nye. Private Arthur Coble—" and so on, and so on, name after date after number, three hundred and eleven of them, each one carried up from the cellar of me where I had kept them lit like lamps, and the strangest thing, the thing I will tell no one else as long as I live, is that reciting them did not hurt. Reciting them was the first time since I began the second ledger that it did not feel like theft from the dead. It felt like payment.

The little crowd had grown. Dockworkers stopped rolling barrels. A chaplain came down the gangway and did not object. Somewhere a bosun's mate asked whether we'd be long, and someone answered, "Longer than you think, sonny," and there was a laugh in it, the first laugh, the kind that means a crowd has decided whose side it is on.

And up the harbor road, the lanterns arrived.

Eight men. Then twelve. They came at a walk, unhurried, which was worse than running—they moved like men who owned the ground and expected it to admit as much. At their head, in a greatcoat too good for the hour and a cap set at the angle of a portrait, rode Major Corven Halloway.

He dismounted twenty yards away and simply stood, listening, his gloved hands folded in front of him, wearing the expression of a patron at a concert who has not yet decided whether to applaud.

I did not stop. Wren had said don't stop. I didn't stop.

"Private Henry Lumm, died the ninth of May—"

"Sister Vane." Halloway's voice reached me across the quay, mild as morning tea. "This is irregular."

"So was the fourteenth of March," I said, and went on with Lumm.

* * *

*Wren*

I watched Halloway watch her, and I understood him finally, completely, in a way I never had across all those requisition tables. He was not angry. Anger would have been relief—he could have worked with anger. What he wore instead was the baffled patience of a man confronting arithmetic that refuses to balance. He had built his fraud out of the certainty that the dead keep no accounts. And here was a woman in a nurse's cape doing the books.

His adjutant leaned in and murmured. Halloway raised one finger—an orchestra conductor's gesture—and the twelve men halted.

Then he did the thing I had dreaded more than arrest. He walked forward alone, into the lamplight, into the crowd, and stood directly before Emrys, courteous as a dancing partner.

"—died the twenty-third of May, manifest 4-9-3-1—"

"Sister." He let the word hang. "You are distressed. You have clearly suffered a bereavement of judgment. Whatever grievance you imagine—"

"Captain Aldous Wren," Emrys said, louder, turning her head just enough to find me, "reported these discrepancies in writing on three occasions in the winter of nineteen sixteen. His reports were suppressed. His death was recorded in error—or not in error, Major, depending on how much you knew about that ambush and when you knew it."

Every head on that quay turned to me.

Halloway turned too, and for one unguarded instant I saw it cross his face—the true calculation, swift and naked: *he is supposed to be ash; ash signs nothing.*

I stepped forward. My legs behaved. I have wondered since what held them up, and I believe it was simply that three hundred and eleven names were holding the ground beneath me, and the ground does not buckle under a weight it is expecting.

"I am Captain Aldous Wren," I said, "late of the Fourth Battalion. I am officially deceased. I am also, as of this moment, extremely difficult to kill twice." A ripple went through the stevedores—laughter's grim cousin. I took the pen Quill offered. "Major, you once told me paperwork was the spine of the army. I intend to give it a spine."

Halloway's hand drifted, very slightly, toward his holster.

And the customs clerk—that grey, frightened, magnificent little clerk—said loudly, "There's a naval witness regulation, sir. Article Nine. A commissioned officer's sworn statement given before passengers and crew of a departing mail vessel can't be interrupted short of mutiny proceedings. My uncle wrote the interpretation. It's famous in our family."

It was nonsense. Some of it was even true. The bosun's mate produced a whistle. The chaplain opened a prayer book to, I suspect, a random page, on principle.

Halloway looked at the crowd. At the correspondent writing without pause. At the ship, whose captain had appeared at the rail with a telescope and the expression of a neutral power observing a border incident.

He looked, briefly, at me—and I swear there was a flicker there of something almost like professional respect, the look of one craftsman acknowledging another's finished work.

Then: "Arrest them all," he said quietly. "Charge: sedition and theft of military documents."

And nobody moved. Not the twelve men. Because twelve men, it turns out, are quite willing to commit sedition, but very few are willing to be seen committing it in front of a journalist, a chaplain, a ship's captain, and a crowd that has begun, horribly for the major, to take notes.

That was the moment I bent to the writing-board and signed my name—Aldous James Wren—in full view of everyone, and beneath it Emrys Vane signed hers, and Quill dated it, and the tide, which cares nothing for any of us, began to turn.

* * *

*Emrys*

They did not take us. That is the anticlimax I must report honestly, because I had braced for shackles and got, instead, a lawyer.

The ship's captain came down himself—a broad, weathered Northerner called Mabbs—and informed Major Halloway that the mail vessel departed on the tide regardless, that his passengers were protected persons under postal convention until they cleared territorial water, and that if the major wished to discuss the matter further he was welcome to file a complaint with the Admiralty, the Post Office, and God, in whatever order suited him. Two of Halloway's twelve men had already drifted backward into the crowd, the way soldiers do when a battle stops being a battle and starts being a story that might be told about them later.

Halloway remounted. He gathered his lanterns. And at the edge of the lamplight he paused and looked back at me, and what he said was not a threat at all.

"You memorized it," he said. Almost wondering. "Three hundred names. In your head. Like some kind of—" he searched, and failed, and settled for, "—nun."

"A clerk," I said. "The army taught me that much. Everything must be recorded, Major. You simply never specified where."

He rode away into the grey. I am told—though that belongs to another chapter, another telling—that they took him at his desk six weeks later, mid-signature, pen still wet. I hope the ink ruined the page.

The bosun called all ashore. Quill closed his writing-board with the reverence of a man closing a reliquary and shook my hand with ink still damp on his fingers.

"Every name?" he asked.

"Every name."

"Then it's already published, Sister. This was just the typesetting." He nodded up the gangway. "You're coming aboard. Both of you. Dead men travel free; I'll write it off as research."

Wren stood beside me at the rail as the ropes came off and the ship leaned out toward the open water, and the quay slid away with its lamps and its barrels and its brave ridiculous clerk, and neither of us spoke for a long time. There was nothing left to carry. For the first time in a year, my hands were empty and my head was not, and the difference felt like being let out of doors after a long illness.

"He'll have lawyers," Wren said eventually. "Friends in the ministry. He'll fight."

"Yes."

"And Bell—Idris—you—there'll be a price."

"There always was. We're only finding out now that it wasn't ours to pay." I looked at him. Eight months dead, and the dawn coming up on his face like a pardon. "Say your own name," I said. "Out loud. Once. Just to hear it work again."

He looked at the water a while. Then, quietly, like a man testing ice:

"Aldous Wren."

"It holds," I said.

Behind us, Nathaniel Quill was already writing, and ahead of us lay London, and a tribunal, and the long slow weather of justice—and in my pocket, where a ledger used to live, nothing at all, which was, at last, enough.
