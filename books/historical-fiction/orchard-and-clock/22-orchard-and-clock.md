## 22. Orchard and Clock

The trees went in crooked, and I have never once regretted it.

People assume a memorial should be straight—rows like soldiers, aligned like a parade ground, everything squared away the way the army likes its dead. But the army had its chance to arrange these men and it declined, striking their names from its ledgers as though omission were a kind of mercy. So I planted them crooked, at odd intervals, wherever the ground seemed softest and the light seemed kindest, and if you stand at the orchard gate in the late afternoon you can see that they lean toward one another. Trees do that, when you give them room. They lean toward each other the way men in a trench did, shoulder to shoulder, telling jokes about the mud.

There are two hundred and six of them. I know the number the way I know my own pulse.

* * *

The orchard belonged to a farmer named Hedley Prosser, who lost two sons at Passchendaele and half his fences to the same shell. When the tribunal ended—when Halloway was arrested at his desk, mid-signature, pen still wet, a detail I confess I treasure beyond all decency—there was talk of a subscription, a monument, a marble something in a town square where pigeons could do their business on the names of the dead. I said nothing at the meetings. I had learned, by then, that I say very little at meetings. But when it was over I went to Hedley Prosser and asked what he meant to do with the south field, the one where the old cider orchard had gone to rot during the war because there was no one left to prune it.

"Dig it out," he said. "Or let it rot proper."

"Let me plant it," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment, the way farmers look at people, which is longer and more thoroughly than anyone else looks at anything. "How many?"

"Two hundred and six."

He took off his cap, which I would learn was what he did instead of weeping. "You'll want help with the holes."

* * *

That was four years ago. The first trees—apples, mostly, with a few pears where the drainage turned sour—came as whips no thicker than my wrist, looking less like trees than like apologies. I planted the first one myself, in March, in ground still cold enough to ache through my gloves, and I said the name aloud as I tamped the earth: *Lieutenant James Corliss, 2nd Battalion, died the eleventh of October, 1917, of wounds that a proper stretcher-bearer and a proper dose of morphine might have made survivable.* The full record. Not the army's version—*died of wounds*—but the true one, the whole sentence, the way a name deserves to be said.

I said all two hundred and six that first spring. It took me eleven days, because I planted slowly and because some of the names caught in my throat like fishbones. Sister Idris came for the second week and read the names for the ones that stuck, her voice steady as a metronome, her hands steady as anything, though I knew what it cost her. Her son was the fourth tree from the gate. She had chosen his spot herself—under the rise, where the morning sun lands first—and she had not asked my permission, and I would not have dreamed of giving it. Some inheritances you simply take.

Tomas Bell came too, in the second year. He arrived without warning, the way he always did, mud to the knees and a grin that never quite reached his eyes, and he planted trees for three days without saying much of anything, and on the third evening he stood among the young rows and said, "You know I carried the manifests that killed some of these men."

"I know," I said.

"And you let me dig their graves anyway."

"They're not graves," I said. "That's rather the point."

He looked at me, and then at the trees, and something in his face let go—a knot untying that had been tied since the causeway, since the checkpoint, since the beautiful lie he told to save us all. "I never planted anything in my life," he said. "My father was a coal man. We planted nothing. There was nowhere to put anything down."

"There's here," I said.

He comes every spring now. He's better with a spade than I am, and he has stopped apologizing, which I count as progress, though I have never told him so. Forgiveness, I have found, is not a thing you hand a man like a receipt. It's a thing you let him earn back a shovelful at a time, and if he keeps digging long enough, one day he looks down and finds the ground has accepted him.

* * *

Aldous Wren does not come often, but he writes.

His letters arrive on the first of every month, in that unmistakable hand—upright, deliberate, the hand of a man who learned penmanship from a martinet and never unlearned it. I keep them in a tin box under my bed, along with the hymnal that carried his letter through the rail depot at Dusk-that-was, the hymnal with the hollow spine and the psalm I can no longer read without my heart doing something undignified. His letters are not cheerful. He is not a cheerful man; the war saw to that, and Halloway saw to the rest. But they are honest, which is rarer and worth more.

*The tribunal has closed its last file,* he wrote last autumn. *They gave me back my name the way one returns a borrowed umbrella—apologetically, and slightly the worse for wear. I am told there is a pension. I have not claimed it. It seems a strange thing to be paid for having been presumed dead. I would rather be paid for having been believed.*

I understood that. I understood it the way you understand a stone you have been carrying.

He came once, in the third spring, without announcing himself—I found him at the gate at dawn, hat in hand, thinner than the photograph in my memory, a scar along his jaw that the photograph had not earned. He walked the rows without speaking for the better part of an hour. Then he stopped at the tree nearest the chapel wall and said, "Whose is this one?"

"Sergeant Milo Ferris," I said. "Died the ninth of November, 1917. Shell fragment. The official list had him down as *wounded, returned to duty.* He didn't return to anything. He died in my ward with my hand on his wrist, and no one wrote it down but me."

Wren was quiet a long while. "I signed the list that erased him," he said at last.

"I know."

"You kept the count anyway."

"Somebody had to," I said. "The army's arithmetic had holes in it you could march a battalion through."

He looked at the young tree—three years in the ground by then, taller than him, its first real crop of apples hanging green and hard—and he said, "My sister has an orchard in Kent. I have not been able to stand in it since 1916." He touched the bark, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to see if it still hurt. "This one I can stand in."

He has been back twice since. He does not write ahead. I suspect he cannot bear to promise a visit and then fail to make it, and so he simply arrives, and the trees do not mind, and neither do I.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about peace: it is not quiet. I had imagined, all those nights in the ward with my notebook under the blanket, that if I ever survived—if the ledger reached its destination, if the tribunal did its work, if Halloway's elegant hands were finally cuffed to something other than a pen—then the noise would stop. The counting would stop. I would sleep like a civilian and wake without arithmetic in my head.

I was wrong. The counting never stops. It only changes what it counts.

Now I count buds in April, and I grieve the ones the frost takes. I count wasps' nests and decide which to leave alone. I count the jars of apples in the cellar—Hedley's wife, Mrs. Prosser, has taught me to preserve, and I am a mediocre student, and she tells me so with the frankness of a woman who has buried two sons and has no patience left for flattery. I count the years since my brother's name was struck from a shipping manifest in a port office, and I count the hour on the chapel clock, and I count, always, the two hundred and six.

The chapel stands at the top of Hedley's field, a small grey thing with a slate roof and a bell that hasn't rung properly since 1915, and on its west wall there is a clock that the village subscribed to have repaired after the armistice. It keeps imperfect time—it loses four minutes a month, and the man who maintains it, a retired schoolmaster named Mr. Fenn, refuses to adjust it more than twice a year on the grounds that "a clock that admits its fallibility is a more honest instrument." I have come to love it for exactly that. It is never quite right, and it never pretends otherwise, and it marks the hour all the same.

The hour it marks—the hour it has marked every afternoon of my life since I was nineteen—is twenty minutes past four. That is when my brother died. Not the date; the date I keep privately, in the tin box, in my own hand. The hour is public, in a sense, though only I know it. Every afternoon at twenty minutes past four, wherever I am in the orchard, the clock on the chapel wall ticks over, and I stop, and I say his name.

*Edmund Vane. Merchant seaman. Drowned the ninth of March, 1909, when the *Corinthian* went down off Ushant with a cargo manifest that said *general goods* and a hold that held something else entirely.* His name was struck from the crew list because the company could not afford the insurance implications of what the *general goods* actually were, and my mother received a compensation letter addressed to *the widow Vane* because the clerk could not be troubled to read the surname twice. She was not a widow. She was a mother, which is worse, and she died not knowing that anyone had ever written her son's name down correctly after that day.

I was nineteen. I read the corrected manifest in a shipping office in Cardiff—I had gone there to argue, nineteen years old and armed with nothing but a birth certificate and a temper—and I saw how easily a name could be made to vanish. Ink, paper, a clerk's indifference. That was the whole of the machinery. You did not need malice. You needed only for no one to be watching.

So I watched. For eight years I watched. In the wards, at night, under blankets, in a notebook that smelled of carbolic and candle smoke, I watched, and I wrote, and when the notebook was taken from me and burned before my eyes on a quay while a man in a well-cut coat smiled his well-cut smile, I discovered that the watching had gone somewhere the fire could not reach. Into the skull. Into the pulse. Two hundred and six names, and I recited them on a dock at dawn while a correspondent named Quill wrote them down in shorthand so fast his pen smoked, and Halloway's men came down the quay at the double, and the mail ship's hawsers came off the bollards, and it was too late, it was all too late for them, and the tide took the names to London the way the tide takes everything, faithfully, without opinion.

* * *

The tribunal sat for eleven months. I testified for three days. Wren testified for five. Bell, freed from his cell by the first writ the tribunal issued, testified for two, and at the end of it the president of the tribunal—a dry, elderly judge with the face of a disappointed heron—asked him if he had anything to add. Bell said, "Yes. I'd like the record to show that Emrys Vane never once asked me if I was worth trusting. She just made me worth it." The stenographer wrote it down. It is in the official record now, which means it will outlive all of us, which I suspect was the point.

Halloway was sentenced to fifteen years. He heard the verdict standing, immaculate, and I am told he thanked the court for its courtesy. I was not in the room. I had chosen not to be. I had spent enough hours of my life watching that man's face; I did not intend to give him another. Sister Idris went instead, and stood at the back, and when the sentence was read she did not smile and she did not weep. She told me afterward, "It was very quiet. Like weather coming. You wait so long for it that when it arrives it's almost ordinary." That is the truest thing anyone has ever said to me about justice.

Idris has her post back. They gave her a medal too, which she keeps in a drawer, because medals are for people who need reminding and she does not. Her son's tree gave its first full crop two summers ago—Bramleys, cookers, sharp and honest—and she made a pie from them and brought it to the ward where she works now, a convalescent home in the next county, and she told the young men there, "This is from a tree planted for a soldier who was left off the lists. Eat it and remember that you are all on somebody's list, whether the army knows it or not." They remembered. Young men always remember Idris. She has that kind of face.

* * *

This morning I was in the orchard at first light, because it is October and the Bramleys are ready and the wasps are drunk on the windfalls, and I went down the rows with a basket and a pair of shears, and I counted.

I always count.

*Corliss. Ferris. Ashdown. Prydderch. Okonkwo—* the tree that took three years to decide it liked English soil and then made up for it with a fury of blossom that stopped the village schoolchildren at the gate. *Whitlam. Doyle. The two brothers, Harl and Emmett Sedge, planted side by side because they had refused to be separated in life and I saw no reason to begin in death.*

Two hundred and six. I counted them the way I counted them in the ward at night—by touch as much as sight, my hand on each trunk in passing, a greeting, a roll call, a small daily parliament of the witnessed.

At twenty minutes past four I will stop, wherever I am, and the chapel clock will tick over, and I will say my brother's name into the ordinary air of an ordinary afternoon, and no clerk anywhere will strike it, because it is written now in more places than ink can reach. It is in a tribunal record in London. It is in a newspaper dispatch that Quill turned into a book—he sent me a copy, inscribed, *For the keeper of the count, who taught me what a byline is for.* It is in the tin box under my bed. It is in me.

The trees lean toward each other in the afternoon light. The apples come in crooked and abundant. Mr. Fenn's clock loses its four minutes a month and admits nothing and apologizes for nothing and keeps the hour regardless, which is, when you come down to it, all that any of us were ever asked to do.

Hedley says the orchard will outlive us all—trees are patient that way, they hold their ground for a century if you let them—and I believe him. Someday a child will climb the Okonkwo tree and never know why it leans, and eat a Ferris apple and never know the man, and that will be all right. That will be the whole point. The names do not need to be mourned forever. They need only to be kept, and keeping, I have learned, is a form of tending, and tending is a form of love, and love—unlike the army's arithmetic—does not strike anyone from its lists.

The light is going gold over the south field. The clock will mark its hour soon, and I will stop, and I will say the name, and then I will pick up my basket and go on down the rows, keeping the count, keeping the orchard, keeping faith—

—and this morning, for the first time in eight years, I noticed I was humming.
