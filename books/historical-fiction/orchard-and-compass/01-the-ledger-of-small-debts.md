## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The counting house smelled of lamp oil, cedar shavings, and the particular sourness of men who had been arguing about freight rates since before dawn. Marguerite Lockridge had been awake since four, which was not unusual, and had already balanced the November grain entries twice, which was.

"Your column's off by eleven cents," said Pieter Voss from the next desk. He did not look up. He never looked up when he accused her of things; it was a courtesy between them, like not mentioning the scar on his knuckle or the way she bit her thumbnail when a number refused to sit still.

"It isn't."

"It is."

She ran her finger down the column again — salt pork, 40 lbs., the Kestrel, $3.20; canvas, 12 yards, $4.80; and there it was, a barrel of tar entered at $1.89 that should have been $2.00. Eleven cents. She corrected it without comment and heard Pieter make the small satisfied sound he made when arithmetic proved him right, which was most mornings.

Eleven cents was nothing. Eleven cents was also everything, because in the Meridian Company's world nothing was nothing. Every cent was an entry, every entry was a debt, every debt was a small hook set in the flesh of somebody's life, and Marguerite had spent fifteen years learning exactly how deep those hooks went because half of them were set in her own.

Her indenture lay in the iron safe behind Mr. Calloway's office, folded into its oilskin envelope with the others: a single sheet of paper stating that Marguerite Lockridge, aged nine years upon signature by her late mother's creditor, owed the Meridian Trading Company fourteen years of labor at board wages, renewable per terms. Fifteen years served. One year remaining, as of the twenty-third of March. She could have recited it the way other people recited psalms. In some ways it *was* her psalm — the only text that had governed her whole adult life, longer than any friendship, longer than her memory of her mother's face.

One year. Three hundred and some days. She caught herself doing this arithmetic the way other clerks caught themselves humming — constantly, unconsciously, a tune worn into the floorboards of her mind. Days remaining against days owed. It never came out even. That was the trick of it. The company had built the ledger so it never came out even.

"You're doing the thing," Ada said, setting a tin cup of coffee at her elbow without being asked.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare through the paper like you can see the bottom of your own account." Ada Quill was twenty-six, sharp-faced, quick-handed, and the only person in the straits who could take dictation faster than Marguerite could audit. She pulled her chair around to the side of Marguerite's desk uninvited, which was also usual. "Drink your coffee before it goes bitter. It went bitter ten minutes ago, but drink it anyway."

Marguerite drank it anyway.

Through the tall east windows the strait showed itself in strips between the pilings of the warehouse: grey water, grey sky, the far island sitting low and blue-green on the horizon like something drowned but not yet admitted. On clear days you could see the smoke from the Sato boatyard chimneys. Today was not clear. Most days lately were not clear, and the company had posted a new notice on the customs door about registration papers for persons of foreign parentage, and nobody in the counting house mentioned the notice, and the silence around it had weight, like ballast in a hull.

* * *

At noon the household ate together in the back room of the clerks' quarters, as they did every day, though "household" was a generous word for what they were. Seven clerks, one cook, two apprentices, and Mr. Calloway presiding at the head of the table when he chose to sit among them, which today he did.

Old Tomas, the cook, had made fish chowder out of whatever the Kestrel had brought in, which meant mostly rock cod and apology. He ladled it with the ceremony of a man serving state dinner.

"Eat," he told Marguerite, piling her bowl fuller than anyone else's. "You've got cheeks like a heron."

"I always have cheeks like a heron."

"Yes, but today it worries me." He said it lightly, the way he said most true things.

Mr. Calloway cleared his throat and the table quieted, not from fear exactly — Calloway was not a frightening man, precisely — but from the trained deference of people whose livelihoods sat in his ledgers. He was fifty-odd, grey at the temples, with the patient hands of a man who had spent thirty years making small corrections. He had been factor here for eleven of Marguerite's fifteen years, and in all that time he had never once raised his voice, which everyone understood to be more alarming than if he had.

"Winter shipments close Friday," he said. "Storm system coming down from the north — the telegraph office gives it forty-eight hours, maybe less. After the bridge closes, nothing moves until thaw. I want the Kestrel loaded tonight and the grain manifest finished tomorrow morning." His eyes passed over the table and settled, briefly, on Marguerite. "Miss Lockridge, I'll want your audit on the season's accounts before we seal the strongroom. You're the only one who catches the tar errors."

A few heads turned. Marguerite felt the familiar heat at the base of her skull — praise from Calloway was a coin that always came with an edge. "I'll have it done."

"I know you will." He returned to his chowder. The conversation resumed around him like water closing over a dropped stone.

Under the table, Ada's boot found Marguerite's ankle. *He watches you*, the pressure said. Marguerite pressed back: *I know.*

It wasn't paranoia. Calloway watched everything; it was his profession. But lately his watching had developed a texture, a particular attention, the way a man reads a river differently once he suspects there's gold in it. Twice this autumn he had come upon her in the counting house after hours and lingered a moment too long before saying goodnight. Once he had asked — casually, over the manifests — whether she still corresponded with anyone across the strait.

*I don't correspond with anyone*, she had answered truthfully, because the truth was safer than any lie. What she did instead was keep a promise under a floorboard, and promises didn't show up in the mail.

* * *

The clerks' quarters stood behind the counting house, two storeys of salt-bleached cedar with a lean-to kitchen and a porch that sagged toward the water garden. Marguerite had lived in it since she was nine years old. She knew its sounds the way she knew the tides: Tomas's cough through the wall at midnight, the third stair's complaint, rain finding the loose flashing over the attic and drumming there until someone climbed up with pitch.

Her room was at the end of the upstairs hall, smallest in the house, chosen by her own insistence years ago because it was furthest from the stairs. Under the bed there was a floorboard that lifted. Under the floorboard there was a space lined with oilcloth, and in the space there was a brass compass in a rosewood case, wrapped in a wool sock, and beneath the compass a lock of hair tied with thread gone brown, and beneath that a woman's diving knife in a sheath of sharkskin, the blade oiled every month for fifteen years so that it would be ready on the day it was needed, though Marguerite no longer allowed herself to think clearly about what day that might be.

She did not go to the floorboard now. She thought about it, the way you think about a tooth. Then she took off her boots, lit the stub of candle she was permitted, and opened the private book she kept inside her ledger — a second ledger, hers alone, kept in her mother's shorthand of dots and strokes that no auditor in the coast district could read.

In it she recorded debts the Meridian Company would never recognize.

*Borrowed from Ada: two hours' mending, October. Repaid: one jar of salve.*
*Tomas: fed me first every night for fifteen years. Unpayable.*
*Emeric Sato: one compass. Held in trust. Interest accruing.*

That last line she had written when she was fourteen and had not changed it since, except to update the interest, which she calculated each year on the anniversary of the promise — a child's joke that had hardened into ritual. Fourteen years of interest now. A compass borrowed in peacetime, promised back when it was safe, and every year since less safe than the last.

The war fever had started three years back and had rolled over the coast like weather, changing the light. The island village across the strait — Emeric's village, her mother's people's trading partners for two generations — had become, in the language of the government notices, *a community of concern*. Their boats were counted. Their licenses reviewed. Men she had known all her life were spoken of in the counting house in lowered voices, as though distance alone could make strangers of them.

And Marguerite sat on this side of the water with their grandfather's compass under her floorboard, and said nothing, and audited barrels of tar, and was very good at her work.

She blew out the candle before the thought could finish forming, because thoughts that finished forming had consequences, and she was not ready. Not tonight. Tonight there was a storm coming and a grain manifest due and one year left on a piece of paper in an iron safe, and she lay in the dark listening to the wind come down the strait and told herself, as she had told herself for fourteen years, that there was time.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the sound of the bridge chains.

The tidal bridge was the strait's single crossing — a timber span on floats, winched open for shipping and closed against the tide, groaning on its chains like an old man getting out of bed. In winter they closed it altogether and the two shores became two countries. Marguerite stood at her window and watched the lamplight of the bridge crew moving along the span, and felt the season turn beneath her feet: the last boats going over today, the water belonging to storms after that.

Downstairs, the house was already stirring. Tomas had the stove roaring. Pieter stood at the window in his shirtsleeves looking north at a sky the color of wet slate.

"That'll hit by Friday night," he said. "Maybe Thursday."

"Then we'd best get the Kestrel loaded tonight," Marguerite said, and poured the coffee, and carried her cup to the table where her strange family assembled itself around her one by one out of the cold — and it was there, watching Ada argue with Pieter about rope weights while Tomas burned the porridge and swore in two languages, that Marguerite understood with sudden terrible clarity how much she loved these people, and therefore how much she had to lose.

The company owned her year. The government owned the water. The war owned the air everyone breathed.

But the compass under the floorboard belonged to a boy across the strait, and a promise, unlike a debt, could not be held in trust forever.

At her desk that morning, opening the strongroom and shaking out the season's accounts, she wrote the date at the top of the page in her neat auditor's hand, and beneath it, where no one would ever look, in dots and strokes only the dead could read:

*Interest due.*
