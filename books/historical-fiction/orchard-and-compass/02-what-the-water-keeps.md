## 2. What the Water Keeps

The tide went out at four o'clock and took the light with it, the way it always did in October, and Marguerite Lockridge stood at the counting house window with a pencil behind her ear and watched the strait turn the color of wet slate.

She had been watching the water for fifteen years. That was the arithmetic of her life: fifteen years of indenture to the Meridian Trading Company, entered in a ledger she had never been allowed to read, against a debt her father had signed before she was old enough to hold a pencil. Fourteen hundred and sixty-two weeks. She had done the division herself, once, late at night when the others were asleep — how many tides that came to, how many low waters, how many times the reef off the point had shown its black teeth and hidden them again. The company owned her days. The water kept everything else.

"Marguerite." Ada's voice from the back room, where the lamp burned over the shipping manifests. "The Kestrel manifest won't balance. There's forty pounds of salt pork unaccounted for."

"It's not unaccounted for," Marguerite said, not turning from the window. "It's on the *Petrel*. Calloway split the shipment on Thursday and never amended the entry. He thinks I haven't noticed."

"You've noticed."

"I notice everything." She said it lightly, but it was true in a way that sat heavier than a joke. Fifteen years of noticing. Fifteen years of entering other people's debts in columns so straight they could have been ruled by God, while her own account — what she owed, what she was owed, what she was worth as anything other than labor — appeared nowhere.

She turned back to the ledgers. The compass was under the third floorboard from the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth, and she did not look at it, because looking was a habit she could not afford. But she knew it was there the way you know a bruise is there. It had been there for five years now, through two inspections and one flood and Ada's endless sweeping, and every day it lay beneath the floor of the Meridian Trading Company like a small treason ticking away its own patience.

* * *

Her mother had gone into the water on a morning so clear the mountains stood up out of the sea like they were showing off.

Marguerite was eleven. She remembered that the way you remember the center of an explosion — everything else around it blurred, but that morning sharp enough to cut. Her mother had waded out at slack tide with the diving knife in her belt and the glass-bottom viewing box under her arm, and she had looked back once, and she had smiled, and Marguerite had thought: *she'll be back by noon, we'll have abalone, I'll grind the shells for mother's buttons.*

The reef channel ran deep that year. Deeper than usual, her mother said, which meant the pearls would be good, which meant the money would come, which meant maybe another season off the debt. Her mother dove the way other women prayed — with total attention, total surrender, and no expectation of being answered. She could read water the way Marguerite later learned to read ledgers: current, temperature, the color of the swell, all of it telling her exactly where she was and what the sea intended.

She did not come back by noon.

They found her on the second day, three miles down the strait, caught in the kelp off Sato Point. The tide had tried twice to give her back and failed twice, and on the third try it succeeded, and Marguerite had stood on the shingle with her father's hand heavy on her shoulder and understood, at eleven years old, something that took most people a lifetime: that the water does not keep things out of malice. It keeps them because keeping is what water does. Grief is just the tide refusing to surrender what it holds, and holding, and holding, until one day it gives the body back and keeps the rest forever.

What the water kept of her mother: the diving knowledge. The knife. The names of the reefs — she had named them all, in her own language and then in English for the traders' charts, and one of them she had named for her daughter, though no chart ever printed it. What the water gave back: nothing Marguerite could spend, except this — that she knew the strait better than any man in the trading post, better than the pilots, better than the patrol crews who came later. She knew where the channel ran deep and where it lied about running deep. She knew the hour the fog came off the water and the hour it lifted. Her mother had taught her all of it before she taught her letters, and the letters were the only part anyone had ever paid her for.

* * *

Emeric Sato came to the counting house on a Tuesday in June, the summer she was fourteen, carrying a crate of boat nails he was too proud to let her carry for him.

He was seventeen that summer, all elbows and sunburn, his forearms already scarred with the little white lines of a boatbuilder's apprentice. His father's yard across the strait built the fastest hulls on the coast, and everyone said Emeric would take it over, and Emeric always said nothing when they said it, which was his way of agreeing without committing to anything.

The war fever had started by then. You could feel it coming the way you feel weather — not in any single fact but in the quality of the air. Men in the post spoke louder. The newspaper used words like *infiltration* and *fifth column* about villages it had never bothered to name correctly. And the Sato family, whose boats every fisherman in the strait depended on and had depended on for thirty years, had begun to be spoken of in the conditional tense. *If they're allowed to stay. If things get bad.*

That was the afternoon it happened. Not a ceremony. Nothing either of them would have called important at the time.

Emeric set the crate down and didn't leave. He stood at the counter while she checked the nails against the invoice, and he kept looking at the window, at the strait, and finally he said, "My grandfather's compass. You know it?"

"The brass one. Your father keeps it on the mantel."

"My grandfather carried it across an ocean," Emeric said. "It's the only thing from before. My father says if it comes to confiscations, they'll take it first, because it's foreign-made and it looks valuable, and nobody will care that it's the only thing we have that's older than this country's interest in us." He turned the crate ninety degrees, not looking at her. "I want you to keep it."

She stopped writing. "Me."

"You're on this side. Nobody watches clerks." He said it flatly, without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "And you're—" He stopped himself, and Marguerite waited, and he didn't finish, and she was glad and sorry in equal measure that he hadn't. "Keep it until it's safe. That's all. When the fever passes, I'll come for it. Or if it doesn't pass — you bring it back yourself. Across the strait. Promise me."

The word *promise* hung between them over the counter, among the invoices and the boat nails, absurd and enormous.

"Emeric," she said, "if it comes to confiscations, crossing that strait will be—"

"I know what it will be." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were his grandfather's eyes, dark and patient and absolutely certain of their own ground. "You dive. Your mother dove. Everyone knows the Sato boats, but your mother knew the water better than my father does, and she taught you. Don't tell me you didn't learn it."

She had learned it. Every reef, every channel, every lie the tide told. Knowledge with nowhere to go, buried under fifteen years of salt pork and canvas and other people's debts.

So she said, "All right. I promise."

And that was the whole of it. No oath, no handshake even — he'd had tar on his hands. Just a boy and a girl and a brass compass passed across a counter like contraband, which is what it became, and a sentence spoken in peacetime that would take six years and a war to come due.

* * *

Now she stood at the window again, evening coming on, and looked across the strait at the island village.

From the counting house you could see the smoke of it rising thin and domestic above the tree line. You could see, on clear days, the roof of the boatyard, the long shed where the hulls cured. What you could not see from here — what you needed the government's new field glasses, mounted last month on the bluff in a wooden tower that had gone up in nine days without a single permit anyone would acknowledge — was the detail. The tower saw detail now. Two soldiers rotated through it in shifts. They watched the fishing boats go out and counted them going in. They watched the school. They watched the well.

Ada came to stand beside her, following her gaze. "They've got a searchlight on it now," she said quietly. "Did you see? Went up yesterday. Sweeps the water all night."

"I saw."

"Fishing boats can't put out after dark anymore. Half the village works the night sets." Ada said it carefully, the way she said everything that mattered — lightly, watching Marguerite's face as she said it. "Mr. Okada rowed over Tuesday to file a protest with the factor and was told protests are now considered 'unpatriotic correspondence.' Whatever that means."

"It means the words have stopped meaning anything," Marguerite said. "It means they can arrest you for the shape of a letter."

Ada was quiet a moment. Then: "You've been strange lately. Since the bridge closed. Since the regulations." She wasn't accusing. She was offering — an open door, held patiently, the way Ada held everything. "Whatever it is, you could—"

"There's nothing." Marguerite made herself smile. It cost more than the smile should have. "Ledgers don't leave room for strange."

Ada looked at her a moment longer, and Marguerite felt the floorboard under the hearth like a heartbeat under her feet, and Ada said, "No. I suppose it doesn't," and went back to the lamps.

Marguerite stayed at the window until the smoke across the water thinned and vanished into the dark, until the searchlight came on and began its slow, idiot sweep of a strait it could not possibly understand. Somewhere under her feet, wrapped in oilcloth, a brass needle pointed north, faithful and useless, toward a country that had decided her friend's family was made of enemies.

Five years ago it had been a promise. Tonight, standing in the lamplight of the company that owned her, listening to the tide come in over the flats, she understood it was becoming something else. A summons. A debt entered in a ledger no clerk kept, accruing interest all this time, waiting for collection.

Three days later, a fisherman named Brandt would press a folded paper into her hand at the dock and walk away without meeting her eyes, and the promise would arrive in two hands she recognized, asking to be honored.

But that was three days off. Tonight there was only the water, and the light sweeping it, and the ache of a life measured entirely in entries not her own — and somewhere across the black strait, a family counting its remaining nights the way she counted weeks, one by one, toward zero.
