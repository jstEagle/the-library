## 3. The Bridge Season

The storm came up from the southeast on a Tuesday, the way storms did, and by Wednesday morning the bridge was shut.

Marguerite heard it before she understood it — the deep, arrhythmic boom of surf finding the pilings at full tide, a sound like a drum beaten under the floor of the world. She stood at the counting house window with her ledger open and watched the tidal bridge disappear into spray. It was less a bridge than an argument between engineering and tide: a timber causeway laid across the shallows at low water, usable six hours out of every twelve, closed without ceremony whenever the sea decided to reclaim its territory. In summer it ran traffic all day. In winter it might open twice a week. By February, not at all.

"Bridge season," Ada said behind her, setting down the tea tray with the particular finality of a woman delivering bad news along with the sugar. "You know what that means."

"Everything ships now or waits for spring."

"Everything ships now." Ada poured. "Calloway wants the Kestrel loaded by Friday and the warehouse counted to the nail. He said—" she paused, savoring it, "—he said, and I quote, 'I want this place so empty by December first that a mouse couldn't find a crumb.'"

"He didn't say that."

"He absolutely did. Then he sneezed into his handkerchief and pretended he hadn't."

Marguerite laughed despite herself. That was Ada's gift — she could take the temperature of a room and report it as comedy. They had shared the attic room above the counting house for nine years, since Ada arrived at seventeen with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for shorthand that made her indispensable to men who believed no woman could spell. Between them hung an understanding never once spoken aloud: that the Meridian Company had given them each other, if nothing else that belonged to them.

Outside, the strait was building itself into something serious. Whitecaps in the middle passage already, and the light sweeping from the government station on the point — the new light, installed in August, its lamp turning with a slow patience that Marguerite had come to think of as hostile. It swept the water, swept the far shore, swept the water again. The island village lay directly in its path, four miles across, close enough on clear nights to see smoke rising from the Sato boatyard chimneys.

She had not seen smoke from there in eleven days.

* * *

By Thursday the rush was on, and the counting house became a machine with everyone inside it.

Mr. Calloway worked from six in the morning, his office door open because closed doors invited questions and Calloway hated questions more than he hated inefficiency. He was not old — fifty perhaps, though he carried himself like a man who had been fifty for thirty years — and he was not unkind, which was the trouble. Cruelty you could hate cleanly. Calloway's kindness arrived wrapped in obligations, and by the time you found the string, the knot was around your wrist.

"Miss Lockridge." He appeared at her desk at mid-morning with a shipping manifest rolled in his fist. "The winter consignment for Fort Alder goes on the Kestrel. I want your audit on it before she sails — every crate, every weight, matched against entry. If anything is short, I want to know before the customs man does, not after."

"You'll have it tonight."

"I'll have it by four." Not rudeness; arithmetic. He knew her speed better than she did. He turned, then stopped. "There will be a household meeting at seven. All clerks. Attendance isn't optional."

"New regulations?"

Something crossed his face — quick, quickly gone. "Yes."

He walked back to his office and Marguerite looked at the manifest and did not see it. New regulations. She had been reading the word *regulations* in company correspondence since July, always attached to the island village, always growing longer. First: registration of all residents of enemy-alien heritage. Then: restrictions on boat movements after dark. Then: confiscation of wireless equipment — as if the Satos, who owned two rowboats and a half-built schooner, had been running a telegraph station out of their loft.

Her mother had told her once, hands rough with salt, holding Marguerite's face between them: *The sea doesn't care whose side you're on. Remember that when men start caring.* Marguerite had been twelve. She had thought her mother meant weather.

* * *

At four she delivered the audit. At five the rain started in earnest, horizontal, driven, the kind of rain that made the whole coast feel like the inside of a wave. At seven the clerks assembled in the long room — fourteen of them, from gray-bearded Hollis who had kept the fur ledgers for forty years, to young Peter Finch, nineteen and homesick, who still wrote to his mother in Derbyshire every Sunday.

Ada stood beside her. Old Marta the cook hovered at the door with the pretext of checking the stove. Calloway stood at the head of the room with a paper in his hand, and beside him — and this was new, this made the skin go cold on Marguerite's arms — stood a man in a government uniform, civilian-cut but unmistakable, with a leather case and the flat patient look of someone being paid to wait.

"You'll forgive the theater," Calloway began. His voice was dry, apologetic, exactly calibrated. "Most of what I have to say concerns you very little. But Mr. Reeve has come over from the district office, and it's better you hear these things from me than read them on a posted notice."

Reeve nodded, saying nothing, establishing nothing.

"Effective Monday, the following measures apply to all persons resident or trading within the strait district. One: the tidal bridge will operate under permit only. No crossing without documentation, and documentation will be issued through this office." A rustle went through the room; half the clerks lived on the mainland side and crossed daily. "Two: all vessels belonging to persons registered as enemy aliens are subject to seizure without compensation. Three: correspondence to and from restricted zones may be opened and inspected. Four—" Calloway paused, and Marguerite saw him choose the words the way he chose entries, precisely, each one defensible, "—any employee of the Meridian Company found conveying goods, documents, or messages to restricted persons without authorization will be considered in breach of contract and liable to prosecution under the wartime measures act."

The silence had texture. Rain against the windows. The light from the point sweeping past, once, twice, painting the room in slow stripes.

Peter Finch raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Sir — does that mean letters? Personal letters?"

"It means everything, Mr. Finch."

"But my mother—"

"Write to your mother," Calloway said, and for a moment his voice lost its ledger-flatness. "Write whatever you like. Understand that other people may read it. That's the whole of the new world, I'm afraid: write as though you're understood to have nothing to hide, or don't write."

Hollis, from the back, grumbled: "And the village folk? The ones across the water? Half our trade comes through their boats. What are we to tell them?"

"You are to tell them nothing," Reeve said, and it was the first time he'd spoken, and his voice was mild and terrible. "You are to conduct company business per company instruction. The restricted community has been notified through official channels."

*Official channels*, Marguerite thought. A notice nailed to a post in a language half of them read badly, posted by men they had learned in fifteen years of good faith not to fear. The fishing families had traded across this strait for three generations. The Satos had built half the boats on both shores. And now a stranger with a leather case used the phrase *restricted community* as though he were describing a disease.

She became aware that Calloway was looking at her. Not staring — Calloway never stared — but his attention rested on her the way a finger rests on a line of figures he intends to return to. She kept her face doing what it always did: attentive, neutral, slightly tired. Inside, something was moving fast, the way water moves under ice.

The meeting broke up. Clerks filed out into the rain, voices too bright, jokes too quick. Ada squeezed her arm. Marta pressed a heel of bread into Peter Finch's hand because he looked pale. Small kindnesses, moving underground, beneath the regulations like roots beneath a road.

Calloway caught Marguerite at the door.

"A moment." He waited until the room emptied. "The audit was clean."

"It usually is."

"It usually is." He looked out the window at the strait, at the light sweeping it. "Miss Lockridge, I'm going to say something once, and then neither of us will ever refer to it again. You've been with the company fifteen years. You're the best auditor I've had, including the men I paid three times your wage. Whatever happens with these new measures — and things will happen, I regret to say, things I won't be able to soften — remember that this office runs on precision and discretion. Both of which you have in excess." He turned to her. "Whatever else you may be carrying, set it down. For your own sake. Do you understand me?"

It was as close to an accusation as kindness could get. *Whatever else you may be carrying.* Did he know about the compass? Could he know? No — impossible. The floorboard was under the third shelf in the records annex, under twenty years of dead accounts, and no living soul had seen her lift it except—

Except no one. Except her mother, who was dead, and Emeric, who was four miles away and forbidden.

"I understand you, Mr. Calloway," she said.

"Good." He almost smiled. "Go home. Dry off. Eat Marta's stew before Finch finishes it."

* * *

That night the storm peaked. Marguerite lay awake in the attic listening to the building groan and thinking about crossings.

Her mother had taught her the strait the way other mothers taught prayers. *The reef channel runs south of the kelp line; at slack water you could walk it in places, at flood it drowns; the patrol boats never watch it because no fool would try it.* Her mother had known because her mother had dived it, pearl-shell and abalone, alone, in the years before the company hired women only for shore work — before the year the water took her. Marguerite had been thirteen. The last thing her mother said about the sea, the actual last thing, said laughing on the dock with rope burns on her palms: *It keeps what you give it. Give it nothing you can't lose.*

Across the strait, invisible tonight, the village sat dark under its new status. Emeric's father would be registering his boats this week. Handing them over, maybe. Or hiding the papers — the yard deed, the title to the *Kestrel's* little sister schooner — somewhere a confiscation order couldn't reach.

Somewhere like a compass case. Somewhere like a floorboard in a trading post owned by the very company enforcing the seizures.

Marguerite turned her face to the wall. The rain eased toward dawn, and the light on the point swept and swept, patient as a creditor.

* * *

Friday the bridge reopened for six hours and the company fed itself through the gap like a flood through a sluice. Crates went over two hundred strong; the Kestrel took on coal and salt beef; Ada requisitioned, Hollis certified, Marguerite audited until her eyes swam. At noon she stood on the dock watching the last cart cross, and saw, among the few foot passengers hurrying the planks before the tide took the causeway back, a figure she knew — old Brandt, the fisherman, canvas hat pulled low, walking with the deliberate casualness of a man carrying something in his coat.

He did not look at her. He passed within six feet of her on the gangway, and his hand brushed his coat pocket, once, deliberately, the way a man touches a thing to confirm it's there.

Their eyes met for half a second. He looked away first.

The tide closed over the bridge. The light on the point swept the emptying water. And Marguerite Lockridge stood on the company's dock with her heart going hard and strange, knowing that whatever was folded inside that fisherman's coat would be in her hands before the day was out — and that once it was, every regulation announced in the long room, every careful kindness of Mr. Calloway, every wall going up around the people she loved would have her name written somewhere on the other side of it.

Three days, she had thought. Now it was three hours.

She went back inside to finish the audit, and her hands, for the first time in fifteen years of perfect entries, were not entirely steady.
