## 4. A Letter in Two Hands

The fisherman came on a Thursday, which was the day the Meridian Company bought cod and therefore the only day his boat had any business at our dock.

I knew him by his oilskins before I knew him by his face — a broad-shouldered man named Tomaq who fished the northern strait and sold to us at a fair price because he couldn't afford not to. He'd been coming since my first year in the counting house. He had never once asked for credit.

"Ledger says you're owed eleven cents from the October overage," I told him, counting it out onto the scarred oak counter. "And there's a bonus of two bits for the size of that last haul."

He didn't look at the coins. He looked past me, at Ada sorting invoices by the window, at young Pell sweeping the back room, at the ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning. Then he looked at me, and something passed across his face like weather.

"You've got a package," he said. "From up the strait."

"We don't take private freight," I said. It was true. It was also the wrong thing to say, and we both knew it.

"It's not freight." He set a wrapped parcel on the counter — small, flat, heavy with the particular weight of oiled canvas folded around paper. "It's addressed to M. Lockridge. That's you?"

The whole room seemed to lean in. Ada's pen stopped. Pell's broom went quiet against the floorboards.

"That's me," I said.

Tomaq pushed the parcel across the counter with two fingers, the way you might push something that could bite. "Came aboard at the narrows," he said, loud enough for anyone listening. "Drifting. No name on the boat it came from. You want it or not?"

"I want it," I said.

"Cod price is going down this winter," he added, still loud. "Tell your factor."

"I'll tell him."

He took his eleven cents and his two bits and left. The door swung shut behind him, and the counting house exhaled, and I stood there holding a piece of oiled canvas as if it were a live coal.

Ada appeared at my elbow without seeming to cross the room. She was twenty-six, four years older than me, sharp-faced and kind-eyed, and she could read a man's silence better than most people read print.

"Drifting parcels," she said. "In November."

"The sea gives gifts."

"The sea gives dead fish and drowned men." She kept her voice light, pitched under the noise of Pell's broom resuming. "Open it upstairs. Not here."

Upstairs was the clerks' quarters — three rooms above the counting house where six of us slept, ate, argued about chess, and knew every sound each other made. But it was also mine in a way the counting house never could be, and Ada was right: whatever was in that wrapping did not belong to Mr. Calloway's sight lines.

I waited until midday, when the household emptied for the meal shift and I claimed one of my invented headaches, which were becoming less invented by the week. I sat on my bunk with the door wedged shut and unwrapped the canvas with fingers that would not stay steady.

Inside were two sheets of paper.

The first was in a hand I didn't know — blocky, careful, the letters of someone taught to write late and determined to do it well. It read:

*To the clerk at Meridian post. This comes by Tomaq, who asks nothing. The boy Emeric asked me to carry it and I said yes because his father carried my father out of the surf thirty years ago and no debt like that dies. Burn this after reading.*

That was all. A stranger's letter, carried through water and risk, asking nothing but that I burn it.

The second sheet was in a hand I knew better than my own.

Emeric Sato wrote the way he did everything — quickly, leaning hard into the downstrokes, as though the words might escape him if he let up. I hadn't seen that handwriting in five years. I hadn't seen him in five years either, and the last time had been at the autumn market, before the war fever came, when we had still been able to stand three feet apart in a crowd and pretend we were strangers.

He began without greeting, as though greeting were a luxury the page couldn't afford.

*Marguerite. If you are reading this then Tomaq got through and I am sorry for what it cost him and sorrier for what it may cost you. I will be quick because quick letters survive and long ones get read aloud in courtrooms.*

*They took Father on Tuesday. Four men in government coats, very polite, very thorough. They say he is being questioned about the boatyard — about who built what for whom, and whether any hull that left our slip ever carried anything it shouldn't. You know as well as I do what the answer to that is: boats carry things, that is what boats are for. But they have decided the question first and are working backward to the evidence, and evidence is wherever they say it is now.*

*The boats are seized. All nine. Chained at the company wharf with red tags on them like carcasses. Mother is allowed to keep the house for now but they have posted notice that inventories begin next month, district by district. Ours is near the end of the list, which means we have perhaps six weeks before strangers walk through our rooms writing down everything my grandfather ever earned.*

*Here is what I am asking, and I need you to hear that I have turned it over forty ways and cannot find another shape for it:*

*Grandfather's compass. The brass one with the cracked face. You remember — you held it the night of the festival and said it pointed wrong and Grandfather laughed and said it points where it likes.*

*It is not in the house anymore. You know where it is. I gave it to you because I was fourteen and frightened and the war was coming and I believed — I still believe — that some things survive by being given away. I have never once regretted it. But the confiscations reach Mother's house in six weeks, and after that any paper with our name on it belongs to the government, and there is more than sentiment in that compass case. There is a thing inside it that must not fall into their hands, and if it does, it won't just be lost. It will be used. Against us. Against everyone whose name appears on it.*

*I am asking you to send it back. Not bring it — I know what the bridge rules are now, I'm not a fool, and I would never ask you to cross. Wrap it, weight it, give it to Tomaq on a buying day. He'll run it up the strait at night. That's all. That's everything I ask.*

*If it can't be done, then it can't, and I release you from the promise. I mean that. What you've carried these five years was never yours to protect and I had no right to make it so. But if it can be done, do it soon. The lists move faster than the tides.*

*Yours in the old way,*
*Emeric*

I read it twice. Then a third time, sitting very still on the bunk while the noon bell rang below and the household clattered back in, and my headache became entirely real.

There was a thing inside the compass case.

Five years I had kept it under the floorboard beneath the third stair of the back hall, wrapped in waxed cloth, and in five years I had never once opened the case itself. It wasn't mine to open. That had been the whole architecture of the promise: I hold what isn't mine, unopened, until the world becomes safe enough to return it. I had honored the letter of it so completely that I had missed what lived inside it.

I thought about the night he gave it to me. We were fourteen, standing on the shingle below his father's boatyard, and the war fever had just begun — the newspapers full of dark words about the island villages, the talk in the market gone brittle. Emeric had pressed the compass into my hands and closed my fingers over it with both of his, and his hands were shaking.

"When it's safe," he said. "When the fever passes. Bring it home."

"What if it doesn't pass?"

"Then bring it home anyway."

I had thought he meant the compass. I had spent five years thinking he meant the compass.

* * *

The floorboard came up the way it always did, silently, a skill I'd practiced until it was perfect — wedge the knife point, lift from the nail-end, ease the board free, lower it back flush. Beneath it, the waxed cloth bundle lay in its nest of cedar shavings, dry and undisturbed, patient as a seed.

I carried it to my room and set it on the blanket and sat looking at it for a long time.

Outside, the strait was doing its gray afternoon work, pushing the tide through the narrows, rocking the company boats at their moorings. Somewhere north of that water, nine seized vessels wore red tags. Somewhere beyond them, a man who built beautiful hulls sat in a government room answering questions that had already been answered. And somewhere in between, a fisherman named Tomaq was gutting his catch and keeping his mouth shut, having already paid more than I had.

I unwrapped the cloth.

The compass was smaller than memory made it. Brass, green with salt bloom at the hinges, the cracked face webbed like ice on a puddle. His grandfather's hands had worn the casing smooth on two sides, and I found myself turning it over and over, learning those worn places with my thumbs, feeling like a thief even though I was the keeper.

The case opened with a click that sounded enormous in the empty room.

Inside, where a spare needle or a spare wick might live in any sailor's instrument, there was a folded packet of papers, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with fishing twine. My hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange part — all the trembling of the last hour gone, replaced by the flat clarity my mother used to call diver's calm: the body deciding, below the level of thought, that panic spends air.

I untied the twine.

Deeds. Three of them, and a bill of sale, and a registration certificate bearing stamps I recognized from the counting house files — legitimate stamps, government stamps, the kind that could not be forged because I myself had helped verify hundreds of them. The deed to the Sato boatyard. The deed to the family house. The title papers for the *Kestrel*, the flagship hull old man Sato had built with his own hands. Every document a confiscation order would hunt for, gathered into one place years ago and hidden in the one object nobody would think to inventory.

Not hidden. *Given.* Given to a girl on a cold shingle beach, by a boy who understood before any of us did what was coming, and who chose to trust her with the entire legal existence of his family.

He hadn't asked me to keep a keepsake. He had asked me to keep the proof that his family existed.

I wrapped the papers back exactly as they'd been, closed the case, and sat with it in my lap while the afternoon died outside the window. The arithmetic assembled itself without my permission, the way ledger figures do.

Sending it by Tomaq — that was the plan Emeric offered, the safe plan, the plan that required nothing from me but a parcel handed over on a public dock. Except Tomaq had already carried one letter through guarded water. The patrols on the strait had doubled since the seizure; Calloway had shown me the new schedule himself, almost proudly, two weeks ago. A second run by the same boat, same route, same month? The odds weren't odds. They were a countdown.

And even if Tomaq got through — the compass would arrive at a house awaiting inventory, in a village where every stranger was watched, into the hands of a family whose men were already being taken for questioning. A parcel could be intercepted. A parcel had no judgment, no speed, no mother's knowledge of the reef channels.

A person carrying it did.

I put the compass under the floorboard again, board flush, shavings settled, and went downstairs with my heart going like a pump in a flooded hold.

* * *

Supper that night was beans and salt pork and Pell's ongoing grievance about the chessboard, which someone had knocked off the shelf and chipped. Ada kept glancing at me. I kept not looking back, which fooled no one, least of all her.

Afterward, when the others drifted to the common room, she caught me at the wash basin and spoke low, without turning her head.

"Tomaq's parcel."

"Inventory records," I said. "Private correspondence from a supplier."

"Mm." She scrubbed a pot with the concentration of a surgeon. "Marguerite. When you decide whatever it is you're deciding — and you're deciding something, you've had the look since noon — remember that the back stairs creak on the fourth step and I'm the one who oils them."

My hands went still in the water.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's what makes it dangerous." She handed me the pot to dry. "Goodnight, M. Lockridge."

I stood at the basin long after she'd gone, drying a pot that was already dry, and listened to the tide come up the strait in the dark. Six weeks. The lists moved faster than the tides.

Somewhere north of that black water, a promise made in peacetime was waiting to be kept, and every day I spent calculating was a day the government spent walking toward a brass compass with a cracked face.

I had told Emeric, at fourteen, that I would return it when it was safe.

The time for safe was over. The time for true had begun — and I did not yet know that the man who owned my debt was, at that very hour, drafting new security regulations with my village's name in the margin.
