## 5. The Factor's Kindness

Calloway had learned long ago that a man could tell more from the way someone held a pen than from anything they wrote.

He stood in the doorway of the counting house with his coat still wet from the walk up from the dock, watching Marguerite Lockridge work through the morning's entries. She sat very straight, as always — she had the posture of a woman who had been corrected once and never needed correcting again — and her hand moved down the ledger column with a fluency that made most of his other clerks look like they were carving their numbers into stone. She didn't check figures twice. She checked them once, thoroughly, and then trusted herself, which was rarer than talent and worth more.

Fifteen years he'd watched her do this. Fifteen years of watching a girl arrive at fourteen with salt still in her hair and her mother's debts transferred onto her name, grow into the best auditor on the coast, and never once ask for anything she hadn't earned.

Which was exactly why the letter on his desk troubled him.

It had come three days ago, forwarded from the district office, one of a dozen circulars about the new security regulations. This one concerned correspondence. Any letters passing through company channels to or from persons resident in the restricted zone across the strait were to be logged, opened where practicable, and reported. The language was careful. The language was always careful now. *Persons resident in the restricted zone.* As if you could restrict a village the way you restricted a warehouse, lock it and post a notice and consider the matter administered.

Emeric Sato lived in that zone. Emeric Sato had lived there since Calloway had first come north twenty years ago, back when the boy's father built boats on the far shore and the two communities crossed the tidal bridge so often that the planks wore smooth in the middle. Calloway remembered those years. He remembered buying cedar planks from Sato's yard because Sato's cedar was better and cheaper and honest, and he remembered Marguerite Lockridge, fourteen years old and already keeping accounts for her mother's diving crew, standing on the bridge in summer with her sleeves rolled, arguing prices with a boatbuilder's son who argued back.

He wasn't sure anyone else in the company remembered that. But he did.

And last week, when the fisherman Okada had come in with his catch and his manifest, Calloway had noticed — because noticing was the whole of his profession — that Okada had spoken to no one, sold his fish to the buyer, signed nothing, and yet somehow, between the dock and the gate, had paused near Marguerite's window. Had paused, turned slightly, said something too low to carry, and moved on.

She hadn't written anything down afterward. That was what bothered him. Everything went into her ledgers. Everything. If a crate arrived short two bolts of canvas she entered it; if a customer paid a day late she entered it; if the cook borrowed flour she entered it, because Marguerite believed — had told him once, in her flat, unanswerable way — that a debt unrecorded was a debt that grew teeth. And here was a conversation at her window, and no entry, and a look on her face afterward that Calloway recognized because he had worn it himself once, thirty years ago, over a different sea: the look of a person carrying something they have decided not to put down.

* * *

He spent the afternoon pretending to audit the winter requisitions, which meant he spent the afternoon thinking.

There were perhaps six people in the world whose loyalty Calloway would have sworn to without checking the books, and Marguerite Lockridge was one of them, though he'd have resigned before saying so aloud. Not because she was efficient — plenty of clerks were efficient, in the way that a millstone is efficient. Because she was exact. In fifteen years she had never padded an expense account, never flattered a customer, never rounded a number in her own favor by so much as a centime. He had tested her twice early on, quietly, the way you test any instrument before trusting it, and she had caught both tests and looked at him with an expression that said she understood perfectly well what he'd done and would not insult them both by mentioning it.

That was the trouble with instruments that good. You came to depend on them. And the government, in its present temper, had begun asking questions that depended on answers Calloway couldn't afford to get wrong.

The district agent — a young man named Pell, all starch and ambition — had visited twice this month, walking through the counting house with his hands clasped behind him like a man touring a museum of things he intended to confiscate. On the second visit he had stopped at Marguerite's desk, read her name off the duty roster, and made a small note.

"Lockridge," he'd said. "Related to the diver? The one who drowned out past the reef?"

"Cousin," Calloway had said, before Marguerite could open her mouth, and had immediately hated himself for the ease of it. It wasn't true. Her mother had been no cousin of anyone at Meridian; she'd been an independent diver who fell into debt the way divers did and drowned paying it. But "no relation" would have invited more questions than "cousin" closed off, and so Calloway had lied to a government agent on behalf of a clerk, smoothly, without deciding to, and understood in the same moment that he was already compromised — that whatever he thought he was protecting, he had chosen it over the regulations before the regulations had even asked.

Pell had smiled and noted the word *cousin*, and gone on down the row of desks, and Calloway had stood there feeling the shape of the trap he was building around himself and everyone under his roof.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not stop doing: if Marguerite was corresponding with the Sato family — if she had ever corresponded, if there was a single letter in a floorboard or a pocket — then she was, by the government's current definition, handling enemy communications. And the company that employed her was harboring it. And Calloway, who had spent thirty years building a reputation as the one factor on the coast whose books could survive any inspection, was one discovery away from losing everything he had, plus the livelihoods of nine people who called the clerks' household home.

Against which stood a simpler fact, one he kept trying to subtract out of the sum and could not: he did not believe Marguerite Lockridge was capable of treason. He believed she was capable of loyalty so stubborn and so badly aimed that the government might mistake it for treason, which was worse, because it would destroy her just as thoroughly while leaving her convinced she'd done right.

* * *

At six he sent the others home and asked her to stay.

She came into his office the way she did everything, without wasted motion, and stood with her hands folded — waiting, patient, faintly wary. Lamplight did her no favors; it showed the gray coming in early at her temples and the lines around her eyes that she'd earned honestly, entry by entry, year by year.

"Sit, Miss Lockridge."

"I'd rather stand, Mr. Calloway."

Of course she would. He sat himself, and poured two glasses of the decent whisky he kept for suppliers and emergencies, and set one in front of her, and she looked at it the way she looked at unsolicited discounts — politely, and without touching it.

"There's a matter I want your opinion on," he said. "Professionally."

"All right."

He slid the circular across the desk. She read it fast, the way she read everything, but he watched her eyes slow at the middle paragraph — *correspondence originating from or directed to persons within the restricted zone* — and he saw the stillness come into her, the particular stillness of a woman counting very carefully.

"The company has obligations under this," he said. "You understand that."

"I understand that."

"And the company's obligations are carried by its people. Its clerks." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the blotter. "If correspondence of that kind passed through this office — or through any office adjacent to this office — the person best positioned to recognize it would be the person who reads everything anyway."

Her eyes came up. They were steady. They were always steady; that had never once been evidence of calm.

"You're asking me to report on the mail."

"I'm asking you to be thorough. Thoroughness has always been your virtue."

"For fifteen years you've paid me to count crates."

"And I've never regretted a shilling of it. Which brings me to the second matter." He reached into the drawer and took out the paper he'd drafted that afternoon, rewritten four times, and laid it beside the circular. "Your indenture has eleven months left. Eleven months of wages owed against the principal, plus interest, plus the fees — you know the schedule better than I do, God knows you've audited it yourself. It comes to a figure. I've taken the liberty of having it calculated."

She didn't look at the paper. She looked at him.

"The company proposes to buy out the balance," he said. "Today. Effective immediately. You'd hold free papers by Friday."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark the tide was turning against the pilings, that long groan the old building made every night, like something shifting in its sleep.

"Why?" she said.

Because I'm fond of you. Because you're the finest mind this company has ever employed and I've watched you spend fifteen years paying for a drowning. Because if Pell finds what I think he might find, I'd rather owe you freedom than complicity. Because I am offering you the only version of mercy the law still permits me, and I hate that it comes shaped like a transaction, and I am going to say none of this.

"Because the company values thoroughness," he said. "And thorough people should belong to themselves."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the answer there is."

She stood there for a long moment, and he watched her do the thing she always did — run the sums, weigh the entries, look for the line hidden below the line. He knew what she'd find. He wanted her to find it, some part of him wanted her to find it, because a clean deal between two people who respected each other was the last decent thing left on this coast and he was trying to give her one.

But her face didn't soften. If anything it went quieter, and quietness, in Marguerite Lockridge, had always been the sound of a decision being made somewhere he couldn't audit.

"If I found such correspondence," she said carefully, "you'd want it brought to you. Not to Agent Pell."

"To me first. Yes."

"And what you did with it after would be your judgment."

"My judgment within the regulations."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Within the regulations," she repeated, tasting each word like a coin she suspected of being clipped. Then she picked up the whisky, finally, and drank it down neat, and set the glass on his desk with a small precise click, closing the transaction whether he'd closed it or not.

"Thank you for the drink, Mr. Calloway. Good night."

She went out. He heard the outer door, and then the smaller door to the clerks' household, and then nothing but the tide working at the pilings.

* * *

He sat alone for an hour with both papers in front of him — the circular and the buyout, the stick and the carrot, though he'd meant it as neither. He'd meant it as kindness. He turned the evening over and over and could not make it lie flat.

Here was what gnawed at him, past midnight, lamp burned low: she hadn't refused. A guilty woman would have refused, or blustered, or asked why he was asking. Marguerite had listened, weighed, accepted the terms like a professional accepting terms — which meant either that there was nothing to find and she saw no harm in humoring an old man's caution, or that there was something to find and she intended to decide, herself, privately, how much of it he deserved to see.

Both possibilities frightened him, and for opposite reasons, and he could not determine which fear was the honest one.

He thought of her at fourteen on the bridge, arguing prices with the Sato boy, and how she'd won, and how the boy had laughed — actually laughed, delighted, as if losing to her were a kind of gift. Calloway had watched that from the end of the bridge with a manifest under his arm and thought, idly, *that girl will go far,* and given it no more weight than a man gives any pretty scene on any ordinary evening.

Now the government had drawn a line down the middle of the strait and posted men on it, and every ordinary evening of twenty years was suddenly contraband.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark the building groaned against the tide, patient, enormous, indifferent, holding its ground between the water and the shore, which was, he supposed, what he was doing too.

On Monday, he decided, he would walk past her desk at the usual hour and watch her hands on the pen. A man could tell more from that than from anything written down.

He only hoped — and the hope surprised him with its size, its nakedness, its complete uselessness as business strategy — that whatever she was carrying, she would let him help her put it down before someone else made her drop it.
