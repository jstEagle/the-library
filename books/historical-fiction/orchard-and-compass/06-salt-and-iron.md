## 6. Salt and Iron

Her mother had taught her to read water the way other mothers taught daughters to read scripture: patiently, with the certainty that salvation lived somewhere in it.

*The strait lies to itself twice a day,* she would say, kneeling at the tide table nailed inside the counting house's back door, her finger moving across the columns like a finger across a rosary. *Slack water is a door. It opens for twenty minutes, maybe thirty if the moon is generous. You go through the door or you wait for the next one. The sea does not negotiate.*

Marguerite had been nine. She had thought her mother was talking about fish.

Now she stood before the same tide table — newer paper, same nails, the company's brass tack stamped over the corner where a diver's thumbprint had once worn the varnish away — and understood that her mother had been talking about everything.

She copied the figures onto scrap in her clerk's hand, small and exact, the hand that had entered ten thousand debts without error. Spring tide, four days out. The moon three days past new, rising late, which meant darkness early and long. She wrote *reef channel* and then, feeling the absurdity of writing it where any clerk might see, scratched it out and wrote instead *the low field east of the oyster beds,* which meant nothing to anyone but her.

Except it did mean something to one person, and that person was dead, and Marguerite was beginning to understand that the dead keep ledgers of their own.

* * *

She rehearsed the crossing the only way she could rehearse it from shore: with her eyes closed, standing in the dark of the coal shed after the household slept, her hands moving through the sequence her mother had drilled into her against a day that had come anyway.

The reef channel ran between Gull Rock and the oyster beds, a seam of deep water no wider than a skiff's beam in places, unmarked on any chart the Meridian Company kept because the company had no use for water it couldn't freight across. Her mother had named its turns for her when she was small enough to be carried home from it asleep: the First Gate, where the current bent south around the rock; the Needle, where the channel narrowed and the water went glass-still even in weather; the Long Room, a stretch of open green water beyond the reef where a swimmer could stand on nothing and see both shores at once.

No patrol boat watched it. Patrol boats watched the bridge, the harbor mouth, the ferry lane — watched the places where men who drew government wages believed crossings happened. The reef channel was a woman's route. It had always been a woman's route. Her mother had taken it at dawn with a knife in her belt and pearls in her mouth, and the patrols had never once looked down into it, because looking down required believing there was something worth finding below the surface of ordinary days.

Four days. Slack water at 3:40 by the almanac, though the almanac lied by as much as ten minutes this far north, and her mother had taught her the correction — watch the kelp. When the kelp off Gull Rock stood straight up, trembling like a held breath, the door was open. When it lay flat and streaming east, the door was shut and would stay shut whether you knocked or not.

She rehearsed it until her arms ached from strokes she wasn't taking, until the cold of the coal shed got into her shoulders and she could feel the water already, the black cold of the strait in November that killed a strong man in eleven minutes and had let her mother live thirty years in it because her mother had never once fought it. *You don't swim across the strait,* she'd said. *You agree to be carried, and you steer.*

Marguerite opened her eyes in the dark. Her breath made a small ghost in front of her.

Somewhere across that black water, Emeric Sato was waiting for a compass she had promised to return fifteen years ago, and the tide didn't care about either of them, and that was the mercy of tides: they kept their accounts without malice.

* * *

"You've put salt in the tea again," Ada said.

Marguerite looked down at her cup. She had. The blue crockery bowl of salt sat where the sugar usually sat, because Ada had moved the sugar Tuesday and Marguerite hadn't noticed anything had changed in the world since Tuesday except the moon.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize to me, apologize to Mr. Prewitt, who took a full swallow before he noticed." Ada sat down across from her in the clerks' kitchen, which was really just the back end of the counting house with a stove in it, and studied her the way she studied an invoice that wouldn't balance. "Third time this week you've gone somewhere. Not out. Somewhere *in.* I can't find you in your own face lately, Maggie."

"I'm tired. End-of-quarter."

"It's October. Quarter-end is December." Ada turned her cup a quarter-turn, the way she did when she was deciding how much truth a moment could hold. "Mr. Calloway asked me yesterday if you'd seemed unwell. I told him you'd been working too hard on the Kestrel account, which was a lie, because there is no Kestrel account. I invented a ship for you. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I committed fraud on your behalf before breakfast."

Marguerite felt the guilt arrive like cold water in her boots. "Ada—"

"I'm not asking." Ada raised one hand, flat, the gesture of a teller closing her window for the day. "Whatever it is, you'll tell me or you won't, and either way I've already decided where I stand, so asking would only embarrass us both. But eat something. And stop putting salt in things."

She got up, put bread and dripping in front of Marguerite without being asked, and went back to her ledgers humming something tuneless, and Marguerite sat in the warmth of a kindness she could not explain herself out of and understood that this was the worst part — worse than the cold water, worse than the patrol boats, worse than Calloway's careful eyes.

The worst part was that she was lying to people who fed her.

* * *

The household noticed in its separate ways, the way a body notices weather.

Old Prewitt, who had kept the company's correspondence since before Marguerite was born and who now mostly kept the stove, remarked loudly at supper that the tide tables were misfiled, someone had been at them, and glared in no particular direction while saying it. He re-filed them himself every morning thereafter, and every morning they were slightly wrong again, and every morning he corrected them with a sigh that said more than he knew.

Young Fenwick, sixteen and all elbows, caught Marguerite counting under her breath during the evening prayers Prewitt insisted on — she was calculating tidal intervals against the clock on the mantel, lips moving — and stared at her with the naked alarm of the young, then said nothing, then began, inexplicably, doing his own arithmetic exercises at the same hour every night, which gave her cover she neither requested nor explained.

Even Mrs. Okafor, the cook, whose domain ended at the kitchen door and whose silence extended considerably further, set aside the liver and onions one Thursday and served smoked fish instead, saying only, "Keeps well. If a person were traveling." Then she went back to her pots as though she had said nothing at all, which she had, and which everyone heard, and which no one acknowledged.

That was the thing about the clerks' household that Marguerite had never been able to teach herself to see plainly until now: they were all of them fluent in the language of things not said. Fifteen years of living above a counting house, of salaries entered and debts deducted, had taught them that the truest entries were the ones made in margins, in silences, in a plate of smoked fish set down without comment.

They were covering for her. All of them, each in their own small currency, without knowing what crime they were bankrolling. Or maybe — and this was the thought that kept her awake after the lamp was out — maybe they suspected enough to know, and chose not to know, and the difference between those two things was a knife-edge she couldn't locate.

She lay in her narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the house settle and thought: *I am going to do this alone, and it will be the loneliest thing I have ever done, and they will make it less lonely without my permission, and I will never be able to repay them.*

Her mother would have said: *Then don't repay them. Carry it. That's what carrying is.*

* * *

On the third day, Calloway found her at the tide table.

She heard his step behind her and did not turn — turning quickly was a confession; she'd learned that much watching arrested men. She finished writing the figure she was entering, capped the inkwell, squared the page.

"Miss Lockridge." His voice was mild. It was always mild. Mildness was how the Meridian Company bit. "The Sato confiscation inventory came through from the government office this morning. I've set it on your desk. It wants auditing before Friday."

Something went through her chest like a boat through thin ice.

"The Satos' assets," Calloway continued, watching her with those pale, patient eyes, "are being catalogued as enemy property. Boats, tools, holdings. There will be a line item, I expect, for personal effects. The family will want their inventory accurate. Companies are often careless with other people's property." He paused. "You're precise with other people's property, Miss Lockridge. It's one of the reasons I value you."

"Yes, sir."

He stood a moment longer, and Marguerite understood that he had come to say exactly what he'd said and not one word more, that the whole speech was a key turned slowly in a lock to see if it would catch — and then he left, and she stood at the tide table with her heart going like a hammer on wet planking.

Because she had looked at the word *Sato* and her face had done something. She didn't know what. But Calloway had seen it. Calloway saw everything, that was his profession, seeing was what the company paid him for — and he had handed her the inventory not because it wanted auditing but because he wanted to watch her audit it.

She walked back to her desk on legs that belonged to someone else.

The inventory was there, tied with government string. She untied it. Page two, line fourteen, in a government clerk's indifferent hand:

*One (1) mariner's compass, brass cased, engraved — seized as material evidence, held pending disposition.*

Held. Pending. The compass was not in Emeric's mother's house. The compass was in a government strongroom, or a patrol office, or an evidence locker, and every hour she spent rehearsing slack water and reef channels was rehearsal for a crossing with nothing to carry.

Marguerite sat very still. Outside, the strait glittered, indifferent, keeping its own books. Four days to spring tide. Three, now.

And somewhere between the government office and the strongroom, in some crate or drawer or evidence ledger, was the only thing in the world she had ever promised anyone — and she had just been handed, by the factor himself, the first official document telling her exactly where it was kept.
