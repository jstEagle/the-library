## 7. Inventory of a Household

Ada Quill had been cleaning the counting house for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about dust: that it settled thickest where secrets were kept, that it told the truth about which rooms went unused, and that it could be persuaded to lie, if you swept it properly, about who had been where.

She was thinking of none of this on the morning the government men arrived. She was thinking of linseed oil.

They came up from the dock at nine o'clock sharp, two of them, in grey coats too good for the weather, carrying a leather folio stamped with an eagle she didn't recognize. Mr. Calloway met them at the door himself, which he never did for anyone. Ada watched from the washroom doorway, a rag in one hand and a pail at her feet, wearing the particular invisibility that came with her position. A cleaning woman was furniture. Furniture heard everything.

"Meridian Trading Company," said the taller of the two, reading from his folio as though confirming the building's existence rather than its name. "By order of the Crown, we're conducting a full inventory of assets held in trust. Records, ledgers, correspondence, and any property belonging to persons designated under the Alien Restrictions Act."

"You'll want the strong room," Calloway said. His voice had gone flat and courteous, the voice he used with suppliers who tried to renegotiate contracts. "I'll have my senior clerk open it."

Ada kept her eyes on the floorboards and moved her pail out of their path. The tall one stepped around her without looking. The shorter one — younger, with ink-stained fingers and the restless eyes of a man who counted things for a living — glanced at her once, catalogued her, dismissed her.

Furniture, she reminded herself. Be furniture.

But her heart was going like a hammer on wet canvas, because *records*, and *correspondence*, and *property belonging to persons designated* — those words had a shape, and the shape fit someone she knew.

* * *

They spent the morning in the strong room with Hollis, the second clerk, who unlocked it with hands so steady they might have been someone else's. Ada carried tea in at ten and again at noon, and both times she made herself look at nothing while seeing everything: the tall agent running his finger down ledger columns, checking names against a list; the young one opening drawers and weighing papers in his hand as though suspicion had a specific gravity; Calloway standing by the door with his arms folded, watching them handle his books the way a man watches strangers hold his children.

The company's records were clean. Of course they were clean. Marguerite Lockridge had audited those books for fifteen years down to the halfpenny, and Marguerite did not leave loose entries lying about like unfastened buttons. By two o'clock the agents had found nothing but exemplary bookkeeping, and their disappointment had begun to show in the way the young one slammed drawers.

It was the correspondence that nearly undid everything.

"Outgoing letters, incoming letters, filed by date?" The tall one tapped the pigeonholes along the strong room's west wall.

"Every letter since 'ninety-one," Hollis said.

"And before?"

"Burned annually. Company policy." Calloway's voice didn't change at all. "Paper is expensive. Storage is more so."

The tall agent looked at him for a long moment, and Ada, standing in the doorway with her tray, saw something pass between the two men that was not quite hostility and not quite respect. Then the agent nodded, and wrote something in his folio, and the moment was over.

They left at four. But at the door, the young one turned back. "We'll return monthly," he said. "Unannounced. The designation covers all property connected to restricted persons, wherever found." His eyes went past Calloway's shoulder, into the counting house proper, into the long room where the clerks' desks stood and the great iron safe squatted and the floorboards ran in their patient rows toward the sea-facing windows.

Wherever found, Ada thought. She scrubbed the doorstep that evening until her knuckles bled, and thought about *wherever found* until the lamplighter came.

* * *

Marguerite was not at supper.

That wasn't unusual — she kept late hours in the audit season, and October was always audit season — but tonight Ada noticed it the way you notice a missing stair. The household ate in the long kitchen behind the counting house: Hollis with his head in yesterday's shipping gazette, young Peters telling a story about a seal that had climbed onto the dock and stolen a mackerel off the line, old Mrs. Vane the cook ladling out fish stew with the economy of a woman who had fed clerks through three recessions and one smallpox scare. The lamp hissed. The rain worked at the windows. It should have been an ordinary night.

Ada couldn't taste the stew. She kept hearing the young agent's voice. *All property connected to restricted persons, wherever found.*

After the plates were cleared, she took her candle and went back through to the counting house, telling herself she was only going to bank the fire. The fire needed banking. That was true. It also gave her a reason to be in the room alone, moving along the desks, straightening the blotting paper, doing the small nightly rituals that kept a place feeling inhabited.

Marguerite's desk was the third from the window. Ada had dusted it eleven years. She knew its habits the way she knew her own hands: the scratch in the oak where Marguerite tested her nibs, the ink ring permanently ghosted into the wood, the drawer that stuck unless you lifted the left side. And beneath the desk, near the wall, there was a floorboard that sat a hair proud of its neighbours. Ada had noticed it years ago and assumed rot. Rot was everywhere in a tidal building; the sea got into everything eventually.

Tonight, sweeping, she caught the edge of the board with her broom and it rocked. Not rot, then. Not nailed down the way the others were. She stood over it for a long moment, candle low, rain loud on the glass.

She should leave it. She knew she should leave it. Whatever was under a loose floorboard in a counting house during a season of confiscations belonged to whatever person had put it there, and prying was not love.

She knelt anyway.

Her fingers found the groove worn into the board's edge — worn smooth, deliberately, by years of the same grip in the same place. Someone had opened this many hundreds of times. She lifted it the way you lift a sleeping child's arm, carefully, apologetically, and set it aside, and looked down into the dark.

A brass compass lay in a nest of oilcloth. Old brass, greened at the seams, heavy-looking even in the candlelight, with a hairline crack across the crystal and initials engraved on the case back that she couldn't read from above. Beside it, wrapped separately, a folded square of paper gone soft with handling — a letter, or had been once. And under both, filling the cavity's end, a slim knife with a bone handle and a blade that still held light.

Ada put the board back. She didn't touch anything else. Her hands were shaking, which annoyed her profoundly, because she had buried a husband and raised a sister's son on a widow's pension and never once shaken over money or death. But this — this was different. This was a secret with teeth.

She banked the fire. She took her candle upstairs. She lay in her narrow bed in the clerks' house listening to the rain and the tide turning under the pilings, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all day.

Restricted persons. Enemy aliens. The village across the strait, fenced now with new regulations, its boats impounded, its men questioned. Emeric Sato's family, though nobody in the household spoke the name aloud anymore. A boy Marguerite had known before the war fever, before any of it — Ada had pieced that much together over fifteen years of shared bedsit walls and shared suppers, the way you piece together a coastline from passing ships.

A compass hidden under a floorboard. Property connected to restricted persons. Wherever found.

If the agents came back — and they would come back, monthly, unannounced, the young one had promised it with his ink-stained fingers drumming the doorframe — and if they ever pulled up that board, they wouldn't ask Marguerite questions first. They'd simply take her. And everyone who shared her roof, her table, her silence, would be taken a little way along with her. Mrs. Vane. Peters. Hollis. Herself.

Ada turned her face to the wall.

Here was the thing she understood now, lying awake at forty-six with rain in her ears: knowing was not neutral. She had spent her whole life believing that a clean conscience meant clean hands. But she had lifted that board. She had seen what lived underneath. From this night forward she was no longer a woman who knew nothing; she was a woman who knew and said nothing, and that was a cargo she would carry in the same hull as Marguerite, whether Marguerite ever asked her aboard or not.

And Marguerite hadn't asked. That was the part that lodged in her like a fishbone. Fifteen years, and the girl had trusted her with everything except this — had decided, somewhere in that precise, closed interior, that Ada's safety required Ada's ignorance. As if ignorance were a gift. As if being spared were the same as being loved.

She could confront her. She could demand the truth, make herself co-signer instead of accessory-by-accident. Or she could say nothing and let the weight stay where it lay.

In the morning, Marguerite came down late, pale, with the look of a woman who has been calculating tides in her sleep. Ada set the porridge in front of her and watched her stir it without eating, and thought: *she's planning something, and it's going to get her drowned, and I'm already in the boat.*

"I'll need the front room scrubbed Thursday," Marguerite said, not looking up. "Company surveyors coming."

"Thursday," Ada said.

"And Ada—" For a moment Marguerite's eyes came up, and behind all that careful bookkeeping there was something raw and quick, a bird against glass. "If anyone asks. The last few weeks. Where I've been, what I've been doing. You haven't noticed anything."

It wasn't a question. It was worse than a question. It was an instruction delivered as a confidence, asking her to lie before she'd been told the truth.

Ada picked up the porridge pot. "Noticed what?" she said.

Marguerite held her gaze for three seconds — Ada counted them, the way she counted everything — then looked down and began, mechanically, to eat.

That night Ada added a line to her own private ledger, the one no auditor would ever see. *One compass, one letter, one knife, known.* Under it, after a long time staring at the ceiling, she wrote the entry that frightened her most, because it was the only one she couldn't balance:

*One friend, in danger, who thinks I don't see. Debt outstanding.*

Outside, the tide turned. Somewhere across the black water, in a guarded village full of confiscated boats, a boy's mother was waiting for a letter that had already been read by other hands — and in four days, by Ada's own rough count of the almanac hanging in the kitchen, the spring tide would come.

She didn't know yet what Marguerite intended. But she knew the shape of preparation when she saw it — the extra oilcloth appearing in the stores, the questions about skiff requisitions, the almanac pages gone soft at the corners from handling. She had cleaned this house for eleven years. She knew what was missing before it was missed.

Thursday, she told herself, scrubbing the front room windows until they shone. Thursday she would decide whether love meant keeping the secret or sharing it.

Then she remembered the young agent's parting glance at the floorboards, and scrubbed harder, and knew that Thursday was already too late to choose innocence. There was only the choosing of sides now — and hers had been made silently, kneeling in the dark over a loose board, the moment she put it quietly back and said nothing at all.
