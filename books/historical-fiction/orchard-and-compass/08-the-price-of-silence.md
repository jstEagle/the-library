## 8. The Price of Silence

They took Hollis on a Tuesday, between the second bell and the tea things.

Marguerite heard it before she understood it — boots on the counting house stairs, too many boots, the particular cadence of men who have been given permission. Then the door opened without a knock and two of them came in wearing government grey with brass buttons gone dull from salt air, and behind them, hands cuffed in front of him like a man being shown a card trick, walked Hollis Fen.

Hollis, who was twenty-three and sang off-key while he reconciled freight manifests. Hollis, whose mother sent him dried apples every autumn and whose handwriting slanted left because of a childhood fever. Hollis, who had sold a sack of flour to Mrs. Okada at half price last month and entered it in the ledger as spoilage.

That was all. That was everything.

"Meridian Trading Company," said the taller of the agents, producing papers with the flourish of a man who enjoyed his own authority. "We're detaining this employee for questioning regarding irregular transactions."

"Irregular transactions." Calloway had come out of his office and stood with his hands flat on the counter, and Marguerite, watching from her desk by the window, saw that his knuckles were white against the wood even though his voice was perfectly level. "Define irregular."

"That's what the questioning is for, sir."

"He's been in my employ four years. His accounts balance to the cent. I'll want his books before you go."

"You'll get copies when the investigation concludes—"

"His *books*, Mr. Agent. This is a chartered company. The charter predates your office. I don't release company property to anyone carrying a warrant that doesn't name the property."

There was a silence in which Marguerite could hear the tide turning under the dock pilings, the long slow exhalation of the strait. The agent looked at Calloway, and Calloway looked back, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with law: the old arithmetic of men who both served powers larger than themselves and knew precisely how much each of them mattered.

The agent surrendered the ledger. He did not surrender Hollis.

At the door, Hollis turned. He looked not at Calloway but at the clerks' desks — at Ada, frozen with a pen above an invoice; at young Prewitt, who had gone the color of tallow; at Marguerite. And he smiled. It was a terrible thing, that smile, apologetic and brave and meant to comfort them, as though *he* were the one imposing on *their* morning.

"Don't let my fern die," he said. "It's on the windowsill. It likes to be turned."

Then the door closed and the boots went down the stairs and Marguerite sat very still with her hands in her lap and thought: *a smaller kindness than mine.* A sack of flour. Half price. Entered honestly, even — he hadn't even lied well — because Hollis could no more falsify a ledger than fly, because four years of Calloway's discipline lived in his spine. He had simply written *spoilage* where he should have written *charity* and trusted that no one would read closely enough to care.

Someone had read closely enough to care.

* * *

The household gathered that evening in the kitchen behind the counting house, which is where all decisions in that building had ever truly been made. Mrs. Tarn, the cook, kept the stove hot whether or not anyone ate, on the principle that grief and deliberation both require warmth. Ada sat with her arms crossed tight, Prewitt with his head nearly in his hands, and old Mr. Voss, the retired bookkeeper who boarded with them and had outlived three governments, sat by the fire sharpening a pencil that did not need sharpening.

"They'll hold him forty-eight hours," Calloway said. He had come down from his office, which he almost never did; he stood by the stove looking, Marguerite thought, older than his fifty years, older than the building itself. "Possibly seventy-two. They're not charging him yet because they don't know what to charge him with. Selling below cost isn't a crime unless they decide kindness is contraband, and they haven't quite worked up the nerve to say so in writing."

"Then we testify," Ada said. "We go in and we say what he is. Four years, balanced books, a good man. Character evidence. It counts for something."

"It counts for something," Calloway agreed. "And everyone who gives it gets a file."

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water.

"A file," Prewitt repeated. "You mean — they write your name down."

"I mean they write your name down, and the name goes into a drawer in Victoria, and the drawer stays there after this war fever passes, because drawers are the one thing governments never give back. You'd be marked. Not charged, not punished. Marked. There's a difference, and the difference is that marking waits."

"And if nobody testifies?" Marguerite asked quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She had spoken from her corner without planning to, the question rising out of her like water finding its level.

"Then Hollis stands alone," Calloway said. "And stands a fair chance of being made an example of, because examples require witnesses and he has none willing."

"The Okada woman will speak for him."

"Mrs. Okada cannot enter the building where the hearing is held. Her presence requires a pass. Passes are issued at the discretion of—" He stopped himself. "No. She can't."

"So it's us or no one."

"It's us or no one. Yes."

Voss spoke then, from his chair, in the mild voice of a man who had watched empires come and go and found most of them tedious. "In 'eighty-five, in the salmon trouble, they arrested a cannery foreman for feeding striking workers. I testified. Cost me nothing at the time. Cost me a promotion I'd been promised, ten years later, so quietly I couldn't even prove it was the testimony. That's how it works now. Not the cell door. The slow door. The one that opens just slightly less often than it used to."

"We're not doing this to protect our promotions," Ada said sharply.

"No," Voss said. "We're doing it because Hollis turned his fern toward the light every morning and called it a day's work done. But I'd have you understand what you're buying. Kindness has a price list. It's just never posted where you can see it."

Silence again. Marguerite looked around the kitchen — at Ada's set jaw, at Prewitt chewing his thumbnail, at Mrs. Tarn stirring a pot she wasn't cooking — and understood that they were each privately calculating, the way she had once calculated tides for a crossing, exactly what their names in a drawer would cost. Prewitt had a sweetheart in town whose father distrusted him already. Ada had a sister. Mrs. Tarn had a son on the mainland she hadn't seen in six years because passage cost more than wages allowed.

Survival, Marguerite thought, was a debt that mounted interest in other people's names.

"I'll testify," she said.

"Marge—" Ada started.

"I'm already marked. Or near enough. An indentured clerk with no family and no prospects has very little left to lose, and what little there is, is less than Hollis's freedom." She said it lightly, and hated herself for how easily it came, how practiced the self-erasure sounded even to her own ears. "I'll go first, actually. If they see the company's senior clerk standing up for him, maybe it gives the others cover. Maybe it makes it look like conviction instead of conspiracy."

"That's not how files work," Calloway said.

"No," she said. "But it's how people work. Sometimes."

He looked at her across the kitchen then, and she held his gaze, and between them lay the unspoken thing — the offer he had made her weeks ago, inform and be free, the indenture dissolved like sugar in warm water — and she watched him decide, visibly, not to say any of it in front of the household. It was a courtesy. It was also a leash.

"Testimony is voluntary," he said finally. "I won't order it. I'll state the facts of his employment myself, in writing, under the company seal, which carries weight without exposing any individual clerk. Those who wish to appear besides that may do so." He straightened. "Those who wish to may also not. No one in this house will think worse of them. I'll think no worse of them. That's an order."

"Yes, sir," Prewitt breathed, with relief so naked it was almost indecent.

Ada stood. "I'll go."

Voss put down his pencil. "In 'eighty-five I learned the slow door. In 1899 I've stopped caring about doors entirely. I'll go."

Mrs. Tarn turned off the stove.

* * *

Calloway caught Marguerite in the corridor afterward, lamplit, narrow, smelling of lamp oil and cold sea through the shutters. He did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice; raising a voice was spending capital, and Calloway never spent what he could leverage.

"A word about your generosity."

"My generosity?"

"Standing first in line to be marked. Very noble. Also very convenient, for a person in your position." He studied her face the way he studied a suspect column of figures — patiently, thoroughly, giving it every chance to correct itself. "A marked clerk can't be bought. Did you consider that? The moment your name is in that drawer, my offer dies. The government owns a piece of you, and I can't dissolve what they've claimed."

She felt the trap close gently, elegantly, the way all his traps closed. Testify, and lose the one exit from her indenture — the exit he'd dangled like a lantern. Refuse, and watch Hollis stand alone while she preserved her own value.

"You're telling me not to testify."

"I'm telling you the price has changed since Tuesday morning. I make offers based on markets. The market moved."

"Then withdraw the offer."

Something flickered in his eyes — surprise, or respect, or the accountant's distress at a figure refusing to balance. "You would rather keep a promise to me you intend to break than free yourself honestly."

"I never promised to inform. You offered. I didn't accept."

"You didn't refuse either. You did what you always do, Lockridge. You waited. You're the best waiter I've ever employed. And waiting only works when the other party doesn't know what you're waiting *for*." He stepped closer, and the lamplight carved the lines of his face deep. "I've watched you for fifteen years. I've seen you hide things in plain sight so long I sometimes believe you've hidden them from yourself. Whatever you're carrying — and you are carrying something, you carry it the way your mother carried her pearls, low and close — whatever it is, the tide is going out on the time you have to carry it safely."

Her blood went cold and stayed cold. He could not know. He could not. But Calloway had built a fortune on reading half a ledger and pricing the whole, and she understood suddenly that he did not need to know what she carried. He only needed to know she carried.

"Hollis sold flour," she said. "Half price. That's the whole of his crime. If the price of testifying to that has gone up, then the price of silence has gone up faster, and I find I can't afford it."

She turned and walked toward her room, and his voice followed her down the corridor, quiet as a closing ledger:

"The offer stands until Friday, Lockridge. After Friday, the market closes. Decide what you're buying."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat on her bed with the loose floorboard pried up and the compass in her hands, its brass case warm from the hiding place, the needle trembling toward north and past north, toward the strait, toward the far shore, toward Emeric Sato, whom she had not seen in fifteen years and who had asked her in a fisherman's smuggled letter for one thing only: his grandfather's compass, before the confiscations reached his mother's house.

Across the room, the window rattled in the wind coming off the water, and she thought of Hollis's fern on the counting house windowsill, needing to be turned toward the light, and of Hollis somewhere tonight in a holding cell, having committed the crime of a sack of flour, while she sat in the dark with treason in her lap and her freedom priced at Friday.

The needle steadied. North, and past it.

Below her floor, faintly, she heard Calloway's office door open, and his boots cross to the strongroom, and stop.

Then, after a long moment, start again — toward the stairs. Toward the counting house floor. Toward the loose board beneath the third desk from the window, where the dust, she realized with a horror arriving slow and total as cold water, had been disturbed for days.

And someone had noticed.
