## 9. Ninety-Six Hours of Tide

The almanac did not lie, but it made you work for the truth.

Marguerite sat in the counting house long after the last lamp downstairs had gone dark, the tide tables spread before her like a confession she hadn't yet made. The stove had burned down to a red eye in its grate. Outside, the strait muttered against the pilings, that low continuous argument the water held with the land all winter.

She worked the numbers the way her mother had taught her — not with the company's arithmetic, which was about money, but with the older kind, which was about survival. Spring tide. Four days out. The moon would be new, pulling the water higher and lower than any other day of the month, and when it fell, it would fall far enough to strip the reef channel down to its bones.

That was the trick no patrol boat ever learned: everyone feared the reefs at low water. But there was one channel — her mother had named it Marguerite's Run, half a joke, half a christening — where the ebb scoured the sand deep enough for a skiff to thread through on the flooding tide, if you entered at slack and never stopped moving. At any other time of month, the channel was a graveyard. On this one night, with fog sitting fat on the water the way it did after a warm day and a cold sea, it was a door.

Four days. Ninety-six hours of tide between her and Emeric's letter, still folded against her breastbone like a second heartbeat.

She checked the figures a third time. Moonrise at 6:41. Slack before flood at the reef at approximately 11:20, allowing for the barometric pressure dropping — the glass had been falling all evening, which meant wind from the southeast eventually, which meant fog first. She could be across the strait and hidden in the reeds below the Sato boatyard by two in the morning, and back before the bridge watch changed at dawn, if everything went perfectly.

Nothing went perfectly. Her mother had said that too, the last summer, laughing, mending a net by lamplight: *Perfect is what the mainland calls weather they haven't met yet.*

She folded the tables away and pressed her palms flat against the ledger desk until the wood grain printed itself into her skin. Four days to become a traitor. Or four days to become, finally, exactly what she had been since she was fourteen years old standing on a dock holding another family's compass — someone who kept what she promised.

* * *

Ada knew something was wrong before Marguerite said a word. That was Ada's gift and her menace: she read people the way she read manifests, catching the discrepancy no one else noticed.

"You've worn a groove between your cot and the window," she said that night, not looking up from her darning. "Three steps, turn, three steps. You did it eleven times while I counted the heel-taps of old Prewitt coming up the stairs."

"I'm thinking."

"You're calculating. Thinking looks different." Ada bit her thread and finally raised her eyes. She had a face built for ledgers too — plain, patient, exact — and behind it a mind that missed nothing and forgave selectively. "Whatever it is, you've been carrying it alone for weeks, and you're bad at it, and I'd like to sleep sometime before spring."

Marguerite stood at the window. Three steps, turn. Below, the strait lay black and silver-shot under a waning moon, four nights from emptying itself for her.

"Ada. If I tell you something, you'll be in it. Not adjacent to it. In it. There's no version where knowing is safe."

"I'm already unsafe. I work for Meridian and I have opinions." She set down the darning needle. "Say it or don't. But don't decide for me what I can carry. That's Calloway's habit, deciding for people what they can carry."

So Marguerite told her.

All of it. The compass under the floorboard for fifteen years. The boy on the dock, fourteen years old, pressing his grandfather's instrument into her hands because his father said war fever passed faster than confiscation orders and someone outside the village had to keep what the village couldn't. Her mother's channel, her mother's knife, her mother's drowned certainty that the water gives back only what you ask for correctly. And now the letter — the father arrested, the boats seized, the confiscations moving house to house toward an old woman who had nothing left but a deed she didn't know was hidden inside a brass case in a floorboard in an enemy counting house.

When she finished, the room was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the tide turned against the piles with a sound like a great animal shifting its weight.

Ada didn't gasp. Ada didn't weep. Ada picked up her darning again, made three stitches, and put it down, and that sequence — Marguerite understood later — was Ada deciding.

"The reef channel," Ada said. "Your mother's. The one the charts mark as unsound."

"It's sound four days a year."

"And one of them is Thursday."

Marguerite felt something loosen in her chest that might have been terror or relief or both wearing each other's clothes. "You looked at the almanac."

"I audit, Marguerite. It's what I do." She stood, crossed the small room, and took Marguerite's hands — cold, chapped, steady. "Here's what happens now. You don't cross alone in a borrowed dinghy like a drowning woman in a ballad. We do this properly, the way we do everything in this house. You'll need a skiff that won't be missed until Friday. You'll need your absence covered — I'll say you're down with the flux, I'm good at nursing, everyone knows it, no one will want to check. And you'll need someone who knows where you are every minute, so if you don't come back, someone knows where the water took you."

"No one can know—"

"Someone already knows. Me. Since ninety seconds ago." Ada's grip tightened. "Fifteen years you've carried this alone, and look what it's made of you — a woman who apologizes for existing in her own life. No more. If you go, you go as us, or you don't go at all."

*The us* landed in Marguerite like a struck bell. All these years she had thought the promise was a weight she bore solo, a private debt in a private ledger. She had never once considered that love meant co-signing.

"There's a man in the household," Marguerite said slowly, "who reported unusual activity. Calloway's watching the counting house at night. If he sees—"

"He'll see me fetching broth for a sick clerk." Ada smiled, thin and fierce. "I've been invisible for thirty years, my dear. It's the one advantage of being Ada."

They sat together then, two women at a table with a candle burning low, and planned a crossing the way other women planned weddings: hour by hour, contingency by contingency. Which skiff — the old one, hull scarred, unlisted on the winter manifest. What food — dry, salted, wrapped in oilcloth. Where Marguerite would beach, how long she'd allow, what signal Ada should watch for from the bluff at dawn — a lamp flashed twice, or nothing, and nothing meant grief.

At some point Ada said, "Tell me about him. The boy."

And Marguerite found she could. That was the strangest mercy of the whole night — that after fifteen years of silence, the story came out whole, with laughter in it even. Emeric at fourteen, solemn as an auditor, explaining tides to her as though she didn't live among them. Emeric's grandfather's compass, its needle trembling north like a small faithful heart. The way he'd said *when it's safe* as though safety were a season that always came back, the way herring came back, the way the moon pulled the water home.

"He never asked me to bring it," Marguerite said. "He asked me to keep it. Those are different things. Keeping is easier. Bringing means crossing."

"And you're a bringing sort of person," Ada said. "God help us both."

* * *

On the second night, the barometer fell further and the fog arrived early, lying along the strait like something sleeping. Marguerite stood at the window and tested the air the way her mother had taught her — wet finger raised, the taste of the wind, the smell of kelp and cold iron. Good. Fog was a friend that asked no questions.

But fear arrived with it.

She dreamed that night of the dive she had never seen, her mother's last, reconstructed over fifteen years from other people's fragments: the green light going down, the pearl bed, the current that came without warning off the reef shoulder. In the dream her mother surfaced just long enough to hand her something through the water — not pearls, not the knife, but the compass, brass and cold, saying words that dissolved before they reached air.

She woke with her hand pressed flat against the floorboard over the hiding place, as though she could hold the past down by main strength.

Thursday. Two days. The numbers were fixed; only her courage remained unfixed, and courage, unlike tide, did not appear on any almanac.

* * *

On the third night, Ada came up to the attic room carrying two cups of tea and the expression of a woman about to commit bookkeeping fraud, which, Marguerite reflected, was precisely what she was doing.

"Requisition's drafted," Ada said. "Skiff logged out tomorrow evening for net repair at the lower racks. My handwriting, Mr. Prewitt's authorization — he signs anything after his second glass, bless his failing eyes. If anyone asks, the nets were torn in the last blow and you volunteered, being handy with cordage." She set the cup down. "One thing remains."

"What?"

"You." Ada sat on the edge of the cot. "Tomorrow night you walk out of this house a clerk of the Meridian Company, and you come back — God willing — something else entirely. Before you go, I want to hear you say why. Not the promise. The why under the promise."

Marguerite thought about it for a long time. The candle guttered; outside, the fog pressed at the glass like a palm.

"Because I watched them measure my whole life in entries I never wrote," she said at last. "My debt, my labor, my mother's grave, even my friendships — all of it in their ledger, their ink, their hand. And that compass is the one line in the world that was written by someone else, for someone else, and trusted to me freely. Nobody owns it. Nobody audits it. It's just — mine to carry, and his to receive. When I give it back, it'll be the first thing I've ever finished that the company didn't pay me to finish."

Ada nodded slowly, and reached into her apron pocket, and laid something on the blanket between them: a small purse, heavy with coin.

"Savings," she said. "Eleven years. If you don't come back, it buys passage east for a woman with no family and no history, and she starts over somewhere the water doesn't know her name. If you do come back—" she pushed it toward Marguerite, "—you'll need fare of your own, because after tomorrow you can't spend a company token anywhere on this coast without leaving a signature."

Marguerite stared at it. "Ada, I can't—"

"You can and you will. I've done the sums, and the sums say a self-owned woman needs capital." Ada's voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "Ninety-six hours ago I didn't know any of this existed. Now I'm in it to my neck, and do you know what I've discovered? I'm not afraid of the danger. I'm afraid of how glad I am. Fifteen years of watching you apologize for breathing, and one conversation, and suddenly there's a person in my room instead of a ghost."

Marguerite took the purse. It was warm from Ada's pocket, and heavier than coin.

"Two lamps from the bluff," Ada said. "If you make it back. And Marguerite — the tide doesn't care about promises. Mind the channel. Enter at slack, never stop moving, and whatever the water shows you down there, however it calls you — you come back up. Your mother would say the same, if she could get a word past my lecturing."

"She'd like you."

"Everyone does. It's my curse." Ada blew out the candle, and in the dark, the two women sat shoulder to shoulder at the window, watching the fog swallow the strait, waiting for Thursday the way you wait for a verdict.

Below them, the counting house slept, its ledgers closed, its secrets stacked in the dark like cordwood. Somewhere in the building, a floorboard hid a compass pointing north, and somewhere beyond the fog, a guarded village waited for a boat that hadn't left yet.

One day of tide remaining.
