## 10. What We Owe the Dead

The path to the bluff had grown over since spring, and Marguerite climbed it the way her mother would have — not fighting the salal but parting it, finding the old seams where feet had worn the ground agreeable. The wind came off the strait in long gray shoves. Below her, the tidal bridge lay closed for winter, its planks stacked like a coffin waiting to be filled, and beyond it the island village sat under its new flag of surveillance, smoke rising thin and obedient from chimneys that used to rise however they pleased.

She carried nothing. That was deliberate. You did not come to ask a favor holding things.

The grave was where it always was, third stone past the leaning fir, the marker her father had carved before his hands got too bad: *Seraphine Lockridge, Diver of the Reef Channel, Keeper of What the Water Gives.* No dates. Her mother had insisted. Dates were for people whose lives ended; hers had only changed address.

Marguerite stood at the foot of it with her boots wet from the grass and said nothing for a long while. This was also deliberate. Her mother had never trusted a person who talked before they'd thought.

*You're standing crooked,* her mother said, in the voice she kept now only in memory, salt-roughened, amused even at the bottom of everything. *Weight on your left. You always favored the left when you were nervous.*

"I'm not nervous."

*You came up here in November to talk to a stone. Of course you're nervous.*

The wind moved through the fir above the grave, and Marguerite let herself laugh, once, because there was no one to see and because her mother had hated grief performed in earnest more than anything except a badly kept ledger.

"I need the knife," she said.

There it was. Not the weather, not the company, not Emeric's letter folded against her ribs where it had lived for six days, soft as cloth from handling. Just the need, stated plain, because her mother had taught her that too: say what you want before the tide changes, or the tide will decide for you.

*Do you,* her mother said. And then, after a silence long enough for three gulls to cross the strait: *Tell me why.*

"It's not mine otherwise. It's sitting in my trunk wrapped in oilcloth like contraband, which I suppose it is now, and it's doing no one any good. It should be working. It was made for work." She heard her own voice pick up speed, the way it did when she was justifying an entry to Calloway. "And I'm going across the channel. The reef channel. At slack water, four days from now, if the fog holds. And if something happens out there—"

*Something will happen out there.*

"—then it shouldn't be found in my trunk by some government clerk who'll melt it down for the silver in the rivets. It should be in the water. Where it belongs. Where *you*—"

She stopped. The bluff wind filled the gap.

Her mother's voice, when it came back, was quieter. Not gentle — her mother had never been gentle, gentleness was a luxury of people who didn't dive — but low, the register she'd used below the surface, where sound traveled wrong and every word cost air.

*You're lying to me, little fish. You always lie twice. Once to the world and once to me, and mine is always worse.*

Marguerite crouched. Put her palm flat on the cold stone. "Then ask me properly."

*Why do you want it?*

"Because I'm afraid," she said. "Because I have crossed open water exactly zero times alone, and the last person who tried to teach me how drowned doing it, and I want—" Her voice cracked and she let it, because there was no ledger here, no column for composure. "I want to carry something of yours into the water so that if the water takes me, it's taking both of us together, and neither of us goes alone. There. Is that true enough?"

The wind dropped. On the strait below, a patrol boat ran its slow line along the far shore, a black stitch through gray wool.

*It's true enough,* her mother said. *Now listen, because I'll only say this the way I'd say it if I were still alive, which means once, and meanly.*

Marguerite knelt in the wet grass.

*That knife isn't luck. Everyone in the village used to touch it before a deep dive and call it charm, and I let them, because it made them brave and bravery is worth more than truth most days. But it's not a charm. It's a tool, and tools don't protect you. They only make sure that whatever you meet down there, you meet it armed. The water doesn't care whose steel you carry. The water cared about your grandmother and it cared about me and it will care about you, and the only thing that has ever kept a diver alive is knowing exactly what she owes and paying it before it's called due.*

"I know what I owe."

*You know what you owe the boy. That's easy; debts to the living have faces.* A pause. *What do you owe the dead?*

Marguerite looked at the marker. At the name without dates. Fifteen years she had been answering this question without knowing it was asked — fifteen years of keeping her mother's knowledge in her body like coin held in a closed fist, spending none of it, passing none of it on, because spending it meant admitting it existed and admitting it existed meant the company could tax it, the government could confiscate it, the world could take the last thing that was hers and hers alone.

"I owe you the truth about why I stayed away so long," she said. "I told myself it was prudence. It wasn't. I was afraid that if I dove your channels, I'd find out I was good at it. And if I was good at it, then I'd have to keep going down, and eventually the water would collect. It collected you. It collected your mother. I didn't want to be next in a line I hadn't agreed to join."

*But you've agreed now.*

"I'm crossing for a promise, not for pearls."

*No,* her mother said, and there was something in the memory-voice Marguerite had almost forgotten, the note she'd heard only once, the night before the last dive, when her mother had stood on this same bluff looking at the water and said, *Tomorrow I'll show you the reef channel, little fish, all of it, every turn of it, because a thing kept hidden dies with its keeper and a thing passed down can't drown.* But she had gone down first instead, and the lesson had waited fifteen years for a student. *No. You're crossing because the debt finally has your name on it. All those years you kept other people's accounts. Now here's one of yours.*

Marguerite bowed her head until her forehead nearly touched the stone.

"Then give it to me," she whispered. "Not the knife. The knowing. The channel. All of it, every turn."

*Sit down,* her mother said. *This will take a while, and you'll want to be off your feet.*

* * *

She sat cross-legged in the grass with her eyes closed and let the memory come, and it came the way the tide comes — not all at once but in pushes, each one leaving something behind.

Her mother's voice laid the channel out the way she'd first learned it herself, at fourteen, kneeling in a canoe: *Enter on the flood, never the ebb. The reef runs a hand's width under at high slack — you'll see it as a brown shadow with green water on either side. Green is deep enough. Brown is the coral asking questions. If the current sets north, hug the western wall where the rock folds back; if it sets south, take the middle and pray, because the middle is honest but fast. There's a hole at the third marker rock — your grandmother called it the kettle — where the water spins down and anything light goes around and around until it drowns tired. Paddle hard past the kettle. Never rest near it. Resting near the kettle is how we lost Tomas Iwata in '81, and he was the best swimmer any of us ever saw.*

*Where does the patrol go?* Marguerite asked inside the memory.

*Patrols are lazy the way all new power is lazy. They run the shipping lane because the shipping lane is wide and warm and their stove works there. Nobody with a gunboat wants to thread a reef at night. Fear makes men careful, little fish, and careful men stay in the middle of the channel where they can see their own wake. So you won't be in the middle. You'll be where careful men aren't.*

*And if the fog lifts early?*

*Then you wait. Waiting is not failure. Failure is going when you shouldn't.* A pause, and then, softer: *Your father never understood that. He thought patience was cowardice wearing a coat. Don't be your father on the water. Be me.*

Marguerite opened her eyes. The light had gone from pearl to pewter; she had sat through the better part of an hour and the cold had climbed through her coat and settled in her knees. Down in the counting house, Ada would be covering the afternoon audit, saying she had a headache, saying it smoothly, asking nothing — and Marguerite had not yet earned that silence, had not yet given Ada the truth that would make the silence fair.

Tonight, she thought. Tonight she would tell her. Or tomorrow. Before the window opened. A debt confessed late was still a debt confessed.

She stood, and her legs ached, and the wind pushed at her like a hand testing whether she was ripe.

One thing remained. She turned toward the fir.

The knife was not buried. Her mother had been too practical for burial; rust was a thief worse than any heir. Instead the knife hung inside the hollow of the old fir, wedged in a knot-cavity two arm-spans up, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with pitch, where rain couldn't reach and casual eyes wouldn't look. Her father had put it there after the drowning, following written instructions her mother had left in her dive log — instructions dated a full year before she died, as though she'd known, as though she'd been settling her own accounts in advance.

Marguerite climbed the tree the way she had climbed it at nine and twelve and fourteen, before the drowning, before the company, before the ledgers. Her hands remembered. The bark bit her palms and she welcomed the bite. At the knot-cavity she worked the pitch seal loose with her thumbnail, and the oilcloth came away stiff and pale, and inside it lay the knife.

It was smaller than memory. Everything recovered is. Six inches of blade, dark steel gone almost black with age, a handle of whalebone worn to the shape of her mother's grip — and now, as Marguerite closed her hand around it, being worn to hers. The edge was still keen. Her mother had oiled it before sealing it away; of course she had; her mother had never left a tool to suffer.

*Well,* her mother said, from wherever the dead keep their voices. *Heavy, isn't it.*

"Not really."

*Liar. It weighs exactly as much as it always did. It's you that's changed.* And then, because her mother could never leave a lesson un-nailed down: *Say it.*

Marguerite held the knife up between herself and the graying strait.

"This isn't a gift," she said. "It's a job."

*And the job?*

"To pass it on. Whole. To someone who'll use it and teach it and not let it die in a drawer like I nearly did." She slid it into her belt, where it sat against her hip with the authority of a bone. "Emeric's sister is eleven. She doesn't know the channels yet."

*Good girl,* her mother said, and said nothing else, and the silence afterward was not absence. It was the particular silence of a ledger balanced — the quiet that lives on the far side of a debt acknowledged.

* * *

She went down the bluff faster than she'd come up, the salal whipping her calves, the knife knocking gently against her hipbone with every stride like a second pulse. At the treeline she paused once and looked back. The grave stood small against the sky, patient, undecorated, done with her.

"Four days," she said. "Watch the water."

The strait gave back nothing but the ordinary gray, and somewhere on it, invisible, the patrol boat ran its lazy line, and somewhere south of that, in the counting house, Ada was closing the day's books and telling the others that Marguerite had a headache and needed her supper kept warm, and somewhere nearer than all of them — closer than the fence line, closer than suspicion, in the very rooms where she slept and ate and laughed — someone frightened was already beginning to wonder what all these small kindnesses were hiding.

She felt it before she could name it, the way divers feel a change in pressure before the storm shows: the household's warmth developing a draft. She walked home into the wind and did not yet know that she was walking into it too.
