## 11. The Informer

Ada Fenwick had learned to read a room the way other people read ledgers: line by line, debit and credit, what was owed and what was concealed. Fifteen years in the Meridian counting house had taught her that every silence had a price, and that the most expensive silences were the ones you kept out of love.

She was thinking about this on the morning of the twelfth of December, while she mended a tear in the sleeve of her good coat with black thread, watching Tom Quist across the clerks' kitchen.

Tom sat at the far end of the table with his tea going cold in front of him and a letter open beside his cup — a letter he had read four times since it came on the Tuesday boat, folded and refolded until the creases were soft as cloth. He was not reading it now. He was looking at the window, at the grey light coming off the strait, and his hands were flat on the table the way a man's hands go flat when he is holding himself down.

Ada knew the letter's contents before she knew its shape. Everyone did, in the way of households: Tom's sister was in Victoria, married to a printer who had once printed pamphlets for the island fishermen's association, and the government men had been to the print shop twice. They had taken the presses. They had taken the brother-in-law for questioning. The letter said they might take him again, and that Tom's sister wanted to know if there was any place safer than Victoria, and that Tom — who sent money home every month from a clerk's wage, which meant he sent nearly all of it — should be careful about his own position here, because Meridian employed island men, and the new regulations did not distinguish between an employee and a sympathizer.

Tom had a wife too, back east in Nanaimo, and a daughter he saw at Christmas. He had everyone to lose, arranged in a careful row, and he had spent three days looking at them all through the wrong end of a spyglass.

"Tea's gone cold," Ada said, because that was the kind of thing one clerk said to another, and it cost nothing.

Tom startled like a man caught at something. "I'll make more."

"Don't bother." She threaded her needle. "It's only going to go cold again."

He almost smiled. Then the smile went somewhere else, somewhere private and unhappy, and Ada bent over her sewing and let him have his silence, and hated herself a little for how easily she recognized what was growing in it.

* * *

The trouble with fear, Ada thought later, was that it looked exactly like diligence from the outside.

That was the thing nobody warned you about — that betrayal rarely arrived wearing its own face. It came dressed as caution. It came as a man staying late at his desk, checking entries twice, being scrupulous where he had once been easy. It came as a colleague who suddenly stopped joking in the counting house, stopped singing in the kitchen, stopped leaving his boots by the door where they might suggest a life lived loosely. It came as tidiness. And a household full of frightened people becoming tidy, becoming quiet, becoming careful — well. That was a household already halfway to informing on itself.

Marguerite had noticed it first, or pretended not to notice it, which amounted to the same thing. She had always been the best reader in the house; fifteen years of auditing other people's debts gave a person an instinct for what was hidden in the margins. But Marguerite had her own margins to mind these days, and Ada had watched her husbanding her attention like a woman rationing lamp oil, spending it nowhere she didn't have to, giving nothing away.

Which meant Marguerite hadn't seen what Ada saw. Or had decided not to look. Either way, someone had to.

Ada finished the coat sleeve and put away the needle and thought, with the particular clarity that comes just before a decision you will spend years regretting or defending or both: *I should talk to him.*

And then she thought: *But what would I say? Don't do the thing you haven't done yet?*

* * *

She found him that evening stacking cordwood behind the cookhouse, which was what Tom did when he couldn't sleep — work with his body so his mind would have nothing left over for itself. The woodpile was already absurd, six rows deep against the shed wall, enough fuel for a winter three times as long as any winter the coast had ever produced.

"You're going to put the cook out of a job," Ada said.

"Wood doesn't stack itself."

"No," Ada agreed. "It generally requires a man avoiding his bed."

Tom set another split round on the pile, precisely, bark-side up. For a while neither of them said anything, and the sound of the strait filled the space between them — the long hush and drag of tide against pilings, the gulls settling into the dark.

"My sister writes that the agents keep lists," Tom said finally. He didn't look at her. "Lists of names. Who associates with whom. Who receives letters. Who—" He stopped. Started again. "They took Mr. Ishikawa's son off the ferry last month. Just off the ferry, in front of everyone. Nobody's seen him since."

"I know."

"Nobody knows anything," Tom said. "That's the whole design of it." Another round of wood, set with exaggerated care. "You know what frightens me, Ada? It isn't them. It's that I understand them. I sit here and I think — a list of names, who associates with whom — and some part of me thinks, *well, that's sensible, that's orderly, that's how you'd run a ledger.*" His laugh was terrible. "Fifteen years in that counting house and I can't tell anymore where the arithmetic stops and I begin."

"Tom—"

"There's activity at night." He said it fast, the way a man steps off a ledge. "At the counting house. Lights after hours. Someone moving around down there when the household's asleep. I've heard it three times this week. And I keep telling myself it's rats, or Calloway himself working late, or — but Ada, if the agents come asking questions, and they ask me, and I say *nothing*, and then it turns out there *was* something—" He turned to her at last, and his face in the lamplight spilling from the cookhouse window was the face of a man drowning in plain sight of shore. "My daughter is seven years old. Her mother is in Nanaimo. If my name goes on a list—"

"Whose name would go on a list?" Ada asked quietly. "Yours?"

"Anyone's. Mine. Whoever they decide was close to it." He was wringing his hands on the handle of the wood-splitter without knowing he held it. "I wouldn't say anything. You know I wouldn't. I'd swear to it. But if they ask me directly — if an agent sits across from me and asks me what unusual activity I've observed in this household, what am I supposed to do, lie? Lying gets you investigated harder. Lying makes you interesting."

"So you'd rather make someone else interesting first."

The words came out of Ada colder than she intended, and she saw them land, saw Tom flinch the whole length of his body.

"That's not—" He set the splitter down. "That is not what I'm doing."

"No?"

"I don't even know what I'm doing." His voice cracked on it. "I know what I'm afraid of. Those aren't the same thing, are they? Tell me those aren't the same thing."

Ada looked at him — this mild, decent, frightened man who had eaten at their table for eleven years, who had taught the younger clerks double-entry and whist and how to get a stove to draw on wet wood — and felt something in her chest fold closed like a ledger snapping shut.

Because here was the cruelty of it, the thing she would turn over at three in the morning for months afterward: Tom wasn't wicked. That was the whole horror. He was afraid, and love was the engine of the fear, and the report he was already composing in his head — she could see it forming behind his eyes, the careful neutral language, *observed irregular activity, unable to identify the party concerned* — that report would be written in the same hand that signed birthday cards for the cook and sent money to a seven-year-old girl in Nanaimo. Betrayal committed out of love. There was no defense against it, because you couldn't hate it. Hating it would be like hating weather.

"Tom," she said. "Listen to me. Whatever you're deciding right now, whatever you've half-decided — wait. One day. Give it one day and talk to me before you talk to anyone else. Can you do that? Will you give me that?"

He looked at the woodpile, at the dark water beyond it, at the lit window of the cookhouse where their household glowed warm and oblivious.

"One day," he said. It wasn't agreement. It was arithmetic — one day bought against a daughter in Nanaimo, and everyone present understood the exchange rate.

* * *

Ada lay awake past midnight doing sums she couldn't balance.

The house ticked and settled around her. Down the hall, Marguerite's room was dark, though Ada had heard her come in late again, heard the particular care of her footsteps on the stairs — the care of a person carrying something, or being something, that could not afford to clank. Three times this week, Tom had said. Lights after hours. Someone moving around down there.

Ada had known about the floorboard since spring. Not by discovery — by inference, which was worse, because it meant Marguerite's concealments had shown through the way bad bookkeeping shows through, small discrepancies in the household's pattern that only accumulated meaning if you loved someone enough to notice. The compass. It had to be the compass; there was nothing else in Marguerite's history that required hiding under a floorboard. And Ada had said nothing, had stepped around the knowledge for months like stepping around a sleeping dog, because saying it aloud would wake it, and waking it would mean choosing.

Now Tom was choosing for all of them, one day at a time.

She got up. She told herself she was getting up to fetch water, and she believed herself about forty percent, which was honest enough. In the hallway the moon came through the east window and laid a pale stripe across the floorboards, and Ada stood in it and listened.

From below — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable — came the sound of the counting house door. Then footsteps. Then a longer silence. Then the small wooden whisper of a board lifting and settling, which Ada had heard perhaps four times in her life and could have identified blindfolded, drunk, underwater.

Someone was checking on the compass. Tonight. Now. While Tom's one-day grace ran out beneath her feet.

Ada stood in the moonlight and felt the shape of the trap close around the whole household: Marguerite below with her secret, Tom above with his fear, and Ada in between, holding both, belonging wholly to neither. She could go down and warn Marguerite — and confirm to any watcher that the two women conferred at midnight. She could go to Tom and beg again — and teach him only that the secret was worth begging over, which would make it worth reporting. Every move protected one person and exposed another. It was like trying to carry water in her hands.

She went back to bed. She did not sleep.

In the morning, Tom was gone early — gone before the kettle, gone with his coat buttoned to the throat and his letter folded in his breast pocket, and when Ada asked the cook where he'd got to, the cook said, "Went down to the office before breakfast. Said he had entries to reconcile."

On a day with no shipments due. On a day when the factor's office opened at nine and the tide tables said the morning boat came at ten.

Ada stood in the kitchen doorway with her hands very still at her sides, and did the arithmetic she had been refusing all night, and arrived at a sum she already knew.

* * *

Mr. Calloway received her at eleven, in the office that smelled of ink and coal smoke and, faintly, of the sea that no amount of respectable furniture could quite exclude. He listened to everything she had decided to tell him — which was everything except the names — with his hands laced on the blotter and his face giving back nothing, because Calloway's face never gave back anything; that was his gift and his menace.

"Unusual activity," he repeated, when she finished. "Nighttime. At the counting house."

"Yes, sir."

"And you don't know who."

"I don't, sir."

He regarded her for a long moment, and Ada understood that he knew precisely what she was doing — offering him smoke so he wouldn't go looking for fire — and that he was accepting it anyway, for reasons of his own, and that being understood by Calloway was nearly as dangerous as being misunderstood.

"Thank you, Miss Fenwick," he said at last. "You were right to bring this to me. A household runs on trust, and trust runs on daylight. We'll want more daylight." Something moved behind his eyes, some calculation completing itself, and when he spoke again his voice had gone mild in the way that made clerks' spines cold. "I'll be attending to the premises myself in the evenings, going forward. There are inventory matters that want my personal attention."

Inventory matters. At night. Alone.

"Yes, sir," Ada said, and walked out of the office with her heart going hard, knowing exactly two things: that she had just aimed the factor's attention at Marguerite's floorboard, and that she had done it because the alternative — letting Tom aim it there first, clumsily, with a name attached — would have ended everything faster and worse.

She had bought time with betrayal. She could only hope the person she'd betrayed would never learn the exchange rate.

Below the office windows, the strait lay flat and pewter-grey, running its tides toward the reef channel, keeping whatever it kept. And in the factor's private drawer, Ada had no doubt, a fresh sheet of paper waited for a fresh entry, and Mr. Calloway had already begun to write.
