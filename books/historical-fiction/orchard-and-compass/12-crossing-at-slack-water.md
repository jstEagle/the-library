## 12. Crossing at Slack Water

The fog came in at dusk the way her mother had promised it would, thick and low and smelling of kelp, and Marguerite stood at the reed line with her hand on the bow of Ada's borrowed skiff and felt, for the first time in fifteen years, that the world was cooperating.

*Slack water at half past nine,* she had written on the slate inside the counting house door, disguised as a shipping calculation. *Fog until morning.* The reef channel would run deep enough for a shallow-draft boat for ninety minutes on either side of slack. No patrol could see ten feet past its own lantern. If she left now, pulled hard for two hours against the last of the ebb, she'd hit the reef at dead water, thread the channel by memory — her mother's memory, handed down like a knife — and be across the strait before the moon cleared the ridge.

She checked the lashings one more time. The compass rode flat against her ribs under the oilskin, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied down with the same knots her mother used to secure dive weights: quick-release on the right, load-bearing on the left. The diving knife sat lower, against her hip, where it had ridden every night this week while she slept in her clothes in case the window moved up on her. It was heavier than it looked. Heirlooms always were.

Behind her, the counting house windows glowed yellow. She could see the shapes of them through the fog-fuzzed glass: Ada at her desk still, hunched over the requisition ledger she'd falsified so neatly that Calloway himself had signed off on the skiff's transfer to "harbor survey duty." Old Marta in the kitchen, pretending not to watch the door. The arrested clerk's brother had come by at supper with tide charts inked on the back of a fishmonger's wrapping paper, and no one had asked him how he'd gotten them, because asking questions was a luxury this household had quietly voted to stop affording itself.

They had made her memorize nothing. That was Ada's genius. Instead they had fed her the crossing piece by piece, folded into ordinary conversation, so that if any of them were questioned later they could honestly say they'd never discussed it. *Cold front moving through,* Ada had said over breakfast. *Water'll drop two feet by morning.* Which meant: the reef will show its teeth; don't trust your soundings. Marta had pressed a parcel of food into her hands and said only, *Eat before you're hungry, girl. Fear eats first otherwise.*

Marguerite pushed the skiff into the water.

The cold climbed her arms instantly, the way it always did, and for one vertiginous second she was fourteen again, standing on this same shore while men carried her mother's body up from the tideline wrapped in sailcloth, and the whole village watched, and Emeric Sato stood beside her saying nothing because there was nothing to say, only pressing something small and brass into her palm as the men passed. *Keep it until it's safe,* he'd said. *My grandfather's. When the fever passes, bring it home.*

The fever had never passed. It had only changed names — from feud to suspicion to war panic to the clean bureaucratic word *enemy alien*, stamped on people who had built the boats and mended the nets of this coast for three generations. And now his father sat in a government jail and his mother's house waited for the confiscation carts, and the compass in its oilskin wrap had become, overnight, evidence.

She rowed.

The ebb was dying but not dead, and she fought it with her shoulders and her breath, counting strokes the way her mother had taught her to count between waves — not to measure distance, but to keep the mind in the body and out of the water where fear lived. One hundred. Two hundred. The lights of the trading post shrank behind her into a smudge, then a rumor, then nothing. The fog closed around her like a held breath.

This was the part she had loved once, listening to her mother describe it. *Out there, nobody owns you. Not the company, not the debt, not the name on any ledger. Out there it's just you and the water, and the water doesn't care what you owe.* Marguerite had inherited the diving knowledge and none of the freedom. Until tonight.

At four hundred strokes she shipped oars and listened.

Her mother's rule: *the reef sings before it shows*. At slack water, in fog, you navigated by ear — the different voices of water breaking over coral versus stone, the hush where the channel ran deep. Marguerite tilted her head and heard it: a long soft hiss to starboard, the outer bar. A deeper mutter ahead, the reef's northern wall. And between them, faint as a whispered secret, the silence of the channel itself.

She adjusted course and rowed on.

* * *

It went wrong at the reef, which is where everything in her life had gone wrong or gone right, depending on how you counted.

The fog should have held. Her mother's charts, learned at her mother's knee, said fog off this coast burned off with the sun, never before midnight, never in October. But weather owed nothing to charts, and somewhere past five hundred strokes the black around her began to thin — not to light exactly, but to texture. She could make out the pale scar of foam along the reef wall. Could see, when she turned, a gray suggestion of open water behind her.

And below it all, far off to the west, a sound she knew from bad nights on the dock: the slow diesel knock of a patrol gunboat.

She froze, oars shipped, the skiff drifting on dead-calm water. The knock grew. Steady. Unhurried. A ship with nowhere particular to be and all night to be there.

*They run the reef line on foggy nights,* the clerk's brother had told her. *But they hug the outer channel. They don't know about the inner cut.* Everyone knew about the inner cut except, apparently, the night the fog lifted early and the gunboat chose to swing wide.

Marguerite did the arithmetic fast, the way she did everything: the gunboat was west of her, maybe six hundred yards, running north along the outer channel toward the strait's mouth. Between her and the island village lay the inner cut, narrow, shallow, invisible from the outer channel unless someone was looking with more than ears. If the fog had held, she'd have been through already. If the fog held another twenty minutes, she might still make it.

The fog thinned further. A star appeared overhead, then another, embarrassed to be seen.

She made her choice the way her mother would have: by committing. She bent to the oars and pulled for the cut.

Two hundred yards. She could hear the channel now, the deep-water hush on her left, the reef grinding softly on her right. One hundred. The gunboat's engine note shifted — a turn? A slowdown? Fifty. The water shallowed under her keel; she felt the skiff's drag change, felt the bottom rising, and stood to lighten the boat and punt with an oar, the way her mother had shown her in a dream she wasn't sure she'd actually dreamed—

The searchlight caught her like a hand closing.

White light, total, absolute. The world became a lit stage and she was on it, one woman standing in a leaking skiff fifty feet from the reef, an oar in her hands, an oilskin over a shape that any lieutenant with binoculars could read as cargo lashed to a body.

"Boat! Halt! You are hailed in the name of—"

The rest went under the sound of her own blood. She dropped into the seat and pulled — not back the way she'd come, that was suicide, the light would track her across open water — but sideways, into the reeds, into the black tangle of salt marsh that fringed the reef's landward edge, the one terrain on this coast where a gunboat could not follow and a searchlight scattered into a thousand green shadows.

The skiff grounded hard. She didn't wait to regret it. She unslung the compass lashings by feel, grabbed the knife, and went over the side into waist-deep water that drove the breath out of her in a white plume the searchlight must have seen, must have seen, must have—

"Stop, or we fire!"

A shot cracked across the water. Not at her — over her, the warning kind, which meant they hadn't positively identified her yet, which meant she was still a shadow in a marsh and not a smuggler confirmed. She waded, then crawled, then dragged herself flat into the reeds as the second shot came, closer, and the gunboat's engine rose to a frustrated bellow somewhere beyond the reed line, circling, unable to follow.

* * *

She lay in the mud for a long time.

Long enough for her wet clothes to stop feeling cold and start feeling like part of her skin. Long enough for the searchlight to sweep the reeds twice, find nothing but marsh grass bending in the wind it made itself, and swing away toward the outer channel. Long enough for the gunboat to give three blasts of its whistle — the signal, she knew from dock gossip, for *contact lost, reporting* — and grind away north.

Somewhere in the middle of it, her teeth started chattering and she clamped her jaw shut because sound carried over water and she was not going to die of a noise she made herself.

When the dark finally settled back over the strait like a lid, she sat up in the reeds and took inventory.

Compass: safe, dry inside its waxed canvas, lashed where it belonged. Knife: safe at her hip. Boots: full of water. Skiff: gone — grounded, swamped, and marked, by morning, as the boat that had tried. Ada's forged requisition would surface in some lieutenant's report within a day. There would be questions. There would be a description of her, or close enough. The household —

The household. Ada's neat forgery. Marta's parcel, still strapped to her back, soaked. The clerk's brother's tide charts, dissolving even now in her pocket. Every person who had touched this crossing had just become touchable.

She should turn back. That was the sane arithmetic. Go back, dry off, invent a story — she'd been checking crab traps, she'd lost her way in the fog, she'd panicked at the searchlight and fled into the marsh like any frightened fool. Ada could burn the requisition page and forge a replacement. The household had survived smaller scandals.

She sat in the mud with the compass against her ribs and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was not going to do that.

It wasn't bravery. Bravery would have looked like going through the cut under the searchlight. What she felt was simpler and colder: the crossing had failed, but the decision hadn't. Somewhere between the shore and the reef, between the first stroke and the shot over her head, the question *will I do it* had quietly closed itself, the way a ledger closes, and what remained was only *when* and *how*. Turning back wouldn't reopen it. Nothing would reopen it. She had rowed past the point where she was the kind of person who turned back, and the person on the other side of that line was someone she'd spent fifteen years waiting to meet.

Her mother had drowned thirty feet from shore, within sight of her own daughter, doing the thing she loved for the family that needed her. Marguerite had spent half her life being angry about that — the waste of it, the closeness. Lying in the reeds with the tide coming back around her legs, she finally understood there had been no waste. Her mother had died owning herself completely, in the one place no company held title. That was the inheritance. Not the channels, not the knots, not the knife. The ownership.

Marguerite got to her feet, water streaming off her, and began, silently, to wade south along the reed line — away from the trading post, away from every road and bridge and lantern, toward the fisherman's hut two miles down the coast where old Wen, who owed her mother a debt no ledger recorded, kept a second boat under sacking that no government inventory had ever found.

Four days. The next spring tide came in four days, and the next fog after that, and this time she would not need the skiff, the requisition, or anything else with the company's name on it. This time it would be her and the water, and the water didn't care what she owed.

Behind her, far across the strait, almost invisible, a single lamp burned in the window of a guarded house where a woman named Sato sat up late wondering if her son's faith in a clerk-girl had been foolishness.

Marguerite looked at it the way sailors look at stars — briefly, to fix her bearing — and walked on into the dark.
