## 13. Interrogation by Lamplight

Calloway had a theory about lamplight that he had never shared with anyone: that it was the only honest light left in the territory. Gaslight flattered. Daylight was indifferent. But an oil lamp on a desk between two people showed everything — the tremor in a hand, the calculation behind an eye, the exact moment a lie was assembled and the exact moment it began to fail.

He trimmed the wick at half past nine and waited for Marguerite Lockridge to come down from the loft.

He did not have to send for her. That was part of what he knew about her. The household had gone to bed by eight — Ada with her mending, young Pell with his arithmetic primers he pretended not to need, Mrs. Okafor's kitchen long dark. And yet within ten minutes of his lamp being lit in the counting room below, he heard the floorboards shift overhead, deliberate as a signature. She would come because she knew he wanted her to come, and she would rather walk into a question than let it sit overnight gathering interest.

That was the other thing he knew about her. She understood interest.

She came down the stairs with her shawl over one shoulder and her hair still braided for sleep, and if she was afraid, she had packed the fear away somewhere he couldn't see it. Fifteen years, and he had never once seen her afraid. He had seen her tired, wet to the knees, furious in the quiet way that made clerks find reasons to be elsewhere — but never afraid.

"Mr. Calloway." She stopped at the far side of the desk, exactly where an employee stood when summoned, and looked at the lamp rather than at him. "You want the Kestrel accounts?"

"I want you to sit down."

Something moved behind her face — not surprise; recalculation. She took the chair across from him. She sat the way she did everything, spine straight, hands folded, as though her body were a ledger and every entry in it balanced.

He let the silence run. It was an old trick and they both knew it was a trick, which should have ruined it, but silence has a way of working even when both parties can price it.

"You were out on the water last night," he said finally.

"No."

The word came flat and fast, and he almost smiled, because she was better than this and they both knew that too. "Marguerite."

"There was fog," she said, slower now. "I walked out toward the point. To look at the water."

"At eleven o'clock."

"I sleep badly in storm season."

"The Kestrel is missing."

Her hands did not move. Her breath did not change. But he had spent fifteen years reading this woman across a desk, through the small theater of debts and receipts and the endless patient arithmetic of other people's obligations, and he saw the thing that did change: the quality of her attention. Before his sentence, she had been listening to him. After it, she was listening to herself.

"Missing," she repeated.

"Requisitioned Tuesday under your authorization code. Signed out of the boathouse yesterday afternoon. Not returned." He turned the requisition slip around on the desk so she could read it upside down, which she could; he'd taught her himself. "Your code, Miss Lockridge. Your hand, near enough. Or someone's very good imitation of it."

"Someone forged it."

"Did they?"

"I don't know. I didn't sign it."

It was, he reflected, a masterful performance. Every note correct. The offense of an honest clerk accused of sloppy paperwork — indignation held on a leash, wounded precision, the faint insult of having her bookkeeping questioned. He had watched her perform smaller versions of it for years, defending entries against auditors who thought they'd caught her in an error and hadn't. She was magnificent at it.

That was the problem. Magnificence, in his experience, was expensive, and somebody always paid for it eventually.

* * *

He poured them each a measure of the company's worst brandy, because the ritual mattered, and pushed hers across the desk. She drank. A courtesy, nothing more.

"Do you know what I did today?" he asked. "Between the four o'clock mail boat and supper?"

"You told me. Inventories. The government men are back Thursday."

"The government men came early. This afternoon. Quietly, without their cart, without their clipboard man." He watched her take that in. "Two of them. They walked the dock, they looked at the Kestrel's empty cradle, and then they sat in my office and asked me questions I did not enjoy answering."

"About the boat?"

"About you."

The lamp fluttered in a draft from under the door. Neither of them looked at it.

"They know," Calloway said, "that a woman matching your description was seen on the water last night, heading east, before the fog closed. They know the skiff that came back washed up in the reeds past the point, oars gone. And they know — this is the part I want you to hear clearly, Marguerite, because I am going to say it once — they know the Meridian Company employs an indentured clerk whose mother was a diver from the village across the strait, and whose name appears in correspondence we were all required to surrender in October."

The room was very still. Outside, the tide was turning; he could hear it in the pilings, the long suck and knock of the strait rearranging itself.

"So," he said. "Here is what I know that they do not. I know that in fifteen years you have never stolen so much as a pencil. I know that your books have survived three audits without a discrepancy, which in this territory is close to sainthood. I know that when the Chen boy was arrested in November, you argued his case with me for two hours and lost, and went back to work the same afternoon instead of sulking or running, and I have wondered ever since whether that was discipline or despair." He turned his glass once on the blotter. "And I know that whatever you did or did not do last night, you came downstairs tonight instead of making me climb up to you. Which tells me either that you are innocent, or that you want something from me."

"And if it's the second?"

"Then you'll be disappointed. Because the only thing I have to give you is the same offer I gave you three weeks ago, and I'm giving it one last time, and then the choice leaves my hands entirely."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only kindness left in the transaction.

Inform. Names, dates, the compass if there was a compass, the letter if there had been a letter, the channel if she'd used the reef passage — and he watched her eyes when he said *reef passage*, watched the flicker she killed too slowly, and filed it away with a grief he did not let reach his face. In exchange: the government men would receive a cooperative witness and lose interest in the Meridian's clerk. And he, Edmund Calloway, factor of the Meridian Trading Company, holder of her paper these fifteen years, would burn her indenture in front of her. Not sell it. Burn it. She would walk out of this building free, with her wages owed and a reference that would open any counting house on the coast.

"One conversation," he said. "One honest hour, and you're a free woman on New Year's Day."

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he found himself studying her the way he studied difficult accounts — looking for the entry that didn't balance, the figure hiding inside a larger sum. He had bought her debt at nineteen, a frightened girl with her drowned mother's calluses still on her hands, and he told himself he had been fair with her, better than fair; he had taught her everything, paid her above scale, defended her against men who thought an indentured woman should fetch and carry instead of reconcile ledgers. He had told himself for fifteen years that what he felt was the pride of a good investment.

Sitting across the lamp from her now, watching her decide something enormous behind those steady eyes, he admitted — privately, in the locked drawer of himself where he kept such things — that it had never been only pride.

"You think I crossed," she said at last. Not a question.

"I think the evidence says so."

"Evidence," she said softly, "is what people write down. My mother used to say the water keeps the true record, and it doesn't show it to anyone."

"Is that a confession?"

"It's a memory." She met his eyes for the first time since she'd sat down, and he felt the contact like a hand laid flat on the desk between them. "You've been fair with me, Mr. Calloway. Better than fair. You taught me that every number is a promise someone made and every page is a chance to keep it or break it. So I'll give you numbers. I was not on the water last night. I did not sign for the Kestrel. And I will not inform on anyone — not the household, not the fishermen, not the people across the strait. Those are my figures. Enter them however you like."

"Why?" The question came out of him rougher than he intended. "Not the refusal — I expected the refusal, God help me. Why won't you tell me why? One reason. Give me one reason to believe there's a person underneath all this loyalty I can't audit, and I'll find a way to protect her. That's all I've ever tried to do here. Protect what's mine."

Something crossed her face then — not anger. Something older and sadder, the look of someone watching a man count the wrong currency.

"You keep saying *mine*," she said. "My books. My clerks. What's yours." She rose, unhurried, and drew the shawl up onto both shoulders. "Fifteen years, Mr. Calloway, and I never once said it wasn't generous. It was. You fed me and taught me and paid my paper honestly, and I balanced every book you gave me, and I'm not ungrateful. But gratitude isn't ownership. It never was. You just wrote it that way."

She walked to the door. Stopped. Did not turn around.

"The government men come Thursday," she said. "Ask yourself what story you want to tell them. Because whichever one you choose, you'll have to live in it longer than I will."

Then she was gone, up the stairs, her steps measured and even, and Calloway sat alone with the lamp and the brandy and the requisition slip with its careful forgery, and understood that she had just told him, in the only language he truly spoke, that his books no longer balanced.

* * *

He slept badly, which was rare, and rose before dawn, which was usual, and stood at the counting house window watching the strait turn from black to iron to the dull pewter of a storm sky. The bridge closure signs flapped on their posts. Down the coast, invisible beyond the point, lay the internment zone, the guard boats, the village where a boy had once made a fourteen-year-old girl promise something foolish and sacred.

Loyalty or defiance. He turned the question over the way he turned a doubtful coin. If loyalty — to whom? Not the company; she had just declined the easiest freedom of her life to avoid proving it. To the dead mother, perhaps. To the household. To some promise he'd never seen entered anywhere, made years ago in peacetime by children who couldn't have known what the century would do with them.

If defiance — then she was braver than any man the government had sent him, and he had spent his whole career learning that bravery was contagious and dangerous, like fire in a lumberyard.

There was a third possibility, and it was the one that kept him standing at the window while the kettle went cold behind him: that loyalty and defiance were, in her, the same thing. That she served something older than either the company or the government, some ledger kept in water and salt that he had no column for, and that she would pay its debts down to the last entry, gladly, and never explain, because explanations were a kind of coinage and she had stopped trading in his currency years ago.

At seven, Ada Voss came down to start the day's work, and paused in the doorway with a look he couldn't read — a look that had weight behind it, a look that had decided something.

"Mr. Calloway," she said. "Before you open the mail. There's something the household needs to ask you."

He turned from the window. Behind Ada, in the hall, the rest of them were assembling — Pell, Mrs. Okafor, the younger clerks, even the boy from the dockside shed — standing together in the gray morning light like a column of figures that had somehow learned to hold its ground.

And Marguerite stood among them, dry-eyed and straight-backed, watching him with an expression he would carry into the new century: not pleading, not defiant. Waiting. As though the next entry were his to make.
