## 14. The Household Decides

Ada found the requisition book before she found her courage, which was the wrong order and she knew it.

It sat in the counting house cabinet where it always sat, between the chandlery invoices and the fuel ledgers, a fat green volume with MERIDIAN TRADING CO. — REQUISITIONS stamped on the spine in gold leaf gone dull. Anyone could open it. That was the genius of the company's bureaucracy: everything was permitted so long as it was written down correctly, signed by the correct hand, filed under the correct heading. The Meridian did not run on trust. It ran on forms.

Ada Quill had been a clerk for eleven years. She could write a requisition that would pass through three sets of hands without a single eyebrow lifting. She had done it for extra lamp oil, for a replacement stove grate, once — memorably — for two hundred pounds of salt pork that had somehow never reached the storehouse but had very much reached the household table during the lean winter of '96. Mr. Calloway had eaten that pork. He had complimented the cook.

So she stood in the cold counting house at six in the morning, before anyone else arrived, with the book open on the desk before her, and she let herself understand what she was about to do.

Not theft. Theft was simple. This was worse: forgery in a company hand, against a company asset, for a woman who hadn't asked her to do anything at all.

* * *

She had known for nine days. Nine days since she'd pulled up the floorboard behind the coal scuttle while tidying — Marguerite was ill, or pretending to be, and someone had to sweep — and seen the brass case sitting there in the dark like a sleeping animal. A compass. Not a company compass; the company's compasses were logged, numbered, cheap. This one was old, with a worn rosewood case and a hairline crack across the glass, and when Ada had lifted it the needle swung and settled and pointed, absurdly, directly at her own chest.

She had put it back. She had said nothing.

But carrying a secret is not free. It accrues interest. For nine days Ada had moved through the household like a debtor, flinching at footsteps, reading guilt into every ordinary glance — until the night Tom Pell was taken.

Tom. Nineteen years old, second-year clerk, a boy who blushed when the cook teased him and copied figures with his tongue between his teeth. They'd arrested him for giving bread to an island fisherman whose boats were seized. Bread. Two loaves and a heel of cheese. The soldiers had walked him past the counting house window in handcuffs and he hadn't looked at them, any of them, because looking would make them complicit and he was nineteen and already he understood the arithmetic of fear.

After that, Ada stopped pretending her silence was neutral. Silence was a choice, same as anything. And if Tom could be taken for bread, then Marguerite — Marguerite with her floorboards and her midnight calculations and whatever it was she was planning, because Ada had watched her study tide tables the way gamblers study cards — Marguerite was going to be taken for something far heavier than bread.

Unless somebody helped her do it fast enough that there'd be nothing left to take her for.

* * *

The requisition took her forty minutes to compose, which was twenty minutes longer than honesty would have taken and half as long as cowardice.

SKIFF NO. 7, she wrote. TRANSFER TO SALMON CREEK STATION FOR SEASONAL LINE INSPECTION. AUTHORIZED: J. CALLOWAY, FACTOR.

The signature was the problem. Calloway's hand was distinctive — a narrow, vertical script with a peculiar hooked F, like a man perpetually catching himself mid-fall. Ada had processed hundreds of his authorizations. She practiced on scrap paper until the hook came naturally, until her wrist learned the small dishonesty of it, and even then she hesitated over the final copy because practicing a man's signature is a strange intimacy, like learning his breathing.

Salmon Creek was real. There really was a line inspection due. If anyone checked — and someone would check, eventually, because the government agents were still in the settlement going through records with their little paper knives — the requisition would buy perhaps a week of confusion. A week was all Marguerite needed. Ada didn't know how she knew that, but she'd watched the woman count tides, and you don't count tides for a journey longer than one.

She signed it. Her hand did not shake until afterward, which she considered a professional courtesy from her own body, delayed billing.

Then she filed it in the outgoing tray, third position, where the morning clerk would collect it without reading it because the morning clerk never read anything, and she went to wake the household for breakfast with her heart hammering like a girl's and her face arranged into eleven years of clerical blankness.

* * *

Old Marta knew before Ada said a word.

This was Marta's way. Forty years cooking for the Meridian, through three factors and one smallpox quarantine, and she had developed the preternatural calm of a woman who has decided that nothing that happens inside her kitchen counts as history. Wars began at her doorstep and she assessed them solely for their effect on rations.

That morning she set out porridge, then set out, beside it, a canvas pack that had not been out of storage since the survey expedition of '94. Dried fish wrapped in oilcloth. Hardtack in a flour sack. A flask that clinked when Ada brushed past it — spirits, and not the weak table kind.

"Marta," Ada said carefully.

"Salt pork goes bad if it sits." Marta didn't look up from her stove. "Better it travels."

"You don't know what—"

"I know a woman who hasn't slept proper in a fortnight and keeps folding maps in her room like napkins." Marta turned, and her face was its usual flat mask of domestic disapproval, which on Marta was the equivalent of another woman's tears. "I know the Sato boy's father used to trade us eels every autumn and pay double what they were worth because my husband drowned off that reef and he never once mentioned it. I know this company fed me when nobody else would and I know it also owns me down to my teeth, and those are two different debts and I keep separate books." She jabbed the spoon toward the pack. "Eat your breakfast."

Ada ate her breakfast.

Later she would understand that this was how the household voted. No meeting was called. No hands raised. The Meridian taught its people to conduct all important business through indirect entries, and so the clerks had learned democracy as a form of accounting: each of them posting their own small treason to a ledger only they could see, trusting the sum to come out right.

* * *

The tide charts came by accident, or by the appearance of accident, which in that settlement was the same thing.

His name was Daniel Pell, Tom's older brother, a salmon boatman with forearms like mooring rope and his brother's same stubborn jaw. He appeared at the counting house on the third day asking — loudly, in front of the junior clerks — about outstanding payment for fish delivered in October, a dispute so mundane that Calloway himself waved it toward Ada to resolve.

Ada resolved it. Paid him. Walked him to the door.

And Daniel Pell, shaking her hand, pressed a folded chart into her palm the way you might return a dropped glove, and said, "Spring tide Thursday night. Channel runs deep after ten. Fog usually comes up off the water around then too, if the wind's southerly." Then, louder, for the benefit of the room: "Much obliged for settling the account, miss. Tell your factor I'll bring herring next month."

He was gone before her heart started again.

She opened the chart in the privy, which felt appropriately conspiratorial and smelled appropriately terrible. It was the strait — the whole tidal strait, hand-annotated in pencil, depths marked in fathoms, and along the eastern edge a channel drawn in fresh ink that did not appear on the company's surveys. Someone had labeled it in a careful fisherman's hand: THE NARROWS. BELOW THIS MARK SHE COMES AND GOES.

Below this mark *she* comes and goes.

Ada stared at the feminine pronoun for a long time. Whoever Daniel Pell meant, she had been crossing that reef channel for years, unseen, unlogged — a diver, a smuggler of one kind or another, some ghost of the other shore. And her route was now in Ada's hand, offered to whoever needed it, no questions asked, because Tom was in a cell and questions were a luxury the household could no longer afford.

Marguerite's mother. It had to be. The drowned woman. The knowledge everyone whispered about and no one named.

Ada folded the chart small and carried it against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

She cornered Marguerite that evening in the woodshed, because the woodshed was the only place in the compound without windows, and because she had rehearsed this conversation through four pots of tea and still couldn't find a gentle way in.

"I forged a requisition," Ada said.

Marguerite looked up from the axe she was not swinging. Lamplight from the kitchen door caught half her face. "You what?"

"Skiff Seven. Salmon Creek inspection. Calloway's signature is surprisingly easy." Ada held out the chart. "Also, Daniel Pell says hello, and the Narrows runs deep after ten on Thursday, and Marta packed enough food for a week because apparently we've all been waiting for you to get around to telling us."

The silence went on long enough that Ada heard the tide turning in the strait below, that low sigh the water made when it changed its mind.

"You shouldn't have done this," Marguerite said finally. Her voice was strange — flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding something heavy at arm's length. "Any of you. If this goes wrong, it isn't just me. Tom is already—"

"Tom gave away bread." Ada stepped closer. "I've given away a federal offense with a company letterhead on it. We're well past the point where caution means anything, Marguerite. Caution now is just choosing which of us drowns alone."

"That's not—" Marguerite stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them she looked, for the first time in fifteen years, like exactly what she was: a woman of thirty-one who had been holding her breath since she was fourteen. "Why?" she asked. "You don't even know what I'm carrying."

"I know it's a promise." Ada said it simply. "I've watched you keep it since the day I met you. Whatever's in the floorboard, it isn't yours and never was, and you've been paying rent on it with your whole life." She pushed the chart into Marguerite's hands. "We're not helping you steal something. We're helping you put it down."

Marguerite looked at the chart. At the penciled channel. At the handwriting that wasn't hers but belonged to her anyway, inherited through strangers, through a dead woman's reputation, through the loyalty of people who had lost things on that same reef.

"The fog," Marguerite said, and her voice cracked on the word like ice underfoot. "If the wind holds southerly."

"It will," Ada said. "Daniel Pell doesn't bet on weather he hasn't smelled."

* * *

They told the others in pieces, in the way of conspirators who have never conspired before and are bad at it.

The junior clerks were told nothing and understood everything; by Thursday morning they had polished Skiff Seven's hull without being asked, and hidden her oarlocks from the boathouse inventory with the casual efficiency of children hiding vegetables. The night watchman, old Hobb, developed a sudden deafness regarding footsteps near the dock after ten o'clock. Even young Whitcomb, who quoted regulations the way other men quoted scripture, was observed walking the long way around the compound — past the patrol office, whistling loudly — at precisely the hours when quiet movement might otherwise be noticed.

Nobody said the word *treason*. Nobody said *helping*. The household simply bent around Marguerite like water around a keel, each person adjusting course by a degree, and the sum of all those degrees was a heading.

On Wednesday night Ada sat with Marguerite in the kitchen while Marta pretended to sleep in her chair by the stove, and they repacked the canvas bag together. Compass and knife went in last — Ada saw them now, saw the knife's bone handle worn smooth by another woman's grip, saw the way Marguerite touched the compass case the way you touch a coffin lid — and were lashed high on the chest where drowning hands could reach them.

"If the gunboat's out," Ada said, "you turn back. You swear it. One attempt, then we wait for the next spring tide, and the next. The company's skiff can be explained twice. A body can't be explained at all."

"One attempt," Marguerite agreed, and lied, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so, because that too was a kind of vote — the household electing, unanimously, to believe in each other beyond all evidence.

Marta snored theatrically from her chair. Outside, the wind had come around to the south, soft and wet, dragging the fog in off the open Pacific like a curtain being drawn across the century.

Thursday was tomorrow.

And somewhere across the black water, in a guarded camp where families waited with their bundles tied, a boy who was no longer a boy was spending what might be his last ordinary night on the wrong side of a strait that was about to be crossed.
