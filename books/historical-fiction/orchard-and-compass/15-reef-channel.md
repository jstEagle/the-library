## 15. Reef Channel

The skiff was called *Petrel*, which Ada said was an omen and Calloway would have called a liability if he'd ever learned its requisition number. Marguerite thought neither thing. She thought about tonnage. She thought about displacement, and how little of it she needed, and how the boat rode so light in the black water that every swell lifted it like a hand testing whether to keep her or drop her.

She had launched from the old salt-works slip below the bluff, past the grave she'd visited three nights ago, past the place where her mother's bones were not but her mother's name was. The tide charts from the arrested clerk's brother lay folded in oilcloth inside her shirt, against her skin, already going soft with sweat. The food the cook had packed sat under the thwart: hard biscuit, salt pork wrapped in cloth, a flask of something that smelled like it could strip varnish. You did not cross the strait in winter on courage. You crossed it on calories and arithmetic, and she had spent four days on both.

Fog came with her off the point, thick as wool, exactly as promised. The spring tide was running. Somewhere behind her the counting house lamps would be lit, and Ada would be standing at the window pretending to count crates, and Calloway — Calloway might be watching the dock, might be watching nothing, might at this moment be writing her name in a report that would outlive them all. She had stopped trying to hold all of it in her head. There wasn't room. There was only the channel now, and the water, and what her mother had taught her before she was old enough to know she was being taught.

*The reef has a door,* her mother used to say, kneeling in the shallows of some long-ago summer, drawing lines in wet sand with a finger worn smooth by rope and salt. *The patrols see a wall. You look for the door.* She had named the reef herself, years before Marguerite was born — named its teeth and its gaps the way you name children, so you could call them home when the light went. And one of the gaps, the deepest, the one that ran green even at half-tide because the current scoured it clean, her mother had named *Marguerite*. A joke then. An inheritance now. A doorway with her own name on it, waiting for her in the dark two miles offshore.

She rowed. She did not use the little sail; sail made noise, and noise traveled over flat water like gossip through a small town. So she pulled, stroke after steady stroke, counting them because counting kept the fear at arm's length the way entries in a ledger kept debts at arm's length, and God knew she had practice at that.

Two miles is nothing in daylight. In fog, at night, in February, pulling against a flood tide that wanted to shove her north onto the reef's teeth or south into the patrol lanes, two miles was a country. Her shoulders burned inside the borrowed coat. The oarlocks creaked, and each creak sounded enormous to her, though she knew — she had been told by men who fished these waters their whole lives — that the sea swallowed small sounds the way it swallowed everything else.

* * *

The first hour was arithmetic. She had planned it that way deliberately: get the numbers done while her hands were strong and her head was clear.

Slack water in the inner channel at 2:40 by her father's old watch, which had belonged to no father of hers but had come with the indenture inventory and never been returned, one more small debt in a life built of them. The flood would begin slackening around two; the door in the reef — her door — ran truest forty minutes before slack, when the scour was strongest and the standing eddies behind the coral heads gave a small boat a corridor of comparatively gentle water. After slack, the ebb set hard southeast, straight across the mouth of the channel, and anything caught there went onto the outer bar or out to sea, and either way the sea kept it.

So: arrive at the reef line by two. Find the gap by sound and feel, because the fog ate sight at fifty feet. Run the channel. Be through, into the lee of the far shallows, before the ebb woke up.

Simple. It had looked simple drawn in pencil on the back of a requisition form in Ada's neat clerk's hand. It looked less simple now, with the world shrunk to the circle of grey around the bow and the compass — not Emeric's compass, she didn't dare use that one, his grandfather's compass stayed lashed to her ribs in its oilskin — but the cheap company bearing-plate on the thwart telling her she was holding her heading within a few degrees of perfect, which meant nothing, because a few degrees over two miles put her on the bar or in the patrol lane and dead either way.

She navigated the way her mother had taught her, without instruments, by the water itself. The flood tide came up the strait from the southwest and struck the reef line and split, and where it split there was a seam — a line of broken, confused water, a whisper of white in the black — and along the seam the current eased, and along the seam the deep door opened. You couldn't see the seam in fog at night. But you could hear it. You could hear the difference between water sliding and water fighting, a difference her mother had tried for years to explain and finally given up on, saying only: *you'll know it when you're drowning, and I'd rather you knew it before.*

Marguerite shipped her oars and listened.

At first there was nothing but the tick of spray off the bow and her own breath and the enormous soft silence of fog on open water. Then, gradually, as she drifted closer to the reef line she could not yet see, the sound arrived: a low mutter somewhere ahead and to the left, the reef worrying the tide, stone grinding water the way a mill grinds grain. And threaded through it, fainter, a hiss — the seam. Water sliding fast and smooth over the lip of the channel.

Her heart slammed once, hard, and settled. *You'll know it when you're drowning.* Not drowning tonight, Mother. Tonight I know it early.

She took up the oars again and pulled for the sound.

* * *

The reef announced itself as a smell before anything else — the sour iodine stink of kelp dragged and released, dragged and released, by the surge in the gaps. Then the muttering grew to a broken conversation of surges, each wave finding a different throat in the rock and gargling down it, and Marguerite understood she was closer than she should have let herself get, and backed water hard, and the stern swung, and for ten terrible seconds she had no idea where the seam was anymore.

Panic is a current too. She felt it take her the way the flood tide takes a loose oar — gently at first, then completely. *Turn back.* The thought arrived fully formed, warm as a lamp, reasonable as an auditor's note: *you can turn back. The fog will cover you. No one need ever know. You could go home, dry your clothes by the stove, and tomorrow the household would be whole and unbetrayed and alive.*

And then the other thoughts, arriving slower, heavier, each one a body the tide refused to surrender: Emeric's face at fourteen, lit by driftwood fire, making her swear on the compass case itself. His father's boats seized. The confiscation lists she had copied herself with her own precise hand, names entered in columns, lives reduced to assets pending disposal — *she had written those entries*, she had balanced those columns, she had been the machine of the company's forgetting and she could not unwrite it but she could carry this one thing across the water. Her mother's voice in the sand: *look for the door.*

Ada's face when Marguerite had finally told her everything, four nights ago, in the dark of the loft above the counting house. Not horror. Not fear for herself, though she had every right to it — she had forged a requisition, she was an accessory, they could hang her or transport her or worse. Just Ada's steady eyes in the lamplight, and her voice: *Then you'd better come back, because I am not forging another one.*

You could not turn back from that. Turning back would make it all cost more than going forward, and Marguerite Lockridge, who had audited fifteen years of other people's debts, understood costs better than anyone on either shore.

She breathed. Once. Twice. The way her mother had taught her to breathe before a dive — slow in through the nose, longer out, until the pulse dropped and the cold became information instead of enemy.

Then she listened again, and found the seam.

* * *

Running the channel took eleven minutes. She would remember them her whole life as a single held breath.

She came in on the seam with the oars feathered, using the current itself to carry her, correcting with short strokes, and the walls of the gap rose on either side — not visible, but audible, the surge climbing the coral heads to starboard, the deeper boom of the scour pool to port, and between them the green water her mother had named for her running smooth and fast as poured glass. The fog glowed faintly with phosphorescence stirred up by the surge, ghost-light blooming along the rocks, and for a moment she saw the channel entire: a river of pale fire between black jaws, and her little boat sliding down the middle of it like a seed down a gutter.

Beautiful. She thought, absurdly, *Mother, you never told me it was beautiful.* And then she thought, *you tried to tell me everything else,* and her eyes stung with spray or something else, and her stroke faltered.

It was the falter that nearly killed her.

A standing wave — a roller where the ebb's first breath met the flood's last strength, a wave that should not have been there yet, that her calculations had placed twenty minutes in her future — stood up across the channel just ahead. She heard it before she saw it, a rising roar, and she had time to think *no* and to dig the oars in and swing the bow to take it square, which was correct, which was the only thing that saved her, and then the wave took the boat anyway.

The *Petrel* rose, hung, and went over sideways.

Cold is a word. What the water did to her was not a word. It closed on her like a fist, drove the air from her chest in one involuntary grunt, and the world became pressure and black and the roar of the wave receding overhead into a muffled thunder. She was under. She was tangled — the sheet line, the spare painter, something around her ankle — and the boat was above her or beside her or gone, and her body did what drowned people's bodies do, which is fight, spend, waste. She clawed upward. The painter snapped taut around her ankle and hauled her down and she drank a mouthful of the strait, ice and salt, and somewhere very far away a calm voice that was her mother's and also her own observed: *this is how they go. This is exactly how they go.*

The diving knife.

Her hand found the belt. Her mother's knife — bone handle, blade her mother had honed on the same stones that honed her own patience — and Marguerite drew it and cut, blind, twice, three times, feeling the painter part, and came up.

Air. Fog. The mutter of the reef. She coughed the strait out of herself in great tearing spasms and hung on the swamped hull, which bobbed beside her, half-full, miraculously upright. The oars were gone. One oar floated nearby, mocking. The bearing-plate was gone. The food — she checked with a numb slap — still lashed under the thwart, God bless the cook and his sailor's knots. And against her ribs, under the oilcloth and the webbing she had lashed there herself with Ada's help in the boathouse, flat and hard and dry: the compass. Still there. Still his grandfather's. Still the whole reason she was dying in a reef channel at two in the morning.

She got an arm over the gunwale and hung there and shook.

* * *

Here is the moment she would tell no one, later, ever: hanging on the hull, in the dark, in the last minutes of slack water, Marguerite Lockridge almost turned back.

Not in panic. Panic was spent. This was quieter and worse — a clear-eyed accounting, the auditor's cold sum. The boat was swamped. Bailing with a tin cup would take an hour she did not have; the ebb was coming and would come hard, and the ebb set southeast across the channel mouth, and a swamped boat with one oar could not fight it. The water in her boots was already a weight. Her hands would stop working soon. The far shore was a mile of open water away, guarded, fog-bound, and she could not even see it.

*Let the sea have it,* said the warm lamp-voice. *The promise too. The sea keeps things. Your mother proved that.*

And she thought about her mother then — really thought, not the sand-drawing memory but the last dive, the one she had carried for twenty years: the morning water flat as pewter, her mother going down off the point with the long-handled blade and the rope basket, waving once, easy, unhurried, a woman visiting a neighbor. The rope going slack. The rope staying slack. The moment when waiting became knowing, and knowing became the rest of her life.

The sea had kept her mother. That was the argument for turning back. But it occurred to Marguerite, hanging there with her lips going blue and the ebb's first pull beginning to drag the hull seaward, that the sea keeping things was precisely the problem. The sea kept her mother. The government kept Emeric's father. The company kept fifteen years of her labor in a ledger with her name misspelled. Everything in her life had been kept — held, confiscated, retained, owned. Nothing had ever been *given back*.

Except by her. Giving back was the one transaction left in the world that she controlled.

She laughed — a horrible bark of a sound, half sob, lost instantly in the fog — and hauled herself halfway over the gunwale, and started bailing with the tin cup.

* * *

Bailing is not dramatic. That is the truth no ballad tells. She bailed for what the watch said was fifty minutes and her body said was a season: cup, heave, cup, heave, arms shaking, breath ragged, the boat riding lower and lower as the ebb strengthened and she fought it with a cup the size of her fist. Twice she had to stop and simply cling while a surge rolled through the channel mouth and tried to argue with her. Once she bailed and wept at the same time and saw no reason to do otherwise.

But the *Petrel* was a small, tough, well-built thing — Ada had chosen well, or the brother of the arrested clerk had, whoever had actually laid hands on her — and slowly, grudgingly, she came light. By the time the water sloshed only at her ankles, Marguerite had worked out her one remaining piece of arithmetic: with one oar, on the ebb, she could not row across the mouth of the strait. But she didn't have to. The ebb set southeast — and the internment landing, the guarded beach below the half-empty village, lay southeast. The current that would kill a fleeing smuggler was, read correctly, a ferry. Her mother had taught her that too, though she'd taught it as a warning. Warnings and directions are often the same sentence, said at different volumes.

She shipped the surviving oar, laid the swamped boat's spare board across the gunwale as a crude paddle-brace, and let the tide take her.

The last of the reef passed to port with a long sighing gurgle, and then it was open water and fog and the ebb carrying her southeast at a walking pace, faster than walking, and Marguerite sat low in the bilge with the compass against her ribs and the knife back in her belt and steered by the feel of current on the hull, the way you steer a sled by your seat. Her hands were ruined. Her shoulder screamed where the painter had wrenched it. Every inch of her was salt and cold and she had never in her life been so awake.

Somewhere in that grey mile she stopped shivering, and knew what that meant, and ate the biscuit the cook had packed, forcing it down with sips of the varnish-stripping liquor, because the body is a ledger too and hers was overdrawn. The biscuit tasted of nothing and saved her life. She chewed and steered and counted strokes of the current, and did not let herself think about warmth, or the counting house stove, or Ada's face, or the lamp-voice, which had gone quiet now, out-argued, paid off.

Then, all at once, the fog thinned — not lifting, just fraying, as if the land beyond were unraveling it thread by thread — and there it was.

The far shore. Low and black under a sky going the color of pewter, which was the color of the water the morning her mother last dove, and Marguerite took that for whatever it was. A line of barbed wire ran down to the beach where the old fish-flakes used to stand. A guard tower, unlit. Boats hauled up and padlocked in rows. And above the beach, the village roofs she knew from summers fifteen years gone, most of them dark, a few with shutters boarded, one chimney smoking thin and stubborn against the grey.

Home of a kind. Prison of a kind. Both at once, and the compass dry against her ribs, and the tide setting her gently, inevitably, toward the sand.

She shipped the board and took up the oar. Ahead, at the water's edge, small in the greying light, a figure stood watching her come — still as a piling, arms wrapped against the cold, and even at two hundred yards, even after fifteen years, even soaked and bent and older, she knew the set of those shoulders, because she had promised them something once and had crossed the sea at the bottom of the night to pay it.

The ebb pushed her ashore. The keel ground into sand. And Emeric Sato began, slowly, to walk down into the water toward her.
