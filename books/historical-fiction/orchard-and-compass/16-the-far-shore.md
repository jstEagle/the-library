## 16. The Far Shore

Emeric Sato had learned to sleep in pieces.

Not the whole cloth of sleep he'd known before the soldiers came — not the deep, unguarded hours when his father's hammering on the boatyard slips was the last sound he heard and the first. Those nights were gone, sold or seized along with everything else with a hull and a name. What he had now was something thinner: dozing in twenty-minute intervals, one ear open for boots on gravel, the way a man learns to sleep in a house where the roof leaks over his bed.

The internment zone had been a cannery once, before the government needed it for people instead of fish. The long sheds still smelled of brine and diesel, the bunk frames bolted where the gutting tables had stood. Three hundred souls when the transports began taking families east two weeks ago; fewer than ninety now, strung through the sheds like beads from a broken necklace. Every dawn another wagon came, and every dawn someone stood at the wire gate with their belongings tied in cloth, waving to the ones left behind until they were small enough to disappear into the fog.

His mother slept badly too. He could hear her breathing through the partition — that deliberate, measured breathing of a woman who has decided not to cry where her children can hear her. His sister Hana slept like the dead, which was its own kind of mercy. She was twelve and had decided, with the ruthless adaptability of children, that this was an adventure that had gone on slightly too long. Emeric envied her and hated himself for envying her.

He lay in the grey dark listening to the tide. You could hear it here even overland — the strait working against the bar at the harbor mouth, a long slow exhalation, the sound of water doing what water does regardless of who owned the shore. His grandfather's compass had been taken from him in September, along with the boats, but Emeric still knew the water the way he knew his own hands. Somewhere past that bar, past the point where the patrols turned back at dusk, was the trading post. And somewhere in the counting house there, behind whatever lockable thing she'd found, a girl he hadn't seen in five years was keeping a promise made to a fourteen-year-old boy who no longer existed.

*When it's safe,* he had told her. *Keep it till it's safe.*

It had never been safe. It had only ever been less dangerous by degrees, and then suddenly, all at once, the most dangerous object on either shore — because it proved what he was. Where he came from. That his family had crossed this water in both directions for three generations, trading, building, marrying into the orchard families on the far side, until the war fever arrived and made crossing itself a crime.

He should have told her to burn it. He'd thought about writing that — *burn it, forget me, keep yourself* — but the letter went out with the fisherman before he could find the courage, and what he'd written instead, in the cramped hand he'd been ashamed of since school, was: *bring it home.*

Selfish. He'd lain awake many nights since, turning that word over. Selfish as a man asking a drowning stranger to carry his gold.

The bell above the shed door rang at half past five.

It always rang at half past five. The guard — a heavy, red-faced man named Prosser who was not cruel, which somehow made it worse — walked the rows waking everyone for the day's waiting, because waiting was the work now. Wake, queue, count, wait. Eat if there was food. Wait again.

Emeric rose, folded his blanket into the regulation square, and stepped out into the dawn.

The fog had come down thick overnight. It lay over the compound like wet wool, beading on the wire, softening the guard tower into a smudge. Beyond the fence the ground fell away to the shingle beach, invisible this morning — you could stand ten feet from the water and hear it but not see it, the whole strait reduced to sound. The patrol boats would be blind in this. Everyone said so. Everyone said it quietly, in the way people say things they have no power to act on.

He got Hana up, walked her to the mess line, watched her eat her rice porridge with the appetite of someone who had not yet learned to taste fear. His mother sat with the Oshiro women, mending nets that belonged to no one anymore, because the boats were gone but the hands remembered their habits. The morning proceeded the way mornings did here: a rehearsal of ordinary life performed without props, without a stage, without an audience except the men in the tower.

At seven the transport horn sounded down the coast, and the compound did what it always did — flinched, collectively, like a body hearing its name called.

But the wagon didn't come. The horn sounded again at eight and faded away, and Prosser came down the row shaking his head: weather. No transport in fog like this. Another day of waiting granted to them like a gift, though none of them could decide whether more time was mercy or sentence.

Emeric spent the late morning at the fence line, ostensibly checking the net racks, actually watching the water he couldn't see. It was a habit he despised in himself. Looking south. Looking toward the post, toward the bridge that had been closed all season, toward a floorboard in a counting house where a promise lay wrapped in oilcloth waiting for him to have the nerve to claim it or the grace to let it go.

He was still there at noon when the dog started barking.

* * *

The compound kept a dog — a brindled, half-feral thing that belonged to nobody and everybody, that slept under the cookhouse and answered to nothing but its own appetite. It never barked at guards. It never barked at gulls. It barked twice in the whole time Emeric had been inside the wire, both times at things coming out of the water.

Now it stood at the north end of the shingle, hackles up, barking at the fog.

Emeric told himself he was going down to quiet the animal. He told himself a great many things in the four minutes it took him to walk the length of the beach, each of them a lie, because some part of him had understood from the moment the dog spoke exactly what was standing in the surf line, and had understood it with a certainty that felt older than hope.

She came out of the water the way no living person comes out of water — slowly, bent, one hand pressed flat against her thigh as though holding herself together, dragging each leg forward like cargo hauled up a ramp. Her clothes were black with sea. Her hair had come loose and hung in ropes. When she reached dry shingle she went down on her knees, not collapsing but deliberately, the way you kneel in a boat, and stayed there, breathing.

Behind him, distantly, the dog stopped barking. Behind him, distantly, someone shouted — Prosser's voice, then the duty officer's. He would remember later that he did not turn around. He would remember that the whole world narrowed to a woman kneeling on the stones with the fog closing behind her, and that every rule, every wall, every consequence the century had built waited politely outside that circle.

"Emeric."

Her voice was wrecked — salt-scraped, barely a voice at all, more the memory of one. But he knew it. Five years, and he knew it the way you know a chord struck once in childhood.

"Marguerite." He was moving now, running the last stretch of shingle, going down beside her. "God — Marguerite—"

"I lost the boat." She was shaking so hard the words came out in fragments, chattered between locked teeth. "Reef channel. Went through the reef channel — my mother's — I couldn't hold—" A cough took her, deep and wet, and she rode it out with her eyes shut. "Swam the last of it. Lost the boat."

"You swam—" He looked at the water behind her, featureless, indifferent, the water that had taken his father's crewmen and half his grandfather's fleet and, in a sense, her mother. "That's a mile of current. In winter. Alone."

"I know." She lifted her head. Her lips were blue-grey. Her eyes were absolutely clear. "I calculated it. Tides run slack at nine. I calculated it wrong by forty minutes." Something moved across her face that was almost a smile. "My mother would be furious."

He got an arm under her and hauled her upright, and she weighed nothing, or felt like nothing, because adrenaline makes birds of us all. Up the beach, boots on gravel — Prosser and two others, coming fast, shouting something about the wire, about regulations, about how in God's name—

"She's a trader," Emeric heard himself say, loud and steady, a voice he didn't recognize leaving his own mouth. "From the Meridian post. She came across to trade and got caught by the tide. She's half-drowned. Are you going to stand there or are you going to help?"

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Prosser — decent, dim, obedient Prosser, who had carried old Mrs. Oshiro's trunk to the wagon himself rather than let the younger guards handle it — swore under his breath and came down the shingle, and the world resumed its rules around the exception it had just permitted.

They got her into the cookhouse, stripped off the worst of the wet, wrapped her in blankets that smelled of diesel and fish. Someone built up the stove. Someone else — Hana, appearing from nowhere with the fearless competence of the young — put a cup of hot barley tea in the woman's hands and held it there until her fingers closed around it.

And Emeric knelt on the cookhouse floor in front of her, close enough to see what the years had done. She wasn't fourteen anymore. She wasn't even the clerk he'd imagined, ink-stained and precise, a figure in the company's ledgers. She was gaunt and salt-burned and older than her years, with lines around her eyes that hadn't come from paperwork, and she looked at him with an expression he couldn't read — appraisal, maybe, or grief, or the particular attention of someone measuring how much of a person survived the interval.

"You're taller," she managed.

"You're insane."

"Yes." The almost-smile again. "That's been noted."

He laughed — a short, broken sound that embarrassed him instantly, laughter in a place like this, with his father in a cell in Victoria and the wagons due any day. But she laughed too, or tried to, and the sound cracked in her chest, and then neither of them was laughing and neither of them was speaking and the stove ticked and the fog pressed white against the windows and five years sat in the room with them like a third person.

"I owe you an apology," he said finally. "For the letter. For asking. I should have told you to burn it."

"You should have." She drank the tea. Her hands were still trembling; the cup clicked against her teeth. "I considered burning it. Many times. I want you to know that."

"And?"

"And it isn't mine to burn." She set the cup down on the bench. And then she did something he had not expected, though he would spend years afterward understanding that he should have: she straightened, wincing, and began unwinding the sash at her waist — a wide belt of oiled canvas, soaked through, that she wore beneath her coat like a second skin.

Inside it, lashed against her body through a mile of winter current, was a knife with a bone handle worn smooth by another woman's grip, and below it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with tarred twine, a shape the size of a hymnal.

"It's yours," Marguerite said. "It was always yours. I was only keeping it."

She worked the twine loose with fingers that wouldn't quite obey her, and he found himself reaching out to help, and then the oilcloth fell open in both their laps and there it was.

His grandfather's compass. Brass case gone green-black at the seams, the glass crazed with one hairline crack, the needle swinging — he could see it swinging even now, even here — settling, steadying, pointing its patient north. The lid bore the engraving his grandfather had paid a Hong Kong silversmith to cut when Emeric's father was a boy: *SATO & SONS, BOATWRIGHTS, ALL POINTS TRUE.*

All points true.

He touched it the way you touch a relic, or a wound. It was warm. Of course it wasn't warm — it had spent the night against her body and then in the sea, it should have been cold as stone — but it felt warm, the way a word spoken in your childhood language feels warm.

"My mother kept it in her sewing box," he said, and his voice failed him on the second word, and he stopped, and started again. "After the raids started. She said — she said if they ever came for the house, the compass shouldn't be found on us. Because it proves we're boatbuilders. Generations of it. It proves we belong to the water more than the water belongs to anyone's empire." He swallowed. "She gave it to me the night before the arrests. I gave it to you the week after. I was fifteen. I didn't understand what I was asking."

"No," Marguerite said quietly. "You asked me to keep it safe until it was safe. And I've been waiting five years for it to become safe, and it never did, so eventually I understood that the promise wasn't conditional." She looked at him, and her clear grey eyes held something that was not pity and was not admiration and was larger than both. "It was just a promise."

Outside, the fog was thinning. He could see it thinning — the wire emerging from the white, the guard tower resolving into its hard geometry, the shingle brightening toward the color of bone. Somewhere down the coast the transport horn sounded a third time, closer now, and this time no one said *weather.* This time the compound began to move, the way bodies move when they hear their name called.

"They'll take us east on tomorrow's tide," Emeric said. "If the fog lifts. If the wagon comes." He turned the compass over in his hands. "You crossed a strait in winter to bring a boy a toy, and by the time you get home they may have taken everything it was meant to prove."

"That's why I brought it tonight." She leaned forward, and now her voice dropped, and the clerk surfaced in her — the precision, the ledger-keeper's economy. "Listen to me, because the fog is lifting and I have perhaps an hour before this beach fills with soldiers and questions I can't answer. Your grandfather didn't hide anything in this compass. Your father did. Before the arrests. Do you understand me? Open the case."

He opened it. The hinge fought him — swollen, salt-seized — and then gave, and the brass backing plate inside the lid sat where it had always sat, except that it sat a quarter-inch proud of its seating, the way a floorboard sits proud when something is beneath it.

He had grown up in a boatyard. He knew wood, he knew fit, he knew when a joint was telling the truth. His hands shook too badly to manage the plate, so Marguerite reached over and did it for him, prying it up with the edge of the diving knife, and beneath it, folded eight times into a space smaller than a cigarette tin, was paper.

He didn't unfold it there. Not in the cookhouse, not with Hana watching from the doorway and the horn sounding down the coast and the fog lifting off the water like a curtain rising on a play whose ending everyone already knew. He held it against his chest, this folded weight of eight-times-folded paper, and he looked at the girl who had carried it across a mile of killing water because a fifteen-year-old had once asked her to.

"Why?" he said. It was all he could manage. Why any of it. Why her.

Marguerite looked at the compass in his hands, and then out the fogged window toward the water she would have to cross again, and when she spoke her voice was very quiet.

"Because my mother drowned in that strait," she said, "and everyone told me the water takes things and doesn't give them back. And I decided that I would spend my life proving otherwise."

The horn sounded again, much closer. Boots on gravel outside the cookhouse. Marguerite pulled the blanket higher around her shoulders and became, in the space of a breath, simply a half-drowned trader caught by the tide — but her eyes met his over the rim of the tea cup, and in them was a message as clear as any flag:

*Whatever happens next — I'm not sorry, and I'm not finished.*

The door opened. A lieutenant Emeric had never seen stood in it, backlit by the brightening fog, with a requisition of questions in his face and the transport wagon rattling somewhere behind him down the coast road.

"You," the lieutenant said, looking directly at Marguerite. "Where exactly did you come from?"
