## 17. What the Compass Points To

The guard at the gate had stopped asking Emeric Sato's name. That was the first thing he noticed about the fourth morning — not the frost on the wire, not the smell of the latrine trench steaming in the cold, but the guard, a young man with chapped hands and a rifle he held like a tool he hadn't finished learning, who simply nodded him through when he came down to the water with his bucket.

"You're the boatbuilder's boy," the guard had said yesterday, almost apologetic, as if the arrest of a family were a weather event they were both enduring together.

"I'm the boatbuilder," Emeric said, and the words tasted strange, because his father was alive somewhere in a holding pen at the customs house, and the yard on the point was sealed with government paper and wax, and the boats were gone, all eleven of them, requisitioned or confiscated or simply taken, the verbs blurring the way official language blurs everything it touches. But the sentence had felt true anyway. Somebody had to be it.

Now he crouched at the tide line with his bucket and watched the strait turn from iron to pewter to something almost soft, and he thought about compasses.

His grandfather's compass sat in his coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, against his chest. It had arrived an hour before dawn in the hands of a girl who should have drowned getting it to him — that was the only honest way to say it. She'd come up out of the reeds below the internment fence like something the sea had decided to give back, soaked through, lips gray, hands so cold she couldn't work the knots on her own bundle, and she had pressed the compass into his palms and said, "I kept it. I'm sorry it took so long." As if fifteen years were a delay in correspondence. As if the whole weight of the war fever, the bridge closing, the confiscations, his father's arrest — as if any of that could be laid at her feet as lateness.

He had wanted to say a hundred things. He had said, "You're freezing," because he was a coward, and then he had wrapped her in the blanket his mother still had — one blanket, they had three left between five people — and sat with her while her shaking slowed, and neither of them had spoken, because what was there? *Thank you* was a coin too small for the debt. *I'm sorry* was worse. So they'd sat in the dark behind the cookhouse while a sentry smoked two posts away, and the compass had lain between them on the blanket like a third person who understood everything.

She'd left before first light. Refused the offer to stay hidden in the loft above his mother's kitchen — refused it fast, without explanation, which meant the explanation was dangerous. She'd said only: "Someone has to open the counting house tomorrow. If I don't come back across, they'll come looking over here." And then, standing at the reed line with the frost coming off the grass in breaths, she'd turned and looked at him the way you look at a house you grew up in when you know you won't live there again. "Emeric. Your grandfather's compass. Why did your father send it to me, all those years ago? Why not just hide it here?"

And he had said the truth, which was that he didn't know, that he'd been fourteen and the war fever had been a rumor then, that his father had pressed the brass case into the Lockridge girl's hands on the dock one evening in September and said only, *Keep this where no one will look for it, and give it back when it's safe,* and that Marguerite had said yes the way children say yes to solemn things, completely, without understanding the interest that promise would accrue.

She'd nodded once and gone into the grass, and he had stood there feeling the shape of something he couldn't yet name.

Now, sitting at the tide line with the bucket forgotten beside him, he unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

The compass was smaller than he remembered. Of course it was; he'd been fourteen, and everything from childhood arrives shrunken when you finally hold it again. A brass case the size of a pocket watch, the lid dented along one edge — he knew that dent, had been told the story a hundred times: his grandfather dropped it hauling timber the year the yard built its first schooner, and swore more at the wood than the brass. The needle under glass, the worn cardinal points, the hinge that opened with a sound like a small sigh.

It was, he thought, a poor thing to have crossed a strait for. A poor thing to have cost a woman her indenture — because he'd heard already, the news moving through the camp the way bad news moves, carried by fishermen who weren't supposed to talk to them: the Lockridge girl had been seen going into the water, had lost her skiff, had surfaced half-dead on the far shore. They said the company would be looking for her. They said worse things about what happened to people caught carrying anything across.

For this. For a compass worth perhaps two dollars at the trading post.

Except.

He was turning the case over in his hands, feeling for the hinge, when his thumbnail caught on something wrong. The base plate — the thin brass disc inside the lid, the one that lay against the card — sat proud. Not flush. He had opened this compass a thousand times as a boy, had pried at the plate out of boredom until his father caught him and went white and took it away for a week. He'd thought his father angry about the instrument. Now, watching his own fingers shake slightly, he understood that his father had been afraid.

The plate lifted. Beneath it, folded eight times, flattened by years of pressure into something like cloth, was paper.

He didn't unfold it at the tide line. Some instinct older than thought made him stand, make himself casual, glance down the fence line — the sentry was walking his post the other direction, shoulders up around his ears against the cold — and carry the compass and its secret back toward the row of tar-paper cabins where his family was quartered, past the mess queue, past the women boiling water, into the room his mother had partitioned with a hung sailcloth, where the morning light came through a crack in the boards and fell in a single bright line on the floor.

There, alone, he unfolded the paper.

* * *

It was a deed.

Not a copy — the original, the engrossed instrument, signed and sealed twenty-two years ago, transferring title of the Sato boatyard, its ways, its sheds, its waterfront lease, from Haruki Sato, shipwright, to Haruki Sato and heirs, freehold, registered at the land office in the county seat. His father's signature at the bottom, younger than Emeric had ever seen it, and beneath it the registrar's stamp, the embossed seal pressed so deep into the paper that he could feel it with his fingertip like braille.

He read it four times. The words swam and steadied and swam again, and each time they said the same thing: *the yard is ours.* Not leased. Not mortgaged to the Meridian Company the way half the waterfront was mortgaged, not held under some arrangement that a wartime order could dissolve like sugar in rain. Ours. Freehold. Registered. Recorded.

And untainted — that was the word his mother used later, when he showed her, when she held the deed at arm's length because her eyes were failing and her hands were not quite steady, and read it aloud in a voice that started strong and ended in a whisper. Untainted. Every other document the family owned was in the seized house or the sealed office or the confiscated ledgers — every bill of sale, every registration, every scrap of proof that the Satos had ever existed as owners of anything. The government men had taken paper the way they took boats: comprehensively, officially, with receipts. But this one page had spent fifteen years in another jurisdiction entirely, sleeping under a compass card in the counting house of the company itself, hidden in the one place no confiscation order would ever think to look — because why would the enemy's deed be in the enemy's strongroom?

Because his father had put it there. Because in September of a peaceful year, a man who trusted no future had looked at his fourteen-year-old son's friend — the clerk girl from across the water, serious beyond her years, the one who dove reefs like her mother — and had chosen her. Had handed her the compass with its buried heart and said, *keep this where no one will look for it.*

No one will look for it. And no one had. Fifteen years of audits and inventories and searches, and the deed had sat ten feet from Mr. Calloway's own ledger desk, guarded better than any vault could guard it, guarded by its own insignificance.

"Your grandmother's idea," his mother said. She had sat down on the bunk by then, the deed smoothed flat on her knee, her palm resting on it lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "She never trusted the land office after the burn. She said paper survives by being somewhere else. She said—" and here his mother's voice did the thing it did when she was quoting the dead, going slightly formal, slightly musical, "—she said *a promise is a place you can store a thing.*"

Emeric looked at her. "Marguerite didn't know."

"She couldn't have." His mother folded the deed along its old creases with great care. "If she'd known, she'd have crossed that water years ago, or died trying, or told someone, or—no. Your father chose well precisely because she didn't know. She kept the promise, not the prize. There's a difference, and the second kind of keeper sells you out eventually, and the first kind crosses a strait in winter."

He went outside. He needed air, or distance, or simply to move, because something was happening in his chest that had no name he knew.

* * *

Here is what Emeric Sato had believed since the night the soldiers came: that the past was over. That the world before the war fever — the yard with its smell of cedar and hot pitch, the schooners sliding down the ways, his father's voice calling measurements across the shed roof — that all of it belonged to a country that had ceased to exist, the way the bridge belonged to a season that wouldn't come again. Grief had taught him that lesson so thoroughly he'd stopped noticing it was a belief rather than a fact. You survive by assuming the loss is total. Totality, at least, is simple.

But the deed said otherwise. The deed said the past had been waiting, patient as seed, in a floorboard across the water. The past had been kept — not by strategy, not by hope even, but by a girl's plain fidelity to a sentence spoken on a dock when both of them were fourteen. *When it's safe, I'll bring it back.* It wasn't safe. She brought it back anyway. And inside the keeping, unknown to keeper and kept alike, had slept the only document in the world that could call the yard theirs again.

Faithfulness outlasted its own forgetting. That was the phrase his mind kept returning to, turning it over like the compass card, needle swinging and settling. His father had forgotten — had to have forgotten, or he'd have asked for the compass back in letters before the arrests; unless he hadn't forgotten but had counted it lost, written it off among the war's early casualties, one more thing the sea of events had swallowed. Either way, the promise had gone on working in the dark, doing its slow labor, for years, unattended, unpaid. A promise, his grandmother had said, is a place you can store a thing. He would add to it now, privately, walking the frozen mud of the compound road with the compass heavy in his pocket and the light rising off the mountains: *and sometimes the thing stores itself there too, and grows.*

What could it save? He made himself think like a clerk, coldly, because the question mattered too much to answer with his heart. The confiscations ran on paper — orders, inventories, liens. Everything the government had taken, it had taken by asserting the family had no provable claim: alien property, abandoned assets, seized pending review. Review. The word was everywhere on the notices nailed to the fence, and everyone in the zone had learned that *pending review* meant *never*, but review required documents, and documents could be produced. A freehold deed, original, sealed, registered, predating every order — carried to the right lawyer, filed at the right land office, waved in front of the right tribunal — it might not win. Nothing might win. But it was a weapon, and they had possessed no weapons at all until last night, when a half-drowned girl had walked out of the reeds carrying one wrapped in oilcloth.

And more than the yard. Names. A deed proved a family had been here, had built, had owned, had signed and been signed for — proof of existence, in a season designed to erase exactly that. His mother had understood it instantly, holding the paper to the light: "They can take the boats," she'd said. "They've taken the boats. But this says we were somebody before they decided what we were. Paper remembers. Paper is the memory that argues back."

* * *

In the afternoon he found his mother mending net in the cabin, and they planned the way their family had always planned anything — quietly, in fragments, with an ear cocked for boots on the boards.

"The lawyer in Port Ellery," his mother said. "Old Vasseur. He drew the deed. He'll be seventy if he's a day, but he argued the mill case against Meridian and lost only because the judge was bought, and he hates them for it. If anyone will touch our paper, it's him."

"He'll need to come to us, or we'll need to get to him, and I can't cross the fence line."

"No," his mother said. "But fish can." She tied a knot, pulled it tight. "Tomo Ishikawa runs herring through the checkpoint twice a week. The guards search his hold and find fish, because that's all there is. But Tomo owes your father thirty years of favors, and Tomo's daughter reads law books she buys from the seamen's mission, and nothing about that family does only one thing." She looked up. "We'll need a copy made first. Two copies. The original stays hidden — you understand? The original goes nowhere near a soldier. We hide it the way your father hid it. Somewhere no one will look."

"Where?"

His mother almost smiled. It was a ruined, exhausted, magnificent smile, the first he'd seen on her face in weeks. "Under the floorboards, where else? We are a family of the obvious, my son. We just bury it deep enough."

That evening he sat with his sisters while they did the homework the makeshift school assigned, and he let them believe he was reading, and instead he held the compass under the table and opened the lid and closed it, opened and closed, the small sigh of the hinge, the needle swinging north and settling north, always north, faithful to a pole none of them would ever see. His youngest sister, Reiko, seven years old and sharp as a new blade, glanced over.

"That's Grandfather's compass," she said. "Papa said it was lost."

"It was kept," Emeric said. "By a friend. Across the water."

"Did she bring it back because she missed us?"

He thought about how to answer that — about the strait, the storm, the patrol gunboat, the indenture, the counting house, the floorboard, the fifteen years. About a promise made by children and honored at the price of nearly everything. Reiko waited with the terrible patience of the young.

"She brought it back," he said, "because she said she would. Before you were born. Before Papa gave it to her, even — she promised him she'd keep it safe, and keeping it safe meant bringing it home."

Reiko considered this with the gravity of a judge. "That's a long time to remember a thing."

"Yes," Emeric said. "It is."

"And did she remember the whole time? Or just at the end?"

And there it was — the question he'd been circling since dawn, asked plainly by a seven-year-old over arithmetic. Did Marguerite Lockridge carry that promise consciously through fifteen years of ledgers and tides? Had she woken some mornings with the compass under her floorboard and felt its weight, or had it slept in her like everything sleeps in us, surfacing only when the water demanded it? He didn't know. He suspected she didn't either. He suspected it didn't matter. The promise had been remembered whether or not the rememberer remembered — faithfulness deeper than memory, stored in the body, in the floorboard, in the brass, waiting.

"Both," he said finally. "I think it's like the tide. It doesn't decide to come in. It just never fully goes out."

Reiko nodded slowly, satisfied, and returned to her sums, and Emeric sat in the lamplight with the compass open in his hands, and outside the sentry walked his post and the frost came down off the wire like breath, and somewhere across the black water a girl who should have drowned was opening a counting house as if nothing had happened, keeping a ledger that did not know yet what it contained.

Two copies. Tomo Ishikawa. Old Vasseur in Port Ellery. Hide the original.

It was a small plan, a fragile plan, a plan made of fish and old men and floorboards — and it was more than they'd had yesterday. He closed the compass. The hinge sighed. North settled in the glass, steady as a held breath.

Then, near midnight, boots on the compound road — two pairs, unhurried, official — stopping at the door of the cabin, and a voice with the flat courtesy of a man delivering bad news regularly saying his family's name through the boards.

"Emeric Sato. Open the door. We have questions about your visitors."

Across the water, dawn was hours away. Here, the lamp flickered once, as if the flame itself had drawn breath.
