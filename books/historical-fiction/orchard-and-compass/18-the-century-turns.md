## 18. The Century Turns

The fog came back for her on the return crossing, as if the strait had decided she'd suffered enough.

Marguerite crouched in the bilge of the borrowed skiff, one hand on the tiller, and let the current do what her mother had promised it would. The reef channel ran beneath her like a dark vein. She knew it by sound alone now — the hiss of water over coral, the deep-throated boom where the swell broke against the outer shelf — because her eyes had stopped being useful an hour after midnight and her hands had gone past numb into something that belonged to someone else.

She was soaked through. She was shaking so hard the compass case rattled against her ribs where she'd lashed it inside her coat. But it was empty now, or nearly — she'd given Emeric the deed along with the compass, watched him open the brass lid by lantern light and go very still, the way a man goes still when the ground shifts under him. His mother's hands on her face. His sister's arms around her neck. The whole half-empty village gathered in the guardhouse yard at dawn like people waiting for a train that might never come, and then her, dripping on the gravel, holding out a dead man's compass to a living boy.

*Return it when it's safe,* she'd said at fourteen. Nothing about the world had been safe since. She'd kept the promise anyway. That was all any of it ever was: doing the thing you said you'd do, long after the person who heard you say it forgot why it mattered.

The far shore came up gray and shapeless. She found the gap in the reeds by memory, grounded the skiff in the shallows with a grinding crunch that would cost Ada another forged entry to explain, and waded the last thirty yards with the water at her thighs, then her waist, then her knees as the bottom rose to meet her.

The dock at Port Ellerslie stood black against the graying sky. New Year's Eve. Somewhere behind her, across the water, the internment zone was waking to its first morning of 1900, and here the town would sleep until noon, and the counting house ledgers waited in their drawers with every debt in the territory entered fair and square except the ones that counted.

She came up the ladder rungs of the dock pilings on legs that barely answered, and stood, and looked down the length of the boards.

Calloway was sitting on a bollard at the far end.

He had a lantern at his feet, turned low, and his coat collar up, and he was watching her with the particular patience of a man who has been cold for a long time and has decided not to mind. He did not stand. He did not speak. He simply sat there in the blue-gray hour before dawn, at the end of a century, at the end of a wet plank walk, waiting for her to come to him.

Marguerite's first thought, absurdly, was of the ledger. *One skiff, company property, returned damaged. One clerk, absent from quarters, fourteen hours.* Her second thought was that she had left the diving knife in the reeds, shoved under a tussock, and some part of her grieved it like a limb.

Her third thought was: *he waited.*

Not the soldiers. Not the patrol. Him.

"You'll want to wring that coat out," Calloway said, "before you hang it. Salt ruins the wool."

She walked toward him. Each step was a negotiation with her own body. The boards were slick with frost and she gripped them with her toes inside her ruined boots and thought, distantly, that if she fell he would probably catch her, and that this fact made everything worse rather than better.

"How long have you been here?" Her voice came out scraped.

"Since eleven." He glanced at the lantern. "I brought two candles' worth of oil and used most of it deciding whether I was a fool."

"Were you?"

"I haven't finished the calculation." He stood then, unhurried, and she saw that under his coat he was holding a folder — the stiff government-issue kind, tied with the red string the agents used. Her name was on the tab. She could read it upside down at twenty feet. *LOCKRIDGE, M. — RE: UNUSUAL ACTIVITY, COUNTING HOUSE.*

"Ada's requisition," Marguerite said. "The skiff."

"The skiff. The cook's stores. Tide charts in a hand I didn't recognize. A clerk who saw you burning something in the stove last Tuesday and was too frightened to say your name, which is how I know it was you." He ticked them off like ledger entries, quiet, without heat. "You've been careless, Marguerite. Or you stopped caring which. I haven't decided which concerns me more."

"And the soldiers?"

"There are no soldiers on this dock at this hour because I did not call them." He said it flatly, and let it sit between them. "There is a report in my hand that could be on an agent's desk by eight o'clock. There is a boat in the reeds that belongs to my company. There is a woman standing in front of me who crossed open water in winter, alone, at night, to keep a promise to a family the government has declared enemies of the state." He paused. "Tell me I'm wrong about any of it."

The fog moved. Somewhere out in the strait a buoy bell clanked, patient as a heartbeat.

"You're not wrong," Marguerite said.

"No," Calloway agreed. "I'm never wrong about the accounts. It's people I get wrong, occasionally, when I want to." He looked down at the folder, and his thumb moved once along the red string, and stopped. "Do you know what happens to indentured clerks convicted of aiding enemy aliens? Because I do. I've read the statutes twice. It isn't prison, mostly. It's the debt transferred to whoever takes custody of you, and custody goes to the lowest bidder. You'd spend the rest of your life paying a stranger for the privilege of existing."

"I know what happens."

"Do you." Not a question. He studied her face the way he studied a column of figures that wouldn't balance — looking for the error, or maybe for the proof that there wasn't one. "Fifteen years I've watched you audit this company's debts down to the halfpenny. Fifteen years. I promoted you over men with seniority because your columns never lied to me. And the entire time, you were keeping a second set of books, weren't you. Somewhere in that precise head of yours. Debts owed to a drowned woman and a boy across the water, and interest accruing, and you never once entered it where anyone could see it."

"Yes," she said.

"Why?"

It was such a small question for such a large hour. The sky was going from gray to pearl at the eastern edge. In the town behind them, faintly, a church began to toll — not midnight yet, but the old year dying in installments, bell after bell across the harbor parishes.

Marguerite thought about lying to him. She was too tired and too cold and too far past the far shore of her own life to manage it.

"Because my mother drowned carrying other people's things," she said, "and I promised her I wouldn't die owing any. Because Emeric Sato's grandfather gave that compass to a fourteen-year-old girl on a beach, and everyone who could have witnessed that promise is dead or arrested or across the water, and if I don't keep it, it stops having ever been made. That's why. You wanted a reason in the accounts. There it is. Enter it however you like."

Calloway said nothing for a long moment. The bells went on. She counted six of them, seven, and realized they weren't tolling the year out at all — they were ringing it in, the parish clocks running ahead, or the century arriving early, impatient, the way everything arrived eventually whether you were ready or not.

1900. She had crossed the strait in one century and stood on this dock in another, and the only witness was the man who owned her debt.

"I offered you freedom once," Calloway said. "A month ago. Inform on the correspondence, and the last year of your indenture dissolves. You refused. I've spent a month wondering what kind of fool refuses her own liberty." He held up the folder. "And now you cross open water in winter for a compass, and I finally understand. It isn't loyalty. It isn't treason. It's bookkeeping. You keep your promises the way I keep my ledgers. To the halfpenny. Even when the account is ruinous." He almost smiled; it was a terrible thing to see, that almost. "God help me, it's the only kind of integrity I've ever actually believed in."

"What will you do?"

He untied the red string.

The sound of it — silk sliding off paper — was louder than the bells. Marguerite stood absolutely still while he opened the folder, took out the single sheet within, the clerk's careful unsigned statement of unusual activity, and held it over the lantern.

"Burn it," she said. Not a request. A dare, or a prayer.

"That," said T. Calloway, factor of the Meridian Trading Company, "is the general idea."

The paper caught, curled, went to ash on the frozen boards. He ground it under his heel with the thoroughness of a man closing a ledger, and then he closed the empty folder and put it inside his coat, and the two of them stood there in the smoke-smell and the bell-noise and the coming daylight, and neither of them moved.

Then he extended his hand.

Marguerite looked at it. A dry hand, warm from the pocket, the hand that had signed her indenture fifteen years ago and could have signed her destruction tonight and chose, instead, at the turn of the century, on a freezing dock, to shake hers.

"It isn't a pardon," Calloway said quietly. "Understand that. If I ever find you've traded company secrets across that water — cargo, prices, anything with a figure attached — I will burn you myself, and I'll sleep fine afterward. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is me acknowledging a debt. Yours to the Satos, mine to the best clerk I ever had. We're even, as of this moment. Don't make me regret the arithmetic."

She took his hand.

His grip was firm and brief and human, and it was all three things at once — the pardon, the accusation, the admission that he had watched her for fifteen years and understood her better than she'd ever wanted anyone to. When he let go, her fingers ached where the cold had already gotten into them, and she couldn't have said which ache was which.

"The skiff," she said. "It's holed below the waterline. I'll work it off—"

"You'll do nothing of the kind. Ada's requisition was flawless. As far as the company is concerned, that skiff ran aground on a routine survey." He picked up his lantern. "Go home, Marguerite. Change out of those clothes before you lose the feet. The household will want to see you alive."

He walked up the dock toward the town, small against the brightening water, and did not look back.

Marguerite stayed where she was. The bells had finished. The new century lay over the strait like the fog lifting — thin, uncertain, full of weather to come. Across the water, invisible now in the haze, a boatyard deed slept inside a brass compass, and a boy she had promised nothing to and everything was reading it by lamplight, and somewhere in the reeds her mother's knife waited for the tide to give it back.

She flexed her burned-cold fingers, once, and started up the dock after the man who had just freed her without saying so.

There would be terms. There were always terms. But the ledger, for the first time in fifteen years, felt like it might be turning pages instead of weighing her down — and tomorrow, or the day after, she intended to read exactly what price he meant to write in it.
