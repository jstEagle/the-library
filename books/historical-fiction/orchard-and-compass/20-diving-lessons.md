## 20. Diving Lessons

The tide taught patience before my mother ever did.

I learned that on the bluff above the strait, at six years old, watching her stand at the water's edge with her arms folded and do nothing — nothing but watch the sea pull back from the reef like a curtain drawn by a careful hand. I had wanted to run straight into it, into all that glittering exposed world of anemones and crab holes and the black spines of urchins. She had held my wrist without looking down.

*The water will tell you when,* she said. *Your job is to listen.*

Twenty-two years later, in the first week of April, I stood on a different shore — the shingle beach below the Sato boatyard — with my arms folded, doing nothing, while a fourteen-year-old girl fidgeted beside me and tried very hard not to be impatient and failed completely.

Hana Sato had Emeric's jaw and her mother's dark, level eyes, and she had been awake since before dawn because I told her to be, and she had not once asked why. That was promising. That was the whole test, actually, and she was passing it without knowing there was one.

"Is something supposed to be happening," she said. It was not quite a question.

"The water is happening," I said. "Watch."

The strait was running out. The ebb pulled the surface into long silver seams, and below them, where the reef lay just under the skin of the sea, the water went pale and then glassy and then, for the space of ten breaths, perfectly still — a window opening in the current. The old divers had called it the door. My mother had never given me a better word for it than that.

"There," I said.

Hana leaned forward. "It stopped."

"It didn't stop. It turned." I crouched and drew in the wet shingle with a stick: a curve for the shore, a ragged line for the reef, an arrow for the ebb. "The tide comes through the strait like a river, faster than you'd believe. Out there—" I tapped the deep channel between the islands— "it runs four knots on the flood. A strong swimmer can't make headway against it. You'd swim all day and go backward. But here—" the ragged line— "the reef breaks the current. There are gaps in it, channels your grandmother worked before either of us was born. Inside those gaps the water goes slack twice a day, maybe twenty minutes each time, maybe less. That's when you dive. Not when it's calm-looking. When it's *stopped*-looking. Calm is a lie the surface tells."

She watched the window in the water close — the seams returning, the pale patch over the reef beginning to comb and ripple as the flood found its way back in. She watched it the way my mother would have wanted: not with fear, exactly, but with the particular attention that is the beginning of respect.

"My grandmother dove there?" Hana said. "A woman?"

"Women dive better than men. We're built colder and we're built meaner about it." I said it deadpan and was rewarded with the first almost-smile of the morning. "My mother learned from her mother. I learned from mine. Nobody wrote any of it down, because paper drowns and memory doesn't. So today we start with what you can carry in your head."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we start again. And the day after. Until you can find the door blind, in fog, by the feel of the water on your face." I stood and brushed the shingle from my knees. "That's the whole curriculum. It takes years."

"We don't have years," Hana said quietly. "The boats are gone. Papa's still—" She stopped herself. Even now, four months after the releases and the rescinded orders and the slow, humiliating return of the confiscated skiffs, she did not say *arrested* out loud on this beach. The word was still too heavy to lift.

"No," I agreed. "You don't have years. So we'll waste none of them standing here." I nodded at the water, where the flood was building toward its full voice. "Lesson one ends now. Lesson two is lunch, and while you eat, you're going to recite the channels back to me until you hate them. Then, when the tide makes its next door—" I checked the sun's height against the ridge of the far island— "you're going to float above the first gap and look down at the garden your grandmother planted, and you're not going to touch anything. Floating only. Can you float?"

"I grew up on the water."

"That's not what I asked."

"Yes," she said, with Emeric's stubbornness. "I can float."

"Good. Because the reef forgives nothing, and neither will I. Your grandmother worked these channels for thirty years and she lost two sisters to them. Respect first. Everything else after."

I said *your grandmother* and meant it, though she was no blood of mine and I no blood of hers. Inheritance, I was learning, is a thing you can hand across a beach like a bucket, if both hands are willing.

* * *

The boatyard smelled of cedar shavings and fresh tar, and it was the best smell in the world that spring, because it was the smell of a thing un-drowned.

They had reopened it in February — Emeric and his mother and whatever neighbors could be spared from rebuilding their own lives — working under the recovered deed like men and women walking on ground that might still open beneath them. The government had returned the property on paper in January; the actual possession had taken longer, required a magistrate's visit, an inventory of every tool, and a final signature from a factor of the Meridian Company attesting that the deed was genuine.

Calloway had signed it himself. He had come across on the mail packet, stood in the half-ruined yard with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, read the deed twice, and signed it without a word beyond "The ink is dry." Then he'd looked at me — I had crossed with him, my first time back inside the internment zone since December, traveling now on free papers that still felt like someone else's clothes — and he'd said, "Lockridge. Your ledger is still open. Don't imagine otherwise."

He hadn't meant it as a threat. That was the strange thing about Calloway: even his warnings came wrapped in a kind of rough guardianship. He had burned my report in December with bells ringing, and neither of us had ever spoken of it, and we never would. Some pardons only work if they stay silent.

Now I climbed the yard's wooden stair from the beach and found Emeric planing a stem piece, shirt-sleeved despite the chill, sawdust gold in his hair. He looked up, saw Hana dripping beside me, and set the plane down.

"Well?" he said.

"She found the first gap on her second try," Hana said, before I could answer. "Marguerite says most people take a week."

"Marguerite is generous with praise and stingy with everything else, so if she said it, it's true." Emeric came to the rail and looked down at his sister with an expression I recognized — the one I used to catch on my own mother's face when she thought I wasn't watching: pride and terror holding each other up. "Did she tell you about the sisters who drowned?"

"She told me."

"And did it scare you?"

"A little."

"Good," Emeric and I said together, and then looked at each other, and something passed between us that needed no ledger entry at all.

The yard was half what it had been. Two of the three sheds were roofless skeletons, and the slipway was silted, and the great adze-marks of the old builders were weathered gray on the timbers that remained. But the frame of a new boat stood in the middle of it all, ribs rising like the skeleton of some patient whale, and around it the noise of hammers went up all day, and boys who had spent a winter behind wire now carried planks on their shoulders with their chins high.

Emeric walked me along the frame while Hana went to help their mother boil pitch. "We've got the deed, we've got the tools they didn't burn, and we've got orders," he said. "Two fishing boats from families up the inlet, and — this is the part you won't believe — a commission from Meridian."

"From *Meridian*."

"Calloway sent a man last week. The company needs lighters for the summer freight run, and apparently every other builder on the coast is booked or burned out or both." Emeric smiled crookedly. "He specified in writing that the work is awarded 'on commercial merit alone.' His clerk must have written that sentence three times to get it to sound sincere."

"He means it sincerely," I said. "That's the terrible thing about him. He means everything kindly and it damns him anyway." I touched the nearest rib, the cedar smooth as bone. "Take the commission, Emeric. Take his money and build his lighters and let every plank remind him what he did and didn't do. That's better justice than a trial."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I think about everything a lot. It's the curse of clerks." I looked out at the strait, blue and innocent in the afternoon light, hiding its four-knot river and its reef teeth and the whole geography of grief underneath. "Besides, a company that owes you money is a company that has to keep talking to you. Trust me. I spent fifteen years learning how debts work."

* * *

The lessons settled into a rhythm, the way tides do.

Every third day, when the ebb made its door in the early morning, Hana and I went down to the shingle. I taught her the way my mother taught me: never by telling, always by showing once and then making her show me back. She learned the count of breaths that warmed the body before immersion — thirty slow ones, no more, because hyperventilating fools the diver and drowns her. She learned to equalize her ears with a swallow and a wiggle of the jaw. She learned to drop through the door at slack water and let the flood lift her home afterward, riding the current like a hand under the small of the back.

And she learned the garden.

That was my mother's word for the reef flats: the *garden*, where the pearl oysters grew in their scattered beds like rows only the diver could see. My mother had mapped it all in her head — which gap opened onto which bed, which bed the oysters thinned in cold springs, which rocks sheltered octopus that would steal your catch and your knife both if you let them. I had carried that map through fifteen years of ledgers and counting-house dust, through an indenture and a storm and a crossing that had nearly ended me in the reef channel, and I gave it away now piece by piece, and the strange thing — the thing I had not expected — was that giving it away made it heavier, not lighter. Every fact I handed to Hana became more real by becoming shared. Knowledge kept is a rumor. Knowledge given is a fact.

"Here." I surfaced beside her one bright May morning, the oyster bed ghostly below us in twelve feet of clear water. "See how they lie? Not clustered. Spaced. Like orchard trees." The word arrived from somewhere deeper than memory — from my mother's voice, twenty-five years gone, saying it in this same water. *Like an orchard, little bird. You don't strip an orchard. You tend it.* "You take one in three. Never the brood stock, never the small ones. If you strip the bed, you're not a diver, you're a thief robbing your own grandchildren."

"Grandmother's rules?" Hana asked, treading water.

"Mother's rules. Same rules, probably. They travel." I spat salt and grinned at her, and she grinned back, and for a moment the two drowned women were so present in the water between us that I nearly looked around to greet them.

By June, Hana could hold four minutes. By July, she brought up her first oyster — a small one, wrong season, which we put back — and screamed her triumph underwater, which cost her half her air and was worth it. Emeric stood on the beach with his arms crossed, pretending to inspect a hull, watching us the entire time.

One evening in late July, he walked me up the hill road toward the cottage where I lodged with the widow Okada, and halfway up he stopped.

"My mother wants to give you a share," he said abruptly. "Of the yard. Not wages. A share. She's had the paper drawn."

"Emeric—"

"Don't argue yet. Hear the reasoning, because it's hers, not mine, and she'll want it reported accurately." He ticked points off on sawdust-white fingers. "One: you have knowledge the yard's divers need, and knowledge should own equity or it walks away. Two: you saved our name off the bottom of the sea, and my mother does not permit debts to stand. Three—" he hesitated, and his ears went faintly red— "three, she says a family is a thing you build, not a thing you're born into, and she's decided you're building materials."

The hill road, the evening light, the smell of tar and cut cedar rising from the yard below. I thought of a counting house on the far shore, and a household of clerks who had voted with forged requisitions and packed lunches and silence, and a factor who had waited on a dock with a report in his pocket while the century turned.

"I have a family already," I said. "Two of them, apparently. I'm richer than any clerk has a right to be."

"So that's a yes."

"It's a yes." I laughed, surprising myself. Fifteen years of measured entries, of a life owed hour by hour to a company ledger, and now shares in a boatyard and a diving apprentice and a widow's spare room. Freedom, it turned out, was not one grand thing. It was a pile of small owned things, accumulated like an orchard grows: slowly, ring by ring.

"One condition," I added.

"Name it."

"Hana dives the deep channel before she's sixteen, and I want you to hate it. I want you to stand on that beach every single time, arms crossed, terrified, saying nothing. Because that's what my mother did for me, and it's the finest gift anyone ever gave me — being trusted enough to be allowed to be afraid."

Emeric looked at me for a long moment. "You're a strange woman, Marguerite Lockridge."

"I'm a self-owned woman," I said. "It comes with strangeness."

* * *

August brought the first launch.

They named her the *Kestrel*, after a bird that hangs motionless in wind, and the whole village came down to the slipway to see her take water. Hana wore her good dress and stood beside me in the crowd, and when the bottle broke and the hull slid sweet and true off the ways, she grabbed my hand and squeezed it hard, and neither of us pretended it was for balance.

Afterward, in the golden mess of the celebration — someone had produced a fiddle, someone else had produced sake of uncertain provenance — Hana pulled me aside behind the new shed, and I knew from her face that something had come in the post.

"This came for you," she said, and held out an envelope. "The ferryman brought it up. It's from across the water."

The handwriting on the front was precise, upright, unmistakably a counting hand. Miss M. Lockridge. No title, no company name — he wouldn't put that in writing, not after December — but I knew the pen strokes the way I knew the sound of the tide turning.

I opened it there in the shadow of the shed, with fiddle music on one side and the sea on the other.

Three lines. Calloway wasted nothing.

*Miss Lockridge — Ada Quill has resigned her position effective month's end, citing family reasons, and the household asks after you more than is professional. The autumn audit approaches, and certain entries remain unsettled. If you happen to be crossing on the September packet, I will consider it a business call.*

Below the signature, smaller, almost grudging, in the manner of a man adding a postscript he'd rather not be caught having written:

*The garden by the harbor gate bloomed this year. First time since the storm. Thought you'd want it entered somewhere.*

I read it twice. Hana watched my face with a diver's patience.

"Bad news?" she asked.

"No," I said slowly, folding the letter along its counted creases. "An invoice. From a friend who insists he isn't one." I looked out across the strait, at the far shore hazed blue in the evening light, where a bridge was being rebuilt and a counting house stood with its lamps lit and a woman named Ada had done something brave enough to resign over, and a garden — *our* garden, the one the clerks' household had planted in the crack between the harbor gate and the seawall, the one I'd left weeded the morning I crossed — had bloomed without me.

"When's the September packet?" Hana asked.

"The ninth." I tucked the letter into my belt, where the knife used to ride. "I have debts to settle."
