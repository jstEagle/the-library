## 21. The Ledger Closed

The counting house smelled different in spring.

Ada Quill noticed it the moment she pushed open the door on the last Monday of April — salt and cut grass instead of coal smoke, the long windows flung wide for the first time since the government men had come through with their inventories and their lists. Sunlight fell across the counting table in bars, and the dust motes rose in it like something being released.

She had kept the ledgers alone for five weeks now. Five weeks since Marguerite had walked out of this room with her mother's knife at her belt and nothing else to her name, and Calloway had stood at the window watching the strait until dark, and no one in the household had said a word about where she'd gone or why, because everyone already knew, and knowing was its own kind of silence — the good kind, the kind you keep for someone rather than against them.

Five weeks of entries in Ada's hand only. Her figures were neat but they were not Marguerite's figures. Marguerite's columns had a way of leaning slightly leftward, as if even numbers wanted to go somewhere. Ada's stood up straight and stayed put.

She was balancing the fuel accounts when the door opened behind her, and she knew without turning who it was, because Calloway never knocked and the clerks never came in that quietly, and besides — there was a way Marguerite had of entering a room now, like someone who had paid for the right to.

"You're supposed to be across the water," Ada said, still writing.

"The boatyard can spare me a day." Marguerite came around the end of the table and set down a canvas bag, and out of it, one by one, she took things: a pair of boots, salt-stiffened, laces gone; a tin of tea; a folded requisition form; a small paper-wrapped parcel that clinked faintly, like coins, though Ada suspected it wasn't coins. She laid them all in a row along the counting table's edge, deliberate as an auditor laying out evidence.

"What is this," Ada said. It wasn't really a question.

"Settlements." Marguerite pulled the other stool over and sat, and it was strange how natural that was, how the two stools had always sat side by side at this table and how the room seemed to exhale at the return of the arrangement, like a joint finding its socket again. "I've been keeping a ledger of my own. Across the water. It occurred to me I owe this household more than I ever owed the company."

"Marguerite—"

"Humor me. I'm good at this part." She opened the big ledger to a fresh page and ruled a line down it with the bone folder from the drawer — *her* old drawer, Ada realized, which nobody had touched since January, which Ada had cleaned out and re-cleaned out and finally just left as it was, the pencil stubs and the tide almanac and the pressed flower from the orchard above the strait that Marguerite had never explained and Ada had never asked about.

Marguerite wrote a heading: *Household Debts — M. Lockridge, debtor.* Then she began, aloud, in the flat clear voice she used for reading figures:

"To A. Quill: one pair of boots, borrowed November last, worn through the soles crossing the reed flats. Value, four dollars. Interest, compounded by guilt, considerable." She glanced up. "I meant to return them before winter. You know that."

"I know you stole them," Ada said, "which is worse and better."

"I'll pay for new ones."

"Don't you dare. Those boots crossed open water. They're worth more ruined than new." Ada picked them up, turned one over, looked at the split sole and the dried salt crusted white along the seams. She had spent most of February telling herself Marguerite was dead, and here was the proof otherwise, written in leather. She set them back down gently, the way you'd handle a letter that mattered. "Keep them. Consider them a gift against services rendered."

"That's not how a ledger works."

"No," Ada agreed. "But it's how a family works, and you taught me the difference."

Marguerite looked at her a moment too long, then bent back to the page. Her pen moved. *Boots: forgiven.*

* * *

The tea went next. "To the kitchen, Mrs. Okafor's account: forty-one nights of supper kept warm past closing, without asking why I was late, without asking where I went, without asking anything at all, which was the whole of it." The parcel of coins — it was coins after all, Ada could see the edge of a silver dollar through the paper — went into the center of the table. "Mrs. Okafor will say the food was company food and cost me nothing. Mrs. Okafor will be lying. Pay her anyway."

"She'll take it and then cook you a dinner worth twice that and refuse to let you leave until you've eaten it."

"I'm counting on it."

Then the requisition form. Marguerite smoothed it flat on the wood, and Ada felt her chest tighten looking at it — the Meridian letterhead, the forged signature in the factor's style, *one skiff, survey work, upper channel,* dated the night of the second crossing. The document that had put every one of them at risk. The document Ada had inked herself at midnight while Marguerite stood in the doorway dripping fog.

"That one isn't yours to settle," Ada said. "That's mine."

"It's both of ours. That's what I'm trying to get at." Marguerite tapped the forged signature. "You committed treason with a fountain pen. For me. Without me asking. Do you understand what that did to me? I spent fifteen years owing this company everything — my food, my bed, my apprenticeship, my mother's debts. I thought I understood debt. And then you handed me a skiff I hadn't earned, signed by a man who'd have flogged us both if he'd caught us, and I couldn't enter it anywhere. It didn't fit in any column. It broke my arithmetic."

"Good," Ada said. "Your arithmetic needed breaking. You counted yourself like inventory."

Marguerite's jaw worked. Outside, a gull went over the roof, crying once.

"I did," she said finally. "For fifteen years. Line by line. Wages minus board minus interest minus penalty, and every year the remainder smaller, and every year believing the remainder was *me* — that whatever was left over when the company finished subtracting was all there was of Marguerite Lockridge." She turned the ledger around so Ada could see the fresh page, the neat entries, the boots forgiven, the tea accounted. "This is the first book I've kept where I'm the debtor and the debt is love. It doesn't balance, Ada. It can't. Every entry comes out wrong — I owe more than I can pay, and the people I owe it to keep forgiving it, and forgiveness isn't a number."

"So what do you do with a ledger that won't balance?"

"You close it anyway. You write the last line honestly and you stop." Marguerite wet her pen. At the bottom of the page, beneath the requisition entry, beneath *silences kept, uncountable, value beyond recovery*, she wrote: *Carried forward: all of it. Unpayable. Accepted.*

* * *

They were still sitting there — Ada with the cold tea Marguerite had poured from the kitchen urn, Marguerite with her sleeves rolled like she'd never left — when the outer door opened and Mr. Calloway came in with his hat in his hand, which he did only when he was about to be either kind or terrible, and Ada had learned in eleven years that with Calloway those were often the same act wearing different coats.

He stopped when he saw Marguerite. For a long moment nobody spoke. The sunlight moved on the floor between them like something cautious.

"You're off the company rolls," he said at last. "Officially. As of March. There was paperwork."

"I know. I read the paperwork."

"Then technically you're trespassing in my counting house."

"Technically," Marguerite agreed, "I'm settling accounts."

His eyes went to the ledger, to the row of objects, to the boots and the coins and the forged requisition lying there in daylight like contraband — which it was, still, though the war fever had passed enough by April that contraband had begun to mean less, the patrols thinned, the island families drifting home to their half-ruined village. Ada watched him look at the forged signature and watched him recognize his own hand in it, copied well enough to sting, and watched him decide — visibly, the decision moving through his face like weather — not to mention it.

"There's one entry missing," he said instead.

Marguerite frowned. "What?"

He crossed to the shelf, took down the master indenture ledger — the great black book, the one with her name in it fifteen years deep — and carried it to the table with both hands, the way you carry something heavy that has also been a burden. He opened it to her page. Ada had seen that page a hundred times without seeing it: *Lockridge, M., apprenticed [date], debt inherited [sum], terms, penalties, carried forward, carried forward, carried forward,* year after year after year, the remainder shrinking by fractions, a life amortized.

Calloway took the pen from Marguerite's hand — took it gently, almost formally, the way you accept a sword offered hilt-first — and dipped it, and in the final column, beneath the last carried-forward figure of the last year of her indenture, he wrote four words in his precise factor's script:

***Paid in full.***

He blotted it. Closed the book. Set it on the table between them.

"For the record," he said, "that's not mercy. Mercy would have been releasing you in January when you were standing in my office lying to my face with your chin up, and I nearly did, God help me, because I've managed clerks for thirty years and I know the ones worth keeping, and you were the best I ever had. No — this is arithmetic. An employee who will cross a strait in flood tide for a promise made at fourteen is worth more unowned than owned. Any factor who can't see that deserves the company he keeps." He put his hat back on, which meant he was leaving, which meant the speech was over. "The boatyard's deed held. I heard. Good. Someone in this territory ought to own something outright."

He got as far as the door.

"Mr. Calloway." Marguerite stood. "The report you burned. The night of the bells. I never—"

"Don't," he said, without turning. "It's entered nowhere. What's entered nowhere didn't happen." A pause at the threshold, his hand on the frame, the light from outside making a bright edge of him. "Though I'll say this much, since we're settling accounts. Fifteen years I watched you count other people's debts down to the farthing and never once ask what you were owed. Worst bookkeeping I ever saw. Best character." He stepped out into the spring air, and his voice came back through the open door, dry as ever: "Lock the ledger when you're done. Some things should stay closed."

* * *

Afterward they sat in the quiet a while, the two of them, the black book between them on the table like a headstone that had learned to breathe.

Ada ran her thumb along the cover's edge. "Fifteen years," she said. "And it ends in four words."

"It doesn't end." Marguerite was looking at the fresh page of her own ledger, the one with the boots and the tea and the silences, the column that would never balance. "It closes. Ending is when nothing follows. Closing is when everything does — it's just not in this book anymore." She reached out and turned the master ledger toward the wall, spine out, done. Then she took up her pen one more time and, at the very bottom of the household page, below *accepted*, added a single line that made Ada's throat tighten:

*Debtor discharged. Gratitude carried to new account.*

"Which account?" Ada asked.

Marguerite smiled, and it was her mother's smile, Ada realized — the one from the photograph in the drawer, the diver, the woman the sea had kept — aimed at some horizon neither of them could see yet.

"The one I haven't opened," she said. "There's a hillside above the strait that used to be an orchard. Emeric's grandmother planted it, before. And there are children over there who think the reef is just rocks, and a boatyard that needs hands, and—" she stopped, laughed a little at herself, at the size of it, "—and a century starting. Somebody ought to keep its books properly. Somebody who knows what a person is worth."

She stood, and buttoned her coat, and gathered the boots and the empty canvas bag, and at the door she paused exactly as Calloway had, framed in the same spring light, and Ada understood that doors in this house had always been thresholds, that every departure here had been a promise wearing a coat.

"You'll write," Ada said. Not a question either.

"Every month. First of the month. You'll have my figures." Marguerite grinned then, young suddenly, the girl from fourteen years ago surfacing like something the tide had finally decided to give back. "Check my sums. You always found my errors."

"You never made errors."

"No," Marguerite agreed, stepping out. "I made choices. There's a difference, and you're the only one who ever kept the books on both."

Her footsteps went down the dock boards, quick and even, a woman going somewhere. Ada sat a moment longer in the sunlit counting house, then drew the household ledger toward her, uncapped her pen, and under *gratitude carried to new account* entered the first figure of the new book: *One friend, returned. Balance: everything.*

Outside, the ferry bell rang across the strait, and the gulls went up, and the century — young, unwritten, wide open — waited for its first honest entry.
