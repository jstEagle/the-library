## 22. Orchard and Compass

The apple trees came back before anything else forgave the hillside.

Marguerite had not expected that. She had expected years of bare ground and stubborn grass, of wind coming off the strait with nothing to slow it, of a landscape that remembered being cleared for war and could not be persuaded to forget. But trees are patient in ways people are not allowed to be. The rootstock had survived under the soil all through the confiscation years, and when Emeric's sister Hana put out her first grafts in the spring of 1904 — scions from an orchard two islands north, bought with money earned mending nets nobody else wanted to mend — the old roots woke up like something that had only been pretending to be dead.

Now, eleven springs after the century turned, the hillside above the strait was green again. Not the green it had been — the original orchard, the Sato family's orchard, had been cut down by soldiers who did it badly and left the stumps like bad teeth — but a new green, grafted onto old wood, which was its own kind of argument.

Marguerite stood at the top row with a pruning knife in one hand and a basket of early apples at her feet, and she watched the bridge.

It had been closed for eleven years. She had watched it close from the wrong side of the water, watched the sentries go up at both ends, watched the timbers weather and warp until the whole span looked less like a bridge than like a long grey sentence written across the strait and then crossed out. There had been talk of tearing it down twice — once in 1903, once in 1908 — and twice the government had found reasons to leave it standing, because governments are better at keeping things they don't want than admitting they were wrong to take them.

But this morning there were no sentries. This morning there were carpenters on the middle span, small figures in the distance, and bunting going up along the rails in colours that had been chosen by a committee, and below the bridge, riding at anchor in the bright morning water, a ferry.

*The Kestrel*, her name painted fresh on the bow. Forty feet, twin masts stepped but furled, a wheelhouse that still smelled of sawdust and pitch. Marguerite had walked her decks in March, before the paint went on, running her palm along the joinery the way you'd check a horse's legs before buying. Emeric had built her. He had built her the way his father built boats — planks steamed and bent by hand, every seam caulked tight enough to hold against the reef channel itself — and when he'd asked Marguerite what name she should carry, Marguerite had thought of the little falcon that hunted the bluff in winter, hanging motionless over the current before dropping like a held breath finally let go.

She'd said, *Something that waits and then moves fast.* And Emeric had laughed, because he knew exactly what she meant, and *Kestrel* went into the registry.

Below her, down the switchback path, the boatyard was already loud. It reopened in 1906 — the deed had come out of the compass case, and the courts, to everyone's astonishment including their own lawyer's, had agreed that paper hidden in brass since before the war was still paper, still binding, still proof — and now there were eleven men and three women working the slips where Emeric's father had worked, and the sound of adzes carried up the hill all day like slow applause.

Hana ran the orchard. That had been Marguerite's doing too, though she'd never claimed it as such. In the first spring, when the replanting was nothing but hope and rootstock, Marguerite had stood with the girl at the top of the hill and taught her what her mother had taught her about reading water — and then, because the girl had asked, what her mother had taught her about reading land. Where the fog pooled. Which slopes caught the morning sun and which kept frost. How to tell a tree that would bear from a tree that would only take up room. Hana had been fourteen then, the age Marguerite had been when she made a promise on a dock, and she had listened the way children listen when they understand they are being handed something heavier than instructions.

The orchard was hers now. The yard was Emeric's. The house at the top of the hill — built in 1907, cedar and glass, with a porch that faced the water because Marguerite had refused to face any other direction — belonged to both of them, in the way of things built together and argued over and repaired together and never once regretted.

And inside the house, on a nail beside the door where anyone leaving could see it, hung the compass.

* * *

She had gone down to the dock before dawn, though there was no need. Everything that needed doing had been done weeks ago; today was ceremony, and ceremony was Ada's department now even from four hundred miles away — she'd wired instructions, a list, and a warning: *If Calloway gives a speech I will hear about it if it takes me a week.*

Calloway had come, of course. He was seventy-one and walked with a stick he pretended was ornamental, and he had taken the coastal steamer north for the opening because, as he put it, he had spent eleven years being paid to know what happened on that strait and he refused to miss finding out. He stood at the rail end of the dock in a good coat, hatless, white-haired, looking exactly like a man who had once tried to buy a woman's freedom with a betrayal and been refused, and who had spent the rest of his life trying to earn the difference.

He'd managed it, more or less. After the report burned — after New Year's Eve, 1899, bells ringing and the two of them standing on the frozen dock with the evidence of treason between them like a third person — he had never once used what he knew. He had released her from indenture with a document so dry it read like an apology, and when she'd asked him why, really why, he'd said: *An employee who crosses open water for a promise is worth more free than owned,* and she had known it was a lie shaped like kindness, and accepted it, and discovered over twenty years that it was also, in its grudging way, true. He had written to her every quarter since. Short letters. Ledger-like. Weather, prices, the health of the counting-house clerks, one line always at the end that was never sentimental and was therefore entirely so: *Accounts remain open.*

This morning he had shaken her hand on the dock and held it one beat longer than a handshake requires, and neither of them had said anything about docks or bells or burning, because some pardons are only issued once and do not need renewing.

The ferry crossing was set for noon. By half past ten the dock was crowded — boatyard families, orchard families, the fishermen whose fathers had smuggled letters through the war years and who now told the story with embellishments their fathers would have disowned, the schoolchildren born after the closing for whom the bridge had always been a ruin, a legend, a place you dared each other to walk halfway out on before the memory of sentries stopped you. Old Mrs. Okada, who had waited eleven years on the far shore and lost a husband to the transports and gained him back, miraculously, from a camp in the interior in 1906, sat in the front row of chairs with her hands folded and her face turned toward the water like a woman at a church she had been exiled from.

Marguerite stood at the edge of it all with salt wind in her hair and apples on her breath — she'd eaten one on the walk down, the first of the season, tart enough to make your eyes water — and felt the thing she felt most mornings now and still had not gotten used to: the absence of debt.

For fifteen years her life had been entries in other people's books. Owed to Meridian. Owed to the household. Owed to the dead, to the drowned mother, to the boy on the dock, to the promise. Even after her release she had owed — owed Ada the silence, owed Calloway the unasked question, owed the strait a crossing it nearly collected. A life measured in entries not her own.

But somewhere in the last decade the ledger had turned over. She planted trees now instead of paying them off. She signed her own name at the bottom of her own accounts, and the name was hers, and the accounts balanced.

* * *

At noon the band played — badly, cheerfully, a piece that might have been a march in some country that had never heard of this strait — and the ribbon across the bridge approach came apart under a pair of ceremonial shears wielded jointly by a government man who had had nothing to do with the closing and Mrs. Okada, who had had everything to do with surviving it.

The crowd cheered. The *Kestrel* cast off.

Emeric had the wheel himself. Marguerite watched from the dock as the ferry came around — slow, dignified, her wake spreading in a wide white fan — and began the crossing that eleven years of politics had forbidden and forty minutes of good engine work would complete. Halfway out, right over the deep water where the reef channel ran, Emeric brought her head round for a moment toward the northern shore, toward the bluff, toward the grave Marguerite had visited every year since 1900 with a knife at her belt and permission already granted — a salute, or a thanks, or simply the habit of a boatbuilder's son acknowledging the water that had raised and nearly ruined all of them.

Then he straightened up and made for the far pier, steady as a promise kept.

Marguerite did not cry. She had done her crying in cold water and lamplight and graveside wind, and what she felt now was quieter than crying — a settling, like ballast finding its place, like a tide reaching the top of its rise and holding, just for a moment, perfectly still.

Ada arrived in September, as she did every year, ostensibly to inspect the cider operation and actually to sit on the porch arguing happily with Emeric about everything. She had married in 1905, a surveyor named Pell who understood that his wife's first loyalty lay four hundred miles northwest and had wisely decided to share it. They stayed in the spare room. Ada always slept late her first morning and always emerged saying the same thing: *Your fog is louder than ours.*

In October, when the pressing was done, the four of them would walk up to the top row of the orchard at dusk, when the light came low off the water and turned the whole hillside to hammered copper, and pick the last fruit by hand, because Hana insisted machines bruised what hands would carry gently, and because none of them had ever quite stopped treating abundance as something you verified yourself.

* * *

That evening, after the crowds had thinned and the bunting had begun to sag in the dew, Marguerite climbed the path alone to the house.

The porch lamp was lit. Inside, the compass hung on its nail beside the door, brass worn bright at the edges from twenty-three years of hands, the glass slightly clouded, the needle swinging lazy and sure.

It didn't point north anymore. Everyone said so. Visitors noticed it, frowning, adjusting their assumptions: a compass that lied, a compass that had somehow gone soft. But Marguerite knew better. The needle hadn't failed. The house had simply been placed where the compass's oldest habit and the family's newest home happened to agree — and so it pointed at the door, at the porch, at the hillside and the strait beyond, at everything that had been promised and returned and rebuilt.

Her mother had died owning nothing but knowledge and a knife. Marguerite owned an orchard's worth of tomorrow, a share in a boatyard, a name on deeds, and the only thing she had ever stolen: a promise, carried across open water, delivered intact.

She took the compass down and held it a moment, feeling the weight of it, the warmth the day had left in the brass. Through the window behind her the *Kestrel* rode at her new mooring, lamps lit, ready for tomorrow's first run — the first scheduled crossing in eleven years, ordinary as bread, extraordinary as resurrection.

Tomorrow Hana wanted to talk about extending the top row to the ridge, where the fog broke earliest. Tomorrow there were grafts to check and a hull to fair and a letter from Calloway to answer — *accounts remain open* — in a hand that grew shakier each year and a friendship that did not. Tomorrow, ordinary days, self-owned, promised, kept.

Marguerite hung the compass back on its nail. It swung, settled, steadied.

Home, pointing home, in a house with the door unlocked.
