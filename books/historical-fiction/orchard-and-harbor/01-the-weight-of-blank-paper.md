## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

There was a particular sound a copperplate made when it was nearly finished—a hush, almost, as though the metal itself were holding its breath. Oscar Thackeray had learned to listen for it twenty years ago, kneeling at Aldous Ren's bench with ink on his knuckles and his master's voice in his ear: *You don't finish a map, boy. You stop arguing with it.*

He stopped arguing at half past four on an October afternoon, three weeks before the armistice deadline that no one in Vellmar spoke of aloud and everyone counted like coins.

The plate before him showed the eastern coast—thirty miles of it, from the chalk cliffs at Sterren Point down to the mudflats where the river gave up and became sea. Every depth sounded, every shoal charted, every tide-mark noted in Ren's cramped, beautiful hand and transferred by Oscar's steadier one. It was, by any honest measure, the finest coastal survey ever made of those waters. And if all went as it had gone for five years, no living soul outside this shop would ever see it.

Oscar set down the burin and flexed his fingers, which had begun to ache in the cold the way they never had at thirty. The stove ticked. Somewhere above him, a shutter banged in the wind off the harbor—the third time today; he would have to speak to Mrs. Havel about the loose hinge, and she would agree warmly and do nothing, because doing nothing was how Mrs. Havel kept her windows clean and her neighbors' business dirty.

He allowed himself, as he did each evening at this hour, one full minute of looking at the work.

The atlas lay open on the great table: forty-one plates completed, bound provisionally in gray boards that Ren had labeled, in a joke only two people alive would understand, *Agricultural Surveys of the Interior Provinces*. Forty-one plates of harbors, channels, sandbars, depths—the true anatomy of a coastline that both armies wanted and neither understood. And then the forty-second place, empty. A blank leaf where the final chapter should be: the deepwater channel through the Narrows, the one feature that decided everything, the page Ren had been carrying when he died.

*Was killed for*, said the part of Oscar's mind that kept accounts. He preferred *died*, the way he preferred *the late unpleasantness* to *the war* and *went to sleep* to whatever actually happened in the small hours when he lay awake listening to the harbor bells. A man could survive almost anything if he phrased it gently enough.

The minute ended. He closed the boards, squared them, and returned them to their place beneath the floor panel behind the paper cabinet, under a layer of obsolete charts of a coastline so boring that even thieves had skipped it twice during the occupation years. Then he lit the lamp in the window—the shop's single concession to being open—and turned the sign that read THACKERAY & REN, ENGRAVERS, EST. 1887.

Ren had been dead five years. The ampersand remained. Oscar told himself it was for the customers.

* * *

There were, these days, perhaps six customers a week, and four of them were Mrs. Havel's relations wanting something framed cheaply. The war had done what the war did to shops like this: taken the apprentices, taken the orders, taken the brass instruments one by one to be melted, until what remained was a thin man of forty-six selling picture frames in a city that had stopped believing pictures were necessary.

Oscar had arranged matters so that this was exactly what he appeared to be.

It had taken years of careful dullness. He priced things slightly too high so that buyers drifted elsewhere. He kept the good stock in back and the mediocre stock in front. He had cultivated, over long practice, a manner he thought of as *the wallpaper*: polite, forgettable, faintly apologetic, a man you described afterward as "the fellow in the map shop—no, not that one, the other one." When soldiers had come through in '16 demanding lists of everyone's trade and sympathies, he had handed over a list so accurate and so tedious—*engraver; sells frames; once repaired a sextant for the harbor office; no politics; poor eyesight*—that the clerk had yawned and moved on.

Invisibility, Oscar had found, was not a talent but a discipline, like grinding pigment or holding a line steady. You practiced it daily. You let your coat go shabby at the cuffs. You forgot names charmingly. You became, to the city, a piece of its furniture—and furniture, unlike people, was not conscripted, arrested, or shot against the seawall at low tide.

That last item was on the list of reasons. There were others. He did not consult them.

He was pricing a set of prints of Alpine lakes—he had never seen the Alps; nobody in Vellmar had seen much of anything beyond artillery range for five years—when the bell over the door rang and Mrs. Havel came in with the air of a woman delivering news she has already sold twice.

"Your stovepipe's smoking," she announced.

"Is it?"

"Smoke, out the top, like a factory. Old Lindqvist saw it and asked me if you'd taken up smelting."

"Tell him I've gone into armaments," Oscar said mildly. "It's very profitable these days."

She snorted, which was her version of laughter, and settled herself onto the stool by the counter without invitation, as she had done every few days for eleven years. Mrs. Havel was seventy if she was a day, built like a mooring post, with a face that seemed assembled from weather and disapproval. She owned the building. She also owned, though she would have denied it under oath, the most comprehensive intelligence network north of the customs house, operating out of four windows, a shared courtyard, and an unshakable conviction that anything worth happening was worth reporting.

"Coal ration's cut again," she said. "Third time since summer. They're saying the armistice will fix it." She said the word *armistice* the way other people said *fairy stories*—with hope and contempt in equal measure. "My nephew says there'll be peace papers signed by the twentieth. He says his cousin's regiment already got the order to stop shooting after that date."

"Then we shall know by the twentieth whether your nephew's cousin is reliable," Oscar said, turning a print face-down with great attention. "I'd hold off on celebrating until then."

"You don't celebrate," Mrs. Havel observed. It was not quite an accusation. With her it rarely was; it was more an entry in the ledger. *Thackeray, O.: does not celebrate. Keeps irregular hours. Bought no mourning clothes when his master died, nor yet new boots. Suspected of hoarding something—sugar? opinions?*

"I'll put it on my list," Oscar said. "Between replacing the stovepipe hinge and learning to dance."

"The hinge is mine to replace, and you know it."

"Then I'll learn to dance."

She left shortly after, pausing at the door to deliver her usual benediction—"Mind the smoke, Mr. Thackeray, the whole street will think you're forging money"—and the bell rang her out into the blue cold evening.

Oscar stood a moment in the quiet after she'd gone. Through the window he could see the harbor, or the slice of it the buildings permitted: masts, cranes, the gray line of the boom net still strung across the channel after all these years, sagging like an old hammock. Beyond it, the water went dark. Somewhere out there, past the point where the lamplight quit, lay the channel that Ren had measured and the armies had guessed at, and the difference between measuring and guessing was, at present, roughly the width of a nation.

Three weeks. If the negotiators drew the boundary where the official charts said it should be, the deepwater approach would fall inside the victors' claim, and the consortium that had commissioned Ren's atlas—and then buried it, and then buried Ren—would own the only deep anchorage on the coast, and every tariff and toll and future that came with it.

If the boundary followed the truth instead—

But that sentence had no ending Oscar was permitted to write. He blew out the thought as he blew out the lamp over the workbench, and went upstairs to make bread from flour he was not officially supposed to have, in an oven he was not officially supposed to use, in rooms whose rent was paid in currency that had been worthless for a year and was therefore, perversely, the most stable money in Vellmar.

* * *

The bread, at least, he had gotten good at. Everything else in the evenings tended toward the same shape: the plate brought up from hiding, worked on by lamplight until his eyes watered, hidden again, and the blank forty-second leaf looked at—not worked on, just looked at, the way one looks at a letter one cannot answer.

Ren had died with the measurements in his head and the drafts in his satchel, and the satchel had gone into the sea, and the men who took it had believed they were taking everything. They had not reckoned on an apprentice with a memory trained to hold soundings the way other men held grudges. For five years Oscar had redrawn, from memory and from Ren's earlier working notes, every number the master had ever trusted him with. The eastern coast. The western approaches. The Narrows' outer shelf. All of it rebuilt, plate by plate, in secret, in a shop that sold frames.

All of it except the last page.

Because the last page required numbers Ren had never written down anywhere, and a survey he had made alone, in the last spring of his life, telling Oscar only: *"When it's time, I'll take you through the Narrows myself, and you'll see why no chart of this coast can be printed while certain men draw breath."*

And then Ren had gone out one night in November with his boat and his instruments, and had not come back, and the harbor police had called it an accident for exactly as long as it took everyone to stop asking.

Oscar knew the channel existed. He knew its general course, because Ren had talked in his sleep near the end of that last year—fever, once, and brandy other times—and some of it had lodged. But *lodged* was not *measured*, and a map built on *lodged* was a guess wearing a surveyor's clothes. The atlas was a cathedral with one wall missing, and Oscar had spent five years telling himself that a missing wall was safer than a finished building anyway. Who burns down a house that isn't whole?

Tonight, though, with the bread rising under its cloth and the wind worrying the shutters, the old argument felt thinner than usual. Three weeks to the deadline. Forty-one walls standing. One gap where the truth should be.

"You'd have hated this," he told the empty room, meaning Ren, meaning the caution, meaning all of it. "You'd have had the plates printed off a fishing trawler by now and dared them to catch you."

The room, being a room, declined to disagree.

He washed the bowl, banked the stove, checked the bolt on the shop door below by habit—turned, locked, as it had been every night for five years—and paused at the window that overlooked the street. Fog was coming in off the water, the thick kind, the kind that ate lamps and swallowed footsteps. The gaslight down the way made a soft orange hole in it and nothing else.

A good night, he thought, for anyone who didn't want to be seen walking through Vellmar. He had walked many such nights himself, early on, carrying notes to men whose names he never learned. That life was over; he had retired from it the way one retires from swimming in winter—with relief and a lingering itch.

He was about to turn away when the light caught something at the edge of the fog, down the street, near enough now to have a shape.

Small. Moving slowly. Stopping at his door.

Oscar told himself it was a cat. Vellmar was full of cats; cats had prospered disproportionately in the war years, and several considered his doorstep prime territory. But cats did not stand on their hind legs at doors and remain there, motionless, for the length of time this shape remained motionless, which was long enough for Oscar to notice that his heart had started doing something unprofessional.

The clock in the hall struck three.

At the hour of cats, then. He watched the small shape raise its fist—or paw—and knock, twice, softly, the way one knocks when one has been told to knock and not to be heard.

Oscar Thackeray, who had spent five years perfecting the art of not answering, stood in the dark with his hand already on the latch, and discovered that some disciplines fail you precisely when it matters.

Below, in the fog, the knocking came again.
