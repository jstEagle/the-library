## 2. A Knock at the Hour of Cats

There is an hour in Vellmar that belongs to neither night nor morning, when the tide turns and even the gulls fall silent, and the city seems to hold its breath as though it has forgotten what it was doing. The cartographers of an earlier age would have marked it on their charts as a hazard: unlit water, unreliable currents, no safe anchorage. I had come to think of it as my own private country, population one.

It was in this hour—somewhere past three, by the clock I kept wound out of habit rather than need—that I first heard the knocking.

I should explain that I was awake because I was always awake at that hour. Five years of practice had made a ritual of it. Aldous Ren used to say that ink behaved best before dawn, that the lines went down cleaner when the hand was tired enough to stop arguing with itself. Whether or not this was true, it was true for me, and so every night I sat at the long table under the good lamp with my pens laid out like surgical instruments, and I worked on the atlas that did not officially exist, and I did not think about the fact that the man who had begun it was dead because of it.

That last part took practice too. I had gotten quite good at it, in the way that a man gets good at anything he does ten thousand times.

The knock came at the street door—the heavy one, the one customers never used—and it was small. That is the only way I can describe it. Not the confident double-rap of a delivery man, nor the impatient drumming of someone who believes the world owes him an answer. It was a knock the size of a fist, and a fist the size of a pear, delivered twice, apologetically, and then not again.

I put down my pen.

Let me be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the one luxury I have allowed myself in these five years, hoarded like a miser's coin and spent only here, alone, where no one can tax it.

My first thought was: *no.*

Not *who could that be*, not *how curious*. No. Because I knew, in the way a hunted animal knows weather, exactly what a knock at three in the morning meant in the fifth year of a war. It meant soldiers. It meant requisition notices. It meant a neighbor had finally decided to trade my name for coal or flour or simple safety, and the men who bought such things preferred to collect before dawn, when a shop could be searched without an audience.

I sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the knock came a third time—even softer now, as if whoever stood outside had begun to doubt the door existed at all—and then there was a sound I recognized despite myself, because grief keeps its own library: a small, swallowed sob, quickly strangled, the sound of someone crying the way children learn to cry when they have learned that crying brings the wrong kind of attention.

Well. That was unfair of them. Whatever else the night held, it had no right to bring *that* to my door.

I told myself it might be a trick. I want that recorded. I am a careful man; carefulness is the whole of my biography. I told myself that the armies of two nations, between them, had learned to use crying children the way they used white flags, and that a man in my position—who kept a dead master's forbidden atlas in pieces across three hiding places, who answered to no census, who had spent half a decade becoming furniture—had precisely one duty, which was to sit still until whatever stood outside grew bored and moved along to some other door.

The lamp hissed. My pen lay where I had set it down, its nib drying. I remember being angry at the pen, absurdly, as though it were deserting its post.

And underneath all of that, beneath the fear and the arithmetic of risk like silt under clear water, there was another thing, older, which I had not permitted myself to look at directly since the spring the war began. It was this: the last time anyone had knocked on that door after midnight, it had been Aldous Ren, drunk on cheap brandy and triumph, hammering loud enough to wake Mrs. Havel's entire staircase, shouting up through the letter slot that he had *finished the northern plate* and that I was to come down at once and admire it, and that if I did not he would stand there until morning, and he very nearly did.

No one had knocked since. Not once. Five years, and the door had learned silence the way I had.

I got up. I am still not entirely sure which of the two voices won—the frightened one or the lonely one—or whether they simply agreed, for once, on a single course of action, the way two rival kingdoms will sometimes unite against a common and incomprehensible enemy.

I carried the lamp downstairs. I did not light the shop's front lamps—I was not entirely a fool—and I did not go to the door directly. I went instead to the window beside the counter, the one with the good heavy curtains, and I lifted the curtain's edge by a finger's width, the way one lifts a bandage to see whether the wound has closed.

A child stood on my step.

He was small—seven, perhaps, though war ages children unevenly, like fruit on a neglected tree—and he was dressed in a coat far too big for him, a man's coat, or nearly, with the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs that swallowed his hands. He wore no hat. His hair was dark and cut badly, by someone in a hurry, with shears rather than scissors. And he stood the way children stand when they have been standing somewhere for a long time and have been told, by experience, not to expect anything from it: weight settled, chin down, eyes fixed on the middle distance as though looking directly at the door might make it less likely to open.

Behind him, the street ran empty in both directions, silvered and still, the harbor smell lying over everything like a damp cloth. If someone had brought him, they were gone, or hidden, or watching from a doorway I could not see. The fog was coming in off the water the way it did in autumn, unhurried, proprietary, erasing the far end of the street first.

I let the curtain fall and stood in the dark with my heart going like a pressman's treadle.

*Do not open the door.* This was the voice of five years' discipline, and it was right, and I knew it was right, and I hated it with a suddenness that startled me. Because here is what carefulness costs, which no one tells you: not danger. Danger is merely the price you pay for it, and prices are honest things. What it costs is everything else. Every knock unanswered. Every face on the street unmet. A life conducted at a polite distance from one's own existence, until a man wakes at fifty-one—he had been forty-six when the war began, though he felt considerably older than either figure—to discover that his closest companion is a dead man's handwriting and his liveliest conversation of the week is an argument with a landlady about the chimney.

The child knocked again. Twice. Pear-sized fist. Then I heard him speak—not to the door, but to himself, or to the street, or to God, in a language I didn't recognize, low and rhythmic, the way children recite things they've been made to memorize. A prayer, maybe. Or instructions. *Stand here. Knock softly. Do not cry. Forget the face.*

I opened the door.

The cold came in with the fog and I felt it on my ankles through my socks, and the child looked up at me with an expression I will carry to whatever grave receives me: not hope, exactly. Hope requires belief in possibilities. It was more the look of someone checking whether a thing was true, already braced for it not to be.

He was thinner than the coat suggested. There was a bruise fading yellow-green along his jaw, old enough to be nearly healed. In one rolled sleeve-cuff, clutched the way other children clutch a sweet or a soldier's button, was a folded piece of paper, crushed soft from handling, damp at the corners.

"Are you the map shop?" he said. His Vellmari was accented—eastern provinces, I'd have guessed, though I am no linguist—but serviceable. Someone had drilled him in the question. Perhaps it was the only sentence he had been given.

"I'm the map shop," I said, which was, strictly speaking, a lie. The map shop had been Aldous Ren's, and before that old Herr Brandt's, and I was merely the thing it had grown around, like the pearl around the grit. But the child's face did something at the words—a loosening, a fractional unbending, the look of a walker who has been told *yes, this is the road*—and I found I could not take it back.

He held out the paper.

I took it. My fingers were steadier than I expected them to be; five years of inking fine lines will do that for a man whether he wishes it or not. I unfolded the paper under the spill of my lamp, and the fog pressed in behind the child like a customer eager to see what I had bought.

The note was short. The hand was a woman's, hurried, the letters leaning as though written standing up, or walking, or running:

*Forget the face. Keep the coat. Burn this.*

I read it twice. Three times. The lamplight fluttered. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's bell counted four, muffled, as if apologizing for the hour.

"Who gave you this?" I asked.

The child looked at me. He had grey eyes, I noticed, or grey-green, the color of the sea in the kind of weather sailors refuse to name. He looked at me the way people had looked at Aldous Ren when he asked them questions about places they did not wish to discuss—with a flat, practiced patience, waiting for the adult to finish asking questions so that the adult could arrive, on his own, at whatever conclusion the child had been instructed to let him reach.

"Keep the coat," he said.

"Yes," I said. "It says so." I crouched down, which put us nearly eye to eye, and which also, I confess, put my head below the level of the doorframe, an instinctive bit of cowardice I did not examine too closely. "But coats don't walk to doors by themselves at four in the morning. Who walked you here?"

He said nothing. He was very good at nothing. It occurred to me, with a chill that had nothing to do with the fog, that he had been taught that too—that somewhere behind those grey eyes was a whole curriculum of silence, and teachers patient enough to grade it.

"Are you hungry?" I tried.

Nothing. But something moved in him at the word—hunger, unlike questions, cannot be disciplined into silence—and I saw his throat work once, quickly, and I understood that we had found the crack in the wall.

"It's cold on that step," I said. "Whatever you're not going to tell me, you can not tell me inside."

He considered this. I watched him consider it, actually watch the thought move through him like weather, weighing my face, my lamplight, the open door, the warm dark beyond it, against whatever warnings had been installed in him. Then he stepped past me into the shop, close enough that his shoulder brushed my knee, and the fog followed him in, and I shut the door on the night and shot both bolts and stood a moment with my forehead nearly against the wood, listening to my own heartbeat and wondering, distantly, what Aldous Ren would have done.

Ren would have opened the door before the third knock, and probably fed the boy brandy by mistake, and had a theory about the whole business by breakfast, and been magnificent and impossible and alive. The thought arrived complete and unbidden, the way thoughts of the dead do, and it hurt in the old familiar place, the place I had gotten so skilled at not thinking about.

*Burn this,* the note said. I folded it and put it in my waistcoat pocket instead, next to my heart, where I keep everything I intend to destroy and never do.

The child had stopped in the middle of the shop floor. The lamp I'd left burning on the long table caught him from one side, and he turned slowly, taking in the flat files, the presses, the great drawers labeled in faded gilt—COASTLINES, SOUNDINGS, INLAND WATERS—and I saw him find, among all of it, the unfinished chart pinned above the table, the northern plate, the one Ren had hammered on my door about five years and a lifetime ago, and I saw the boy's eyes fix on it and stay there.

He did not say anything. But he went toward it, one step, two, dragging the enormous coat, until he stood beneath the half-finished coastline with his head tipped back, studying the inked shore and the penciled depths that waited below it like a held breath.

"You know maps," I said. It wasn't a question, quite.

He raised one swallowed hand and pointed—at the coast, at a specific bend in it, a headland I had inked not three weeks before—and made a sound, small and certain, the sound of recognition, of *that one, I know that one.*

Outside, the fog thickened against the windows. Somewhere in the building above, a floorboard settled; Mrs. Havel slept lightly and missed nothing, and it would be hours yet before her curtains twitched and her ledger of the neighborhood opened for the day. I stood in my own shop, in my own carefully invisible life, and understood with perfect clarity that the door I had just bolted had not shut the night out.

It had shut us both in with whatever was coming.

The boy turned from the map and looked up at me, and held out the crushed coat cuff as if presenting evidence, and said, in that careful eastern accent, the only other words he apparently owned:

"Forget the face."

"No," I said quietly, to him, to the note, to the fog at the glass. "I don't think I will."

His stomach chose that moment to growl, loudly, rudely, with the total indifference of stomachs to history—and the ghost of something crossed his face that in another child, in another year, would have been a smile. I lit the stove.
