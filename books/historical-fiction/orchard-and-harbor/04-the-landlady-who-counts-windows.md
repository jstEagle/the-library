## 4. The Landlady Who Counts Windows

Mrs. Havel had counted the windows of Vellmar for thirty-one years, and she could tell you, without consulting a single ledger, how many candles burned behind each of them on any given night.

Not that she kept a ledger. That was the first thing to understand about Marta Havel: she had never written anything down in her life. Writing things down was for clerks and fools. Clerks could be bribed into surrendering their papers; fools lost them. What Mrs. Havel kept was better than paper. It lived behind her eyes, in a memory her late husband had once described as "a cathedral with no doors," by which he had meant it as a compliment, though he'd also slept in the spare room for the last six years of his life, so his judgment on the matter was perhaps not impartial.

She knew which households had taken in cousins from the occupied territories and which cousins were not cousins at all. She knew that the baker's youngest son had not died at Kessel Ridge as the family claimed but had walked into the sea two years before the war gave everyone an honorable excuse. She knew that Herr Brandt the fishmonger watered his herring brine, that the widow Ostrava's "niece" paid rent in foreign coin, and that the Thackeray boy—the mapmaker, Ren's apprentice, the one who moved through the streets like a man trying not to disturb the air—had not left his shop for anything but bread and ink in five years.

Everyone in Vellmar knew something. That was what the war had done to the city, more thoroughly than the shelling ever managed. It had turned every windowsill into an observation post and every neighbor into a correspondent—not of newspapers, but of one another, and of certain parties who paid in coal vouchers, sugar rations, or, if your information was very good indeed, in the kind of silence that kept your own name off other people's lists.

Mrs. Havel was simply better at it than most.

Her building stood at the corner of Lantern Row and the narrow lane that ran down toward the harbor, and the map shop occupied its ground floor like a tenant who never spoke. Above it she kept three rooms for herself and rented two more to a seamstress who was either pious or clever enough to have no secrets worth reporting. From her kitchen window Mrs. Havel commanded the shop's door, the alley beside it, and a generous slice of the harbor road beyond. From her bedroom window she could see the chimney pots of half the district. People assumed she watched out of malice. This offended her. She watched the way a lighthouse watches—not because it hates ships, but because knowing where they are is simply what it is *for*.

On the morning everything changed, she was doing her accounts, which is to say she was standing at her window with a cup of chicory pretending to be coffee, watching the light come up gray over the harbor, when Oscar Thackeray came home at four in the morning carrying a child.

* * *

Now. Let us be precise, because precision was Mrs. Havel's religion, her hobby, and her only remaining vice.

The Thackeray boy—she still thought of him as a boy, though he must be past thirty now; time did that, it went on aging people whether or not you bothered to look at them—had gone out the previous evening just after dusk. She had noted it, because he almost never went out after dusk. He had returned at eleven, alone, wet to the knees, moving faster than his usual careful walk. She had noted that too, and filed it under *interesting*, a folder that had been thin these past five years. The mapmaker was a model tenant and a bore. He paid on the first of the month, in exact change, and he had once spent forty minutes helping her carry a wardrobe upstairs rather than let the porter touch it, and in all that time he had said perhaps sixty words, most of them "careful" and "left" and "I'm sorry."

And then, at four in the morning, he had gone out again—she heard the door, she always heard the door—and come back at first light with a bundle that walked.

That was how she saw it first, through the gray: a man carrying a bundle, and then the bundle shifted, and a small head lifted against Thackeray's shoulder, and a pair of thin legs swung free, and Mrs. Havel's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

A child. A child of seven, maybe eight, small for its age, dark-haired, asleep again before Thackeray had gotten the door open. Thackeray looked up and down the street twice before going in—she saw him do it, saw him check the harbor road and then the alley, saw him hesitate at his own threshold as though the door might bite—and then he was inside, and the latch clicked, and the lamp in the shop window glowed up yellow and stayed yellow until full daylight made it pointless.

Marta Havel set down her cup and considered.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain, the way she liked all things laid out. A child appearing at a bachelor mapmaker's door before dawn was not nothing. In Vellmar, in this year, in this city where the victors' commission was already lodging at the Grand and asking its courteous questions, a child appearing out of nowhere was a document. It was evidence of something—smuggling, desertion, occupation, conspiracy, she couldn't yet say what—but documents were her trade, even unwritten ones, and there were parties who would pay real money, real coal, real flour, for the opening chapter of whatever story this was.

All she had to do was walk four blocks to the commission's liaison office and say: *the mapmaker has a child.*

She stood at her window and did not walk anywhere at all.

* * *

By noon she had learned three more facts, because learning facts was as natural to her as breathing and considerably more productive.

Fact the first: Thackeray had sent for bread—a whole loaf, and milk, actual milk, from the dairy on Cooper Lane, paid for without haggling, which from Thackeray was a confession of emergency. The dairy girl had told the baker's wife, who told Mrs. Havel over the fence with the relish of a woman handing over a gift she didn't know the value of.

Fact the second: the child had not appeared at any window since arriving. Mrs. Havel's view of the shop's back courtyard was excellent, and children, in her long experience, were creatures who gravitated to windows the way moths gravitated to lamps. This one was being kept out of sight. Which meant Thackeray knew it should be kept out of sight. Which meant Thackeray knew exactly what kind of trouble he was holding.

Fact the third, and this was the one that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: when the boy had finally shown at the courtyard window that afternoon—just his head, just for a moment, checking the sky the way birds check for hawks—his face had done something to her.

It was the jaw. Or the brow. Something in the architecture of it, some line she had seen before and could not place, and she had spent the whole afternoon placing it, turning the memory over like a coin worn smooth, until well past supper, when it came to her all at once while she was scrubbing a pot that didn't need scrubbing:

Aldous Ren.

The old cartographer had that same stubborn brow, that same way of holding his chin slightly forward, as though the world were a drawing that needed correcting. She had known him to speak to—everyone on Lantern Row had known him, the great Aldous Ren living above a map shop like a king in exile—and he had died badly, five years back, in a manner nobody discussed above a whisper, and here was a child wearing his face standing in the dead man's apprentice's window.

"Well," said Mrs. Havel to her pot, in the tone she used for conclusions. "Well, well."

* * *

The trouble with knowing things, she reflected that evening, was that knowledge wanted to go somewhere. It was like water that way, or like gossip, which was the same substance in different clothing. You could hold it in your hands only so long before it began finding cracks.

She thought about the liaison office. She thought about coal. Her coal bin was down to its last honest layer, and the winter coming was forecast to be a spiteful one, and she was sixty-three years old and had earned warmth the way anyone earns it, by surviving things. One visit. Four blocks. A few sentences delivered in her best reasonable voice—*I don't make accusations, I only report what I see, I'm sure you'll draw your own conclusions*—and the bin would fill, and maybe the sugar ration would improve, and the commission's men would come politely to the shop with their polite questions, and the child with Aldous Ren's brow would be taken somewhere official, and whatever Thackeray was so carefully hiding would be unfolded on a table under lamplight where sensible people could look at it.

And then she thought about the last time she had reported something.

It had been small. That was the thing she told herself, that it had been small: a name given to a census clerk in the second year of the war, a family on Tanner Street keeping a radio wire hidden in a mattress, and she had reported it for two vouchers and a tin of oil, and she had told herself the family would only be questioned, only fined, only moved along. And the family had been moved along—to a camp, to a coast, to a category of disappearance she had never let herself finish spelling out—and their neighbors had asked her afterward, not accusingly, worse than accusingly, *comfortingly*: "Well, you didn't know, Mrs. Havel. How could you have known?"

And she had known. She had known exactly, the way she always knew, and she had traded it for a tin of oil, and the oil had smelled faintly of fish ever after, or she had imagined it had, for years, every time she opened the cupboard.

Since then she had developed rules. Not morals—she was too old and too honest with herself to claim morals—but rules, which were morals' practical cousins. Rule one: never report anything that gets a person taken away. Rule two: information about children was cursed money. Rule three: when in doubt, wait. Waiting cost nothing and revealed everything, and half the time the problem resolved itself or someone else solved it badly and you learned from their mistake for free.

But rule four, the oldest rule, the one underneath all the others: *survive.* And survival, in Vellmar, had a price list, and the price list was written in coal.

She sat with all of it by her window while the harbor lights came on one by one, and downstairs the shop lamp glowed its steady yellow, and somewhere below her floorboards a man who had spent five years being invisible was presumably feeding a child he had no obligation to feed, and lying awake—I'd wager my good blanket on it—wondering how long he could keep a secret the size of a small boy in a building owned by a woman who counted windows.

"Insolent," she said aloud, meaning Thackeray, meaning the situation, meaning possibly God. "Keeping a secret in *my* building."

* * *

She decided, in the end, on the third morning, and she decided the way she decided everything: practically, with a small ceremony.

She put on her good coat. She took the stairs down—her knees announcing each step like a town crier—and she knocked on the shop door with the brass knocker, three measured strikes, the knock of a landlady, which is a knock that cannot be ignored and cannot be mistaken for anything friendly.

Thackeray opened the door looking exactly as she expected: pale, composed, and terrified in the specific way of a man who has rehearsed innocence and knows his rehearsal is bad. Behind him the shop smelled of ink and, faintly, of porridge.

"Mrs. Havel," he said. "Good morning."

"Is it," she said, and stepped inside uninvited, because the doorway was public and the interior was where the truth lived. "I've come about the rent."

"The rent isn't due until—"

"I know when the rent is due. I wrote the calendar." She let her eyes travel the shop slowly, deliberately, a woman taking inventory. The drafting table. The plates. The smell of milk. "Rent, and heat. The winter's coming, Mr. Thackeray, and you'll want the stove lit day and night now, I expect, with—" she paused, delicately, the pause of a duelist offering first position, "—a growing boy in the house. Children feel the cold."

She watched him go white and then, to his credit, decide not to lie. She watched him open his mouth, close it, and finally say, in a voice sanded down to bare wood: "How much?"

And there it was. Not *what do you want*, not *how did you know*, not even *please*. Just *how much*, which was the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to her in years, and she found, to her considerable irritation, that it stung. As though he'd held out a coin and she'd seen her own face stamped on it.

"Sit down, Mr. Thackeray," she said, "before you fall down. You're no negotiator. Your master was worse." She lowered herself onto the customer's stool, arranged her coat, and folded her hands. "Here is how it will be. My coal bin is empty. My roof leaks over the stairwell and you have a ladder and no fear of heights. And once a week you will take bread to the widow Ostrava, who is proud and starving and will accept it from you because she thinks you're too shy to notice her pride. Those are my terms. There is no second offer."

"That's—that's all?"

"All?" She fixed him with the look she used on tradesmen who tried to round up. "Mr. Thackeray, do you imagine I report children to commissioners? Do you imagine that's the sort of woman I am?" She let him squirm precisely four seconds. "Don't answer. Men are terrible at answering that question honestly. Now. The boy. Is he fed? Does he sleep? Has he spoken?"

"He says one word," Thackeray said quietly. "*Opa.*"

Mrs. Havel felt the word land in her chest, right where the stone was. *Opa.* Grandfather. And the brow, and the chin, and the stubborn way the child had checked the sky from the window, hawk-checking, Ren-checking.

"Aldous Ren's grandson," she said. It wasn't a question, and she watched him understand that it couldn't be, that in this building nothing ever was.

"You can't—" he started.

"I can keep my mouth shut," she said, rising, knees creaking, dignity intact, "which is a different skill entirely, and harder, and costs more, and I've just named my price. Coal, a roof, and bread for the widow Ostrava. Pay me in silence, Mr. Thackeray, and I'll pay you back in the same coin." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and added, without turning around, because it was easier not to: "There are others who count windows in this city. Some of them count for the commission. Some count for men who aren't polite about what they're counting. Whatever you're keeping in this shop—you'd best decide quickly what it's worth, and to whom, before someone else sets the price for you."

She climbed the stairs to her cold rooms, and stood at her window, and watched the harbor road, and waited—as she had waited all her life—for the next thing to come up the street.

It came sooner than she liked. Before the week was out, a carriage with commission plates drew up outside the map shop, and a woman in a gray coat stepped down, consulted a card, and reached for the door.

Mrs. Havel's hand was already on her window latch, counting.
