## 6. The Commission Arrives

Elise Vantrell had learned long ago that a city tells you what it is by its windows.

Paris told you everything and pretended it hadn't. Vienna apologized for showing you. But Vellmar — Vellmar watched back. She had felt it from the moment her carriage crossed the canal bridge that morning: eyes behind lace, eyes behind shutters, eyes behind the respectable fiction of sweeping a step that needed no sweeping. A city of clerks and fishermen and widows who had survived four years of occupation by becoming experts in one another. She respected that. It was, after all, her own trade.

She had come to curate.

The commission's instructions sat in her breast pocket, written in the tidy hand of General Secretary Ombre: *Collect all cartographic materials pertaining to the coastal territories. Assess for historical value. Retain what serves the record.* Everyone at the commission understood what "serves the record" meant. The record was not a ledger; it was a story, and stories required editing. Elise had edited three campaigns' worth of them already. She was very good at it. She had won a small medal for it, which she kept in a drawer because medals were for people who believed their own press.

The shop was exactly where the dossier said it would be: Thackeray & Ren, Cartographers, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered milliner's on Lantern Row. The sign had faded to the color of weak tea. The window displayed two framed maps of a coastline that no longer existed as drawn, a brass compass, and a card in careful copperplate: *Surveys executed. Charts corrected. Inquiries answered.*

*Inquiries answered,* she thought, climbing the steps. We shall see about that.

She knocked. Not the knock of an official — she had deliberately left the commission's seal at the hotel — but the knock of a private caller, mild and unhurried. There were two ways to enter a room like this: like weather, or like a guest. Weather made people bar doors. Guests got invited in.

Footsteps inside. A pause — she counted it, out of habit. Three seconds too long to be simple surprise. Then the door opened.

Oscar Thackeray was thinner than his file photograph, and older-looking, with ink stains worked so deep into the skin around his fingernails they might have been tattoos. He looked at her the way she had seen besieged townspeople look at advancing officers: cataloguing her coat, her gloves, her shoes, calculating how much trouble stood on his doorstep and whether it could be talked into leaving.

"Good morning," she said. "I hope I'm not disturbing work."

"You're not disturbing anything." His voice was level. Almost. "We don't get many callers."

"So I've been told." She smiled the smile that opened more doors than warrants did. "Commissioner Elise Vantrell. Historical section, Armistice Boundary Commission. May I come in? I promise I'm more interesting than I am dangerous, though I grant that's a low bar."

Something flickered across his face — not fear exactly; something wearier and more stubborn than fear — and he stepped aside. "Mind the ink pot. It has opinions."

* * *

The interior of the shop smelled of linseed oil and cold tea and paper that had been waiting a long time to be needed. Elise had visited forty archives in eighteen months and could read a room's relationship with its documents the way a doctor reads a pulse. This room's pulse was wrong. Too tidy. Archives breathed mess; this place held its breath.

Drawers labeled in two hands — one florid, one precise. A drafting table under a dust sheet that had clearly been lifted recently; the sheet lay folded rather than dropped. And everywhere, everywhere, the particular absence of Aldous Ren.

"You knew him," she said, nodding toward a portrait sketch pinned above the desk: a heavy-browed man mid-laugh, caught by someone who'd known how to draw laughter.

"I apprenticed under him. Fourteen years."

"And after?"

"After what?"

"After he died." She watched his hands. People guarded their faces; hands were honest laborers who never learned discretion. Thackeray's went still in the way of hands being ordered to be still. "It was in the harbor district, wasn't it? The official account says robbery."

"The official account," Thackeray said, "says a great many things."

"It does." She let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "That's rather my profession, Mr. Thackeray. Knowing what the accounts say, and knowing what they leave out. The commission is compiling a complete record of the territorial surveys conducted during the war. Both sides commissioned maps. Both sides destroyed maps. What survives is going to matter a great deal when the boundary is drawn."

"And you've come to see what survives here."

"I've come to see what exists here. Survival will be determined later, by committee." She said it lightly, and watched him understand it completely. That was the cruelty of her work, and she knew it: she didn't have to threaten anyone. She only had to describe the machinery politely enough.

Thackeray moved to the kettle without being asked. "Tea. It's salt-adjacent. Ration tea usually is."

"I've had worse in Louvain."

"That's a low bar too," he said, and there — just for a second — the ghost of humor, the man the file called *unremarkable, cooperative, politically inert*. Files were such optimistic documents.

While the water heated, she strolled, unhurried, letting her gloved fingers hover over drawer labels without touching. "Ren was the finest coastal surveyor of his generation. Possibly of any generation. The commission holds some of his early work — harbor soundings from before the war, beautiful things. We're hoping to complete the collection."

"Complete it," Thackeray repeated. He poured the tea. It was, as promised, terrible. He drank his own without flinching, which she noted as either courage or habit.

"There are rumors," she said, "of a final atlas. Nothing substantiated. A rumor with a title attached, even — *Orchard and Harbor*, someone called it, though that may be apocryphal. Rumors attach titles to things the way children name stray dogs. Did he speak to you of any such project?"

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted. She always counted.

"No," Thackeray said.

And there it was.

Elise had heard perhaps nine hundred lies in her professional life, most of them from generals, all of them fluent. This one was not fluent. It came out of him stiff and unpracticed, a man lifting a weight he hadn't lifted in years, and she saw the effort cost him — saw it in the set of his shoulders, the way he studied his tea as if the leaves might offer him a better script. Oscar Thackeray had just lied to a commissioner of the victors' armistice delegation, in his own shop, with his own hands wrapped around a cup he couldn't stop from trembling very slightly at the rim.

She should have pressed. She was trained to press; pressing was half her value. Instead she found herself looking past him, at the portrait of the laughing man, and thinking — absurdly, irrelevantly — of her father, who had spent thirty years in the Ministry of Archives correcting errors that were not errors but decisions, and who had died believing history was a thing that happened to other people's families.

"He must have been a difficult teacher," she said instead.

"The worst." Something real moved through Thackeray's voice, quick as a fish under ice. "He'd make me redo a coastline eleven times because the tide tables were off by minutes no sailor would ever notice. He used to say a map was a promise made to strangers, and you don't break promises to people who'll never meet you."

"Yes," Elise said quietly. "That does sound like him."

Thackeray looked up sharply. "You knew him?"

"I read him." She set down the cup. "Everything the commission holds. Every plate, every marginal note. When you spend months inside a dead man's handwriting, Mr. Thackeray, you start to hear his voice. Yours quotes him." She rose, smoothing her coat. "Thank you for the tea. It truly was awful."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and delivered the sentence she had carried up the stairs like a stone in her glove:

"The commission will be conducting a formal inventory of all map shops, printing houses, and private collections within the city limits before the armistice signing. Soldiers do inventories, I'm afraid. They're thorough and they lack my charm. Anything of significance that surfaces between now and then can be brought to me directly — voluntarily submitted materials receive favorable notation in the record." She smiled. "History is kinder to those who help write it."

Thackeray said nothing at all, which was itself an answer, and they both knew it.

* * *

Outside, the morning had turned bright and indifferent. Elise walked toward the canal without calling her carriage, needing the motion.

She had gone in expecting a hoarder — the type who buried documents in gardens and wept when they were taken. Hoarders were easy. You waited them out. What she had found instead was something far less convenient: a man protecting something out of loyalty rather than greed, which meant pressure would harden him rather than crack him, and a lie told so badly it could only mean one thing.

He was telling the truth underneath it.

There was something in that shop. Something large enough that a politically inert apprentice had broken a five-year habit of invisibility to deny it to her face — and had done it badly, because honesty had atrophied in him like an unused muscle.

Her instructions were clear. Report the suspicion. Request the search detail. General Secretary Ombre wanted Ren's papers found before the negotiators arrived, and he wanted them found quietly, and he wanted certain pages among them never to reach the record at all. She had drafted three reports already in her head on the walk to the shop. All three were accurate. Accuracy was her armor and her alibi both.

And yet.

She stopped at the canal rail and watched the water carry a scrap of printed paper — a broadside fragment, war news, half a headline — under the bridge and out toward the sea. Somewhere behind her, on Lantern Row, a man was standing in his shop deciding what to do about her visit, and somewhere in this city, if the rumor had any spine at all, sat the last true map of the coast, the one thing in Vellmar that could tell the truth about whose water this was.

*Curate it,* Ombre had said. *Assess what serves the record.*

She thought of the boy.

It had been in the dossier's margins, a footnote from a neighborhood informant — Mrs. Havel, landlady, reliable, priced accordingly — reporting an unknown child seen entering the Thackeray premises three weeks prior, no claimant, no registry entry. She had dismissed it as noise. Neighborhoods invented children the way they invented ghosts. But standing at the canal rail with the lie still warm in her ears, the pieces arranged themselves without her permission: a master cartographer murdered for his work; a final atlas rumored and missing; a hidden child with no history arriving at precisely the dead man's own door; and an apprentice lying — clumsily, desperately, loyally — to keep it all in the dark.

It was almost elegant. If she hadn't known better, she'd have said Ren himself had planned it.

Elise took out her notebook and wrote, in her smallest and most official hand:

*Thackeray & Ren: no significant holdings. Subject cooperative. No further action recommended at this time.*

Then she stared at the sentence for a long moment, because it was the first line she had ever written for the record that she did not believe.

She did not cross it out.

She closed the book, put it away, and stood a while longer watching the canal carry its fragments seaward, aware — with the particular discomfort of a professional observer observing herself — that whatever she decided next, she had already, in some small unforgivable way, begun to choose sides.
