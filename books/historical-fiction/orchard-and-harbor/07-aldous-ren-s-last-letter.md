## 7. Aldous Ren's Last Letter

The desk had been locked for five years.

I had walked past it every working day of those five years—past the squat oak monstrosity with its brass fittings gone green at the edges, its drawers shut like a man keeping his own counsel—and every day I had told myself the same comfortable lie: *there is nothing in there you need.* The atlas lived in the flat files beneath the drafting table. Ren's instruments hung on their pegs. His chair, which I now sat in because sitting in my own felt like an admission that this shop was mine and not merely borrowed, creaked in a way I had learned to find companionable rather than mournful.

But the hidden page had changed the arithmetic.

For three nights I had laid Ren's recovered map beside my reconstructed plates and tried to make them agree, and for three nights they had refused. The page showed the harbor channel running deep along the northern spit—deep enough for heavy tonnage, deep enough to matter to any government drawing a line through Vellmar's water—where every official chart I owned, including the ones I had inked myself under supervision during the war, insisted the channel silted out to nothing. It wasn't a correction. It was a contradiction, and contradictions of that size are never accidents. Someone had drawn the official charts wrong on purpose, or drawn Ren's page wrong on purpose, and either way the answer was not going to be found in my own careful hand.

It was going to be found, if anywhere, in the locked desk.

Pip was asleep on the cot in the back room, which is where Pip slept now, and would sleep tonight, and I had stopped pretending to myself that this arrangement was temporary somewhere around the fourth bowl of porridge. The boy slept like a small storm becalmed—one arm flung over his eyes, breath whistling faintly through his nose—and I stood over him longer than strictly necessary before I went to fetch the key.

Ren kept his keys the way he kept everything: with a system so obvious it took me four years to notice it. Not in his pockets, not under the mattress, not in any of the places a searching soldier checks first and last. In the tin of drafting pins on the shelf above the wash basin, buried under a layer of rusted pins, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. I had found the tin years ago and assumed it held nothing but pins. Tonight I dug down past the pins with two fingers, feeling like a thief in my own shop—which, I suppose, in a sense of inheritance if not law, I was.

The key was small, iron, cold. It fit the top-left drawer as though the lock had been waiting, patient as everything else in this shop, for exactly this evening.

* * *

I want to record what the drawer actually contained, because what I expected and what I found were such different countries.

I expected papers about the atlas. Commission notes, survey measurements, perhaps correspondence with whatever authority had funded the work. What I found was ordinary life pressed flat: a receipt for boot repair, dated March 1913; a child's drawing, crayon on butcher paper, of a house with a tree beside it and three figures in front, one of them very small; a ration card, expired, carefully saved anyway; a photograph I did not look at long, because looking at it felt like eavesdropping on grief—a woman with Ren's same stubborn jaw, holding an infant, squinting into sun.

And letters. A bundle of them, tied with string, all addressed in the same hand—the hand from the drawing's margin, where someone had written *for Opa* in wobbling capitals. Letters from a daughter.

I untied the string the way you'd approach a sleeping dog you didn't know. Most of the letters were domestic and dear: complaints about her husband's habit of reorganizing her kitchen, pride in her son's first words, a recipe for plum cake that Ren had apparently requested twice and been sent twice and, judging by the flour stains on the second copy, attempted twice. I read these with my throat tight. This was the man who had taught me to hold a pen like a bird—too loosely and it flies away, too tightly and you crush it—sitting at this very desk reading about plum cake with an expression I had never once seen him wear.

The last letter in the bundle was different. It was not from her. It was addressed *to* her, unsent, undated except for the year—1914, the year he died—in Ren's own small precise script, the script I have spent five years trying to imitate and can now produce convincingly only when I am very tired.

I sat down on the floor, because standing seemed suddenly optimistic, and I read it.

*My dear Mira,*

*You asked me in your last letter who pays for the atlas, and whether the war will make it valuable or worthless, and I told you not to worry your head about such things. That was unkind of me, because you are not a worrier by nature—you get that from your mother—and when you do worry, it is because something has earned it.*

*I will tell you the truth now, though I am ashamed of it, and you may judge me as you see fit. You always were fairer than I deserve.*

*The atlas was commissioned by neither army. When the surveys began, I believed—I let myself believe—it was a scholarly undertaking, funded by the Geographic Institute, that honorable fiction we tell ourselves so we may do interesting work without asking rude questions. But the money comes from men. Merchants. Shipping men, mostly, with interests in the harbor territories, and they paid for the finest coastal survey ever made of this coast not because truth is beautiful but because truth is leverage. Whoever holds accurate charts holds the argument. They meant to use my work as a weapon in whatever peace came after the war, and I told myself that a map cannot help what hands hold it.*

*That lie lasted four years. Then I learned what they intend to do with it. They intend to bury it, Mira. To let the victors draw the boundary along the false channel—the shallow one on the official charts—and to keep the true channel dark until their own ships and contracts have settled the question beyond revisiting. My atlas will sit in a vault while lawyers build a lie on top of it, and no one will ever know the coast was measured honestly at all.*

*I have decided to hide the true harbor plate. I will not say where, even here, even to you, because I do not know whose eyes fall on letters anymore. If anything happens to me, the page must surface on its own schedule, not theirs.*

*I am not proud of how I got here. I took money from men I suspected, because the work was the best of my life and I wanted to do it. That is the whole of my sin, and it is large enough. A mapmaker's job is to put down what is there. I let other people decide what the putting-down was for, and now the only amends available to me are ink and secrecy.*

*Come visit in spring. The boy should see the sea. Tell him his grandfather keeps a shop full of the world and still hasn't seen half of it.*

*Your father,*
*Aldous*

I read it twice. The second time was worse than the first, because the first time I could pretend I was gathering information, and the second time I was simply a man sitting on the floor of a dead friend's shop learning that his dead friend had died ashamed.

He had known. He had known what the consortium intended, and he had hidden the page—not in the desk, not anywhere findable—and then he had walked out into the city one November night and been killed for reasons the constabulary recorded as *robbery, unresolved*, and I had spent five years finishing his atlas believing I was completing a scholar's life's work, when in fact I was completing a weapon he had spent his final months trying to disarm.

Or trying to aim correctly. I genuinely did not yet know which.

* * *

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when I heard the cot creak in the back room, followed by the particular soft shuffle of small feet deciding whether to investigate.

Pip appeared in the doorway dragging the blanket with him like a cape he hadn't grown into yet. He stood there surveying me—me on the floor, surrounded by letters, the drawer hanging open—with the frank appraisal of a customs inspector.

"It's late," I said. "Even for cats."

He considered this. Then he crossed the room, climbed into my lap with the entitlement of the very young, picked up the crayon drawing of the house and the tree, and studied it.

"Opa," he said.

"Yes." My voice came out strange. "I think that's him."

He pointed at the smallest figure. Then at himself. Then back at the smallest figure. And I understood, with a sensation like a floorboard giving way, that he was not pointing at a picture. He was making an argument. *This is me. I was there. This house existed.*

"Was that your house?" I asked gently. "With the tree?"

He touched the crayon tree with one finger, very lightly, the way I touch old vellum. Then he looked up at me and his eyes were full and furious and seven years old, and he said nothing at all, and I held him while he shook, and outside the window Vellmar counted another night toward the armistice with lamps burning in windows that had learned to stay lit.

Afterward he fell asleep against my shoulder, and I carried him back to the cot, and I sat at the drafting table and looked at the hidden page for a long time.

Here is what I knew now, assembled like plates in a folder:

Aldous Ren had taken money from merchants to chart the coast truly. Those merchants wanted the truth buried so they could profit from the lie. Ren hid the harbor plate, was killed, and left behind a daughter named Mira—a name that matched, stroke for stroke, loop for loop, the handwriting on the note pinned to the coat of the child now asleep ten feet from me. *Forget the face,* she had written. Which meant she was alive. Which meant she had gotten the boy out of occupied territory alone, which meant she was desperate, hunted, or both. And Corvane—or whoever moved behind Corvane—had spent five years assuming the buried page stayed buried, right up until someone smuggled it across a border sewn into a child's coat.

Which raised a question I did not enjoy: if the mother had sent the boy to Ren's shop, she must have believed the shop was safe. She must have believed the atlas's secrets were safe here. Either she knew something I didn't—

—or she didn't know her father was dead, and I was going to have to be the one who told her.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the shop did its nighttime trick of seeming larger than it was, full of coastlines waiting for morning.

I had inherited shame, I thought. Ren's shame, folded into the bundle with the plum cake recipe and the crayon house. The master's sins, passed to the apprentice along with the pens. Very well. He had answered his shame with ink and secrecy. Mine, apparently, would require testimony, and a child's memory coaxed back into shape, and possibly a confrontation with men who killed mapmakers.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would start with the gentle part: more questions for Pip, softly asked, bread within reach.

I did not yet know that tomorrow the boy would slip out the door before breakfast and walk himself straight down to the harbor, following a compass in his head that only pointed home.

But that, as they say, is another chapter.
