## 8. The Harbor at Low Tide

The shop had a smell that Pip knew better than his own name, which was a thing he did not have anymore.

It smelled of ink and of the dusty inside of paper and of the oil that Oscar put on the hinges so they would not cry out. It smelled safe. But underneath the safe smell there was another smell, faint as a whisper under a door, and it was salt. Salt came in from the harbor on the wind, through the cracks, up from the stones themselves, and every morning when Pip woke on his pallet behind the counter, that was the first thing he found with his nose before his eyes even opened.

Salt meant *come*.

Pip did not know why it meant *come*. He only knew that when he smelled it, something in his chest turned over like a boat in weather, and pointed, the way a needle points. Except his needle was broken. It spun. Some days it pointed at the door. Some days it pointed at the window where the gulls went by crying their hungry cries. Some days it pointed at nothing at all, just spun and spun until Pip had to sit down because spinning things make you dizzy, even invisible ones, especially invisible ones.

Oscar said, "Stay inside today. The streets are wet and the world is full of fools."

Oscar said this most days. The streets were always wet and the world was always full of fools, and Pip was beginning to understand that these were not really reasons. They were the kind of words grown-ups hung over doors to keep children in, like a curtain made of talking.

But today Oscar had gone out. Oscar went out sometimes, to buy flour or to argue quietly with Mrs. Havel about the coal or to stand very still in front of the map drawers looking at them as though they might say something. And when Oscar went out, the shop went quiet in a way that was different from sleeping-quiet. Sleeping-quiet was warm. This quiet had a hole in it, shaped exactly like the front door.

Pip stood at the window with his nose against the glass and watched the street. A man led two goats past the lamppost. A woman shook a rug and the dust flew up gold in the light. Somewhere down the hill, past all the roofs going down and down like steps, the masts of ships poked up into the sky, thin and white, and the gulls wheeled around them.

*Come,* said the salt.

Pip looked at the door. The door looked at Pip. Doors are very good at pretending they cannot be opened, but Pip had watched Oscar open it many times, and it was only a latch, and a latch is just a small stubborn thing, and Pip was good at stubborn things. Being seven means being good at stubborn things, because you have been practicing since you were six.

He took down his coat — the coat, the one with the secret sewn in it, though Pip did not know about the secret; he only knew the coat was important because Oscar touched the hem of it sometimes with a strange face, like touching a wound — and he put it on, and he stood on the stool, and he worked the latch, which fought him for a moment and then gave up, the way most big things do if you keep at them long enough.

Then the street took him.

* * *

The city was bigger than any map could say. Pip knew this because Oscar had maps of the city, beautiful ones, with little lines for streets and tiny printed names, and Pip had looked at them and thought: *yes, I know this now.* But maps do not tell you that a street can smell of fish and bread and horse all at once. Maps do not tell you that stairs go down between buildings into sudden dark alleys where cats sit like judges. Maps do not tell you that people look at you.

People looked at Pip. He felt it the way you feel rain starting — not drops yet, but the air changing. An old woman selling apples looked too long. Two boys on a corner stopped their game and stared with their mouths open, then whispered to each other. A soldier leaning in a doorway lifted his head as Pip passed, the way a dog lifts its head at a sound.

Pip kept walking. His legs knew the way even though his head did not, and this was the strangest feeling, like being pulled on a string from inside your own knees. Down the crooked street. Past the fountain with the chipped mermaid. Through the arch where the smell of tar began, thick and sharp, mixing with the salt until the salt shouted instead of whispered.

And then the harbor opened up in front of him like a book falling open.

Ships. So many ships. Big ones with black sides and little ones with patched sails, and rowboats bobbing like beetles in a bath, and men everywhere, shouting, hauling ropes, rolling barrels that thundered along planks. Water slapping wood, wood creaking, chains clinking, bells ringing somewhere far off for no reason Pip could find. Gulls screaming insults at everyone equally.

The broken needle in Pip's chest spun faster and faster, and then, suddenly, it stopped.

Stopped dead, pointing.

There was a bollard — a great fat iron post like a mushroom — at the edge of the quay, and beside the bollard there was a crate, and beside the crate there was a spot of stone worn smooth and pale, lighter than all the stone around it. Pip walked to it. He put his hand on the smooth pale spot. It fit his palm almost exactly.

*I sat here.*

He did not remember sitting there. Remembering is like that — a room you know you have been in, but the lamp won't light. He knew the shape of sitting. He knew that someone had held his hand here once, someone with big rough fingers and a voice like a slow wave. The voice had said words. Pip chased the words and they scattered like minnows.

"Opa," Pip said, out loud, to nobody, because that was the word he had, and it was a whole house with no furniture in it.

"You're a long way from home, sprat."

Pip jumped. A dockworker had come around the crate — a huge man, huge like a door is huge, with forearms covered in old rope burns and a beard the color of wet sand. He was looking down at Pip with small bright eyes that were doing a strange thing: going narrow, then wide, then narrow again, like a cat deciding whether the thing in front of it is prey or family.

"I'm not supposed," Pip said, which was true and also all the sentence he had prepared.

"Not supposed to be out? No. I'd say not." The big man crouched down, slow, the way you crouch near a bird so it won't fly. "Where's home, then?"

Pip pointed back up the hill, vaguely, in the direction of everything.

"The map shop." The big man said it flat, not as a question, and something moved across his face like a cloud moving across water. "You live at Ren's old place."

Pip didn't know what to say to that, so he said the other word he had. "Opa."

The cloud crossed the man's whole face then, top to bottom, and settled in his eyes. He sat down right on the cobbles next to Pip, big man on the ground, and for a while neither of them said anything, and it was not a bad silence. It was the kind of silence where both people are listening to the same thing — in this case, the water going *shh, shh* against the hulls, patient as breathing.

"I carried crates for him," the man said at last. "Your — the old gentleman. Aldous Ren. When he was measuring the channel, him and his instruments and his squinting. I carried his boxes and he paid me fair, which almost no one does, and once—" the man laughed, short and rough, "—once he paid me in a map. Drew me my own village, from memory, miles away, and every house on it, and my mother's garden with the gooseberry bushes. Said a man ought to own the picture of where he comes from." He shook his head slowly. "I've still got it. In a tin. Under my bed."

Pip did not understand all of this, but he understood *gooseberry bushes*, and he understood the tin, and he leaned a little closer to the big man without meaning to, the way cold things lean toward warm ones.

"What's your name, sprat?"

Pip opened his mouth. Nothing came out. There wasn't anything in there. That was the whole trouble. He had a name somewhere — he could feel its shape, round at the start — but it had gone down with the ship, wherever the ship was, and all he had was the word Oscar used sometimes, soft and half-asleep:

"Pip," he said. "I think."

"Pip." The man tried it out. "Well, Pip who thinks. You want to know something?" He nodded at the water, at the pale green-brown of it showing the ribs of mudflats where the tide had gone out and left the harbor's bones bare. "That's why you came down here, isn't it. Low tide. Something about low tide."

Pip looked at the mud, at the dark channels running through it like veins, and the broken needle gave one hard twitch and pointed straight out at a line of posts sticking up from the flats, old rotten posts in a row, going nowhere.

*Those,* said the needle, clear as a bell, and then it started spinning again, useless, and Pip's chest hurt with wanting to know.

"There was a boat," Pip said. It was not a memory. It was less than a memory. It was the shadow a memory leaves on the wall. "Little. Night."

The big man went very still. Dockworkers are never still, so when one goes still it is louder than shouting. Then, gently, he moved himself between Pip and the open quay, casual as shifting cargo, and Pip noticed that the man's hand had come to rest on Pip's shoulder, heavy and warm, like a roof.

"Eat something," the man said, in a voice that was not really about eating. "Here."

He fished a heel of bread from his jacket, and Pip ate it, and it was good bread, dense and honest, and while Pip chewed the man watched the quay behind Pip's head the way a lighthouse watches.

Which was lucky. Because that was when the press gang came.

* * *

They came along the quay like a weather front — five men, quick-moving, with a sergeant type in front whose smile was the worst part of him, because it stayed on while everything behind the eyes did sums. They were sweeping the waterfront the way a net sweeps a pond, and they swept toward the crate and the bollard and the boy eating bread.

"Well now," said the smiling man. "What have we here. A volunteer?"

"He's with me," said the big man, not getting up, which somehow made him seem bigger.

"Don't see a name on him."

"Don't see a brain on you, but we all manage."

One of the gang laughed and then stopped laughing fast. The smiling man's smile stayed exactly where it was, which took effort, and Pip could see it taking effort, like a man holding a heavy door closed.

"The fleet takes boys of eight and up," the smiling man said. "This one looks seven. Seven is close to eight. Close is a sea-word. We deal in close."

"He's the mapmaker's grandson," the big man said.

Everything changed. Pip did not understand how or why, but he saw it happen: the smiling man's eyes flicked sideways, the way eyes flick toward a superior officer who isn't there; the other four men suddenly found the harbor fascinating; and somewhere behind the smile, the sums finished, and the answer was *trouble*.

"Ren's lot," the smiling man said, quieter. "Thought that was finished."

"Finished things float up," the big man said. "Ask any dockworker. Ask the tide." He stood up then, all at once, unfolding like a crane, and he was enormous, and the smiling man recalculated again, visibly, and lost.

"We'll be back along this quay Thursday," the smiling man said, to no one, to the water, backing away.

"We'll be here," said the big man agreeably. "We're always here. That's what a quay is."

The weather front moved off and found easier ponds to sweep. Pip discovered his bread had gone stiff in his fist and ate it anyway.

"Thursday," the big man muttered. "Hm. Thursday you'll be behind a locked door if that cartographer of yours has any sense, and if he hasn't—" He stopped, and looked down at Pip, and his face did the cloud thing again. "You shouldn't be out, sprat. Truly. There's men asking after a boy. Have been for days. Asking soft, which is worse than asking loud. Soft askers get answers."

Pip's stomach went cold, colder than the wind off the water. He did not know who was asking. But his body knew. His body remembered things his head had lost, and it remembered being hidden, being carried fast, being told in a weeping voice —

*forget the*

The words wouldn't finish. They never finished. They just rang and rang like the harbor bell.

"Right," the big man said briskly, because grown-ups can hear a child go frightened even when the child says nothing. "Up you come."

He picked Pip up the way dockworkers pick up crates, one arm, easy, and set off along the quay, and Pip rode on the high warm shoulder watching the city swing past below like a view from a masthead. The big man walked fast but not running-fast, the kind of fast that doesn't attract eyes, and he cut through an alley Pip would never have found, and up some stairs, and along a wall smelling of rope and fish guts, and all the time he talked, low, more to himself than to Pip.

"Straight up the hill, past the mermaid, don't look at the corners, corners is where questions live. Old man Ren drew me a map once and I never got to thank him proper, so this'll have to do, won't it, this'll be the thanks. Hold tight, sprat. Nearly there."

At the mouth of the map-shop street, the big man stopped. Set Pip down. Squatted to his level. From here Pip could see the shop door, and standing outside it, a figure — Oscar, back early, and not calm. Oscar was standing very still in the way that meant not-still inside, scanning the street end to end, and the stillness had fear in it, plain as paint.

"Go on, then," the big man said. "And sprat — tell him Brannock sent you home, and tell him the tide's out and won't turn till Thursday, and tell him—" he hesitated, and rubbed his rope-burned knuckles, "—tell him there's soft askers on the quay. Men paying for answers about a small boy. He'll know what to do with that. Or he won't, and then nobody will."

Pip nodded, which was the strongest word he owned.

He ran up the street, small shoes slapping stone, and behind him the big man stood watching until he reached the door, and ahead of him Oscar's face was already changing — fear folding into relief folding into the particular expression of a person counting to ten and needing eleven.

Inside, the latch clicked home, and the bolt, and the salt smell was shut outside, still saying *come*, and Pip stood panting in the middle of the floor and delivered his message, all of it, breathless and jumbled: Brannock, the tide, Thursday, and last, the words that landed in the shop like a dropped anchor —

"There's men on the harbor asking about me. Asking soft. Paying for it."

Oscar went white in a way Pip had never seen, and after a long moment, very quietly, Oscar began pulling down the blinds.
