## 10. The Consortium's Man

He came on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the days Oscar did the accounts, and accounts were where lies lived most comfortably.

The bell above the shop door had been muffled with cloth since the war began—a courtesy to air-raid nights that had long since stopped mattering—so Oscar heard nothing but the change in the light. A shadow across the counter, broad and unhurried, wearing a coat the color of good sherry.

"Master Thackeray." The man's voice was the voice of a man who had never once had to raise it. "I hope I'm not intruding on the sacred work of cartography."

"You're standing on my ink rags," Oscar said.

The man looked down at his boots, laughed as though this were charming, and stepped aside. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned, with hands folded behind his back like a man who had learned that visible hands make people nervous. His smile had the particular warmth of a stove in an empty house—generous, and heating no one.

"Corvane," he said. "Edmund Corvane. Of Corvane & Marsh, shipping. Your master knew me well."

Oscar set down his pen with great care, because great care was all he trusted himself with. "Ren is dead."

"I'm aware. It's rather why I've come." Corvane's gaze traveled the shop with the appreciation of a man appraising more than shelves: the flat files, the hanging maps, the little brass scale for weighing paper. His eyes snagged, briefly, on the door to the back room—the room where Pip was currently eating bread and honey with the concentration of a scholar deciphering a difficult text—and moved on with practiced smoothness.

Too practiced. Oscar had spent five years learning how people looked away from things.

"He spoke of you?" Oscar asked.

"Oh, endlessly. Aldous Ren was the finest mind I ever had the privilege to fund. The harbor charts, the coastal surveys—" Corvane waved a hand encompassing, apparently, all of geography. "I commissioned half his life's work. When he died, I confess I lost track of his papers. War scatters everything, doesn't it? Like a child knocking over a puzzle." The smile again, warmer. "Which brings me to my delicate subject."

"Does it."

"The boy."

* * *

Oscar had prepared, in the five weeks since the knock at the hour of cats, for soldiers, for commissioners, for Mrs. Havel's conscience finally cashing out. He had not prepared for kindness.

That was the trouble with Corvane from the first moment: he was kind. He expressed concern for the child's health, his schooling, his soul. He lamented, with what sounded like genuine feeling, the cruelty of a world that left a boy of seven on a stranger's doorstep in a city still smelling faintly of smoke.

"Relatives," Corvane said. "In the east. Good people. Comfortable. The mother's family—they've been searching for him since the occupation ended. You cannot imagine their relief when word reached me that he'd surfaced here."

"Word reached *you*," Oscar repeated. "How?"

"A network," Corvane said pleasantly. "One develops networks in wartime. People tell one another things. It's how the poor survive, mostly." He let that sit a moment, letting Oscar hear the other half of it: it's also how the rich survive. "I'd like to take him home, Master Thackeray. Today, if the paperwork allows. I have a carriage waiting."

"He has no paperwork."

"Which I can supply. That's rather the point of men like me." Corvane produced, from inside that beautiful coat, a leather folder, and laid three documents on the counter with the tenderness of a man dealing cards he already knew were winners. Travel authorization. Guardianship transfer. A letter of introduction, signed by someone whose name Oscar recognized from the commission's own letterhead.

Everything legal. Everything smooth. Everything ready before Tuesday.

"You came prepared to find him here," Oscar said quietly.

"I came prepared to find him somewhere," Corvane corrected gently. "That he was here is luck. Or providence. Depending on your inclination."

Pip chose that moment to appear.

He did it the way he did everything—sideways, silent, materializing at the edge of vision like a cat deciding whether the room was safe. He stood in the doorway of the back room with honey on his chin and stared up at the tall man in the beautiful coat.

And Corvane's face changed.

It was a small change, a professional's change, smoothed over almost instantly—but Oscar had spent twenty years reading faces over map tables, watching emissaries lie about coastlines, and he saw it. Not recognition. Recognition would have been almost bearable. This was *inventory*. The brief, cold arithmetic of a man confirming a sum.

"There he is," Corvane said warmly, crouching down to the boy's level, which even Oscar had not yet fully dared to do. "Hello, young man. Do you know who I am?"

Pip looked at him for a long, terrible moment. Then he stepped backward, put his hand into Oscar's, and said, with the absolute clarity of a child stating weather:

"No."

Corvane laughed. It rang through the shop like a coin dropped on marble.

"Well," he said, straightening. "Children are cautious. Sensible. But you see the situation, Thackeray. The documents are sound. The family is real. And frankly—" he glanced around the shop, at the dust sheets, at the single lamp, at the careful poverty of it all, "—a boy deserves better than a back room and bread rationed by the gram. Whatever your intentions—and I don't doubt they're decent—you're a bachelor cartographer keeping a secret child in a city full of commissioners. That ends one way, and it isn't the nursery rhyme way."

"I'll consider it," Oscar said.

"Of course. Consider it overnight. I'll return tomorrow at eleven." Corvane gathered his folder, then paused, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate. "You were fond of him. Ren. I could see it just now, the way you keep his desk locked. Sentiment is honorable. But sentiment doesn't survive a hearing, Master Thackeray. Documents do. And I have very good ones."

He left. The bell didn't ring, because he'd noticed the muffling cloth on his way in and lifted it aside himself—courteous even to bells—and after he was gone Oscar stood in the quiet listening to his own heart behave badly.

Pip tugged his hand. "Is he coming back?"

"Yes."

"Don't let him."

Oscar looked down at the boy—at the face everyone kept telling him to forget—and heard himself say, with a steadiness that surprised them both: "I won't."

* * *

The trouble was that fear made Oscar stupid, and he knew it, and knowing it was only half a cure.

All afternoon he turned the encounter over like a suspect coin. The documents were real—that was the frightening part; forgery wasn't Corvane's style, forgery was for lesser men. The relatives were probably real too. Somewhere in the occupied east there likely existed a grieving family, and Corvane would deliver the boy to them or lose him in the machinery between here and there, and either way the boy would be gone and the page sewn into his coat would be gone with him, and whatever Corvane feared would be buried under six hundred miles and a new name.

Forget the face. The note had said it, and now Oscar understood the note hadn't been a plea. It had been an instruction.

But instructions could be traced backwards.

He sat at Ren's old worktable after supper, while Pip slept in the narrow bed that had been Oscar's own during the apprenticeship years, and forced himself through the discipline Aldous Ren had beaten into every student: *when the picture frightens you, stop looking at the picture and look at the margins.*

Margins. Details. The small true things liars forget to furnish.

What did he actually know?

He knew Corvane claimed to be Ren's patron. Plausible—Ren's atlas had been commissioned by money Oscar had never seen the face of, only the results. He knew Corvane knew the boy had surfaced within weeks, in a city crawling with informants, which required exactly the network Corvane admitted to. He knew Corvane had looked at the sleeping arrangement, the locked desk, the dust sheets, and read the whole shop like a chart.

And then, near midnight, pouring himself the last two fingers of a bottle he'd been saving for the armistice, Oscar remembered the gloves.

Not Corvane's gloves. Ren's.

Because there had been a night—five years ago now, the worst night, the night Oscar had spent walking the streets unable to decide whether to grieve or flee—when Mrs. Havel, of all people, had stood in his doorway with her ledger-face and told him the thing she'd seen. She'd done it not from kindness but from the peculiar honesty of informants, who trade in facts the way other women traded in eggs. A gentleman had come to the shop that afternoon asking for Ren. Tall, silver, a coat like poured wine. Ren had gone out with him. Ren had come back alone, late, and had sat at this very table and burned papers in the grate and drunk Oscar's cooking sherry without noticing it wasn't brandy, and had said one sentence that Mrs. Havel, eavesdropping through the floorboards as was her sacrament, had carried up to Oscar like contraband:

*"He wore gray gloves, and he counted my windows."*

Counted the windows. Oscar had thought, for five years, that it was grief talking—Ren paranoid, unraveling, seeing surveillance in curtains. But tonight, replaying the afternoon, Oscar watched the memory of Corvane enter the shop, and watched those pale, unhurried eyes do their slow circuit of the room—

Front window. Side window. The little round window above the flat files that let in the north light.

Three glances. Three windows. Counted.

Oscar set down the glass so carefully it didn't click against the wood.

Gray gloves. He couldn't swear to the color of what Corvane had worn today—it was autumn, everyone wore gloves—but Mrs. Havel had described the coat, and coats like that were not common in Vellmar, not in wartime, not in a neighborhood where the best tailoring was a patched uniform. A merchant who claimed to have lost track of Ren's papers for five years had walked into this shop the week Ren died, taken him out for a final conversation, and returned him home to burn his correspondence.

The boy wasn't an orphan to be placed. The boy was a loose thread, and Corvane had come with scissors dressed as a tailor.

Oscar sat for a long time in the lamplight, and waited for the fear to arrive, the old familiar fear that had governed five years of his life—the fear of being seen, of being named, of the knock on the door. It didn't come. What came instead was something colder and quieter, something with the clean geometry of a coastline drawn from accurate soundings.

Clarity.

They had killed Ren for the atlas and failed to destroy it, because Ren had sewn the truth into a seven-year-old's coat and sent him into the dark with one word—*Opa*—and a woman's handwriting. They had spent five years erasing, and the erasure had a face now, and the face had counted the windows and missed the seam in the lining.

Tomorrow at eleven, Corvane would return with his perfect documents and his patient smile, expecting a tired bachelor to hand over the evidence and go back to invisibility.

Oscar rose, unlocked Ren's desk—not for the letter, though its presence hummed in the drawer like a struck string—and took out instead the hidden page, spreading it flat beneath the lamp. Harbor depths the official charts denied. A channel belonging to no victor's claim. The whole reason for a dead man, a stolen boy, and a merchant's velvet patience.

"They'll want it back," he murmured to the empty shop, and found that the words didn't shake. "Then we'll have to make sure there's too much of it to take."

Above him—or beside him, in the narrow bed—a small voice said sleepily: "Who are you talking to?"

"An old friend," Oscar said. "Go back to sleep, Pip."

A pause. Then, in the dark, with the terrible practicality of children:

"The window man?"

"The window man," Oscar agreed, and turned down the lamp, and began, in his head, composing the list of everyone he would need by morning—because if Corvane had a network, then Oscar needed one too, and there was exactly one woman in Vellmar who sold information by the windowful, and he suspected her price had just gone up.

He only hoped he could afford it before the coal ran out.
