## 11. Mrs. Havel's Price

Mrs. Havel had counted the windows of Vellmar for thirty-one years, and she could tell you without looking that the map shop at 14 Quay Lane had eleven panes in its front window, one of them cracked since the winter of '15, and that the crack ran like a river delta if you held your head at the right angle. She knew which curtains on the street opened before dawn and which never opened at all. She knew that the baker's wife watered her geraniums with the dregs of the tea urn because fresh water cost pride and pride was not rationed. She knew everything worth knowing about Quay Lane, and a good deal that wasn't.

It was this knowledge, she reflected now, standing at her own window with her hands folded in her apron like a woman at a funeral, that had made her dangerous.

She had reported the child. That was the plain fact of it, and plain facts were the only currency Mrs. Havel had ever refused to counterfeit. Six weeks ago—no, seven; time moved strangely now, like a clock in a house where someone was dying—she had written a note to the Office of Civic Registration stating that the cartographer Thackeray appeared to be keeping an unregistered minor on the premises. In exchange, a man with a gray face and a gray coat had given her four coal vouchers and told her she was a good citizen of the restored order.

Four vouchers. Her flat above the shop was warm all through the cold snap. She had slept well, or what passed for well these days, which was sleeping until three and then lying awake listening to the pipes.

And now the consequences were coming up Quay Lane like weather off the harbor, and they were coming toward her own doorstep first.

* * *

She saw them from the window: two men in commission gray walking the street with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been asked for their papers. They stopped at the corner. One consulted a notebook. The other looked up—at the shop, at the windows, and then, briefly, directly at hers.

Mrs. Havel did not step back. Stepping back was an admission, and admissions were for amateurs. Instead she lifted her hand and waved, the way a landlady waves at officials, briskly, as though to say *yes, I see you, yes, I am a respectable woman counting my windows, move along.*

The one with the notebook wrote something down anyway. Of course he did. Writing things down was their whole trade, same as it had been hers, only theirs came with coal vouchers and hers came with nothing but the satisfaction of being right, which, she had learned over thirty-one years, paid worse than coal.

They moved on toward the Custom House. Toward the harbor end of the lane. Not to the shop. Not yet.

Mrs. Havel let out a breath she'd been holding since November.

It was at this point—she would remember this later, the precise domestic absurdity of it—that her kettle boiled over.

She scalded her thumb fetching it off the ring, swore with the vocabulary of a woman who had run a building full of sailors' widows through two wars, and stood in her kitchen sucking her thumb while the steam fogged the little window over the sink. Through the fog she could see, upside down and backward, the reflection of the photograph on her kitchen shelf: Tomas, aged nineteen, in a uniform two sizes too hopeful, grinning the grin of a boy who believed his mother when she said everything would be all right.

Tomas had been dead four years. The letter had used the word *fell*, as though he were a soldier in an old ballad, as though there were honor in drowning in mud at a place whose name she still could not pronounce without her mouth going wrong.

They had quarreled, the last time he'd been home. Over nothing—a girl, a hat, the way he'd laughed at her ledgers. He'd said, "You count everything, Ma. You count the windows, you count the neighbors, you count me." And she'd said, "Someone has to keep account," and he'd said, "Not of people, Ma. People aren't stock."

People aren't stock.

She had reported a child for coal.

Mrs. Havel sat down heavily at her kitchen table, which was scarred with thirty years of teacup rings, each one a conversation she could no longer reconstruct, and she put her scalded thumb flat against the wood and pressed until it hurt more than the guilt did, which was a trick she'd learned from her mother and which worked about as well as it ever had, meaning not at all.

* * *

The child's face was the worst of it.

She had seen him twice from her window—three times, if you counted the morning he'd escaped, though she hadn't known then what she was seeing, just a small figure moving too fast down Quay Lane with the particular urgency of a boy who has somewhere to be and no idea where it is. But the second time, properly, was last Tuesday, when Thackeray had brought him out onto the step to look at the rain.

He'd stood there with his chin tipped up, catching drops on his face, and he'd made a sound—not words, just a sound, a small huffing laugh—and something in Mrs. Havel's chest had turned over like a boat in a swell.

Because he had Tomas's chin. Not literally—Tomas had been fair where the boy was dark, tall where the boy was small—but the *way* of it, the tilt, the stubborn angle of a child deciding the world was worth laughing at regardless of evidence. And she had stood at her window with her hand pressed flat against the glass like a woman at an aquarium, watching a stranger's grandson laugh at rain, and she had thought: *I sold this. I sold this for coal.*

The vouchers were still in her drawer. She hadn't spent the last two. She kept telling herself it was thrift. Thrift was a virtue she could live inside; guilt was not.

* * *

On Thursday, the gray-faced man came back.

He did not come to her door. That was the elegant part, and Mrs. Havel hated him for the elegance. He simply stood across the lane, by the shuttered chandlery, in the drizzle, without an umbrella, looking at the shop. Looking at her window. A man who could wait. A man whose whole profession was waiting for other people to do the decent thing or the terrible thing, whichever came first.

She watched him for twenty minutes. He watched the shop. Neither of them blinked, metaphorically speaking, and Mrs. Havel won the actual contest because she had been staring down tenants since before he was weaned.

Then he touched his hat brim—not a salute, just an adjustment, just enough to say *I know you can see me*—and walked away toward the Custom House.

That evening, Mrs. Havel took out her ledger.

Not the real one—the one she showed to tax men, full of plausible rents and plausible repairs. The other one. The true ledger, kept in a code of her own devising, in which every neighbor on Quay Lane appeared as a symbol: the baker's wife was a small circle with a cross (generous, gossips), the chandlery watchman was a triangle with a dot (honest, half-deaf), and Thackeray the cartographer was a square with a single line drawn beneath it (careful, alone).

She had added the child weeks ago. A small circle beside the square. She looked at it now for a long time.

Then she turned back through the pages, past the war years, past the years of shortages and queues, back to the autumn five years ago when a new symbol had entered her book: a diamond, which meant *official, watch*. Beside the diamond, in her tight hand, she had recorded the only thing of value she possessed, the thing she had traded away her sleep for and never spent:

The name of the man who paid her.

Not the gray-faced man—he was a glove, and gloves changed. The hand inside the glove. Undersecretary Brandt, Office of Civic Registration, who had come to her parlor personally, which no undersecretary does for a coal-voucher errand, and who had asked questions about Aldous Ren's old shop with the careful casualness of a man reading from a list someone else had written. Who had said, "If anything unusual occurs at the premises, Mrs. Havel, the restored order remembers its friends."

She had thought, at the time, that she was being flattered. Now she understood she had been *filed*.

* * *

Friday morning, she baked bread.

This required explanation even to herself. Flour was dearer than conscience these days, and Mrs. Havel was not a baking woman; her oven was for warmth and her kitchen was for accounts. But she had woken at four with Tomas's chin tilted up at the rain behind her eyes, and by six she had decided, and Mrs. Havel's decisions were executed the way she executed everything: promptly, thoroughly, and with a certain grim pleasure in the logistics.

She knocked on the shop door at nine.

Thackeray answered it himself, which surprised her; she'd expected the child. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been sleeping in his chair again—there was ink on his cuff and the particular hollowed look of someone who has been arguing with paper all night. Behind him, the shop smelled of pigment and lamp oil and, faintly, of a boy.

"Mrs. Havel," he said carefully. Landlord and tenant had maintained, over five years, a politeness so complete it was nearly a wall.

"I've baked bread," she announced, holding up the loaf as though presenting evidence at trial. "Don't look at me like that. I bake when I've got a mind to, and I've got a mind to. There's a boy in there needs feeding, and I'll thank you not to tell me he doesn't, because I have eyes, Mr. Thackeray, whatever else may be said of them."

Something moved behind his face—warily, like a fish under ice. "That's kind of you."

"It isn't kindness." She pushed past him into the shop, because she was seventy-one years old and had earned the right of entry, and set the loaf on the worktable among the maps with a decisiveness that dared him to object. "Kindness is free. This is interest."

"Interest?"

"On a debt." She turned and looked at him straight, and she saw him register—slowly, the way careful men register things—that this visit was not about bread. "Sit down, Mr. Thackeray. No—stand, if you like, cartographers always stand, you're all afraid of creasing. I haven't much time. The gray men were on the lane yesterday, and one of them looked at my window the way a man looks at a house he intends to buy cheap."

The silence in the shop changed quality. Somewhere upstairs—or below; she'd never sorted out the geography—a floorboard creaked, small and quick. The boy.

"You've been reporting on me," Thackeray said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse: it was confirmation-seeking, the voice of a man checking a map against the ground.

"Yes." She did not soften it. Softening was for people with clean hands. "Seven weeks ago. For coal vouchers. I told them there was a child here, and I signed my name, and I have regretted it every day since with a thoroughness that would surprise you, and I don't regret easily, I'm too busy for it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because they're coming, and you deserve to hear it from the person who sent them rather than from the boot on the door." She drew herself up. "And because I have something for you, and I want it witnessed that I gave it freely, before God and whatever's left of the law in this city."

From inside her coat she produced a folded paper—not her ledger, she'd copied it out in the night, her best hand, the hand she used for rent receipts—and held it between them.

"The name of the man who paid me. Undersecretary Brandt, Office of Civic Registration. He came here himself, five years ago, asking after Ren's papers, and he's been paying for eyes on this shop ever since. Your eyes, Mr. Thackeray. Mine. The baker's wife's, I shouldn't wonder, though she'd deny it over her own grave and probably will." Her voice, to her fury, wavered. She steadied it the way she steadied everything, by gripping the table. "I gave them a child for coal. I can't undo that. But I can give you the hand that fed me, and I can stand in front of whoever needs standing in front of, and I can say it out loud, in daylight, with my name attached. That's the price I'm paying. It isn't enough. It's what I have."

Thackeray took the paper. He read it twice. When he looked up, there was something in his face she hadn't seen in five years of tenancy—something that might, in a less careful man, have been called gratitude, but in him was more like recognition. The look of a mapmaker finding that two lines he'd drawn separately, years apart, met exactly.

"Mrs. Havel," he said. "Why now?"

And there it was: the question she'd been rehearsing answers to since Thursday, all of them dignified, most of them lies. She looked toward the stair where the floorboard had creaked, and she thought of a chin tilted up into the rain, and she abandoned every answer she'd prepared.

"Because my son died telling me people aren't stock," she said, "and it took me four years and a loaf of bread to hear him." She picked up her basket. At the door she paused, and—because she was who she was—added, practically: "The boy should eat the heel first, it's the best part, and tell him if he crumbs on your maps I'll hear of it."

She was halfway across the lane when the shop door opened behind her.

"Mrs. Havel." Thackeray's voice, low and urgent. "There's a hearing. At the Custom House. Soon—I can't say when. If it comes to testimony—"

She turned around in the middle of Quay Lane, in the drizzle, an old woman with an empty bread basket, and she heard herself say the sentence she had been walking toward all week without knowing it:

"Then write my name down, Mr. Thackeray. You cartographers are good at that. Write it down where it counts."

Behind him, in the doorway of the shop, half-hidden by his coat, a small dark-haired boy peered out at her with Tomas's chin and the beginnings of a question on his face—and down at the harbor end of the lane, unhurried as tide, the gray men were coming back.
