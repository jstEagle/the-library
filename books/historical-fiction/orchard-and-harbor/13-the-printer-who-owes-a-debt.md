## 13. The Printer Who Owes a Debt

Jozef Malin had three rules about his basement press, and he recited them to himself every morning the way other men said their prayers: *print nothing with a name on it, print nothing worth dying for, and never, ever let the ink smell of anything but ink.*

He was breaking all three before breakfast.

The trouble had started five years ago, in the autumn of 1914, when a cartographer named Aldous Ren had come down the alley behind Malin's print shop at two in the morning, bleeding from a wound above his ear, and knocked on the loading door with the particular rhythm of a man who knew he might not be answered. Malin had answered. He still wasn't entirely sure why. Perhaps because Ren had brought bread — good bread, white flour, impossible to find even then — as if arriving wounded at a stranger's door were a social call that required a hostess gift.

"Your press," Ren had said, pressing the loaf into Malin's hands while blood dripped onto the cobbles. "I need it for one night."

"For what?"

"For nothing, if you say no."

And that, God help him, was the answer that had done it. Most men would have argued, or begged, or threatened. Ren had offered him the door out of it, wide open, no shame attached. Malin had stood there holding warm bread in one hand and his own cowardice in the other, and found that the cowardice weighed less than he'd expected.

So he'd printed whatever it was — forty pages, sealed in oilcloth, Ren standing watch at the top of the stairs with a pistol he clearly didn't know how to hold — and by dawn the pages were distributed to twelve houses across Vellmar, and Ren was gone, and Malin went back to printing wedding invitations and funeral notices and the occasional seditious pamphlet about turnip prices, which was the only sedition a printer could safely commit.

Three weeks later, Ren was dead in the harbor district, officially a robbery victim, unofficially nobody's business at all. And Jozef Malin discovered that he had become the kind of man who owed a debt to a dead man, which is the worst kind, because the dead cannot be paid and cannot forgive and cannot, under any circumstances, tell you the debt is settled and let you sleep.

* * *

For five years he had waited. Not patiently — Malin had never been patient in his life; his wife Lotte said patience was a virtue he kept meaning to acquire the way other men meant to learn the violin — but *attentively*. He kept the press oiled and true. He kept a crate of the good paper, the heavy stock that wouldn't yellow, buried under the coal cellar floor where damp couldn't reach it. He kept, wrapped in oilcloth inside the crate, the forty pages from that night, though he'd never read them past the first one, because the first one had been enough: a coastline drawn so lovingly it made him homesick for places he'd never been.

And every few months, someone came asking questions. A polite clerk here, a friendly merchant there, once a very charming gentleman with silver cufflinks who wanted to know whether Aldous Ren had ever entrusted anything to local tradesmen before his death. Malin had smiled his shopkeeper's smile — the one that suggested a mind of warm porridge — and sold the man four hundred wedding invitations at an excellent price.

"You have a lovely establishment," the silver-cufflink man had said on his way out.

"It's the ink smell," Malin had said. "Very calming. Like church."

It was not like church. It was like linseed oil and burnt varnish and thirty years of spilled turpentine. But the man left, and Malin counted the money twice, because paranoia and greed make fine bedfellows.

Now it was October of 1919, the armistice negotiations were crawling toward their deadline like a snail crossing a treaty table, and Oscar Thackeray — thin, gray, careful Oscar Thackeray, whom Malin had known since boyhood and avoided for five years with the discipline of a monk — was standing in his shop at eight in the morning, dripping rainwater on the welcome mat, saying:

"I need you to print something, Jozef."

Malin looked at him over the counter. Then at the door. Then back at Thackeray.

"Come downstairs," he said, "before you say another word out loud. You're standing in front of my window display of christening announcements, and I'd hate for the angels to overhear."

* * *

The basement smelled the way it always did — like the inside of a violin, Lotte said, though she had never been allowed down to verify. The press sat in the corner under its canvas shroud, and beside it, the long steel table where Malin composed type by hand, letter by letter, the way his father had and his grandfather before him, back when printing was a religion and printers were its stubborn priesthood.

Thackeray unwrapped his package on the steel table, and Malin's breath went out of him.

Plates. Copper plates, hand-engraved, in a style he recognized the way a man recognizes his mother's handwriting. Coastlines. Soundings. Harbor depths recorded in numbers that contradicted every official chart printed in the last twenty years. And in the lower corner of each plate, small as a signature on a love letter, the mark Malin had seen only once before, five years ago, by lamplight, at two in the morning: a tiny orchard tree above a wave.

"He finished it," Malin said quietly. "The atlas. He actually finished it."

"The last plates are mine. I completed them after—" Thackeray stopped. Started again. "After. He left instructions."

"Of course he did." Malin ran his thumb along the edge of a plate, feeling the clean cut of the graver, the confidence of a hand that never second-guessed itself. "Aldous Ren planned his own funeral down to the flowers. I once watched him correct a map that was already published. Sent a letter to the publisher's widow explaining, gently, that her late husband had drawn the river six feet too far east, and could she perhaps issue an errata slip, and he'd pay for the printing himself." Malin laughed, and it came out wrong, half a cough. "She sent him back a fruitcake."

"I remember the fruitcake," Thackeray said. "It was terrible."

"It was *terrible*. We ate all of it."

They stood together in the silence that follows a shared memory, which is a different silence than the ordinary kind — warmer, heavier, like a coat you put on that belonged to someone who can't feel the cold anymore.

Then Malin asked the question he'd been keeping in his pocket for five years.

"What do you want me to print?"

"A broadside. The key plates. The harbor channel especially — the depths, the shoals, the true boundary. Enough that when the boundary commission meets, the truth is already on walls and lampposts and in ten thousand hands. They can't erase what everyone has read over breakfast." Thackeray's voice was steady, but Malin noticed his hands weren't. "I need several hundred copies. Good paper. In five days."

"Five days." Malin walked slowly around the press, touching it as he went, thinking aloud the way he thought best. "The press is sound. The rollers need re-covering, I can do that today. Ink I have. Paper—" he glanced at the coal cellar door, "paper I have. Three hundred copies of a broadside this size, single-sided, that's... two full days of pressing if nothing breaks. One day to set the text around the plates. Half a day to proof. Call it three days, if I don't sleep, and I've got a wife who'll notice, because she notices everything, it's her one flaw besides the singing."

"And if something breaks?"

"Something always breaks. That's printing." Malin turned to face him. "But here's my question, Oscar, and you'll answer it honestly or you'll leave through the coal chute: who knows you're here?"

"No one. I told Pip—"

"Pip?"

"My— the boy. I told him I was going out to buy flour. Which, incidentally, I also intend to do, so the lie holds up under inspection."

"The boy. Ren's grandson." Everyone in the whisper-network knew by now; secrets in Vellmar traveled faster than bread prices. Malin studied Thackeray — the gray face, the careful eyes, the man who had spent five years being so invisible that people genuinely forgot he existed mid-conversation — and saw what the war and the waiting and now the child had made of him. Something was burning in there after all. It had just been a very small flame, in a very deep well, for a very long time.

"Who else knows about the plates?" Malin pressed.

"The commission suspects. There's a historian, Vantrell, she came to the shop. She's sharp. Sharper than the soldiers they send. And there's a consortium man, Corvane—"

"Silver cufflinks?" Malin asked. "Charming smile? Bought wedding invitations off me at a criminal markup and complimented my ink smell?"

"That's him."

"Then we're already in the same book, Oscar, we're just reading different pages." Malin pulled the canvas off the press entirely, folding it with the care he gave to flags. "That man has been in my shop twice. Once asking about Ren, once buying enough stationery to open a monastery. I sold him a ream of paper I'd personally urinated on—"

"You didn't."

"—metaphorically, Oscar, metaphorically, keep up — because I didn't want him leaving happy. So yes. Corvane. The commission. And now me. That's your list of people who know, and I'm the newest name on it, and you should know what kind of name you've added." He wiped his hands on his apron, though they were already clean. "I owe Aldous Ren my life. Not figuratively. Literally. Because of those forty pages he printed here in 'fourteen, a man in the customs house — Brenner, you remember Brenner? — recognized his own name in a list of men marked for detention, and got his family on the last boat south. Brenner wrote to me from Marseille. His granddaughter plays the piano now. Badly, he says, but loudly, which he considers a victory." Malin's throat did something inconvenient. He cleared it. "Ren saved a family I never met, using my press, in one night, and then he died for it, and I have spent five years keeping this machine oiled like a widow keeps a lamp in the window. So when you ask whether I'll print — Oscar, I've been printing this in my head since 1914. You've merely arrived to pick up the order."

Thackeray looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Malin had never seen him do: he sat down, heavily, on the paper stool, and put his face in his hands.

"I'm frightened," Thackeray said, to the floor.

"Well, obviously," Malin said kindly. "Anyone sane would be. Would you like tea or brandy? Lotte says fear requires both, in sequence."

* * *

They worked out the plan over tea — brandy being reserved, per household law, for after sundown — and it was during the third cup that Malin raised the matter he'd been turning over like a stone in his pocket.

"There's a risk you haven't named," he said. "The press makes noise. Anyone listening at the street grate will hear a press running for two days straight. My neighbors hear everything. Half of them report to whoever pays, and the other half report for free, out of civic enthusiasm."

"So we muffle it."

"We muffle it, yes, and I've done that before — blankets, straw, running at night. But Oscar, listen to me." He leaned forward. "If this goes wrong, they won't come for you first. They'll come for me. You're a mapmaker; you can claim ignorance, you're half-ghost anyway. But a press is a *place*. It can be seized, burned, taxed, licensed into oblivion. If Corvane wants to end this, he doesn't hunt the truth — he kills the thing that multiplies it. That's me. That's this machine. My grandfather built it. My father bled on it — literally, he caught his sleeve in the gears, lost two fingers, and finished the run one-handed rather than stop the press. It's the only inheritance I've got, and I'm telling you plainly: I'll risk it. But know what I'm risking."

To his credit, Thackeray didn't flinch, and didn't patronize, either. He said, "Is there anything I can do to protect it?"

"Yes." Malin pointed the teaspoon at him. "You can succeed. Nothing protects a press like a truth that wins. Print runs get burned, Oscar, but *facts*, facts that reach ten thousand breakfast tables — those survive arson. So don't print timidly. Print so completely that burning it becomes the scandal."

Thackeray almost smiled. It was a rusty mechanism, that smile, clearly in need of oil, but it moved. "You should write manifestos, Jozef."

"I write wedding invitations. Same fire, smaller font."

* * *

Lotte came down at noon with bread and cheese and a look that said she had known everything since Tuesday. She surveyed the copper plates laid out on the steel table, the shroud folded off the press, the two grown men hunched over them like conspirators in a school play.

"So," she said. "It's finally him."

"How did you—"

"Jozef. Sweetheart. You took the good paper out of the coal cellar in March and put it back twice since. You've cleaned the press every Sunday for five years like it was going to communion. And last night you slept on the *sofa*, facing the door, which you only do when you're planning something either heroic or expensive." She set down the tray. "This is heroic, I hope? We can't afford expensive."

"Lotte—"

"I'm not scolding. I'm *catering*." She kissed the top of his head, which reached her conveniently at shoulder height, and turned to Thackeray. "Oscar Thackeray. You look terrible. Have you been eating?"

"Reasonably."

"That means no. I'll send food to the shop. Don't argue; arguing burns calories you haven't got." At the stairs she paused, and her voice softened into something that wasn't caterer or wife or any role Malin could name. "Mr. Thackeray. The boy upstairs in your shop — the whole street knows, whatever Mrs. Havel thinks she's concealing. My sister teaches at the charity school. Children talk. That boy remembers things." She looked at the copper plates, at the little orchard-tree-and-wave mark, and crossed herself quickly, the way working women do — briskly, like tying a knot. "Print fast, gentlemen."

When she was gone, Malin began laying type. He chose the large body size, the one his father had called the *town crier*, and as he set the headline letter by letter — REN'S ATLAS: THE TRUE COAST OF VELLMAR — he found his hands were steadier than they'd been in years.

There was a strange arithmetic to debt, he reflected, sliding the letters into the composing stick. For five years he'd carried this one, and it had gotten heavier, the way debts do, compounding nightly in the dark. But now that the payment had finally come due — now that his hands were actually moving, the press actually uncovered, the paper actually ordered up from the cellar — the weight was lifting instead of growing. Hope, it turned out, felt exactly like a debt coming due: terrifying, unavoidable, and a tremendous relief to finally be paying.

By evening the forme was locked. By midnight the first proof came off the press, and Malin held it up to the lamp with ink still wet enough to smudge, and there it was: the harbor, the depths, the channel no army owned and every chart lied about — Aldous Ren's patient, posthumous, absolutely unforgiving truth, multiplying.

"Three days," Malin said aloud to the empty basement. "Hold together, old girl. Three days, and then they can burn us if they like — we'll have already spoken."

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard Lotte singing. Off-key, as always, and loudly, which he considered a victory.
