## 14. What Pip Remembers

The shop in the morning smelled like the inside of a pencil.

Pip knew this because he had spent eleven days learning everything about the shop, and the smell was one of the first things he'd learned. It was wood shavings and ink and something sharp like rain on stones, and underneath all of it there was bread, because Oscar toasted the ends of the loaf every morning and called it "the baker's apology," which Pip did not understand but liked very much anyway.

Oscar was at the big table with the lamps lit even though it wasn't dark. That meant important work. Important work happened under lamps, Pip had decided, because lamps made things serious, the way hats made people tall.

"Sit with me," Oscar said, and pulled out the low stool, the good one, the one that spun if you were sneaky about it.

Pip sat without spinning. He was saving the spinning for later, when it would count for more.

"I want to ask you some questions," Oscar said. "Not school questions. Not questions with right answers."

Pip considered this. "Then why ask them?"

Oscar smiled in the way that made his face look like a folded map being opened slowly. "Because sometimes the answers are already in you, and I just need to help them find the door."

Pip thought about doors. He knew about doors. There had been a lot of doors lately, and most of them had been bad ones — doors that opened onto boats, doors that opened onto strangers, one door that opened onto nothing but dark and the sound of a woman crying, which was the worst door of all and which he kept shut inside himself with both hands.

"Okay," he said.

Oscar didn't get paper. Pip noticed that. Grown-ups always got paper when they asked questions, and then they wrote things down, and the writing-down part was what made your stomach go cold. But Oscar just took the little brass wheel off the table and set it spinning, and they watched it spin together until it wobbled and fell flat, and somehow that made it easier to breathe.

* * *

"What's the first thing you remember?" Oscar asked. "Anything. The oldest thing. Like the bottom stone in a wall."

Pip closed his eyes. Under his eyelids there was a whole country of pictures, but most of them were foggy at the edges, like windows breathed on.

"A garden," he said. "With trees in lines. Not wild trees. Trees that were told where to stand." He opened his eyes. "And somebody saying the names wrong on purpose to make me laugh. *Apfel.* No wait. She said it right. Somebody else said it wrong."

Oscar went very still, the way he went still around the map drawers, careful-still. "Trees in lines. An orchard?"

"Orchard," Pip agreed, pleased. It was a good word. It had an *orch* in it like a small orchestra.

"And who was the somebody else?"

Pip reached into the fog. His hand came back mostly empty. "Big boots. A coat that smelled like tobacco and church. He held me up so I could pick one, and I picked the one that was too high, because that was the trick, you always picked the one that was too high." He frowned. "I don't know if that's true or if I made it up."

"That's how remembering works," Oscar said gently. "You hold it up to the light and see if it casts a shadow. Keep going."

* * *

There was a house after the orchard. Pip remembered the house in pieces, the way you remember a dropped plate.

A kitchen with a blue jar. A woman singing something with only two notes, over and over, like a wave. Hands — her hands — floury, quick, folding a letter closed, and him watching from under the table because hiding under tables was a game then, before it became a job.

"She wrote letters," Pip said. "Lots of letters. She licked the envelope and I always wanted to lick the envelope and she never let me, and once she let me and it tasted like glue and disappointment."

Oscar laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and Pip felt warm all the way down, because making Oscar laugh was better than spinning on the stool, possibly even better than bread.

"Do you remember any of the letters? What was in them?"

"They went to Opa." Pip said the word carefully, the way you carry water. "She wrote to Opa across the water, and Opa wrote back with drawings instead of words sometimes. Little drawings. Boats. Depth numbers. I didn't know what depth numbers were then."

"And now?"

"Now I grind them," Pip said proudly. "Into pigment. For maps." He had learned the word *pigment* four days ago and deployed it whenever possible.

Oscar's stillness again. Careful-still. "Can you remember anything your mother said? In the kitchen, or later?"

Pip went to the worst door. He stood outside it for a moment, the way you stand outside a door when there's a dog behind it.

"She said my name," he said finally. "Not Pip. My other name. She said it like it was made of glass."

"What was it?"

He tried. He honestly tried. But the name lived behind the door with the crying, and every time he reached for it the door shook, and shaking doors do not open.

"It starts soft," he offered miserably. "Like a breath."

Oscar nodded slowly and did not write it down, because there was no paper to write it down on, and Pip loved him a little bit extra for that.

* * *

The night crossing came easier than the name, which didn't make sense, because the crossing should have been the frighteningest thing. But memory was not fair. Memory was like the harbor tide — it gave things back in the wrong order, and never the things you asked for.

"There was a boat," Pip said. "A small boat with an engine that coughed. And it was raining, except it wasn't raining, it was spray, and a man wrapped me in canvas like I was cargo." He paused. "I didn't mind. Cargo doesn't have to talk. Cargo can sleep."

"Were you alone?"

"No. There was a woman." He saw her suddenly, clear as a lamp: gray coat, hair coming loose, holding a bundle that was not him. Holding papers. "She wasn't my mother. My mother was—" The door rattled. He backed away from it. "The woman on the boat cried the whole time but quietly, the way you cry when there are soldiers nearby. And there was another man. Big. He talked to her at the dock before we left."

Oscar leaned in, slow, like approaching a bird. "What did he say? Can you hear it?"

"He said..." Pip listened inside himself. The voice was there, big and smooth like furniture. "*Forget the face.* That's what he said. Forget the face, and nobody will look for either of you. He said it twice. He said things twice when he wanted them to stick, like nailing a board."

"Did he have a name? Did anyone call him anything?"

"Herr—" Pip chased it. "Herr Cor... Corvane? Corvane. Like a *corvette*, almost, but wrong." He looked up. "Why is your face doing that?"

"My face is doing nothing," said Oscar, whose face was doing a great deal. "Go on. Take your time. There's bread waiting and nowhere else in the world we need to be."

That was the best sentence anyone had ever said to Pip, and he told Oscar so, and Oscar pretended to be very busy with the brass wheel while his eyes did something shiny.

* * *

They worked through the morning the way you work through a knot — a little pull, a rest, a little pull. Oscar never pushed. When Pip's forehead went tight, Oscar taught him how the dividers opened and closed, click-click-click, and let him measure the distance between two imaginary harbors, and the measuring cooled his head down like a spoon.

Between measurements, the pieces came.

The gray-coat woman had given him the note. He remembered that now, definitely, because he remembered being annoyed — she'd sewn it into his coat lining without asking, jabbing him twice with the needle, and he'd said *ow* and she'd said *be brave, be brave, my brave one,* and those words had stuck to him ever since like burrs.

"The note lady and the boat lady are the same lady," Pip announced. "I checked."

"How did you check?"

"In my head." He tapped his temple. "Her crying sounded the same. Crying has a sound, you know. Everyone's is different. Hers went downhill."

Oscar looked at him for a long time. "Downhill," he repeated softly. "Yes. I suppose it does."

"And the man who said forget-the-face — he wasn't on the boat. He was on the dock, and then he was gone, but I saw him again once after. Through a window. In a town with a clock that had no hands." Pip stopped. The window-memory was fresh, startlingly fresh, as though he'd only just now climbed up to look through it. "He was talking to soldiers. Pointing. Not at me. At a building. And laughing. He laughed like a door closing."

"You remember a clock with no hands?"

"The war broke it," Pip said, as if this were obvious, because it was obvious. "Everything with hands lost them."

Oscar got up then and walked to the window and stood there with his back turned for a long moment, and Pip, who had spent eleven days learning everything about the shop and considerably more than eleven days learning everything about grown-ups, understood that this was a kind of crying too. Grown-up crying went sideways instead of downhill.

* * *

At noon Mrs. Havel brought up soup and pretended she hadn't, setting it inside the door with the expression of someone returning a misdelivered package. Before she turned to go, she looked at Pip for longer than usual. Her eyes did the downhill thing.

"You've got your mother's chin," she said abruptly, and left.

Pip stared at the closing door. Then at Oscar. "Does she know my mother?"

"Mrs. Havel knows everything," Oscar said, "except how to admit it." He ladled soup into two bowls. "Eat. And then — only if you're willing — I want to ask the hardest question last. You can say no."

Pip ate. The soup had barley in it, which he fished out and lined up along the bowl's rim like a fleet. When the fleet was complete, he put down the spoon.

"Ask it."

Oscar crouched so they were the same height, which grown-ups only did when something mattered. Outside, the harbor gulls argued about a fish; inside, the lamps hummed their serious hum.

"Your name," Oscar said. "The one your mother said like glass. If it comes back — today, tomorrow, next year — it will matter very much. To commissions and committees and men in offices who keep ledgers of faces. But it matters most of all because it's yours, and nobody gets to take it, and nobody gets to give it back but you. Do you understand the difference?"

Pip thought hard. "It's like a map," he said. "Somebody can copy it wrong on purpose, but the real coastline doesn't care. The real coastline is still there under the ink."

Oscar made a sound that was half laugh and half something wet. "Exactly that. Exactly, exactly that."

* * *

It came back at dusk, of course. Things that live behind doors prefer dusk; everyone knows that.

Pip was grinding pigment — malachite green, his favorite, because it smelled like crushed summer — when the mortar's rhythm loosened something in him the way rocking loosens a tooth. He was thinking about the orchard, the too-high apple, the trick of always picking the one too high, and the woman's two-note song, and the floury hands folding the letter —

— and the letter had a name on it. Written over and over, dozens of letters, dozens of envelopes, and he had watched her write it so many times that his own finger had learned its shape on the tabletop in flour.

He stood up so fast the stool spun without him, which felt like theft.

"Oscar." His voice came out strange, bigger than usual. "Oscar, come here, come here *now,* I have it, I have—"

Oscar came at a speed Pip had never seen from him, knocking over the dividers and not caring.

"She wrote it on the envelopes. I watched. I practiced it in the flour." Pip's heart was banging like the press downstairs at Malin's shop, bang-bang-bang. He held out his hand, palm up, and traced the letters in the air above it, slow, the way you handle something made of glass. "**Mirelle.** Her name is Mirelle. Mirelle Ren."

The word landed in the room and rang there.

Oscar did not move. Oscar did not blink. Somewhere below, Mrs. Havel's floorboard creaked its evening creak, and the harbor gulls went on arguing, and the world continued being the world, but in the shop on the corner everything had stopped, because Oscar was looking at Pip as though Pip had just unrolled a coastline that every chart in the empire swore did not exist.

"Say it again," Oscar whispered.

"Mirelle Ren." And then, because saying it once had broken the door clean off its hinges, the rest came pouring through after it, all of it, everything: *"She's not dead. She's not dead, is she? The boat lady cried downhill, but Mama — Mama was alive when I left, she was alive, she kissed me and said be brave and she was ALIVE—"*

"Yes," Oscar said, catching him as he crashed forward, lifting him clean off the ground, and Oscar's voice was wrecked and shining and certain. "Yes. Alive. And now, Pip — now we have a name, and a name is a coastline, and no one — no committee, no ledger, no man named Corvane — no one gets to redraw her."

Pip buried his face in Oscar's shoulder and cried uphill for the first time in eleven days, which is to say: loudly, messily, and entirely without fear.

Neither of them heard the knock at the street door below — three soft raps, patient as a debt — nor Mrs. Havel's footsteps hurrying down the stairs to answer it.

But the knock was there. And it did not go away.
