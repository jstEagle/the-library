## 1. The Weight of Paper

The cart from Dunsford let her off at the bottom of Mellowbrook's only real street just as the church bell rang the half hour, and Cyrus Halloran counted the strokes the way she counted everything now—twice, silently, and with the suspicion that one day the numbers would stop agreeing.

Fourteen strokes for a half hour past noon. A town that kept its own time. That was something to know.

She adjusted the strap of her satchel and became a traveling clerk. It was not a transformation so much as a settling, the way water settles into the shape of its vessel. Shoulders slightly rounded—clerks carried too much paper and sat too long at other men's desks. Eyes down but not furtive; a clerk who avoided eye contact entirely was a clerk with something to hide, and a clerk who sought it was a clerk looking for work. The trick was to look through people, at the ledger of the world behind them, and to appear faintly bored by both.

The coat did the rest. It was a good coat, gray wool, cut for a man of middling height and unremarkable means, and in its left lining—between the canvas and the cloth, stitched flat by her own hand in a rented room in Corren with the shutters closed—lay eleven sheets of rice paper bearing the diplomatic ciphers of the Republic of Vessany. Or what remained of it. She had stopped saying *the Republic* even inside her own head three weeks ago, somewhere on the coast road, because a name said too often begins to sound like an elegy, and she could not afford elegies. Elegies were heavy. Everything she carried had to weigh nothing at all.

*You are Tam Weller,* said the papers in her satchel, issued under a seal that had been genuine once and was now merely convincing. *Clerk, second grade, attached to the Commission for Reconciliation of Accounts, traveling under authority of the Provisional Directorate.* The Provisional Directorate existed. That was the beauty and the horror of it—it existed the way a candle exists in wind, flickering in three cellar offices in a city that had already changed flags twice this year. By spring it might exist nowhere at all except in documents like these, which is why she carried them carefully. Dead governments were survived by their paperwork the way dead men were survived by their debts.

She walked up the street at the pace of a man with no appointment worth hurrying for.

Mellowbrook sat two miles south of the old border line, close enough to smell the new regime across the river and far enough to pretend otherwise. The town had the look of places that had decided survival was a trade practiced daily: shutters freshly painted, queues orderly outside the provisioner's, imperial proclamations pasted on the customs house wall with their corners already curling but nobody daring to peel them. Someone had chalked a small blue circle on the well housing—a mark she didn't recognize, which meant either a loyalist sign or a collaborator's tally or children being children. She noted it and moved on. In Mellowbrook, everything would be noted. That was the whole discipline of the thing.

Her instructions, memorized and burned, had been simple in the way that only dying institutions can make things simple: *Wait in Mellowbrook. Keep the codes on your person. Contact will find you within the month. Do not seek contact. Do not write anything. Do not die.* The last instruction had been added in a different hand than the others, smaller, pressed harder into the paper. She suspected whose hand. She had spent four years learning not to think about whose hand.

Aldric Vane had taught her that trick first, back when thinking about him had been permitted and pleasant: *A courier's mind is a room with one chair in it, Cyrus. Whatever else comes in, you put it out again before it sits down.*

She had gotten very good at empty rooms.

---

The lodging house was called the Sign of the Ledger, which she took for an omen either excellent or terrible and chose, by policy, not to decide. Its landlady was a widow named Mrs. Anselm, broad and quick-eyed, with flour on her forearms and the particular hospitality of a woman who charged fairly and forgave nothing.

"Business?" she asked, pen hovering over her register.

"Clerking," Cyrus said. "Reconciliation of accounts. I'm told there's estate work around Mellowbrook since the—since things changed." She let the pause do the work. People filled silences with their own politics, and whatever they filled them with told you who they were.

Mrs. Anselm filled hers with a sniff. "There's accounts enough to reconcile, God knows. Half the farms upriver changed hands this summer, and not one of them by honest sale." She wrote *T. WELLER, CLERK* in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages. "Front room, top of the stairs. Supper at six, and if you want your boots blacked there's a boy comes round Thursdays."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, pay me. Three marks the week, in advance."

Heads of households. Names in registers. Boots blacked by a boy who came on Thursdays. This was the texture of safety now—not walls and passwords but the accumulation of small, verifiable facts that made a man boring. Boring men were never searched. Boring men were remembered wrongly and described vaguely, and vague description was the best armor a disguise could wear.

She paid four days' advance in mixed coin, counted out with the fussiness of a clerk who resented every coin leaving his purse, and climbed to her room.

It was small and clean and had one window facing the orchard wall. She set the satchel on the table, sat with her back to the door out of habit, and did nothing for a full ten minutes except listen. A town teaches you itself in sounds before it teaches you in sights. Cart traffic on the north road—heavy wheels, army pattern. A drill whistle from the garrison yard, two notes, repeated. Children in the lane behind the kitchen, playing some game with counting-out rhymes that had probably been treasonous rhymes last year and would be patriotic ones next year, depending on how the wind sat. And underneath it all, the bell, keeping its stubborn private time.

When the room had told her all it knew, she opened the satchel and inventoried it as though checking another man's accounts: spare shirt, razor, ink bottle, three pens, a blank notebook with the Commission's watermark, a tin of mending wax, and forty-one marks in coin folded into a stocking. Nothing that could hang her except the coat, and the coat never left her body while she slept any more than her skin did.

She touched the lining once, over the heart, where the paper lay. Eleven sheets. Eleven sheets that could buy a pardon, start a war, save a life, or get several thousand people killed depending on who read them and in what order. They weighed less than a letter from home. She had weighed them on a druggist's scale in Corren, out of some grim curiosity, and the number had offended her: less than an ounce. All that death and hope, lighter than a letter from home.

By the fourth day she had Mellowbrook in her head the way a clerk has a ledger: ruled, balanced, cross-referenced.

The market ran Tuesdays and Fridays, thick with farm carts until midmorning. The garrison detachment numbered perhaps sixty men under a Captain someone called Breck, and they changed the checkpoint rotation on the north road every third day, which meant it wasn't really a rotation, it was a habit, and habits could be scheduled around. The customs house posted new tariff sheets each Monday, and a queue of merchants formed by seven, and merchants waiting in queues talked, and talking merchants were a newspaper that delivered itself free of charge. The provisioner's wife was the sister of the postmaster, and the postmaster read letters aloud to illiterate customers in his back room, which meant the post office was the true parliament of the town. None of this went in the notebook. The notebook held figures for hay tariffs and marginalia about weather. The real record lived in the room with one chair.

She learned faces, too, and their weights. The miller's son limped from a wound he didn't discuss. The customs officer with the gray mustache was honest, which made him predictable, which made him safe. His junior, a young man with a chinful of ambition, checked papers twice and enjoyed doing it—mark him. The priest said Mass for the old rite quietly and the new rite loudly, hedging his bets between heaven and the occupation, and Cyrus found she couldn't dislike him for it. Everyone in Mellowbrook was hedging. The whole town was a hedge. Hedges kept things alive through winter; that was their function.

She gave herself a routine because routines were camouflage. Ink and paper at Halloran's stationer's—the coincidence of the shop's name had startled her for half a breath on the first day, and she had used the startle deliberately, remarking on it to the shopkeeper with a dry smile, turning a liability into a joke, because a joke was memorable and memorable was fine when the memory was harmless. *Weller of the same name as your shop, what are the odds,* and the shopkeeper had laughed and given her a discount on blotting sand, and now she was *the clerk who shares our name*, which was a far better identity than any forged paper could issue.

In the afternoons she mended ledgers for Brenner the chandler, who paid her in tallow candles and gossip. Old ledgers, war-damaged ledgers, pages swollen with damp, entries crossed out by three different occupying administrations until the columns looked like battlefields. She reconciled them with real pleasure, actually—there was comfort in arithmetic, in a world where two and two still made four regardless of whose flag flew over the countinghouse.

"You've a gift for this, Weller," Brenner said on the sixth day, watching her untangle a double-entry mess that three men had abandoned. "Ever think of settling? Town could use an honest pair of hands with figures."

"Traveling work suits me," Cyrus said.

"Hm." Brenner sucked his pipe. "Suits most men till it doesn't. Then they wake up forty-five with no garden and wonder where the years went."

"A garden," Cyrus repeated, noncommittal, filing the word away.

"There's plots behind the churchyard. Widow Ostrey keeps the keys. Half the town grows beans there since the food levies started. You'd want to ask her."

He meant nothing by it. He meant exactly nothing by it, a chandler making conversation, and yet that night Cyrus lay in her narrow bed and felt the trap of it close gently around her: to be believed in, truly believed in, a place must be able to imagine you staying. A man with no garden was a man passing through, and men passing through drew eyes. Men with bean rows were furniture.

She asked Widow Ostrey for a plot the next morning. The old woman looked her over with the frank appraisal of someone who had buried a husband and two sons and had no patience left over for nonsense.

"Clerk, are you? Clerks don't know soil."

"I grew up on a holding," Cyrus said, which was true, which was the kind of lie she preferred—truths bent only slightly at the joints. "My father believed a man should always be growing something he could eat."

"Hm. Your father sounds sensible. Plot nine, by the wall. It's gone wild since the Kesslers left. You'll have it clean by month's end or I give it to the cooper's boy."

"Yes, madam."

"And Weller—" Ostrey's eyes were sharp as awls. "Whatever it is you're really here for, don't dig anywhere near my wall. I count my chickens."

Cyrus held her face perfectly still, which was the hardest work she'd done all week. "I'm here for the accounts, madam."

"Mm," said Widow Ostrey, and went off counting something.

Plot nine was a rectangle of neglect maybe ten paces by six, choked with bindweed and the skeletons of last year's bean frames. She worked it in the hour before supper, when the light went long and soft and the orchard beyond the wall threw shade across the ground, and she worked it slowly, because hands busy in soil had a way of loosening the mind's discipline, and loose minds were dangerous cargo.

But there was no one to perform invisibility for among the bean rows. That was the treachery of gardens. Alone, with dirt under her nails, she was not Tam Weller. The name thinned. Underneath it, in the dark loam where names rotted down into what they'd been before, there was a girl from a hill holding who had learned cipher-work at sixteen because she had a head for puzzles and a family that needed the fee, and a young lieutenant who had stood under flowering apple boughs—

She put it out of the chair. One chair, one occupant. The occupant was bindweed.

She pulled bindweed.

The orchard beyond the wall was older than the town's newest griefs—half apple, half pear, planted in ranks by some prosperous dead man who had expected the trees to outlive him usefully, and they had. Now soldiers from the garrison came on alternate mornings with saws and a surveyor's chain, measuring trunks and marking the straightest ones with blue paint for army timber. Gun carriages, someone said. Barracks roofing. Each morning there were fewer branches and more stumps, and the townspeople walked past on the road with their eyes fixed on the middle distance, because looking directly at a thing being dismantled was a form of participation, and everyone in Mellowbrook had become very skilled at not participating.

Cyrus watched from plot nine, her hands in the dirt, and understood the orchard better than the surveyor did. She had grown up around felled orchards. Armies ate wood the way fires ate it, and afterward the ground remembered the shape of every missing tree for decades, pale circles in the grass, ghost-rings where roots had been.

*This is what we are now,* she thought, surprising herself. Not the republic—republics were documents and borders and other abstractions. This. An orchard being measured for timber by men with paint pots, while everyone who loved it studied the middle distance.

She knelt there a moment longer than the weeding required.

Then she finished the row, washed her hands at the pump, put Tam Weller back on like the gray coat, and went in to supper.

On the ninth evening, Mrs. Anselm caught her on the stairs with a lamp in one hand and a letter in the other.

"For you, Mr. Weller. Came up with the carrier from Dunsford." She handed it over with the frank curiosity of the professionally hospitable. "Your people know where you are, then?"

"My mother," Cyrus said. "She writes whether I answer or not."

"Mothers do." Mrs. Anselm's voice went briefly somewhere else, some interior room, then returned. "Supper's cold by now, but there's bread."

Up in her room, Cyrus turned the letter over. It was addressed in a fair round hand to *Mr. T. Weller, at the Sign of the Ledger, Mellowbrook*. The seal was a plain wafer, unmarked. No sender's name on the reverse.

She did not open it immediately. She sat with it in her lap and listened to the house settle—boards ticking, rain starting against the window, the landlady below scraping a pot—and made herself consider the possibilities with the cold bookkeeping the work demanded. It could be a check from the Directorate: a loyalty test, a timing exercise, a way of confirming the address without exposing anyone. It could be a warning. It could be the contact protocol itself, wrapped in domestic paper, because the best hiding place for a message was inside something that looked like nothing. It could be, in the worst accounting, bait—written by people who wanted to know who collected it and what they did next.

Or it could be exactly what it pretended to be. A mother's letter. There were mothers still writing letters into the collapse; she had seen the postmaster reading them aloud, sons' names and sons' silences, the whole war conducted in fair round hands.

She slit it open with her pen knife along the top edge, cleanly, the way archives opened correspondence.

Inside was a single sheet. The handwriting shifted halfway down the page—from the round commercial hand into a tighter, slanted script she recognized with a jolt she kept strictly off her face, though her face was alone, though her face was allowed.

*Dear Tam,* the first part read, in the mother-voice, full of weather and small news and a request to wrap up warm. Then, lower, cramped against the margin as though squeezed in hastily before posting:

*The orchard at home is being cut. Come see it before the spring if you can. Ask for the plot by the wall. —A friend of your father's.*

No name. No date beyond the postal mark. But the phrase *plot by the wall* was not a phrase; it was a key. Her instructions had contained no contact protocol beyond *wait*. Whoever wrote this knew where she slept, knew the cover, and knew—or guessed—the one detail she'd been given by a chandler five days ago. Either her network was better informed than she'd feared, or someone in Mellowbrook was reporting on her movements with a speed that made her spine go cold and quiet.

And beneath the tradecraft, underneath the analysis, in the part of her that kept no ledger: *A. V.*, in that slanted hand, after four years. He was alive. As of however many days ago a carrier took to walk from wherever this was written, Aldric Vane was alive and writing to her in a dead man's script.

Cyrus fed the letter to the candle flame and watched the mother-voice curl and blacken first, then the margin, then the slanted hand, and held the last corner until the fire kissed her fingertips, and dropped it in the dish.

Rain on the window. Bell tolling nine, patient, private, wrong by no clock but its own.

She sat in the dark with one chair in her mind, and for the first time since Corren, she let something else sit down in it: not the promise, not yet, not the memory of flowering boughs—but the arithmetic. Because *come see the orchard before the spring* was an invitation, and invitations required travel papers, and Tam Weller's papers were genuine, which meant they were worthless for anything that mattered.

Tomorrow she would need to begin forging a document worthy of the man whose handwriting she had just burned.

She got out her pens and uncapped the ink.
