## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at half past eight on a Tuesday, which Cyrus would later decide was the worst possible hour for it. Early enough that a clerk should be awake and at his accounts. Late enough that any man pretending to be somewhere else would already have had time to rehearse.

She was mending the hinge on the garden gate when the sound reached her—two raps, precise, official, spaced like punctuation. She finished seating the pin first. That mattered. A man startled by a knock was a man with something to startle about.

Through the hedge she could see the figure waiting in the lane: a woman, mid-thirties perhaps, wearing the grey-and-black sash of the Imperial Census Bureau over a travel coat too good for the mud of Mellowbrook. She carried a leather folio under one arm and stood the way soldiers stand when they've stopped being soldiers but haven't found anything else to do with their spines.

Cyrus wiped her hands on her apron, arranged her face into the mild, faintly put-upon expression of a working clerk interrupted, and opened the gate.

"Morning," she said. Flat, local, unhurried.

"Census of persons and holdings," the woman said. "By authority of the Provisional Administration. I'm Ferran. Odile Ferran." She said her own name the way other people presented credentials—as if the name itself should open things. "You are?"

"Halloran. Cyrus Halloran." The lie came out smooth as water off a slate roof. Nine months of practice. "I keep books for Master Ellery at the grain exchange, and I lodge here with Widow Sarel."

"Widow Sarel is your—" A pause, pen already moving. Ferran wrote as she spoke, a habit that unsettled people who weren't trained for it. Cyrus was trained for it. She kept her eyes on the folio's edge, not the pen.

"Landlady. I rent the back room and the garden plot."

"The garden's yours to work, then?"

"As much as any garden is anyone's. I dig where she tells me."

Something flickered at the corner of Ferran's mouth—not quite a smile. "May I come in? The forms go faster at a table."

They went faster nowhere, Cyrus knew. But refusing an interview invited a second visit, and second visits came with escorts. She stepped aside and gestured toward the cottage door.

* * *

Widow Sarel was real, which helped. That had been the first rule of Mellowbrook: build the disguise out of true materials. The widow was seventy-one, deaf in one ear, delighted by company, and genuinely believed her lodger was a quiet young man from the southern provinces who sent money home to a sister. Cyrus had done nothing to create that belief except never contradict it. The sister existed. There was even a sister—Maren, dead three years now, buried outside Vessany City under a name that no longer appeared on any registry. Dead sisters made excellent correspondents. They never wrote back, and grief explained any irregularity in the letters.

Sarel fussed over the census taker, produced elderflower cordial, and was gently steered back to her chair by the arm. Then it was table, folio, ink, and the small dry scratch of the imperial pen.

"Name."

"Cyrus Halloran."

"Age."

"Twenty-six." Twenty-eight. Close enough; faces lied more persuasively than numbers.

"Occupation."

"Clerk. Ledgers, invoices, correspondence. Master Ellery will vouch."

"I'm sure he will." Ferran didn't look up. "I spoke to him yesterday. He says you're careful with figures."

"He'd say that or I'd find new employment."

That earned her a glance—a quick, level look that lasted perhaps a second longer than courtesy required. Cyrus held the returned gaze with exactly the amount of patience a bored clerk would offer a bureaucrat: tolerant, faintly amused, already thinking about his afternoon. Inside, she was doing arithmetic of a different kind. *He spoke to you yesterday.* So this wasn't a random sweep. Ferran had been in town two days at least, asking questions before she asked them formally. That was not census procedure. That was investigation wearing census clothes.

"Household members," Ferran said. "Everyone who sleeps under this roof more than three nights in a month."

"Myself. Widow Sarel. Her granddaughter comes Thursdays to help with washing."

"And others?" The pen paused. "People who come and go. Visitors. Men who stay a night or two."

"No one stays. It's two rooms and a loft, madam. Where would they sleep?"

Ferran wrote something. Cyrus couldn't read it upside down and didn't try—trying was how people got caught reading. Instead she looked mildly toward the window, at the garden, at nothing.

"You keep the plot yourself?"

"Mostly weeds. The soil's tired."

"What do you grow?"

"Beans. Onions if the onions cooperate. Some herbs for the kitchen—widow likes thyme in everything." True, all of it, and that was the point. Every answer anchored to something verifiable. A lie built entirely of lies collapsed under its own weight; a lie built on top of a life could bear a man's whole weight.

"Thyme," Ferran repeated, and wrote it down, and Cyrus felt the first cold thread slide down between her shoulder blades, because there was no reason on earth to record a lodger's herbs. Census forms counted heads, ages, trades, livestock. They did not count thyme. Unless the form wasn't the point. Unless the writing itself was the method—filling the page with harmless details so the subject relaxed, so the rhythm of question-and-answer became comfortable, so that when the real question arrived it arrived disguised as one more entry in a dull morning's ledger.

It was well done. Cyrus almost admired it.

* * *

The questions circled for another quarter hour. Ferran wanted to know when Halloran had arrived in Mellowbrook (March, before the thaw), where he'd lodged before (with a cousin in Drenmark, deceased since—grief again, useful grief), whether he could produce papers of movement (he could, and did, and watched without watching while Ferran examined the travel writ issued by a provincial office that no longer existed). The writ was genuine. Forged documents were for roads and checkpoints. For a doorstep, you wanted paper that was real but untraceable—the bureaucracy's own debris, scavenged from offices that had burned or fled. An empire in expansion generated infinite paperwork and verified none of it. Cyrus had learned to live in the gaps between stamps.

"Papers in order," Ferran said finally, sliding the writ back across the table. And then, pen lifted, eyes still down: "One more thing. Not on the standard form. My supervisor asks after travelers. People passing through who might need recording for later reference. Has anyone stayed with you, worked beside you, or passed through Mellowbrook recently whom you remember? Anyone notable."

*There it is.*

Notable. The word sat in the air like a fishhook in clear water. Cyrus let two seconds pass—long enough to suggest thought, short enough to suggest innocence—and said, "Grain buyers come through most weeks. A carter named Petch who talks too much. Last month a surveyor for the army timber requisition, very rude about our fences." She shrugged. "Nobody notable. Notable men don't come to Mellowbrook."

"No," Ferran agreed softly. "They don't. And yet." She wrote one final line, longer than the others, and blotted it with a small square of sand. Then she closed the folio, and the click of the clasp sounded, absurdly, like a lock turning.

Cyrus walked her to the gate. At the hedge, Ferran paused.

"You'll be in town long, Mr. Halloran?"

"As long as the ledgers need me."

"Mm. Ledgers always need someone." The census taker settled her sash and glanced once—at the garden, at the beans, at the tired soil—and Cyrus understood, with a certainty that arrived all at once and fully formed, that Odile Ferran had already decided something about her. What, she couldn't say. Whether it was suspicion or mere professional appetite, she couldn't say either. "Good day, Mr. Halloran. You may see me again. Verification visits are standard."

"Of course," Cyrus said. "Anything for accurate records."

Ferran's mouth did that thing again—that almost-smile, filed away unfinished. "Almost no one says that," she said, and walked off down the lane with her folio under her arm, leaving Cyrus standing at a garden gate with dirt under her fingernails and ice in her chest.

* * *

She gave it until noon before letting herself think about it properly, because thinking too fast produced conclusions, and conclusions produced decisions, and decisions made at noon were usually wrong by dark. Instead she worked. She copied invoices for Ellery through the morning, her pen steady, her sums exact, her face arranged into its permanent mild attention. The work was a kind of prayer. Numbers behaved. Columns balanced or they didn't, and neither outcome required you to become someone else.

But underneath the arithmetic, the morning kept replaying.

*You may see me again.*

The problem wasn't the questions. Questions were weather; you dressed for them. The problem was the thyme. The problem was the way Ferran wrote while listening, capturing everything indiscriminately, building a document that was mostly noise—which meant that buried in the noise, somewhere, was a line about a lodger named Cyrus Halloran whose sister was dead and whose cousin in Drenmark was dead and whose entire history consisted of the departed. A page full of ghosts, written in a government hand, destined for a government file.

And files had a way of finding each other. That was the thing about ledgers nobody told you when you learned your letters: a name written down does not sit still. It gets copied. It gets cross-referenced. It waits, patient as seed, for the season when some clerk in some office draws a line from one column to another and a man who never existed is suddenly, catastrophically, present.

She had spent nine months being no one, and she had been good at it—good enough to forget, some evenings, watering the beans in the last light, that the forgetting was a technique. Today the mask had developed a seam. Not torn. Just visible, if you knew where to look. And Odile Ferran, Cyrus was nearly sure, knew where to look.

At midday she ate bread and hard cheese at the widow's table and answered Sarel's cheerful, half-deaf chatter about the granddaughter and the price of soap. Then she took her cup of barley tea to the garden and stood among the bean rows doing the only planning that could safely be done: none. No new routes scouted. No papers moved. Any change in routine now would be a confession written in behavior, legible to anyone comparing this week against last. The discipline was to do exactly what she had always done, while inside, quietly, every contingency began turning over like stones.

Except one stone wouldn't turn. It just lay there, flat and heavy, with a word on it.

*Recorded.*

Her name was in an imperial book. Not her name—she corrected herself, standing very still among the beans—but the name. Cyrus Halloran, clerk, twenty-six, grower of beans and thyme, nephew of a dead cousin, brother of a dead sister. A fictional man had just been born into the empire's memory, and fictional men died easily, but they also *led* places, because everyone who came looking for Cyrus Halloran would follow the trail he'd left, and the trail ended at a garden gate in Mellowbrook, at a woman wearing him like a coat.

She crouched, pressed her thumb into the soil, felt the cool damp of it. Somewhere north, past checkpoints and garrisons and the whole grinding machinery of occupation, Aldric Vane waited in whatever cell they'd put him in, believing—or having stopped believing, she couldn't know which—that a promise made in a peacetime orchard still bound anyone to anything. The codes sewn into her coat lining weighed nothing. They had always weighed nothing. It was the being-seen that had weight, the sick slow realization that anonymity wasn't a wall but a window, and someone had just pulled back the curtain to check whether anyone was home.

"Mr. Halloran!" Sarel called from the doorway, cheerful through her deafness. "There's a letter come for you. The carrier just left it."

Cyrus rose, brushed the soil from her knees, and fixed her face into place one more time.

Inside, on the table, beside the widow's sewing basket, lay a single envelope. No seal. No return direction. And in the corner, in a small precise hand she did not recognize:

*From the Bureau of Census and Records. With thanks for your cooperation.*
