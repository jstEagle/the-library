## 3. What the Orchard Remembers

The requisition notice went up on the market post on a Tuesday, nailed there by a boy too young to shave, and by Wednesday the whole of Mellowbrook was talking about the orchard as if it had already died.

*By order of the Provisional Authority for the Eastern Districts: all standing timber of suitable dimension to be surveyed and marked for army use. Owners to cooperate. Resistance will be treated as sabotage.*

Suitable dimension. Cyrus read the notice twice, committing it to memory the way she committed everything—fully, once, no second look. The orchard above the town had belonged to a widow named Brannock who had died in the first autumn of the war, and her nephew in the south had neither the means nor the interest to contest what happened next. The plot had passed informally into the hands of whoever tended it, which for three years had meant the traveling clerk who paid a modest rent and asked no questions about why the deed had never changed hands.

Her. It had always been a strange arrangement to be grateful for. A woman alone could not lease an orchard; a minor clerk with a letter of introduction from a Mellowbrook merchant could, provided everyone agreed not to look too closely at the clerk's soft jaw or his habit of keeping his hat brim low against the sun. She had learned early that people saw what the paperwork told them to see. The ledger said *Cyrus Halloran, clerk*, and so a clerk is what they beheld—a thin, forgettable young man with ink-stained fingers and an orchard he kept out of some private sentiment.

She climbed the hill road at first light, before the surveyors were due, wanting the trees to herself one more morning.

There were sixty-two of them when she'd come. She counted now out of habit, walking the rows: fifty-eight. Two had come down in the winter storm, and two more had been felled piecemeal by neighbors too frightened or too hungry to care whose apples they were burning. Fifty-eight remained, most of them old stock—Bramleys and a few crooked pears that predated the republic itself—and all of them, as of Wednesday, technically the property of an army that had never existed when those pears were grafted.

She put her hand flat against the nearest trunk. Bark rough as unshaved cheeks. She had learned grafting here, in this orchard, in another life.

* * *

Five years ago, in the spring of the last good year, she had come up this same hill carrying a basket of ledger paper for the widow Brannock's accounts, and found a man standing among the pear trees with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, arguing gently with a bee.

"You're in his way," Cyrus had said. "He's decided about that blossom."

"Then he's more decisive than the Ministry of Trade," the man said, turning. He was maybe forty, gray coming in at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone accustomed to being listened to and no longer sure he wanted it. "Aldric Vane. I'm auditing the widow's estate. Or I was, until the bee made a better argument."

"Cyrus Halloran." The name came out of her mouth smooth as a coin from a well-worn purse. She had been wearing it for two years by then and it fit better than her own name did. "Clerk."

"Clipping coupons?" He glanced at the basket. "For a dead woman's orchard?"

"Somebody has to keep the numbers honest. Dead women can't defend themselves."

Something shifted in his face—not surprise exactly, but recognition, the way one craftsman knows another's work. "No," he said slowly. "They can't. And living ones are hardly better at it." He picked up his coat and slung it over his shoulder. "Walk with me, Master Halloran. Tell me what you know about grafting, because I suspect you know everything about something else entirely."

She should have walked away. Every instinct trained into her across two years of false names said: *do not be interesting*. Interesting people get remembered. Remembered people get described. Described people get found.

But it was spring, and the light came down through the pear blossoms like something poured, and she was twenty-four years old and had not spoken honestly to another human being in eleven months. So she said, "I know that you take scion wood from the variety you want, and you cut it in winter, and you set it into hardy rootstock that can survive where the fancy tree cannot. And then," she heard herself add, "you wait years, and mostly you get nothing, but sometimes you get fruit."

Vane stopped walking. He looked at her for a long moment with an expression she would spend the rest of the war trying to forget—the expression of a man finding water in a desert he'd stopped believing held any.

"That," he said quietly, "is also how a republic survives."

They met after that every week or so through the whole of that long, fat summer. He would come up from the capital on ministry business that grew thinner and more desperate each visit—he never hid it from her, not after the third meeting—and they would walk the rows while he talked. Not state secrets, not at first. Just talk. The kind of talk that assumes tomorrow: whether the new bridges would hold, whether the Ministry's reformists could outlast the reactionaries, whether apples stored in sand kept better than apples in straw. Ordinary talk. The luxury of ordinary talk.

He knew what she was by midsummer. He never asked how a girl came to be a clerk named Halloran, only once said, watching her prune, "You cut away anything that would draw the eye. Even the branches."

"Even the branches," she agreed.

"Doesn't the tree mind?"

"The tree doesn't get a vote."

He laughed at that—a real laugh, startled out of him—and she understood later that this was when he began building the promise. Men like Vane did not ask for things directly. They planted. They set scion into stock and let the seasons do the persuading.

It came in October, the last warm week of the last good year. They stood at the top row where the ground fell away toward the capital, its smoke smudged thin against the horizon, and he handed her an apple off the oldest tree without asking, the way you handle something that belongs to both of you already.

"If war comes," he said. No preamble. He had clearly been rehearsing for weeks. "Not border skirmishes—I mean real war, the kind that eats ministries. If the republic falls, there will be a record that has to survive outside it. What we did. What was done *to* us. Names, dates, decisions—who signed what, who profited, who looked away." He turned the apple in his hands. "Paper burns. Archives burn. People who remember inconveniently get remembered *against their will*, permanently. But a dispatch carried by nobody—a clerk, a ghost, a person who appears in no registry—" He looked at her then, and she saw that he was ashamed, and that he was going to ask anyway. "I would need someone invisible. Someone who could carry a testimony north through whatever comes, and deliver it to hands that would act on it."

"And you're asking a dead woman's orchard keeper."

"I'm asking the only honest bookkeeper I've ever met." He held out the apple. "You'd have the right to refuse. That's the whole point of what we built—all of it, the whole rotten beautiful experiment—so that a person could say no."

She took the apple. She ate it right there, looking at him, juice running down her wrist, because saying yes with your mouth full seemed somehow less solemn and therefore more survivable.

"Yes," she said.

"Even though you don't yet know what you'd carry?"

"Especially then." She wiped her wrist on her sleeve. "I've spent three years being no one, Aldric. Let me at least be no one *for* something."

He nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony. No oaths, no documents. In the republic they had believed in, promises between people were supposed to be enough. That belief was the first casualty, though it took the rest four more years to follow.

* * *

Now she stood among the fifty-eight remaining trees with the requisition notice folded in her pocket and her thumb pressed against the bark of the oldest pear, the one they'd eaten under, and tried to feel like a courier instead of a mourner.

The surveyors arrived at mid-morning: two men and a boy with chalk and a measuring line, moving down the rows with the brisk indifference of men pricing coffins. They marked trunks with white crosses. *Suitable dimension.* The Bramleys went first—thick, straight, forty years of growth worth a season of gun carriages. Then the big pears. The boy with the chalk hesitated at the oldest tree, the one at the top row, and looked back at his supervisor.

"That one's hollow," the supervisor said, glancing at it. "Skip it. Rotten core, no good for timber."

Rotten core. Cyrus kept her eyes on her ledger as they worked, making small marks, being a clerk. The tree wasn't hollow. It had a scar where lightning had struck it decades ago and healed over it, a seam of hardened wood running up the trunk—but the heartwood was sound. She had checked it herself, years ago, on a day that had felt idle and now felt like providence. Sound as a drum.

The boy moved on. The oldest tree went unmarked.

She watched them chalk white crosses onto forty-one trees and thought: *they're marking them the way Odile Ferran marked me.* The census taker's pen had crossed her name into an imperial form nine days ago, and Cyrus still woke some mornings with her heart going like a rabbit's, seeing that small neat script. Being written down was being fixed in place. Being fixed in place was being aimed at.

And now the same thing was happening to the orchard. Everything that stood tall got measured. Everything that got measured got taken.

When the surveyors left, she climbed to the top row and put her ear against the old pear's trunk, the way you might listen at a door. Wind moved through the upper branches. Somewhere below, a cart creaked under a load of something requisitioned.

"Aldric," she said aloud, to no one, to the tree, to a man she hadn't seen in four years and might never see again, "if you're alive, I'm still coming. You'd better be alive. I didn't eat that apple for nothing."

The wind answered with blossom-fall, late and thin this year, white petals settling on the fresh chalk crosses like snow that didn't understand what it was landing on.

She stayed until dusk. Then she took out her knife and, working quickly, in the failing light, cut six lengths of scion wood from the oldest tree's healthiest limbs—finger-thick, bud-bearing, wrapped in damp cloth and tucked into the oilskin roll that lay against the diplomatic codes sewn into her coat lining. Anyone searching her would find a clerk's grafting supplies and think nothing of it.

Let them take the timber. Let them burn the branches. Wood cut in winter sets into hardy stock and waits years, and mostly you get nothing.

But sometimes you get fruit.

She was halfway down the hill road, dark coming on, when she saw the lamp moving along the fence line below—a lantern carried at the pace of someone official, unhurried, certain of welcome. And beside it, unmistakable even at that distance, the silhouette of Odile Ferran, census taker, walking up toward the orchard gate with a folder under her arm and her shadow stretching long behind her across Cyrus's only remaining country.
