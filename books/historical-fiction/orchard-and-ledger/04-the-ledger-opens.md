## 4. The Ledger Opens

The Bureau of Enumeration kept its provincial office above a cooper's shop on Tanner's Row, and by mid-afternoon the smell of oak shavings rose through the floorboards so thickly that Odile Ferran had learned to taste it. Oak and glue. She had worked here eleven weeks, and in that time she had come to understand the empire not through its edicts or its garrisons but through its paperwork: the way a conquest announced itself first as a form, then as a filing system, then—only much later, when it was too late to matter—as a flag.

She sat at the third desk from the window, the one with the warped leg shimmed with a folded requisition slip she'd placed there herself, and entered names.

That was the work, when you reduced it to its bones. Names, in, names, filed. A household was a column. A person was an entry. An occupation was a code letter—*A* for agricultural, *T* for trades, *C* for clerical—and a clerk, she had discovered, was almost always *C*, because clerks were the sort of people who filled out their own census forms correctly and left nothing for her to infer.

Mellowbrook had been easy, which should have warned her. Border towns were supposed to be difficult—smuggled relatives, dead brothers still drawing grain rations, wives who'd become widows only on paper to keep a farm out of requisition. Mellowbrook had given her two days of clean columns and polite answers, and one garden plot at the edge of town where a traveling clerk named Halloran grew cabbages and answered questions like a man reciting lines he'd rehearsed but never quite believed.

Odile dipped her pen, wrote *Halloran, C., occupation: clerk (itinerant), residence: lodging at Wren's, garden plot no. 4,* and moved to the next line.

Then stopped.

The nib hovered over the paper, leaving a small black bloom of ink that spread like something growing.

It was nothing. She told herself it was nothing. But there was an itch behind the entry, the kind of itch a good enumerator learned to respect the way a sailor respects a change in wind. Eleven weeks of forms had trained her ear to hear the difference between an answer and a performance, and Halloran had performed. Not badly. That was the trouble. Bad liars tripped; good ones were smooth all the way down, and smoothness in a border-town clerk was about as natural as silk on a turnip.

She pulled the prior registries.

The empire had inherited the republic's records intact—the victors always did, because the first thing any state wanted to know was who it had conquered—and the Vessany civil rolls for Mellowbrook went back nine years, bound in cracked green leather that smelled of dust and old authority. Odile worked through them methodically, cross-referencing by surname first, which was how the manual prescribed it, and then, when *Halloran* surfaced nowhere, by initial, by trade, by anything.

Nothing.

Nine years of registries. Births, deaths, marriages, conscriptions, tax assessments, grain allotments, the militia levy of four years ago that had taken every man between sixteen and fifty and recorded even the ones who'd fled. Nine years, and not one Halloran anywhere—not in Mellowbrook, not in the surrounding parishes, not in the migration ledgers that tracked every soul the war had shuffled across three provinces.

People did not appear from nowhere. The war made people disappear constantly; it made them reappear almost never.

She sat back. Through the window she could see the cooper's yard below, a boy stacking staves, and beyond it the slate roofs of Mellowbrook going gold in the late light. Somewhere down there, a cabbage garden and a borrowed room and a woman—or a man; the form said male, the voice had pitched low, the shoulders under the coat had been squared in a way that suggested effort—who existed in exactly one place in the entire administrative world: the fresh page under Odile Ferran's pen.

*I created this person,* she thought, and the thought arrived with a strange vertigo, because it was true in a way that had nothing to do with ink. Before yesterday, Halloran had been invisible. Unregistered, unrecorded, untaxed, undrafted—invisible the way ghosts are invisible, the way the empire preferred its people until it could count them. And now there was a line. A single line of her handwriting, and everything that flowed from a line: the follow-up inquiries, the verification visits, the cross-checks against the garrison's deserter lists, and after that the Bureau's second-tier procedures, the ones described in the manual in careful bureaucratic language that meant *disappearances*.

The manual called discrepancies "irregularities of record." Her supervisor, Magistrate Corven, called them *opportunities*, and he pronounced the word the way other men pronounced *promotion*.

She looked at the entry again. *Halloran, C.*

There was a version of tomorrow in which she simply finished the page, closed the register, and let the name sleep. Entries got lost all the time. Pages stuck together; files migrated; the war ate its own paperwork faster than any fire could. A name that appeared in no prior registry could remain a name in no future inquiry, if the hand that held the pen decided it.

And there was another version of tomorrow. In it, she flagged the anomaly, initialed it in red the way the manual instructed, and walked it up the stairs to Corven's office, where the magistrate would put down his tea, read the entry twice, and look at her with the particular bright attention he reserved for subordinates who brought him leverage. Discrepancies were rewarded. He'd said so at her interview, before she'd understood what he meant. *The empire pays for precision, Ferran. Sloppy provinces stay poor; accurate ones get funding, and accurate officers get noticed.* He had a way of saying *noticed* that made it sound like both a reward and a threat, which perhaps it was.

Eleven weeks ago she would have flagged it without hesitation. She'd taken this posting for reasons that had seemed solid as ledger-lines: steady pay, imperial service, and—she was honest enough with herself, alone, at night, to admit it—advancement. The Ferran family name was attached in every surviving record to a republican soldier, Tam Ferran, listed missing after Sennet Field, and Odile had learned what a tainted surname earned you in the new order: nothing, unless you earned your way out of it. She had thought accuracy would be her ladder. Count things correctly, file things correctly, be useful, and eventually be worth more than your brother's ghost.

Now she held a pen over a name that had no history, and found that the ladder felt different from underneath.

Because here was what eleven weeks of census work actually taught you, the thing the manual never said: every entry was a person who had sat across from you and chosen what to tell you. The forms pretended otherwise—they pretended that households were facts—but Odile had sat in kitchens where women gave ages wrong by ten years to keep sons off the levy lists, and she had written down the false ages, because the alternative was writing down the true ones and letting the machinery do what the machinery did. She had developed, without meaning to and without approving of it, a private taxonomy of lies. There were lies of greed, lies of shame, lies of fear. And there were lies of survival, which had a texture she recognized now, a carefulness, a rehearsed smoothness—

Like Halloran's.

She pressed the nib to the margin, hesitated, and did not write the red initials.

*You don't know anything,* she told herself. *A missing registry is not a crime. Records burn. People move.* All true. Also beside the point, and she knew it, because the question was never whether Halloran had done something wrong. The question was what happened to people who surfaced as irregularities in the third year of an occupation, and the answer was that they stopped surfacing at all.

Below the window, the cooper's boy dropped a stave, swore with impressive fluency, and picked it up. Ordinary sounds. An ordinary town. Somewhere in it, a clerk was probably watering cabbages, unaware that his existence had just become a question mark in green leather and fresh ink.

Odile thought about her brother. She tried not to—it was an indulgence, and indulgence was a luxury of the pre-war world—but the thought came anyway, the way it always came when she handled names. Tam had been a name in a ledger once too. Missing after Sennet Field: six words that had ended him administratively while leaving him biologically unresolved, and Odile had spent two years understanding that the unresolved were the worst off, because the empire could do nothing with them and therefore forgot them entirely. No death benefit. No clearance. Just a line in a military roll that no one would ever update, because updating required either a body or a bureaucracy interested in looking, and the empire was interested in neither.

Names were debts. Someone had told her that once—her mother, maybe, back when her mother still said things worth keeping. Every time you write someone down you owe them something. She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, holding a pen over *Halloran, C.*, owing a stranger she'd met for twenty minutes in a garden.

The light was failing. Down the row, the other enumerators were closing their registers, blowing out lamps, gathering satchels. From upstairs came Corven's tread, unhurried, the tread of a man whose day always ended exactly when he decided it did.

Odile squared the pages. She entered the last household—a widow named Brask, three goats, one son of conscription age whom the widow claimed was dead, and whom Odile had heard, two nights ago, whistling in the goat shed. She had written *son deceased* in her fair round hand, and slept fine, and told herself the whistle was a cat.

Then she came back to Halloran.

Here is what she did: nothing clever. She copied the entry cleanly into the master register, complete and accurate, because omitting it would be falsification and falsification, if discovered, was the one sin the Bureau hanged people for. But she did not flag it. She did not initial it red. She did not carry it upstairs. She let the entry sit among forty others, ordinary as gravel, one line of many in a province full of lines, and she closed the book on it.

For tonight, the name stayed asleep.

She was buttoning her coat when the stairs creaked and Corven descended into the office, carrying his teacup with the saucer balanced on top in a trick he performed to signal, obscurely, his superiority to spillage. He was a dry man, narrow as a ledger spine, with the habit of reading people the way he read reports: quickly, for errors.

"Ferran." He glanced at the master register, closed on her desk. "Mellowbrook complete?"

"Complete."

"No irregularities?"

The question was casual. Nothing in his voice or posture insisted on an answer. That was the craft of the man—he built his traps out of politeness and waited to see who stepped in them.

"None worth noting," Odile said.

Corven's eyes rested on her a moment longer than the sentence required. She watched him decide—whether to press, whether to probe—and saw, with a cold little thrill, the exact moment ambition won whatever internal argument he'd been having, and he smiled instead.

"Discrepancies get rewarded," he said mildly. "When you find one. When you find several, they get remembered. Officers who notice things go far in this bureau." He set his cup on her desk, precisely centered. "I'll want Mellowbrook verified within the month. Spot checks. You'll conduct them yourself—you know the households now. Start with anyone who struck you as..." He searched for the word with evident pleasure. "*Interesting.*"

He collected his cup and climbed back upstairs, and Odile stood alone in the cooling office with the lamp guttering and the smell of oak shavings rising through the floor like the building itself was breathing.

Start with anyone who struck you as interesting.

He might as well have handed her a leash and pointed it at a cabbage garden.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the master register sat closed on the desk, and inside it, in her own fair round hand, a person named Halloran existed now in exactly one place in the world—one line, one page, one binding of green leather—and Odile understood with terrible clarity that she had not saved the stranger at all. She had only delayed herself. Verification was coming regardless; Corven had ordered it; refusal was a signature on her own file. Within the month she would walk back to Mellowbrook with official sanction to be curious, and the only choice left to her was what to do when curiosity found what it was looking for.

She let herself out into the street. The evening smelled of rain coming and woodsmoke already arrived, and somewhere south of Tanner's Row, past the shuttered shops, a garden she'd visited two days ago lay dark and quiet, its keeper presumably asleep, unaware of being interesting.

*One month,* Odile thought, pulling her collar up against the damp. *Find out what you've written down before someone else reads it.*

Behind her, in the darkened office, the ledger held its breath.
