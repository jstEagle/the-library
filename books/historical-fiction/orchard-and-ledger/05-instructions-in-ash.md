## 5. Instructions in Ash

The knock came at half past two in the morning, and it was wrong.

Cyrus knew every legitimate sound Mellowbrook made after dark—the groan of the cooper's sign in wind, the dog behind the chandler's that barked twice and lost interest, the creak of her own floorboards as they took on the night's cold. She had catalogued them the way other clerks catalogued ledger entries, because a woman who had spent six years being no one understood that safety lived in patterns, and death lived in deviations.

Three knocks. Pause. One knock. That was the protocol. What she heard instead was one knock—soft, slurred, like a knuckle dragged rather than struck—and then a weight settling against the door itself, the way a body settles when it has run out of arguments with gravity.

She lay still for a count of thirty, listening to her own heart do arithmetic it had no business doing. The codes were sewn into the lining of her grey coat, four inches of oilcloth stitched flat against her left hip, and she caught herself reaching for them before she had decided anything else.

*If war came,* she had promised, seven years ago, under trees heavy with fruit and the fat complacency of peacetime, *I will carry your testimony north myself.*

War had come. The trees were going to the army's sawmills plank by plank. And someone was dying against her door.

She rose without lighting a lamp. In the dark she moved through the borrowed rooms of her borrowed life—the clerk's narrow bed, the clerk's spare table, the clerk's single window overlooking the garden plot she tended because tending things was what invisible people did—and lifted the bar from the door.

He fell across the threshold like something unloaded from a cart.

She got the door shut and barred before she looked down at him properly, and then she wished she hadn't looked so quickly. He was maybe twenty-five and had been handsome once, in the way of men who smiled easily, but fire had taken the left side of his face from temple to jaw, taking the ear with it, and whatever had happened after the fire had taken most of his blood. His coat was republican officer's cut with the insignia ripped off, leaving only pale rectangles where the thread had held. His boots were gone. Someone had removed his boots, and that detail frightened her more than the burns did.

"Name," he rasped, when her hand touched his shoulder. "Give me the name first. Protocol."

"Coldharbour," she said. "Cyrus Coldharbour." It wasn't her name. It was the shell around her name, the whelk she'd built herself into. "You're at the Mellowbrook drop. You're safe enough for now."

"Sennet—" He coughed, wetly. "They burned Sennet Station. Tuesday. I carried the packet out through—" another cough, longer, folding him nearly double— "through the orchard rows while it went up. They were shooting the runners in the open. I went through the trees."

"You walked from Sennet?" That was three days' travel for a healthy man on good roads. For this man it should have been impossible.

"I stole boots at Fetter's Cross. Wrong size. Left them." He laughed, or produced the sound that laughter becomes when a man is past caring how it lands. "A clerk's report would note my poor planning."

Cyrus found herself almost smiling. Almost. "Let me see you by lamplight. Then we'll trade wit."

* * *

By lamplight he was worse than the dark had suggested, which was its own kind of information. She worked over him with the methodical hands she'd learned from a field surgeon in the last year of the border war—wound, burn, wound again, inventory of damage—and he talked, because burned men talk, because silence lets the pain organize itself into ranks.

His name was Pellam Ostrow. She did not ask; he gave it anyway, which told her he thought he was dying, and which meant the protocols protecting both their identities had already been spent like a drunkard's wages.

"They came at dusk," he said, while she packed the burn along his ribs with honey and clean linen from her own stores. "Not imperial regulars. They wore our colors, Cyrus. Our colors, and they shot the couriers in the yard and put the torch to the archive room with the code clerks still inside."

"Loyalists," she said quietly. Remnant factions, the ones who wanted the republic's secrets buried with the republic itself. She had known they existed the way you know a storm is coming—by pressure, by birdsong, by the smell of the air. Now the storm had a body count.

"Worse than loyalists. Organized loyalists. Funded." His good eye found hers and held it with terrible steadiness. "The packet I carried has the northern route. Or half of it. The rest burned."

"Half a route is worse than none," she said. "It tells the enemy where to wait."

"It tells *you* where not to go." He let his head fall back against her table leg. "The northern road through Harrowgate is closed—compromised, watched, every checkpoint photographing faces. There's a secondary crossing at the old mill road past Tarnbeck, but it's cold, unstaffed, and the pass beyond closes with the first snows. Which are early this year. Everyone says the snows are early."

"And Vane?"

Something moved across the ruined half of his face—not quite a flinch, but the ghost of one. "Kettermoor garrison. The prison block. He was taken at the river crossings in the autumn, held for interrogation, and now they're keeping him alive, which should frighten you more than if they'd hanged him. Men they hang are finished with. Men they keep are being used."

"For what?"

"I don't know. But the packet says it, in cipher, and I couldn't read the whole of it before—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the general catastrophe of his condition— "before everything. Half the pages are ash. What survived says Kettermoor, says the garrison commandant's name begins with D, says the delivery window is eleven days. After that the cipher keys rotate and your codes become waste paper."

Eleven days. Cyrus looked at the man bleeding slowly into her floorboards and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: three days back to health he would never have, four days north even by the mill road if the weather held, and winter leaning on the calendar like a creditor.

"You can't travel," she said. "You know that."

"I know that." He closed his eyes. "That's why they sent me to you and not the reverse. A dead courier delivers nothing. A dead message is just paper. But a living clerk—" the ghost of the smile again, awful on the burned side, gentle on the whole side— "a living clerk who has spent years learning to be nobody can walk through gates that stop everybody else."

"The codes stay sewn where they are," she said, more sharply than she intended. "I'm a census entry and a garden plot. That's all."

"Are you?"

She stood, busied her hands with the lamp wick, gave herself something to adjust. Outside, far off, a cart rattled through the square—imperial hours, requisition traffic, the ordinary machinery of occupation grinding through the small hours. Somewhere in that noise was a world that wanted Aldric Vane silent and Cyrus Halloran nonexistent, and it was very large, and she was very small, and the distance between those facts had been her entire strategy for six years.

"Cyrus." Pellam's voice had changed—quieter, stripped of the gallows humor. "There's a page I saved from the fire. Not in the packet. I memorized it instead, in case the packet was taken off my body." He recited, slow and exact, the way men recite things they have repeated to themselves through pain: *"The bearer carries the testimony of Aldric Vane, given freely at the orchard at Mellowbrook, in the year before the war. It names the traitor of Sennet Field. Deliver it whole or not at all. Partial delivery serves the traitor."*

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when a passenger shifts their weight without meaning to.

*It names the traitor of Sennet Field.*

Sennet Field. The rout that had broken the republic's army in an afternoon, the surrender that had opened the capital's throat, the disaster every survivor had a different story about and no story fit. And Vane—he had been there. He had commanded the third column. He had come home from it gray-faced and silent and had asked her, weeks later, among the apple trees, to promise.

She had thought she was carrying diplomatic dispatches. Routine traffic, the last formal breaths of a government in exile. Paperwork. She had told herself paperwork for months, because paperwork could be delivered by anyone and forgiven by history.

This was not paperwork. This was a confession with a target drawn on its face.

"How much did you read," she asked, "of the surviving pages?"

"Enough to know the commandant at Kettermoor isn't holding Vane out of duty." Pellam's voice dropped further. "And enough to know why the loyalists are burning their own archives. Whatever Vane's testimony says about Sennet Field, some of our own people would rather it stayed ash." He paused. "Some of them may be closer to you than you think. Check everyone. Even the ones who taught you."

Even the ones who taught you. Her mind went, unwillingly, to Master Ellery at the relay office, who had trained her hand at cipher and called her *the steadiest student I ever ruined*, and to the contact schedule pinned inside her memory, and to the question she had never let herself ask: *who else knows a clerk lives in Mellowbrook?*

* * *

Dawn found her in the garden, kneeling in the frost-stiffened earth with dirt under her nails, doing nothing useful whatsoever.

Pellam slept—or lay in the shallow imitation of sleep that burned men manage—under her bed, dosed with willow bark and laudanum she had bought over two months from three different apothecaries so no single purchase would seem remarkable. The floorboards beneath him were scrubbed. His ruined coat was folded in the stove, waiting for a match. By tonight he would be a traveling peddler convalescing at a cousin's farm, a fiction she would build around him board by board until it bore weight, or until it collapsed, whichever came first.

But that was later. Now there was only the garden, and the light coming up gray over the orchard wall, and the arithmetic.

Eleven days. A compromised northern road. An alternate crossing that closed with snow. A prisoner whose survival was itself a warning. A confession that named the traitor of Sennet Field—a confession she had promised, among these very trees, to carry north *myself*, she remembered, she had insisted on the word *myself*, and Aldric had laughed and said *you always did insist on carrying things*, and she had said *someone has to*, and neither of them had imagined that the someone would be standing in a frost-killed garden counting days against winter.

She pulled a dead stalk of beans and crumbled it between her fingers. The garden had been her idea, when she'd taken the cover: a clerk keeps a plot, it explains the dirt under the nails, the early rising, the purchases of twine and seed. A disguise with roots in it. She had expected to find the work tedious. Instead she had found, over months, that she liked it—that the beans had come up honest, that the onions kept their appointments, that here was a corner of the world where her attention produced exactly what her attention promised. A life measured in harvests instead of escapes.

Pellam's words sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *A living clerk can walk through gates.*

She could refuse. She could pass the packet sideways, find another runner, argue that a burned courier and a compromised route meant the network was blown and the wise move was burial—bury the codes in the garden, literally, under the bean rows, and let the republic's last secrets compost into the soil of a town that had never asked her anything except her name on a census form.

Odile Ferran's neat handwriting surfaced in her memory, unbidden. *Halloran, C.* Two letters in an imperial ledger, and how long before some ambitious clerk cross-referenced them against a missing-persons list?

No. Burying the codes meant burying Vane's testimony, and burying the testimony meant the traitor of Sennet Field went unnamed, unpunished, possibly promoted. Meant Odile's brother—if Odile had a brother, if half the missing soldiers of Sennet Field had families still waiting—stayed a question mark forever.

Meant her promise became what promises become when you feed them excuses: a pleasant memory, a thing you tell yourself about yourself.

She looked at her hands. Clerk's hands now, ink-stained, gardener's-rough. Six years ago they had been a soldier's hands, and before that a girl's, and the girl had stood under flowering apple boughs while a tired colonel said *if anything happens to me, the truth goes north, and it has to be someone the empire would never look at twice*, and she had said yes before he finished asking, because that was who she had been before the disguises: someone who answered.

The sun cleared the orchard wall and lit the frost silver, and the felled stumps at the orchard's edge stood in the light like teeth.

Eleven days.

She stood, brushed the dirt from her knees, and went inside to wake Pellam Ostrow, because the first thing a forged set of travel papers needed was a traveler to forge them for, and the second thing it needed was a reason, and she had just realized—with the calm, cold clarity that had carried her through six years of being nobody—that the best reason in the occupied territories was a grieving sister traveling north to petition a garrison commander about a brother imprisoned at Kettermoor.

All she had to do was become someone new. Again. Quickly.

And somewhere across town, in an office smelling of ink and starch, a census taker named Odile Ferran was sharpening a pencil over the Halloran discrepancy, and neither of them yet knew how badly each would need the other before the snows came early.
