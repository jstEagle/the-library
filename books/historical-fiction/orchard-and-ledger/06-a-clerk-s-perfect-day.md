## 6. A Clerk's Perfect Day

Cyrus Halloran woke before the bells, as she did every day, and lay in the dark listening to Mellowbrook arrange itself around her.

The baker's shutters first, then the creak of his cart wheels on wet cobbles. A dog somewhere by the mill. The low argument of the Tessa women over whose turn it was to fetch water. She knew the town the way she knew her own pulse now—every sound filed, every rhythm catalogued. Nine months since the census taker had written her name into the imperial ledger, and still she rose each morning with one question: *does anyone remember me today?*

So far, no.

She dressed as Tammas Ellery dressed: wool shirt gone soft at the collar, waistcoat buttoned to the second hole because Tammas was a careful man who kept his clothes a season behind fashion and his opinions two seasons behind everyone else's. In the washstand mirror, a narrow face with ink stains at the cuticle of the right hand. Tammas Ellery wrote for a living. Cyrus Halloran had once carried a sword, and her hands remembered it even when her face forgot—but the stains helped. People saw what they expected to see. Ink-stained fingers meant ledgers, meant letters, meant a small man with a small life.

She checked the coat lining with two fingers, the way other men checked for a watch. The oilcloth packet was there, flat against the left breast, sewn where a heart would be if clerks had hearts. It had not moved. Nothing had moved in nine months except her own dread, which traveled constantly, without papers.

Downstairs, Mrs. Petch had left bread and dripping under a cloth, and a note in her round schoolmistress hand: *Mr. Ellery — the Hendersons' boy will call about copying the tithe rolls. Be kind to him, he is simple but willing.* Cyrus ate standing up, read the note twice, and burned it in the stove. Not because it mattered. Because burning things was the only habit she'd allowed herself to keep from the old life, and a person must keep *something* or the mask grows into the skull.

The morning was cold enough to show breath. Frost had come early this year, silvering the orchard rows beyond the east fence, and Cyrus walked through it with her collar up, nodding to the carter, nodding to the priest, nodding to nobody, all of it calibrated. Too much greeting made a man memorable. Too little made him strange. The trick—the whole art of it—was to be exactly as visible as furniture.

*You are getting good at this,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Aldric Vane. *That should frighten you.*

It did frighten her. That was the joke of it, the private joke she could never tell anyone: that invisibility was comfortable. That she slept better here than she had ever slept in the capital, in the years when the Republic of Vessany still pretended it had a future, when she'd had a rank and a name people saluted. Here she was nobody, and nobody was safe, and safety turned out to be a kind of slow drowning that felt, from the inside, almost like rest.

---

Ink first. The stationer's shop on Cordwainer Lane opened at eight, and Mr. Quill—for that was genuinely the man's name, a coincidence so perfect Cyrus suspected God of having a sense of humor—kept the good walnut ink behind the counter and sold the cheap gall stuff to everyone else. Tammas Ellery bought the good ink. Tammas was particular about his pens. These were the details that built a man out of nothing: preferences, habits, small unreasonable loyalties.

"Morning, Mr. Ellery." Quill didn't look up from pricing a crate of reams. "Cold one."

"Sharp enough to shave with."

"Ha." He wrapped her bottle in brown paper without being asked. "Two ounces, same as always. You know, my wife says you're the only customer I've got who never haggles."

"Haggling costs more time than money," Cyrus said, and paid, and heard herself say it, and thought: *who taught you that sentence?* She had. Three years ago, inventing Tammas. The sentence had a history now. It had been said so many times it had grown roots. That was the terrifying thing about a cover identity nobody warned you about—it stopped being a costume. It became a second skeleton, and skeletons do not come off.

At the door she paused, because Quill had said something else, something under his breath, and she'd nearly missed it.

"—and they're saying the garrison at Harrow Ford surrendered without a shot. Without a *shot*, Ellery. What kind of army does that?"

"The tired kind," Cyrus said quietly, and went out into the street before her face could do anything a clerk's face shouldn't.

Harrow Ford. That was the last blocking position on the northern road. If it had fallen—and fallen *cleanly*, which meant either treachery or collapse, and lately those were the same word—then whatever window remained open between Mellowbrook and Kettermoor was closing fast. She had been telling herself for a month that she had time. Time was a currency, and the Republic's mint had stopped coining it.

---

The Hendersons' boy arrived at ten, as promised, and spent two hours watching Cyrus mend the tithe rolls with the reverence of the very stupid confronted with competence.

"You write *fast*, Mr. Ellery."

"When you've done it long enough."

"My mum says you used to work for the government. Before. Is that true?"

"Customs house," Cyrus said. "Counting barrels. It's less exciting than it sounds."

"Was it exciting?"

"No."

The boy laughed like she'd said something funny, and Cyrus smiled the Tammas smile—thin, self-deprecating, a smile designed to end conversations—and felt the familiar vertigo underneath it. Somewhere in Vessany there were men who would shoot her for the packet against her ribs. Somewhere else there were men who would hang her for it. And here was a simple boy asking if counting barrels had been exciting, and the correct answer was no, and the terrible answer was also no, because the truth was that nothing had ever been as exciting—or as exhausting—as being no one at all.

She mended the rolls. She was good at it; that was the trap of competence, you couldn't help showing it. Her hand ran down the columns, finding the errors, correcting the sums, and for an hour the work was real work and her mind went quiet, and quiet was dangerous too, because quiet was when the other voice came back. The one that said: *you could stay. You could be Tammas until your hands give out. No one is looking for a customs clerk anymore. Let the codes rot in the lining. Let someone else carry the confession north.*

And the voice that answered—her own, older, harder: *You gave your word in an orchard in peacetime, and words given in peacetime are the only ones worth anything, because they cost nothing when you make them and everything when you keep them.*

---

At noon she ate at the Lamb and Flag, at the corner table, facing the door, because old habits were the last things to die in a spy. Marta brought the stew without asking what he wanted, because Tammas always had the stew, and while she ladled it, Grebb the farrier leaned over from the next table with the day's offering.

"Ellery! Did you hear about Odile Ferran? Census woman? They've got her going house to house again, checking names against the rolls. My brother-in-law says the Bureau pays bonuses for discrepancies. Discrepancies! Like finding mice in the flour." He laughed at his own wit. "Better hope she doesn't find any mice in *your* flour, eh?"

"My flour is immaculate," Cyrus said, and raised her cup, and the table laughed, and she laughed too—actually laughed, a real laugh that surprised her coming out—and for three seconds she was simply a man among men in a warm room in a small town, and it was the most frightening feeling she had ever had.

Because she liked it.

That was the thing she could never write in any report, could never confess to any handler dead or living: some traitor part of her had settled into Tammas Ellery like a body into a bath. The borrowed garden plot she tended on Sundays. The arguments about drainage rates at the parish meeting, which she had won once, memorably, and had then lain awake all night furious at herself for winning. The way Mrs. Petch saved her the crust ends because she'd mentioned once—once—years ago—that she liked them. A life had accreted around her, layer on layer, pearl around grit, and pearls cannot be unmade back into sand.

*How easily this becomes a coffin,* she thought, stirring the stew. *Comfortable. Fitted to your shape. Lid closed gently, from inside.*

Grebb was still talking. "—and they say Kettermoor's filling up with politicals now. All the big names. Traitors and heroes, depends who you ask." He elbowed her. "Any of your old customs friends in there, Ellery?"

"Not a one," said Cyrus Halloran, who had spent four nights mapping the guard rotations of Kettermoor garrison from a smuggler's letter hidden inside a psalter, and whose left breast pocket held the sealed testimony that could pull one of those politicals out of the dark or bury him deeper, depending on who read it first.

"More stew?" asked Marta.

"Please," said Tammas.

---

By candlelight, after Mrs. Petch had retired and the house had gone to its creaking sleep, Cyrus cleared the kitchen table, laid down her blotter, and got to work.

The materials had taken months to gather. Blank travel permits, purchased piecemeal—a sheet here from a careless quartermaster, two there from a drunk garrison clerk in Harrow Ford, back when Harrow Ford still existed to have a garrison. A merchant's seal blank, soft lead, waiting for its impression. The good walnut ink, opened now, its smell rising sharp and bitter in the candlelight like a memory of the capital.

She began with the hand. This was the meditation of it, the hour when Tammas Ellery fell away and something older took the pen. A merchant's hand was different from a clerk's hand—looser, faster, contemptuous of margins. She practiced the signature of Aldous Merrick, grain factor of Thistleford, a man who existed only in her invention and in the two receipts she had copied from a genuine Merrick bill of lading found in a tavern waste basket. *Aldous Merrick,* she wrote, twenty times, varying the pressure, letting the final flourish degrade slightly the way a real man's signature degrades across a long day of signing.

The candle guttered. Outside, frost ticked against the window. Somewhere down the street a patrol passed—the new occupation patrols, imperial grey, boots too heavy for these lanes—and Cyrus sat perfectly still with her pen lifted until the sound faded.

They were getting closer. Not to her—to everyone. Each week the checkpoints thickened along the northern roads like scabs. Each week another name went up on the notice board outside the assize house. Last month it had been a poet. This month, three officers of the dissolved Republican Army, *wanted alive for tribunal*, which in current usage meant wanted alive until the paperwork caught up, and dead shortly thereafter.

One of the three names had been Aldric Vane's.

She had stood in the market square reading it, a clerk among clerks, her heart performing its work with a discipline her heart had learned in places she did not let herself remember. *Wanted alive.* Alive meant a cell. Alive meant Kettermoor. Alive meant there was still, technically, a deadline by which a promise could be kept.

She dipped the pen. Wrote *Aldous Merrick* a twenty-first time. Perfect.

Then she stopped, pen hovering, and looked at what she was doing with clear eyes for the first time in weeks: a woman alone at a table at midnight, forging the papers of a man who did not exist, so that she could travel north carrying the dying words of a country that no longer existed, to free a man whose freedom might destroy what little remained of everyone she had ever served.

If she failed, no one would ever know Tammas Ellery had been anyone else. The garden plot would go to weeds. Mrs. Petch would wonder, briefly, and then stop wondering. The ledger of the world would simply close over the gap where a clerk had been, the way water closes over a stone.

If she succeeded—if the impossible succeeded—she would have to become someone again. Someone with a name. And she found, sitting there in the candle-smell and the bitter ink-steam, that she no longer entirely knew how to do that, or whether the woman under the mask still drew breath, or whether nine months of being no one had quietly finished the job the war had started.

*A coffin fitted to your shape,* she thought. *Lid closed gently, from inside.*

She blew out the candle before the thought could finish, because thoughts like that, unfinished, were survivable, and finished ones had a way of becoming decisions.

---

She was folding the false papers into the flour barrel—wrapped in oilcloth, beneath the winter store, where no searcher looks for anything but weevils—when she heard the bell.

Not the church bell. The front bell of Mrs. Petch's house, rung once, politely, at half past one in the morning.

Cyrus stood in the dark pantry, flour dust settling around her like snow, and listened. Boots on the step. Not one pair. Two. Heavy. The particular heaviness of men who wear their weapons where civilians wear tools.

Through the frosted glass of the fanlight, a shape raised a lantern, and light spilled yellow across the pantry floor, across the barrel, across the fresh-turned flour where, if anyone pulled back the lid, a set of forged travel papers lay six inches below the surface bearing the seal of a grain factor who had never lived.

"Clerk Ellery," said a voice from the other side of the door, smooth as ledger paper. "Open up. We'd like a word about your accounts."
