## 7. The Bureau's Long Arm

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded inside the morning dispatch like a splinter in bread.

*Re: Halloran, C. — Mellowbrook district — discrepancy unresolved — return to verify.*

Odile Ferran read it twice at her desk in the census bureau's regional office, then a third time with her thumb pressed flat against the paper as if she could smother the words by touch alone. Around her, forty clerks scratched and stamped and filed the empire's appetite for names into neat columns, and none of them looked up, because looking up was not part of anyone's job.

Her supervisor, a soft-bodied man named Quillon whose voice always sounded apologetic even when it wasn't, appeared at her elbow with the particular lightness of a man delivering something he wanted to seem smaller than it was.

"Ferran. You flagged a discrepancy last month."

"I noted one." She kept her eyes on her ledger. "A clerk named Cyrus Halloran appears in my autumn count but in no prior registry. No birth record. No tax roll. No militia exemption. Nothing."

"And you went back?"

"Twice."

Quillon nodded slowly, the way men nod when they've already decided something and are merely letting you catch up. "Regional wants it verified in person. On-site. They're offering a commendation credit for discrepancies resolved at source." He set a travel chit on her desk, squared its edges to hers with two fingers. "You have a good eye, Ferran. The Bureau rewards good eyes."

There it was. *Rewards.* He said it the way a fisherman says *bait*, without embarrassment, because between them there was no pretense that anything else was happening.

"Most people don't exist until we write them down," Quillon added, already turning away. "But some people are written down who shouldn't be. That's the other kind of discrepancy. The interesting kind."

She watched him go and felt the old war start up again behind her ribs—the one between the part of her that had learned, growing up poor in a border province, that advancement was survival, and the part of her that had spent three days after leaving Mellowbrook telling herself the clerk with the careful hands was nobody, just a tired functionary in a borrowed garden, and had almost believed it.

Almost was doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

She packed that night. She took the imperial forms, her verification seals, her ink that could not be washed out, and—after a long moment, after telling herself it was habit rather than intention—her brother Tam's last letter, creased soft as cloth from rereading. *Missing in action* was what the army had sent. *Missing* was a word built for things you expected to find again.

She took the early cart to Mellowbrook and arrived as the market was thinning, the town wearing its occupation lightly, the way a coat wears dust. New flags on the customs house. A requisition notice pasted over the old grain-price board. Everything else the same: the crooked lane, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, the orchard on the south slope with its half-stripped trees standing like amputees waiting to be counted useful.

The boarding house where Halloran lodged was exactly as Odile remembered it, which annoyed her. She had hoped memory would have exaggerated it—a cramped room, a narrow bed, a window over the garden—and instead it was precisely this: a cramped room, a narrow bed, a window over the garden, and on the sill a clay pot of rosemary grown leggy from neglect or from someone forgetting they were allowed to want things.

"He's out," said the landlady, Mrs. Tarnow, wiping flour off her hands onto an apron that had surrendered to flour years ago. "Gone to mend ledgers for the chandler. Works all hours, that one. Quiet as a debt."

"When do you expect him?"

"Supper, if the chandler lets him go. Supper, if he eats." Mrs. Tarnow squinted at her. "You're the census woman. Came in autumn."

"I'm verifying records. Routine."

"Routine." The landlady said the word the way one spits out a cherry stone. "Half the town's been 'verified' since the flags changed. You know what we call it? We call it being counted twice so they can charge us twice." But she stepped aside and let Odile in, because refusal was no longer a thing towns like Mellowbrook did, and both women knew it, and neither said it.

Odile did what Bureau procedure demanded. She measured the room. She inventoried the possessions: two shirts, one good coat, mending kit, writing kit, a Bible with the spine cracked at Psalms, a tin cup, a razor worn to a sliver. She checked the registry against the household against the man's declared trade and found—nothing. No crack. The clerk's life was a wall with no door in it, and walls with no doors were, in her experience, either honest or very well built.

It was Mrs. Tarnow who broke it open, without knowing she was breaking anything.

"You'll take tea? I've bread rising. He helps himself to the kitchen when I'm out—told him he could, he pays fair and never underfoot. Mind the flour barrel, it's full to the brim, I keep meaning to—" She was already talking her way down the stairs, and Odile followed, because procedure said verify the common areas, and because some instinct older than procedure had begun to pull at her like a current.

The kitchen was warm and low-ceilinged and smelled of yeast. The flour barrel stood in the corner under the shelf, oak-hooped, lid seated properly, a wooden scoop resting on top.

Everything about it was correct.

That was what stopped her. Not wrongness—rightness. The lid sat too square. The scoop lay parallel to the rim instead of askew, the way a scoop lies when a busy woman drops it and goes. And the flour near the edges, where nobody ever scoops, was smooth in a way that flour is only smooth when a hand has smoothed it.

Odile stood in Mrs. Tarnow's kitchen with her hands at her sides and let herself understand, slowly and completely, what she was about to do, so that afterward she could not claim she hadn't known.

She lifted the lid.

The papers were wrapped in oilcloth, tucked into a hollow scooped into the flour along the back curve of the barrel, invisible unless you knew to dig. Her fingers came back white to the wrist. She unwrapped the oilcloth on the scrubbed table, and there they were:

Travel documents. A merchant's pass in the name of Corvan Ashe, carter, licensed for the northern road. A seal impression—unfinished, the wax test-flawed, the work of someone learning the stamp by repetition. And beneath those, a single sheet in a small, disciplined hand: a list of way stations, distances, dates of market days, the checkpoints marked with tiny crosses and annotated in a clerk's shorthand. *Toll. Papers checked. Sergeant talkative—avoid.*

Not the work of a spy, Odile thought. Spies wrote in cipher. This was the work of a fugitive, which was different. A spy wanted to pass through a country. A fugitive wanted to survive it. The difference was in the annotations. Nobody annotating an escape route notes which sergeant is *talkative*. That was a human note. That was a note made by someone who had stood at that checkpoint and been bored nearly to death by a lonely man with power, and had walked away and written it down so the next version of herself would be ready.

Cyrus Halloran, who did not exist before autumn, was going north. North, where the prisons were. North, where the tribunals were. North, where the lists of proscribed names were read aloud in market squares by men with good voices.

Odile looked at the papers for a long time.

Here was the shape of her life, laid out on a floured table: she could fold these pages into her report and be commended. Quillon had said so. *Discrepancies resolved at source.* A commendation credit meant a transfer to the provincial office, a heated room, a salary that would let her stop counting coins at night. It meant, eventually, access to the military archives—not the public shelves, the real ones—where missing soldiers became missing in action became casualties became, if you pulled hard enough on the thread, names and dates and graves. It meant finding out what had happened to Tam at Sennet Field, or as close to finding out as this empire ever permitted anyone.

All of it, priced at one barrel of flour.

And across the scale, on the other pan: a quiet clerk with careful hands and rosemary going leggy on the windowsill, who paid fair and never underfoot, and who was running north carrying something worth forging seals for, worth burying in flour, worth building a whole false life around like a shell around a seed.

She thought: *I don't even know what you're carrying.*

She thought: *I know what I'd be carrying if I turned you in. I'd be carrying you.*

Outside, a cart went by. Somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up. The bread on the shelf had risen to the lip of its bowl and was beginning to dome, patient, indifferent, proof that some things still happened on schedule regardless of empires.

Odile rewrapped the papers in their oilcloth, exactly as they had been, folds matching folds. She pressed the hollow back into the flour with the flat of her hand, smoothed the surface smooth, set the scoop parallel to the rim, and seated the lid with a sound no louder than a book closing.

Then she stood there, hands still white to the wrist, and understood that she had done something that could not be undone—not because anyone had seen, but because *she* had seen. She now knew what she was. The Bureau had spent six years teaching her what she was: a good eye, a reliable instrument, a woman who advanced. One minute in a kitchen had taught her the rest. Instruments didn't hesitate over flour barrels. Instruments didn't stand in warm kitchens thinking about the difference between spies and fugitives. Whatever she had just become, it was not the thing Quillon rewarded.

She washed her hands at the pump until they stung. The water ran white, then gray, then clear, and she kept washing anyway, because the flour wasn't really on her hands anymore and never had been.

Mrs. Tarnow came back with the tea things and found her at the table with her forms spread out, looking official.

"Anything?" the landlady asked.

"Records are in order," Odile said. "Common areas verified. I'll need to speak with your lodger when he returns—routine confirmation of his declaration. Trade, length of residence, that sort of thing."

"He'll be glad of it, I expect. Man likes things done proper." Mrs. Tarnow poured the tea. "You've flour on your sleeve."

Odile looked down. A ghost of white dusted the dark wool, right at the cuff, a fingerprint of the crime she had just declined to commit.

"Barrel lid," she said. "It sticks."

"It does that." Mrs. Tarnow handed her a cloth, and there was a moment—one breath, no more—when the two women looked at each other across the steam, and Odile had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the landlady knew everything: the papers, the northern road, the whole buried architecture of the quiet clerk's life. Knew it and had decided, years ago maybe, that some things a house absorbs and does not report.

Then it passed, or seemed to, and they drank their tea like two women discussing weather.

Halloran came back at dusk.

Odile heard him before she saw him—the front door, the low exchange with Mrs. Tarnow, footsteps on the stair with the particular evenness of someone who has trained themselves never to hurry. Then he was in the doorway of the parlor, hat in hand, lamplight catching a face she remembered too well: plain, watchful, courteous, a face built to be forgotten and maintained daily like a uniform.

"Mistress Ferran." A small bow. "The census. Again."

"Verification," she said. "The Bureau requires confirmation of autumn declarations. Ten minutes, no more."

"Of course." He sat when she gestured, folded his hands, and answered every question perfectly. Trade: journeyman clerk, itinerant. Length of residence: fourteen months. Property: none beyond listed effects. Kin: none living. Each answer clean, each answer ready, each answer delivered with the mild patience of a man reciting a psalm he'd memorized so thoroughly he'd forgotten it was ever a prayer.

And yet. Sitting across from him with her pen moving, Odile caught it—the flicker, gone in half a heartbeat, when she asked about intended travel. Not guilt. Something more like a man hearing a floorboard creak overhead in a house where he believed himself alone. He knew the papers were in the barrel. He might even know, now, seeing her flour-ghosted cuff earlier or reading some tell in her voice she couldn't hear herself, that she had held them.

He answered anyway. Calmly. Completely. Trusting her, or pretending to trust her, or having decided that trust was simply the next best move on a board where every other move was losing.

"That will be all," she said finally, closing her book. "Your declaration stands confirmed."

"Thank you, Mistress Ferran." At the door he paused, and for a moment the mask shifted—not slipped, *shifted*, deliberately, the way a curtain moves when someone behind it wants you to know they're there. "Mellowbrook is a kind town," he said. "People here mind their own affairs. I hope the Bureau finds that satisfactory."

"I hope so too," Odile said.

He went up the stairs. She gathered her seals and her ink that could not be washed out, and she sat a while longer in Mrs. Tarnow's parlor finishing tea she didn't want, listening to the small sounds of the house settling, and she understood that the line was already behind her. Not ahead. Behind. There had been a moment at the flour barrel when she could have stayed on the Bureau's side of it, and she had chosen—had chosen, with both hands, with the lid seated square—to step across, and the country on the far side of that line had a name she didn't know yet, but she was standing in it now, and the air tasted different.

She wrote her report that night by candlelight in her rented room, and it was a masterpiece of the craft they had trained her in:

*Declaration confirmed. Subject consistent with records. No further discrepancy noted. Recommend closure of file.*

No further discrepancy noted. Six words, and every one of them a brick laid over a flour barrel.

She signed it, sanded it, sealed it. Then she sat with Tam's letter open on her knee, the creases gone soft as cloth, and read again the last line he'd ever written her: *If anything happens, it'll be officers' doing, not ours—you mark me, Odile, the rot is at the top.* She had thought it soldier's grumbling once. Now she thought of a clerk running north with forged papers and a secret heavy enough to build a false life around, and she wondered what the rot at the top had to do with a man who didn't exist before autumn, and whether the two threads were the same thread, and whether—if she pulled—she could follow it all the way to Sennet Field and whatever grave held her brother's name.

In the morning she would file her report. In the morning Quillon would smile his apologetic smile and the file would close and Cyrus Halloran would become, officially, exactly what he pretended to be: nobody.

Unless.

Unless the regional office double-checked, the way regional offices sometimes did. Unless someone else dug in someone else's flour. Unless the file, closed, was reopened by a colder eye than hers.

Odile blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to Mellowbrook's dogs and wind, and told herself she had done the only decent thing available, and knew that decent and safe had parted company somewhere back at the barrel, and slept anyway, badly, the way conspirators sleep.

Two days later, back at her desk in the regional office, she was summoned again. Quillon's face had lost its apology.

"Ferran," he said. "There's been a development in Mellowbrook. Republican remnant activity. The Bureau's been asked to cooperate with a security sweep." He dropped a new chit on her desk, squared its edges, and looked at her a moment too long. "You'll go back. And this time, Ferran—look *harder*."
