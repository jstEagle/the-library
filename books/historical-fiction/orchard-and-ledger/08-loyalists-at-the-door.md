## 8. Loyalists at the Door

They came at dusk, which told Cyrus everything before they said a word.

Imperial men came in daylight, with papers and procedure. Census men came midmorning, with forms and ink-stained fingers. But dusk belonged to the other kind—the kind who had once been her colleagues.

She heard them on the stairs of the boarding house: two sets of boots, unhurried, the tread of men who knew no one would stop them. She was at her desk with a ledger open before her, mending the shopkeeper's accounts as she did every evening, and she did not look up when the knock came. She counted to five. Made them wait. A real clerk, annoyed at being interrupted over figures that were none of their business.

"Come in," she said, flatly, and turned back to her columns.

Two men entered. The one in front was heavyset, gray at the temples, wearing the brown coat of a grain factor with the shoulders cut loose enough to hide what a coat like that usually carried. The younger one stayed by the door and closed it softly, which was worse than slamming it. Soft meant practice.

"Clerk Halloran." The heavyset man's voice was pleasant. That too was practice. "Forgive the hour."

"You have the advantage of me." She kept her eyes on the ledger. "If this is about the Harrow account, I've told Mistress Vetch twice—the interest accrues from delivery, not order. It's all written down."

"We're not here about accounts."

"Then you're costing me candle oil." She made a small correction mark, blew on it, and only then looked up with the weary irritation of a man interrupted at honest work. "State your business or leave it under the door."

The heavyset man smiled. He crossed the room without invitation, took the chair opposite her desk, and sat with the sigh of a tired traveler. Up close he smelled of road dust and horse liniment, but beneath it something else—gun oil, faint and familiar. She'd smelled it in ministries, in safe houses, in the back rooms where the Republic's last decisions were made by fewer and fewer people.

"My name is Corven," he said. "I'm going to say some things now, Clerk Halloran, and you're going to listen the way a careful man listens. Nod if you understand."

"I understand you haven't stated any business."

"Nod anyway."

She nodded.

"Mellowbrook has been a good town for quiet work," Corven said. "Border traffic, grain carts going north, a bureau office that records everything and understands nothing. The Empire thinks this valley is asleep. For two years we've moved things through it—men, messages, coin. And in two years, we have lost exactly one courier." He leaned forward. "A woman. Carrying dispatches out of the capital in the last week of the government. She was never seen again. Not caught—there was no arrest record, no execution notice. Just gone. Vanished into the countryside like water into sand."

Cyrus turned a page of the ledger. Her hands were perfectly steady. Ten years of tradecraft lived in her hands even when nothing else did.

"If she vanished," Cyrus said, "perhaps there was nothing left to find."

"There was always something left to find." His eyes didn't move from her face. "Codes don't burn themselves, Clerk. Someone buried them, or carried them, or sewed them somewhere. We've spent two years assuming the latter. Because the woman who carried those codes was the best we ever trained. Better than me. Better than anyone." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Better than the dead, certainly."

"And what does this have to do with a bookkeeper?"

"Nothing. Probably nothing." He stood, unhurried, and straightened his coat. "But we're asking everyone in Mellowbrook with northern connections. Carters, factors, clerks who mend ledgers for shops that ship goods north. You'll forgive the thoroughness. The times make thorough men of us all."

He set something on her desk. A coin—not imperial scrip but old republican copper, the laurel-side worn nearly smooth. A recognition token. An old one, from before the collapse, from the days when such coins passed between friends.

"For your trouble," Corven said. "In case you remember anything worth remembering. People often do, later. There's an address on the reverse. No hurry. No threat. Just... an open door, for a careful man who might recall a careful woman."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat with the lamp turned low and the republican copper in her palm, turning it over and over, reading the address stamped faintly on its face: *The Drowned Ox, cellar entrance, ask for Tam.*

They knew. Or they suspected. Either way, the net was drawn around Mellowbrook now, and she was inside it with the codes still sewn against her ribs, forty miles of occupied country between her and Kettermoor.

Her first instinct was the old one: move tonight, burn the boarding house address from her mind, be a different person by morning. But Corven's visit had changed the arithmetic. They weren't searching—they were fishing, patiently, with an open door instead of a warrant. Which meant they already had a shape in mind. A woman courier, vanished. If she ran the moment they showed their faces, she confirmed the shape. She would become the answer to their question.

So she stayed. She mended ledgers. She bought ink. She let Mistress Vetch complain about the price of tallow and laughed at the cooper's joke about his lazy apprentice. She performed invisibility flawlessly while every hour the copper coin burned a hole through her pocket and the thought circled: *hunters on every side*. The Empire at her back, the loyalists at her door, and the promise hardening in her chest like scar tissue around a wound that would not close.

On the third morning she went to the square.

It was market day, stalls up, the smell of onions and wet wool. She went to buy thread, which is what a clerk does on market day, and she was standing at Peddler Row when the bells began—not the market bell but the other one, the short harsh pattern that meant everyone should come see something.

She should have gone home. Every instinct said go home, keep your head down, be furniture. But furniture doesn't flee, and fleeing is a thing that gets noticed. So she stayed, and held her basket, and watched.

They brought him out of the guardhouse with his wrists tied. A thin man, stooped, maybe fifty, with the ink stains of a lifetime's clerkship on his fingers. She knew the face distantly—Tomas Ellery, who kept the toll records at the north bridge. A gray little man. A gray little life.

Corven stood beside him on the guardhouse steps, and beside Corven a second man she hadn't seen before, holding a document board, reading aloud in a voice pitched to carry across the square.

"Tomas Ellery, of Mellowbrook. On four occasions in the past year, correspondence bearing republican cipher marks was found to pass through his keeping at the north bridge toll house. On three occasions he reported it to no authority. On the fourth occasion—" the reader paused, savoring it— "he copied it."

The square was very quiet. Market women stood with baskets on their hips. Children had been pulled behind skirts. Nobody ran, because running marks you, and everybody in that square understood the grammar of occupation, whether the flag above the guardhouse was imperial brown or republican green.

Ellery was saying something. Pleading, probably. His voice was too thin to carry; it reached the crowd as sound without words, like a bird striking glass.

"He confessed," the reader said, "under questioning. The sentence is death, per emergency statutes. Both statutes." Meaning: the Empire's law and the Republic's remnant law agreed, which was the dirtiest joke in the valley—that the two sides hunting each other could agree so easily on whom to kill.

Cyrus watched Corven's face while they did it. That was the thing she made herself do, the discipline she imposed because looking away was a luxury she could not afford and did not deserve. Corven watched the killing with the expression of a man auditing a ledger—checking the entries, confirming the sums, finding them correct. When it was done he made a small motion, and the body was dragged toward the lime pit behind the guardhouse, and the market resumed as markets do, stall by stall, voice by voice, the living getting on with the business of staying alive.

Cyrus bought her thread. Her hand did not shake until she was three streets away, and then it shook so badly she had to stand in an alley with her palm against the wall, breathing, counting breaths the way she'd been taught, until her body agreed to pretend again.

*Terror reframed*, she thought, with the cold clarity that sometimes arrived after fear peaked and broke. She had spent years fearing the Empire—the census, the checkpoints, the great grinding machine of occupation. But the Empire was distant, procedural, slow. What had killed Tomas Ellery in the square wore a republican face. Her own side. The people whose cause she carried stitched against her ribs. They would kill her as readily as they'd killed him—not for betrayal but for the possibility of it, for the danger that delivering the codes might expose whatever faction Corven served.

There were hunters on every side, and the cruelest ones spoke her own language.

* * *

That evening she made her decision the way she made all decisions now: quietly, at a desk, with numbers.

The copper coin lay before her on the blotter. The Drowned Ox. Ask for Tam. An open door, Corven had said. But she had watched enough doors open in the last two days to know what waited behind them.

Still—an open door ran both ways. If Corven wanted her to come to him, then Corven expected information. And information could be manufactured. She had forged seals, invented merchants, built whole commercial histories out of ink and patience. She could build a confession too—a partial truth, the most convincing kind. *Yes, I know of the woman courier. Yes, I helped her once, for money, the way I help anyone. She passed north. I can tell you the route.* A fabricated trail, pointing south, buying her the days she needed to go north.

It was dangerous beyond anything she'd attempted since the capital fell. It required walking into the Drowned Ox, into the cellar, into the smell of gun oil, and lying to a man who executed clerks in public squares. But the alternative was worse: waiting in Mellowbrook until the fisherman grew tired of the open door and picked up a spear.

She was still weighing it—ink and nerve against patience and prayer—when she heard the footsteps on the stairs again.

Not two sets of boots this time. One. Lighter. Unhurried in a different way, the tread of someone who wanted to be heard.

The knock, when it came, was soft. Almost apologetic.

Cyrus closed the ledger. Her hand drifted, casually, to the drawer where the bread knife lay wrapped in cloth.

"Come in."

The door opened, and Odile Ferran stepped inside—census bureau coat, document case, that same unnervingly level gaze—and behind her, in the hallway, Cyrus saw the flicker of lantern light and heard, distinctly, the creak of a floorboard bearing weight that no census taker should have brought alone.

"I hope it's not too late an hour, Clerk Halloran," Odile Ferran said, closing the door behind her. "We need to talk about your name."
