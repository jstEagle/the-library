## 10. The Forger's Sabbath

She woke before the bells and did not light a lamp.

There was a discipline to forgery that no one who hadn't done it ever understood: the work was mostly waiting. Waiting for wax to reach the right temperature, for ink to dry to the correct dullness, for the house across the lane to go dark behind its shutters. A forger in a hurry was a forger in a cell. So Cyrus Halloran—clerk, quiet tenant, keeper of a borrowed garden—sat at her table in the grey hour with her sleeves rolled and let the day assemble itself around her while she assembled a dead man's seal.

The merchant she was becoming had a name: Tovan Reiss, grain factor out of Corren Vale, licensed under the occupation's new trade ordinances. She had built him over three nights of study in the customs office records she'd been hired to mend—a man plausible enough to be boring. Boring was armor. The seal was the hard part.

She had cast it from a soap impression taken off a genuine consignment note left too long on the shopkeeper's counter, poured with melted pewter from a broken candlestick, and now she pressed it into red wax by feel alone, eyes half-closed, counting the seconds the way Vane had once taught her to count the pause between lightning and thunder. *One. Two. Three.* Lift.

The impression came away clean. The wheat sheaf sat centered in its ring, the letter R of the Reiss mark crisp at its heart. She held it to the window's first light and looked for the flaw she knew would be there—the pewter had shrunk a hair in cooling, so every impression came out a whisper smaller than the true stamp. A customs man who knew his seals might catch it. But customs men were paid by the wagonload now, not the document, and tired men read what they expected to read.

*Good enough,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because good enough was how people died. *It will do because it must.*

She set the seal aside and took up the codes.

They lived sewn into the lining of her coat, six squares of oilcloth, each one holding a strip of paper thin as onion skin. The cipher itself was nothing—Vane had chosen an old diplomatic key on purpose, something a clerk could carry in her head—but the *instructions* wrapped around it were everything: which offices still answered, which officers could be bought, which name opened Kettermoor's inner gate. She would not cut them out until the last moment. Paper removed from hiding was paper that could be found on a body.

Instead she made duplicates. This was the part the plan demanded and her stomach resisted: a second copy of the code, written out in her own cramped clerk's hand, sealed in oilcloth inside a clay jar, buried where only she could find it again. If she fell on the road, someone might someday dig it up. Or no one would, and it would sit in the dark earth until the occupation ended or didn't, a seed that never got to choose whether to sprout.

She wrote the copy twice, checking each symbol against the original, then burned the practice sheets in the stove and stirred the ash with a poker until nothing remained but grey. The smell of burnt ink hung in the room like a visitor who wouldn't leave.

Outside, Mellowbrook was waking. The baker's chimney began its morning smoke. Somewhere a dog complained about carts. An ordinary sabbath, if you didn't look too closely—and looking closely, she had learned, was a luxury for people whose names appeared nowhere important.

Today she would be very ordinary indeed.

* * *

The carter was called Brann Hulse, and he was the kind of man whose greed and cowardice were exactly balanced, which made him reliable in the way a rotten beam is reliable: it will hold precisely as long as you never ask more of it than it can bear.

Cyrus found him at the Drover's Rest, watering his mules, and did not approach him directly. That was the first lesson of bribery—you never bought a man in front of witnesses, and you never bought him outright. You *let* him sell himself.

"Master Hulse." She set down her tankard at the next table, close enough to talk, far enough to be strangers. "You're running north Thursday? Grain to the Halfway mills?"

"Grain goes where grain's wanted," Hulse said, without looking up. Suspicious already. Good. Suspicious men negotiated harder and talked less afterward.

"I ask because I've freight of my own," she said. "A merchant out of Corren Vale. Tovan Reiss. He wants a trusted man to carry him and his sample cases as far as the bridge, and he pays in advance."

Hulse looked up then. She watched him do the arithmetic she wanted him to do: a passenger meant fees above the requisition rate, fees the occupation couldn't tax because they'd never appear on any manifest. She watched him also do the arithmetic he shouldn't: a stranger with money asking to move quietly north, in a country full of checkpoints, was either a smuggler or worse, and either way trouble wore boots.

"Merchants usually travel their own carts," he said.

"This one doesn't like attention." She slid a coin across—not enough, deliberately. "Neither do I, if it comes to it. I keep Master Reiss's books."

Hulse turned the coin over with one thick finger, unimpressed, and named a figure three times what the trip was worth. Cyrus sighed like a woman being robbed, countered at half his number, and let them settle where she'd planned all along: high enough to buy his silence, low enough that he wouldn't wonder why he was being overpaid, because men who felt cheated asked questions, but men who felt clever kept their mouths shut about their own cleverness.

"Thursday, before first light," he said finally. "The yard behind the mill. You're not my cousin visiting, you're a factor inspecting shipment. And if anyone official asks, I never saw your face before today."

"Master Hulse," Cyrus said, standing, "you have never seen my face at all."

He grunted into his ale. It wasn't agreement, exactly. It was the sound a man makes when he's decided to forget something and is practicing.

She walked home the long way, past the well, past the chandler's, past the notice board where the occupation posted its requisition orders in two languages and its list of proscribed names in one. She did not stop. She did not need to. She knew Vane's name was on it; she'd known since the board went up, because grief does not require reading material.

But her hands, walking, had curled themselves into fists inside her sleeves, and it took the rest of the street's length to make them relax.

* * *

The orchard in late afternoon was all long shadows and falling fruit.

The army's timber crews had come through a week ago with saws and chalk, marking the oldest trees for felling—trunks thick enough for wagon beds and gun carriages. They'd taken nine so far. The stumps stood pale as teeth in the grass, ringed with sap, and the remaining trees leaned their branches over the gaps like people leaning into an empty chair at table.

Cyrus went in past the third stump, to the old Gravenstein at the orchard's heart, the one whose roots had raised a hump in the ground big enough to shelter a buried jar.

She had chosen this tree years ago without knowing why, back when choosing it had only been sentiment—the place where she'd first spoken to Aldric Vane, both of them younger than their titles, arguing about grafting stock while a republic still stood around them like weather they assumed would hold. He'd been a commissioner then, inspecting agricultural yields with the earnest boredom of a man born to ledgers, and she had been nobody at all, a junior archivist sent along to carry his maps. He'd caught her correcting his figures. Instead of taking offense, he'd laughed—actually laughed, hand flat on the bark of this same tree—and said, *"Then you'll do. Someone in this republic should know the difference between what's written down and what's true."*

She dug with a hand spade between the roots, cutting sod in neat blocks so she could lay them back invisibly. The soil came up dark and cool-smelling. She worked fast but not hurriedly, and the jar went into the earth wrapped in oilcloth, the duplicate code coiled inside like a sleeping thing, and on top of it—after a moment's hesitation—she added a second, smaller packet: her real name, written once, folded small. Not instructions. Just the fact of her, in case the world needed someday to know there had been a person here and not only a function.

*If I don't come back,* she thought, pressing the sod home, *let this be the proof I existed. Let some spring find me.*

She scattered leaves over the disturbed ground and stood a moment with her palm flat against the trunk. The bark was rough and warm from the sun. Nine trees gone, and the army would come back for the rest before winter; she understood that the way you understand a sentence you've read halfway and dread finishing. Whatever happened at Kettermoor, this orchard's future was already decided in some office she'd never see.

"Keep it," she said to the tree, quietly, like a fool, like a woman saying goodbye to furniture. "Grow anyway."

The tree said nothing, being a tree, and held its fruit up against the sky regardless.

* * *

Evening came, and with it the last errand, the one that had no strategic value whatsoever and that she had been putting off for two days because she knew exactly what it was.

The garden.

Her plot sat behind the cottage, four rows of beans, two of carrots, a crooked line of onions, a bed of winter greens just coming up. It belonged officially to the widow Tamsen, who leased it to Cyrus for a sum so modest it was practically charity, and who had taught her in the first weeks which end of the hoe was which—"You clerks, you think food comes from documents"—and who brought her cuttings and gossip in equal measure and had never once asked a question Odile Ferran would have asked.

Cyrus knelt at the row's edge and weeded, though the weeding could have waited a week, though there would be no week. Her hands moved and her mind refused to move with them.

Here was the thing she could not say aloud to anyone, least of all to herself: she loved this life. Not the mission—the mission was duty, cold and load-bearing. She loved the *smallness*. The way Mrs. Pellier saved her the soft apples. The way the shopkeeper's boy had learned to expect her Thursdays. The way the widow Tamsen scolded her for planting the beans too deep and then secretly thinned them herself. Two years of pretending to be a minor clerk, and somewhere in those two years the mask had grown roots of its own, and pulling it up now hurt like pulling anything living out of ground it believed was permanent.

*It was never real,* she told herself, working the hoe between the carrot tops. *None of it. The name is false, the trade is false, the whole quiet woman who tends this garden is a document, and documents get burned.*

But her hands kept weeding, gently, thoroughly, leaving the bed better than she'd found it, and that told the truth instead.

Because it had been lived. That was the distinction she kept circling, the one that mattered. A lie merely spoken is nothing. But a lie *inhabited*—a lie you rose early for, sweated over, ate by, laughed inside of—accrues interest. It becomes a debt owed to the person you pretended to be. She had been a real tenant, a real neighbor, a real gardener, whatever her papers said. Those rows of beans didn't care about her name. They had come up for her anyway.

She finished the last row as the light failed. Then she watered everything—even the winter greens that wouldn't need it for days—because a gardener waters, and tomorrow there would be no gardener, and she wanted whoever came after, some stranger assigned the plot by some bureau, to inherit living things rather than dying ones.

At the gate she stopped. Behind her the garden lay black and damp and patient. Ahead, the cottage window glowed with the lamp she'd left lit over her forged papers, and beyond the lamp the whole machinery of Thursday waited: the jar in the orchard, the seal in her kit, the carter, the road, the walls of Kettermoor, the promise.

"You were a good year," she told the garden. Not a goodbye. She wasn't ready for that word, and besides, she intended to come back—if not to this plot, then to some piece of earth somewhere, under some name, and names were a problem for a later hour.

She went inside and barred the door.

* * *

Night. The lamp trimmed low. Cyrus laid out her possessions on the table in the order she would pack them: the coat with its sewn cargo, the merchant's seal and stick of sealing wax, the forged travel papers hidden in the flour barrel where even she pretended not to know them, coin purse, pen knife, the plain clothes of Tovan Reiss folded beneath a false bottom in her sample case.

Last of all she set down the small brass key to the cottage, which she would leave on the windowsill for the widow Tamsen, and beside it a note of apology she'd drafted and discarded four times. *I am called away suddenly. Forgive the state of the beans.* Every version was a betrayal of something, so she settled on brevity and would write it clean in the morning.

From the lane outside came footsteps—two sets, unhurried, heavy. They passed the cottage. Paused. Passed on.

Cyrus sat very still and listened to her own heartbeat count down the silence after them.

Not tonight, then. But soon. The loyalists had been asking their careful questions for a week now, and careful questions were just the slow form of a knock on the door. And Odile Ferran's census ledger sat in a bureau office in the occupied capital with Cyrus Halloran's name in it like a splinter working toward the bone.

Thursday. First light. The yard behind the mill.

She banked the fire, lay down clothed, and put the coat with its paper heart within reach of her hand. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it smelled of cut grass and burnt ink.

In the orchard, unseen, the Gravenstein held its fruit against the coming frost, and beneath its roots a small clay jar kept her name in the dark, patient as a seed, waiting to learn whether it had been planted or merely hidden.
