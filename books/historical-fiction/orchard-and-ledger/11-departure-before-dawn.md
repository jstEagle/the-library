## 11. Departure Before Dawn

The last night in Mellowbrook smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Cyrus felt it in the grain dust of the mill yard, in the way the horses stamped and refused their water, in the low bruised color of the sky over the eastern hills. Weather sense was a clerk's skill too—she'd learned it the way she'd learned everything since crossing into this borrowed life: by pretending to know it until knowing arrived.

She had spent the evening doing ordinary things on purpose. She mended the strap of her satchel with waxed thread, twice as carefully as the job required. She ate the rest of the bread from Tam's shop, crust softened in milk because there was nothing else to do with day-old bread, and found it tasted like every meal she'd ever eaten here, which is to say like nothing, which is to say like a great deal.

The codes rode against her ribs where they always rode—three pages of ciphered dispatch sewn into a second lining inside the coat lining, so that anyone who cut one layer would find honest wool and honest stitching and stop. Only she knew about the third layer. She had built it herself, alone, by candlelight, the way she had built everything.

At half past eleven she put out her lamp.

Not because sleep would come. Because a lamp burning past midnight was a fact, and facts were what Odile Ferran collected.

* * *

The loyalists had come back at dusk.

Three of them this time, not two, and they hadn't bothered with the pretense of being travelers. They had stood in the square under the new imperial notice board—proclamation, curfew hours, tariff schedules, all printed in the flat type of occupation—and waited for her the way men wait when they already know the answer to their question. Master Corven, the one with the soft voice and the soldier's stillness, had watched her cross the square with her ink purchase wrapped in brown paper, and he had smiled.

"Clerk Halloran," he said. "Still keeping accounts."

"Always," she said. "Someone has to."

"We'll call again tomorrow," Corven said. "There are matters we should conclude."

It wasn't a threat. That was what made it worse. It was arithmetic. They had counted her days for her, politely, in public, where refusal would have been noticed. Tomorrow they would come to the room above Tam's shop, and they would search it properly this time—the flour barrel, the floorboards, the coat—and if they found the codes they would burn them in front of her, and if they did not find them they would burn something else instead, starting small.

So: tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight, before the sky finished deciding about the rain.

* * *

The carter's name was Hobb, and he drove grain north twice a week because grain moved even now, especially now, because armies eat first and everyone else eats what remains. Cyrus had paid him four days ago—not with coin, coin left receipts, but with three months of meticulous ledger work for his brother-in-law's feed store, work that turned a tangle of unpaid accounts into a clean column of debts owed and debts forgiven. The brother-in-law had wept a little, which had embarrassed them both.

"You'll ride under the sacking," Hobb said now, in the dark behind the mill. "Third sack from the left wall. There's a gap. You'll feel like a seed."

"I've felt worse," Cyrus said.

"If they stop us, you're my sister's girl, going up to nurse our aunt at Ferndale. Your name's Marta. Don't talk unless they talk to you, and then talk less."

"Marta," Cyrus repeated. One more name to wear and shed. She was becoming a collection of names, each one thin as paper.

Hobb looked at her a moment longer than a man looks at his sister's daughter. "Whatever you're carrying," he said, "I don't want to know it. But I'll say this. My wife's cousin was at Sennet Field." He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Everyone unfinished that sentence the same way now.

He boosted her over the tailgate and she folded herself into the dark between sacks, and the world contracted to burlap smell and the warmth of grain and her own breath, and then the cart lurched forward and Mellowbrook began to leave her through the gaps in the sacking, one piece at a time.

* * *

She saw the town the way a drowning woman sees the shore—in fragments, between waves.

The lamplighter's pole leaning against the corner where he always left it. The notice board, pale in the dark. Tam's shut shop, its sign creaking on its bracket, and above it the window of the room she'd rented for eight months, dark now, holding nothing but the shape of a bed and a cold hearth and a chair where a false woman had sat mending ledgers by lamplight.

She had thought she would feel relief. What she felt instead was theft.

Because Odile Ferran's house sat at the bend where Cart Lane met the north road, and Odile's window was lit.

A single candle, low, the kind that burned while someone sat awake behind it. Cyrus knew that light. She had seen it from her own window a dozen nights, and told herself it meant nothing—that census takers kept odd hours, that paper never slept, that it was no business of hers why a woman with an imperial portfolio chose to sit up alone in a town that was learning to be afraid.

But Odile had found the forged papers in the flour barrel. Cyrus had known within a day—known by the way the barrel's lid sat a finger's width off true, by the flour dust disturbed along one seam in a way no mouse made. Papers found, papers returned, papers unreported. A silence, deliberately constructed, offered across the distance of a street without either woman acknowledging it existed.

And now Cyrus was spending that silence like stolen coin.

The cart rolled past the bend. Through a gap in the sacking, for perhaps four seconds, she could see straight up into that lit window, and she let herself look, because some debts deserve to be looked at.

Odile stood at the glass. Small, straight-backed, her hair down, her face unreadable at this distance—a pale oval above the candle glow. Watching the road. Watching, Cyrus understood with a certainty that felt physical, watching *her go*, and choosing not to move, not to write, not to raise any alarm that the garrison post or the loyalists' watchers might hear.

Four seconds. Then the hedge took the window, and the road took the town, and Mellowbrook was a sound behind her—the creak of Tam's sign, the mill wheel turning slow in water that belonged to no government yet—and then not even a sound.

Cyrus lay among the grain and did the accounting she could not stop herself from doing.

What had Odile bought with her silence? Nothing yet. That was the terrible part. Cyrus had nothing to give her, no payment arranged, no understanding signed. The silence was a gift, and gifts between people who had lied to each other were not gifts; they were loans, and the interest was unknowable. Someday Odile would present the account. Someday she would stand in a doorway and say, *I let you go, and here is what it cost me,* and Cyrus would have to pay, and she did not know the currency.

*I'm using her,* she thought, and the thought had teeth. *The way the republic used me. The way Vane used me—no, not used; trusted; there's a difference; hold onto the difference.*

But was there? In the dark, in the grain, with the wheels turning, the difference got harder to hold. Every promise she was keeping was made of other promises broken. She had promised Tam's ledgers balanced through spring; they would sit half-done now, and Tam would think the quiet clerk had simply vanished, the way quiet people vanish in occupied towns, and would tell himself a story about it. She had promised Mrs. Ansel she'd look at the garden fence before frost; the fence would lean another winter without her. She had promised the garden plot itself—her one act of faith in a borrowed life—one more season.

Promises were debts too. She was leaving town owing nearly everyone in it.

Except Odile. With Odile it ran the other way. And somehow that was the debt that sat heaviest, because it was the only one she couldn't settle by running north.

* * *

They stopped once, at the crossroads below Ferndale, where a lantern swung and boots crunched and Hobb's voice went flat and bored the way country voices do when talking to soldiers.

"Papers. Grain for the garrison depot?"

"Grain for whoever pays," Hobb said, and there was a grunt that might have been a laugh.

"Sack counts match your manifest?"

"Count them yourself if your sergeant's got the hours."

More crunching. A hand slapped the cart's side close enough to Cyrus's hiding place that she felt the wood shudder against her shoulder. Someone walked the length of the cart, unhurried, professional. She breathed the way she had trained herself to breathe in a dozen tight places—slow, shallow, rhythmless enough to be sleep or boredom—and pressed her palm flat against the third page of code through two layers of wool, as if touch could make it more real than the lantern outside.

Then the boots moved off, and the lantern moved off, and Hobb clucked the horse, and the world started rolling again.

Only after the crossroads was a mile behind them did Cyrus realize her hands were shaking, and only then, absurdly, did the grief arrive—not for the danger, but for the garden. For the beans she'd planted in April that would be ready in another fortnight and would never be picked by her. For the row of stones she'd set along the plot's edge to keep the rain from cutting channels, each one chosen, each one placed. Small work. Honest work. The kind of work that assumed tomorrow, and she had done it while carrying the end of a republic against her ribs, and she had loved it anyway, loved it so much it frightened her, because a person can get used to anything, even a mask, even a coffin-shaped life with a lamp and a ledger and beans coming up green.

She cried a little then, silently, into the sacking, the way she had taught herself to cry during the war—economically, without sound, wasting nothing—and when it was done she wiped her face with the heel of her hand and did what she always did next, which was plan.

Kettermoor. Six days north by cart road, less if the weather held and the checkpoints stayed stupid. The garrison town, the prison walls, Vane alive somewhere behind them—alive, the contact had sworn it, alive and unbowed and scheduled for transfer south within the month, which meant her six days had to become five.

And after Kettermoor—she refused to plan after Kettermoor. After Kettermoor was either a name restored or a grave, and graves took no planning.

* * *

Dawn came gray and then gold over the northern hills, and with it the first hard rain, drumming on the sacking above her like a thousand small urgent fingers. Hobb pulled the cart under an oak at the roadside and climbed back to shift a tarpaulin, and when he saw her face he paused.

"Ferndale's an hour on," he said. "After that I turn east for the depot. There's a carrier yard in Ferndale—Marta can find a ride further north easy enough, if Marta exists."

"She exists," Cyrus said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "Thank you, Hobb. When the accounts come due on this—"

"They won't," Hobb said. "My wife's cousin was at Sennet Field. I told you." He said it as if it settled everything, and maybe for him it did. Maybe that was how loyalty worked now: not oaths, not flags, just one shared unfinished sentence, passed from stranger to stranger down a road full of new flags.

He handed her down at the edge of Ferndale an hour later, and she stood in the rain with her satchel and her coat with its three linings, watching the grain cart shrink eastward, and she was entirely alone for the first time in eight months.

No name. No room. No garden. No ledger waiting on a shelf with her careful figures in it. Just the road north, wet and empty, and the pages against her ribs, and the memory of a lit window where a woman stood watch and said nothing.

Cyrus turned her collar up against the rain and started walking.

Behind her, far behind her now, in a town that was already forgetting her, a candle was going out—or being pinched out, deliberately, the way one closes an account one intends to reopen later. She could almost see Odile Ferran dipping the pen. Almost see the entry forming: *no irregularity observed.* The empire's own ink, bent quietly toward mercy, or toward something that wasn't mercy, toward something with a price tag Cyrus couldn't read yet.

*You'll send me the bill,* Cyrus thought northward, at the lit window, at the whole sleeping, occupied country. *I know you will. And God help me, I'll pay it.*

Ahead, past the rain, past the hills, Kettermoor's walls were waiting with Aldric Vane behind them—and somewhere in those same walls, though she couldn't have known it, a man named Dray was already counting what a certain document might be worth.

She walked into the rain, under her own name at last, and the road took her north.
