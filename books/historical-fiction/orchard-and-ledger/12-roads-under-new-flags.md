## 12. Roads Under New Flags

The grain cart smelled of dust and rat droppings and the carter's pipe, and for the first six miles it was the safest place in Vessany.

Cyrus rode in the bed of it among the sacks, her back against a plank wall that had been mended with a different wood than the rest, and she watched Mellowbrook shrink behind her until the orchard was a dark smudge on the horizon and then not even that. She did not look back after that. Looking back was a habit of people who believed they might return.

The carter was called Brannoc, or said he was, and he had taken her coin without counting it twice, which in her experience meant either honesty or contempt. Both were useful. He drove with his shoulders hunched and his eyes on the road and he spoke perhaps ten words an hour, all of them about weather, which made him the finest companion she had traveled with in years.

The northern road was worse than she remembered it, and she had last seen it in wartime.

* * *

They passed the first village before noon. She knew it was occupied before she saw a single soldier, because of the flags.

Every settlement on the old post road had flown something—a guild banner, a saint's day pennant, the blue-and-gold of Vessany gone sun-faded to almost nothing. Now each pole carried the same gray cloth, dyed so heavy it seemed to drink the light, stamped with the imperial double-tower. They hung limp in the windless morning, dozens of them, mile after mile, like a row of drowned men standing up.

"New since spring," Brannoc said, around his pipe stem.

"How long since you came this way?"

"Since the harvest before." He spat over the wheel. "There wasn't a flag then."

The village itself—Harrowmere, according to the milestone, though the milestone had been chiseled smooth and re-carved with imperial lettering—was quiet in the way of places that had learned to be quiet. A woman drew water at the well and did not look up as the cart passed. Two children stopped their game of knucklebones mid-throw and watched the cart go by with the flat attention of children who had already learned which strangers mattered. A dog slept in the road and did not move for the wheels, because dogs, unlike people, still understood whose ground this was.

At the well, someone had nailed a sheet of paper to the post. It bore the imperial seal and three lines of print. Cyrus read it in the time it took the cart to pass: a curfew notice, a grain levy, and a summons—all males between fifteen and fifty—to present themselves for labor registration.

Fifteen to fifty. That was not labor registration. That was a census of who could be marched.

She kept her face turned toward the fields and thought about ink. She had bought ink in Mellowbrook two days before leaving, good iron-gall, and she had enough left for perhaps forty pages of forged manifests if she wrote small. Forty pages. She had once believed the fate of a republic could be sewn into a coat lining. Now she measured survival in ink.

* * *

The second village was burning.

Not wholly—the fire had been put out, or had burned out—but one long barn stood charred to its rafters, and the smell hung over the road like a wet animal. Soldiers were stacked outside it in a loose picket, maybe thirty of them, gray-coated, bored-looking, the particular boredom of men doing occupation rather than war. One of them was counting sacks off a farmer's cart with a stub of pencil, writing nothing down, just moving his lips.

Brannoc slowed the horse without being told to. There was a speed at which carts passed checkpoints and a speed at which they didn't, and every carter on this road had learned it in his spine.

"Hold your papers ready," he murmured.

Cyrus had them ready. They were good work—her own work, done by candlelight over four nights—and they named her Meris Cotter, a dry-goods factor out of Corren's Ford traveling to inspect a consignment. The seal was a merchant's mark she had copied from a wax impression bought off a drunk factor in Mellowbrook for the price of his next three drinks. The drunk factor would wake in a week with a headache and no memory of selling it, which was the best kind of accomplice.

A sergeant waved the cart down with two fingers. He was young, with a chapped face and the look of a man who had been told he was an army and had begun to doubt it.

"Papers."

She handed them through the gap in the boards. He read them slowly, moving his lips, and she watched him read and did not watch him read, both at once—the trick of being present but unremarkable, of being furniture with a pulse.

"Cotter," he said. "Factor."

"Dry goods," she agreed. "Cloth, thread, buttons. Nothing the army wants."

"Everything's something the army wants." But he said it without heat, the way a man recites a lesson he no longer believes, and handed the papers back. "Move along."

As the cart rolled past the picket, she saw what the soldiers were guarding: the barn's remains, and beside it a stack of confiscated timber, cut from living trees, marked with white paint. Orchard stock. Someone's grandfather had planted those rows, and now they were numbered in white like corpses awaiting identification.

She thought of Mellowbrook's orchard, half-felled now, the requisition crews working through it tree by tree. She thought of a young man in peacetime standing under a pear tree with juice on his hands, laughing about nothing, certain of tomorrow.

* * *

They made Cold Ashford by late afternoon, and that was where she heard the names.

She heard them before she understood what she was hearing—a voice lifted in the square, formal and carrying, the cadence of a man reading aloud from an official document. Her first thought was proclamation, and her second was scripture, and her third, arriving cold, was neither.

"Get down," Brannoc said. "We water here anyway. You want to hear it or not?"

She wanted to not hear it. She got down.

Cold Ashford's square had once held a market fountain; the fountain was dry and a platform had been built over it, rough planks and trestles, the architecture of announcements. On the platform stood a clerk in imperial gray with a scroll, flanked by two soldiers and a local man in a black coat who had the settled flesh of someone who had eaten well through a famine. The square held perhaps two hundred people, gathered the way people gather for executions—not close, not far, arranged in that terrible gradient of distance that says: I am here because I must be, and how near I stand is how much I dare.

"—by authority of the Provisional Administration of the Northern Territories," the clerk read, "the following persons are proscribed. Being named, they are subject to arrest on sight. Sheltering them is treason. Speaking their names in defense is sedition. Let all hear and be warned."

He unrolled further down the scroll.

"The list is long," Brannoc muttered. "It's always long now. They read it in every town. Different lists, some of them. Same ending."

Cyrus stood at the edge of the crowd with her hood back and her posture bent into Meris Cotter's tired factor's stoop, and she listened to a government die one name at a time.

The clerk read them in batches of five. Names of colonels. Names of deputies. Names of printers, of schoolmasters, of a woman described only as "the widow Sarelle, of this district." After each batch there was a pause, and in the pause the crowd did nothing, and the nothing was louder than any sound.

Then:

"Aldric Vane."

The world narrowed to a point.

She felt it physically—a contraction, as if the road itself had tightened around her. Aldric Vane, read out in a flat official voice in a dry fountain square in a town she'd never intended to see, his name filed among traitors between a printer and a widow, reduced to ink on a scroll in an empire's hand.

She had known, of course. She had known since the burned courier pressed the partial instructions into her hands and died of it: Vane imprisoned at Kettermoor. Knowing a thing and hearing it proclaimed are different animals. Knowing is private. Hearing is public. Hearing means the whole world has been let in on the fact of him, and the whole world has been asked to hunt him, and somewhere in this crowd, statistically, mathematically, someone will remember having seen a tall man with a scholar's hands and will say so at the right desk for the right reward.

"—former Marshal of the Republican Army," the clerk continued, "charged with the betrayal of the nation's forces at Sennet Field, with sedition, with—"

Cyrus stopped listening, because the words had gone wrong.

Betrayal. *Charged with* betrayal. She stood very still while the sentence assembled itself in her mind and refused to make sense. Vane had not betrayed Sennet Field. Vane had *survived* Sennet Field, barely, and had spent the years since gathering testimony—her testimony to carry, his confession to deliver—that would name the men who had actually sold the army. That was the entire architecture of her mission. That was the promise under the pear tree. If the empire was charging Vane with the betrayal, then either the empire was lying—

—or someone had given them a story to tell.

Her eyes went, without her permission, to the man in the black coat on the platform, the well-fed local, and she caught herself cataloguing him: rings, boots, the way the soldiers deferred slightly to him. Not an occupier. A collaborator of the comfortable kind, the kind who trade information for continuity. Men like that did not invent charges. They relayed them.

Which meant the charge had come from inside. From someone who knew what had happened at Sennet Field and preferred Vane wearing the blame to anyone asking how the army's positions had been known to the hour.

Someone whose name was sewn into her coat lining, encoded in a dead woman's cipher, waiting for the north.

"—let all hear," the clerk finished, rolling the scroll, "and be warned."

The crowd began to dissolve with the practiced slowness of crowds that have learned not to run. Cyrus moved with them, one tired factor among many, and her heart was hammering and her face was a mask and beneath the mask a single thought repeated like a pump stroke:

*They are telling the story backwards. And I am carrying the correction.*

* * *

They camped that night in a spinney off the road, and Brannoc built no fire, and neither of them suggested one.

Cyrus sat against a trunk with her coat across her knees and, in the last gray light, worked the lining seam open with a fingernail just far enough to touch the oilcloth packet. She did not take it out. Taking it out was for locked rooms and desperate hours. She only touched it, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm breathing.

Forty pages of ink. One name on a proscription list. One charge of betrayal that was either a lie or a mirror.

She thought about scale. It was a thing she had avoided thinking about for weeks, because mission-thought requires narrowness—you think about the next mile, the next seal, the next lie—and narrowness had kept her alive. But tonight the narrowness cracked and the whole shape of it pressed down on her: villages flying drowned-men flags, barns burned, boys summoned by age into the machinery, orchards numbered in white paint, names read aloud in squares to crowds trained into silence. An entire country being converted from a place into a ledger. And somewhere in that vast conversion, one small errand—one woman, one packet, one promise made under a pear tree in a world that had assumed tomorrow.

It was absurd. It was a grain of sand insisting on its importance to the beach.

And yet. The charge against Vane proved the opposite of absurdity. Nobody builds a false story about a grain of sand. The empire had bothered to lie about Sennet Field, publicly, formally, in every square on the northern road—which meant the truth of Sennet Field was dangerous enough to spend lies on. Which meant the packet against her knees was not a relic. It was a live round.

She closed the seam. She ate half a loaf and gave Brannoc the other half, and he nodded, which from him was a speech.

"They'll have a sweep at Halfway Bridge," he said, later, in the dark. "Checkpoint's been there since the spring. They turn back anyone heading north without garrison business. Sometimes they turn back everyone, just to watch them turn."

"You've been through it?"

"Twice. Once through, once not." He knocked out his pipe against his boot heel. "The second time cost me a cartload and a tooth."

She lay back against the trunk and looked up through the branches at a sky going hard with stars, and she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the square. Kettermoor was nine days north if the roads were kind. Halfway Bridge sat squarely across the only route worth taking. Between here and there: sweeps, levies, proscription lists, and a story about Sennet Field being told backwards in every square.

And behind her, somewhere south, the loyalists who wanted the codes burned, and Odile Ferran's pen resting in an imperial registry with her name written in it.

One small errand.

She slept in pieces, and dreamed of pear trees felled in rows, numbered in white.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and with it the road filled with other travelers, all drifting north the way debris drifts—refugees mostly, families with handcarts, a man carrying a loom's shuttle and nothing else, as if he had reached into the burning house of his life and saved the one object that made no sense. Nobody spoke much. On roads under new flags, silence is a currency and everyone is poor.

By midmorning they could see the bridge.

Halfway Bridge crossed the Sorrel in two stone arcs, and on the far bank the imperial engineers had built a checkpoint that was becoming a fortification—timber palisade, a signal mast, a line of stakes that funneled all traffic into a single lane no wider than a cart. Even at this distance Cyrus could see the queue: carts, riders, walkers, backed up half a mile along the riverbank, moving at the pace of suspicion.

Brannoc reined in and chewed his pipe and looked at it the way a man looks at weather he cannot argue with.

"Sweep today," he said. "See the mast? Signal's up. Means they're checking everything twice." He glanced at her sidelong, the first time he had looked at her directly in two days. "Your papers are good, factor, but good isn't always enough on a sweep day. They'll want to know why a dry-goods man is going north into garrison country with no cargo."

"I'm inspecting a consignment."

"Mm." He sucked the pipe. "Consignment of what? Your cart's empty except for me and my grain, and my grain's spoken for."

She had answers prepared. She had three layers of answers prepared, and beneath them a fourth that involved the river, the reeds, and a very cold swim. But she looked at the queue—at the families, the shuttle-man, the slow mill of the frightened—and she understood that answers were not going to be the problem. The problem was time. A sweep day meant hours in that lane, hours of soldiers walking the line of carts, hours of eyes, and somewhere in those hours a sharp one would ask Meris Cotter to open her coat against the cold.

Ahead of them, at the head of the queue, a soldier was turning back a family with a handcart. The mother argued. The soldier did not raise his voice; he simply pointed south, and the pointing had the finality of geology.

"Well," Brannoc said, and flicked the reins, and the cart rolled forward into the line. "Bridge is bridge. We'll see what we see."

Cyrus sat among the grain sacks with her forged papers against her ribs and her true errand sewn against her heartbeat, and watched the gray flag over the checkpoint hang dead in the windless air, and waited for her turn to be counted.
