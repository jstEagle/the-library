## 13. The Inn at Halfway Bridge

The queue moved the way queues move under occupation: in fits, in silences, in the particular rhythm of people trying to be forgettable. Cyrus sat among Brannoc's grain sacks and did what she had done for eleven days on the road—counted things.

Counting kept the mind from wandering toward fear. She counted the soldiers at the bridgehead: six visible, two likely in the tollhouse out of sight. She counted the wagons ahead of them: nine, of which four would be turned back by the look of their loads—scrap iron, green timber, a family whose handcart had already been refused. She counted the flags: three gray banners of the Empire, one torn at the fly, none of them new enough to suggest a garrison that expected trouble. That was good. Garrisons that expected trouble searched carts twice.

"You're quiet," Brannoc said, without turning his head. He was a carter built like his own axle, sixty years old if he was forty-five, with hands so cracked they looked like drought land. "Quiet's fine. Just don't be *frozen*. Frozen gets noticed."

"I'm thinking about my ledger," Cyrus said. It was her cover now, refined over the weeks since Mellowbrook: a clerk for a grain merchant out of the southern valleys, traveling to audit accounts at mills upcountry. Papers said so. Ink was smudged in the right places. She had aged the documents herself with tea smoke and careful handling until even she half-believed they were old.

"Ledger," Brannoc repeated, tasting the word. "Man who thinks about his ledger is a man with nothing worse to think about. Good. Stay that man."

The line lurched forward. A soldier walked the length of it, unhurried, boots loud on the planking of the bridge approach. He stopped at each cart, glanced in, asked one question, moved on. At the scrap-iron wagon he asked, "Destination?" and the driver said "Corvel mill," and the soldier said, "Turn it around," and the driver began to argue and then saw the soldier's face and didn't.

When he reached Brannoc's cart he was looking at Cyrus before he finished walking. She had seen that kind of attention before—not suspicion exactly, but the professional habit of a man sorting faces into piles. She made herself the smallest interesting thing possible: slightly bored, faintly put upon, a clerk whose feet hurt.

"Papers."

She handed them down. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be, years ago, in a courtyard in Vessany's capital where Aldric Vane had told a room full of young diplomats: *Your hands will betray you before your mouth does. Teach your hands to lie first.* She could still hear the chalk squeaking against his board. She had thought then that the hardest thing she'd ever do was memorize cipher tables.

The soldier read. He was perhaps thirty-five, with a face like a closed door and the imperial gray worn shiny at the elbows—the uniform of a man who had been issued it long ago and expected no replacement. His name tape said HALLAM. Something in his reading slowed.

"Cyrus Halloran," he said. "That your name?"

"It is." The lie came out level, worn smooth as a river stone from use. Her own name, given away daily, worn like a coat that fit someone else.

He looked at her a moment longer than the papers required. Then he handed them back, tapped the cart rail twice—a signal to whoever watched from the tollhouse—and said, "Unload for inspection."

* * *

They unloaded. That was the cruelty of it; not the refusal, which was quick, but the compliance, which took an hour. Grain sacks came off and went back on while two soldiers probed with bayonets through the straw bedding, through false floors that weren't there, through the space beneath the driver's bench where smugglers were known to hide brandy and pamphlets and men. Cyrus stood to the side with her ledger open, making notes she did not need to make, because a clerk who stopped working during an inspection was a clerk with something else going on.

One of the soldiers found her sewing kit in her satchel and held it up.

"For ledgers," she said. "Bindings tear."

He turned it over. Inside, alongside needles and thread, lay the awl and the curved needle she used for heavier work—for mending a coat lining, say, or opening one. He looked at it. She looked back with the flat patience of a woman who mended things because mending was cheaper than buying.

He put the kit back. He did not search the coat itself. Later, in the dark of the inn, she would lie awake replaying that moment, unable to decide whether she had won by skill or been spared by luck, and knowing that both explanations were equally terrifying, because only one of them would keep working.

At the end of the hour the sergeant—Hallam again, doing his own rounds rather than delegating—walked the cart one final time, laid his palm flat on the last sack, and said, "Grain goes north, clerk goes north. Bridge toll's waived for requisitioned freight." To Brannoc: "You'll be paid in scrip at Corvel. Don't take paper from anyone wearing brown; that issue's counterfeit."

And that was all. They rolled across Halfway Bridge with the river sliding brown and patient beneath the planks, and Cyrus did not let herself exhale until the checkpoint was out of sight behind a bend of alders.

"You were lucky," Brannoc said.

"I know."

"No," he said. "I've been across this bridge eleven times since the flags changed. That sergeant doesn't unload anybody. He turns people back, or he waves them through. Today he invented a third thing." He flicked the reins. "Men don't invent third things for no reason. Either you remind him of somebody, or he wanted a longer look at you and couldn't say why. I don't know which is worse."

Cyrus didn't either. She settled deeper among the sacks and told herself the day was over. The day was not over. The day had only decided where it would finish: at the inn on the far bank, where every traveler held at the sweep was required to lodge the night, so that names could be taken and rooms could be counted, so that no one crossed the empire's veins without leaving a trace.

* * *

The inn at Halfway Bridge was called the Ferryman's Rest, though there had been no ferry since the bridge was built, and precious little rest since the war. Its common room smelled of tallow, wet wool, and barley soup stretched with turnip. A dozen travelers sat at long tables under the eye of an innkeeper who poured thin ale and thinner conversation. On the wall above the hearth hung the imperial proclamation, freshly pasted over older layers, listing proscribed persons in a column of dense type.

Cyrus read it the way she read everything now—in glances, in fragments, letting her eyes pass over the column without stopping, trusting peripheral vision more than focus. She caught three names she knew. None of them was hers. One of them, halfway down, was *Vane, Aldric — former office of state — capture preferred, dead acceptable.*

*Dead acceptable.* Four words, typeset neatly, describing a man who had once spent an entire afternoon teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass because she claimed not to know how, and who had refused to believe her until she proved it, and who had laughed harder than she did when she finally managed it.

She ordered soup and bread and took the corner table with her back to the wall, and opened her ledger, and became furniture.

It almost worked. What broke it was Hallam himself, coming off duty at dusk, hanging his cap on a peg, and sitting down across from her with his bowl unasked, as though the seat had been assigned.

"Sergeant," she said carefully.

"Hallam." He tore his bread and dropped half in his soup. "You write a fair hand, clerk. I saw your ledger at the bridge."

"It's mostly numbers."

"Numbers are honest. That's why nobody trusts them." He ate for a while. Around them the room murmured—carters complaining about scrip, a peddler lying about his profits, two women from a burned-out farmstead sitting close together not speaking at all, which was its own kind of talk. "Where are you bound?"

"Upcountry. Mills at Corvel, then accounts at Thrennford."

"Mm." He stirred his soup without eating it. "There's no mill at Corvel anymore. Burned it myself in the first month. Orders." He said it the way a man reads a weather report for a place he'll never visit. "So whatever merchant sent you north after mill accounts either doesn't know, or wants you carrying something other than accounts."

The room's noise seemed to step back from their table. Cyrus kept her face arranged in mild confusion, the expression of a small functionary confronted by a large fact. "I only do what I'm told, Sergeant. The firm—"

"The firm is a name on a letterhead, and letterheads are cheap." He looked at her then, fully, and she understood that the afternoon's slow reading of her papers had not been boredom. He had been deciding something, and he had decided it hours ago, and this meal was him living inside the decision. "Here's what I know, clerk. I've waved through forty wagons this month carrying grain that feeds the army, and turned back ninety carrying families who just want to be on the other side of a river. I've written down the names of eleven people who were gone from their villages by morning, and I don't ask what happens to them because asking is how you end up on a wall yourself." He nodded, very slightly, toward the proclamation above the hearth. "I fought at Sennet Field. Before that I believed the war had reasons. Now I believe it has *appetites*, and I am tired of feeding them."

Cyrus said nothing. Saying anything was the mistake. But her silence, she realized too late, had a texture to it—the silence of agreement, of recognition—and Hallam heard it.

"That's what I thought," he said quietly. He pushed his bowl aside and leaned forward, forearms on the table, voice pitched under the room's murmur. "I'm not going to ask what you are. Asking makes me complicit in a way I can't take back, and I've got a daughter in Corvel province with a bad hip and a mother who needs medicine, and I can't afford to be brave. Understand me? I am *not* being brave. What I am is a man who checked your papers twice today when once would have done, and let you cross anyway, and I'd like to know—" his jaw worked, "—that it wasn't for nothing. That whatever you're carrying, it isn't going to make more of *this*." He gestured, one blunt finger taking in the room: the burned farmstead women, the proclamation, the thin soup, the whole gray apparatus of the world.

And there it was: the question she had carried from Mellowbrook, from the orchard, from the burned courier's dying grip, now asked by the wrong person at the worst table in the empire. She thought about the code sewn against her ribs. She thought about Vane's promise and hers. She thought about Dray, whom she did not yet know, and Sennet Field, which she did not yet understand, and how the truth of both was folded into her coat lining like a knife wrapped in linen.

"I can't tell you what I carry," she said.

"I know."

"But I can tell you it isn't for making more of this." Her voice stayed level. Her hands, taught long ago to lie first, lay flat and open on the table. "It's for ending some of it. That's all I can give you. If it isn't enough—"

Hallam sat back. For a long moment he studied her with the expression of a man auditing a ledger of his own, weighing entries he couldn't verify against ones he could: her steady hands, her forged-but-flawless papers, her eyes, which she let meet his for one second longer than caution advised because something in her—something older and truer than the clerk—was tired of hiding.

Then he picked up his spoon. "Soup's getting cold," he said. "Eat. Rooms are two coppers. Innkeeper writes names; give him any name you like, he can't spell anyway." He paused. "Dawn muster for northbound traffic is at first light. Carts go through in order of arrival. Be early. The lieutenant who takes the morning shift counts everything twice."

* * *

She did not sleep well. She lay in a narrow bed under a roof beam furred with candle soot and listened to the inn settle, and to rain beginning somewhere after midnight, soft on the shingles like a long exhalation. Twice she heard boots in the corridor—soldiers checking rooms, or claiming they were checking rooms—and twice she moved the coat from the peg to the pillow beside her and waited, and the boots passed.

In the gray before dawn she came downstairs to find Hallam already at the door, cap on, breath steaming, the rain thinned to a drizzle. Beyond him the bridge emerged from mist plank by plank, and beyond the bridge the northern road ran off into country she could not see, toward Kettermoor, toward walls, toward whatever waited at the end of a promise seven years old.

"Cart's waiting," he said. "Brannoc argued himself into the front of the line. Man argues like he drives—slow and unstoppable."

"Sergeant." She stopped beside him. Words assembled themselves and she rejected most of them. Thank you was too small and too dangerous. Instead she said, "Whatever comes of yesterday's third thing—I'll try to make it worth the invention."

Hallam looked out at the road for a while. "My father kept bees," he said, apparently to the bridge. "Told me once: the way you find out if a hive is healthy isn't opening it. It's standing quiet and listening. A sick hive sounds angry. A sound one hums." He put his cap straight. "This whole country's sounding angrier by the week, clerk. Somebody ought to have listened sooner." He stepped back and raised one hand—not quite a salute, not quite a wave—and called to the tollhouse, "Northbound grain, cleared last evening! Let it run!"

The barrier lifted. Brannoc clicked his tongue and the horse leaned into the collar, and the cart rolled onto the bridge, planks drumming softly under the wheels, the river invisible below except as a gray suggestion of movement.

Cyrus did not look back at Hallam. Looking back was a debt she couldn't afford to acknowledge. But she carried him with her anyway, filed alongside the whistling lesson and the burned courier and Odile Ferran's pen scratching her name into an imperial form: another entry in the private ledger she kept of everyone who had touched the errand and been changed by it, however slightly, however unknowingly.

Halfway across, Brannoc spoke around the pipe he wasn't allowed to light in the drizzle. "Sergeant gave you more than soup last night."

"He gave me breakfast."

"Forty years on the roads, girl. I know a man settling an account when I see one." He spat expertly over the side. "Question is what he thinks you owe him."

Ahead, the mist was lifting off the northern road, and the road ran straight as a ruled line toward hills she had never seen, toward the garrison town marked on her smuggled map in ink she had mixed herself. Kettermoor. Two days' travel, if the checkpoints stayed kind. One day, if she abandoned Brannoc's cart and walked.

She put her hand flat against her ribs, over the lining, over the code, and felt the small stiff ridge of it like a second heartbeat.

"He thinks I owe him an ending," she said. "And he's right."
