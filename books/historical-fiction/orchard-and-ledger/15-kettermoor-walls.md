## 15. Kettermoor Walls

Kettermoor announced itself by smell before it appeared at all: coal smoke, tanneries, horse dung, and under all of it the sweetish rot of a garrison town that had been feeding too many soldiers for too long. Cyrus smelled it from the crest of the last hill, where the road bent and the valley opened, and she stood a moment in the wind letting her lungs learn it, because a place you can smell from a mile off is a place you will not be able to leave quietly.

Below her the town sat in its bowl like a stone dropped into grey cloth. Slate roofs. The river running brown between mill walls. And on the rise beyond the houses, squatting over everything, Kettermoor garrison — a rectangle of old imperial masonry that the Republic had never bothered to tear down because no one believed it would ever be needed again.

It was needed now. She could see the new flag on the pole above the gatehouse, snapping flat in the wind, and she could see the scaffolding on the east tower, where men were enlarging something — a signal platform, maybe, or gallows. At this distance she couldn't tell which, and she understood that in the empire's arithmetic the two were often the same structure.

She adjusted the satchel strap on her shoulder, touched the coat lining once — the codes still there, sewn flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat — and walked down into the town.

* * *

The trick to entering an occupied garrison town, Cyrus had learned across three borders and eleven checkpoints, was not confidence. Confidence got you noticed. The trick was to look like a man whose whole life was paperwork so tedious that even the soldiers' eyes slid off him.

So she entered Kettermoor as Master Corvin Ashe, petitioning scribe, late of the river towns, come north to offer his services writing letters for families of prisoners. It was a trade with real demand behind it. Half the villages she'd passed through since Halfway Bridge had someone inside some wall or other, and the empire, efficient in everything except mercy, permitted correspondence — censored, delayed, taxed by the sheet — because permitting it made the occupation cheaper than forbidding it.

Her papers were good. She'd forged them herself over three nights at the inn, aging the ink in a warm oven and cracking the seal fold just enough to suggest a document that had been handled by bored officials twice. Her hands were steady. Her voice, when the gate sentry asked his questions, came out dry and slightly irritated, the voice of a man who resented being delayed by people beneath his professional contempt.

"Scribe," the sentry said, reading upside down. "You'll want the civil office, not here."

"I know where I want. I've done this in four towns."

"Five," said a corporal leaning in the gate shadow, "if you count Drenmark, and nobody counts Drenmark." He grinned. Cyrus gave him the thin smile of a tradesman humoring a soldier, and was waved through into Kettermoor.

Inside, the town was worse than the smell promised. Requisition notices papered the walls three layers deep. A queue of women stood outside the commissary with empty sacks, not moving, just standing, in the way of people who have learned that queues are a form of prayer. Two boys were sweeping the square around a notice board where fresh orders had been pasted that morning, and one boy swept the same clean stone over and over, listening with his head cocked to what the grown-ups were reading aloud.

Cyrus read nothing aloud. She walked with her eyes down and her ears open, and within an hour she knew three things about Kettermoor.

One: the garrison held perhaps six hundred troops, most of them young, most of them already resentful of the posting, which meant officers who compensated with procedure.

Two: the prison was not a separate building. It was the old granary block inside the garrison's inner yard — thick-walled, windowed high, reachable only through the administrative wing. Prisoners' letters went out through the civil office. Which meant the civil office was her door.

Three: Commandant Dray had arrived nine days ago from the south, and the town already hated him in the specific, quiet way towns hate men who make the rules shorter.

She heard his name first in a bakery queue, then again in the civil office itself, from the mouth of a clerk named Peth who processed prisoner correspondence with the weary speed of long practice.

"Dray's tightened the letter rules," Peth told a woman at the counter. "No names of other prisoners in the text. No mention of units. Anything that smells coded gets burned. He reads them himself now, some nights, the ones flagged." He stamped a form without looking up. "He likes reading them. You can tell."

"Why would anyone like that?" the woman asked.

Peth finally looked up, at her, at the room, at nothing. "Because it's the only part of this job where he gets to decide something," he said, and stamped the next form.

Cyrus waited her turn, presented Corvin Ashe's letters of introduction, and negotiated a contract on the spot: scribing services for prisoners' families, three days' trial, paid per page, subject to censor review. Peth barely read the contract. Boredom, Cyrus thought, folding her copy away, is the widest gate in any wall.

* * *

The work was easy and it was terrible.

Families came to the civil office in a slow drip through the morning: mothers, wives, one grandfather with a boy holding his hand, all of them carrying scraps of paper with messages they couldn't write themselves either because they couldn't spell or because spelling it would break them. Cyrus took dictation in her best clerk's hand, kept her face neutral, charged modest fees she half-forgave, and listened.

"Tell him the roof's patched. Tell him the cow calved. Tell him we're eating fine." They always said they were eating fine. Cyrus wrote *we are eating fine* eleven times in two days and did not let her pen hesitate once.

The letters went into a tray. Peth read them, stamped them, and once a day a soldier carried the tray through the inner gate toward the granary block. Once a week — if Dray's censors approved — answers came back the same way.

That was the current. That was the crack in the wall. Everything that moved between the prison and the world moved through the civil office, through trays, through Peth's stamp, through the daily soldier.

Cyrus spent her first two days making herself indispensable to Peth — correcting his backlog, reorganizing his registry by garrison instead of alphabet, laughing once, briefly, at his joke about the Drenmark postal service. By the third afternoon she was allowed to carry a returned-letters tray as far as the inner checkpoint herself, saving the soldier the walk, because the soldier had a girl in the town and Peth owed her a favor.

The checkpoint sergeant glanced at the tray, glanced at her pass, and waved her ten feet into the covered passage that ran along the administrative wing toward the granary.

Ten feet. Not far. But the passage had windows — high, narrow, iron-barred windows set into the granary's upper wall, facing the passage — and on her second trip, with a tray of twelve stamped envelopes balanced on her forearm, Cyrus Halloran looked up.

* * *

Later she would try to remember whether she'd seen him or simply known, the way you know a voice in a dark house. She decided it didn't matter. What mattered was the moment itself: the grey light, the smell of wet stone and latrines, the weight of twelve censored letters, and a face at a barred window eight feet above the ground, turned toward the light the way plants turn.

Aldric Vane.

He was older. The war had taken the flesh off him and given him back lines; his hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like weather. There was a scar along his jaw she didn't recognize. But he stood at the bars with his shoulders squared and his chin level, and he wasn't gripping the bars, wasn't pressed to them, wasn't begging the light. He was simply occupying it, upright, the way a man stands at a lectern.

Unbowed. The word arrived in her mind whole, unasked-for, and she hated how much it cost her to keep walking.

Because she had to keep walking. That was the whole discipline of it. A scribe carrying letters does not stop in a passage and stare up at a barred window. A scribe does not know the faces of traitors. Cyrus counted her steps — she'd counted them on every trip, forty-two from checkpoint to delivery slot — and let her eyes do what eyes do, one upward flick, half a second, nothing a guard watching idly from the far archway would ever flag.

Half a second. Eight feet of air and iron. Forty days of road folded into it.

He was alive. He was alive, and they hadn't broken him yet, and he stood in his cell window like a man waiting for a document he'd been promised years ago.

She delivered the tray. She thanked the receiving clerk in Corvin Ashe's dry voice. She walked back through the passage, forty-two steps, eyes down, heart slamming so hard against the coat lining that she feared — absurdly, instantly — that the sewn dispatches might be knocked out of true, might shift, might show.

Then she was through the checkpoint, out of the inner yard, back in the civil office among the ink pots and the trays, and no one had seen anything except a dull man doing a dull job.

She sat at her borrowed desk. She dipped her pen. She began transcribing a grandmother's message about a calf.

Her hand shook on the fourth word.

She put the pen down, pressed both palms flat against the desk until the wood bit, and breathed the way she'd taught herself to breathe on the night Mellowburn fell: four counts in, seven counts held, eight out. Around her the office scratched and stamped and murmured, indifferent. The empire's machinery did not care that a dead republic's last courier had just seen its condemned general standing in a window. The empire cared about the calf.

*We are eating fine,* Cyrus wrote, in a hand gone perfectly steady again, because steadiness was not a feeling, it was a skill, and skills did not require her heart's permission.

* * *

That evening she sat in her rented room above the chandler's shop and laid the situation out on the table the way she'd once laid out ledgers: coldly, line by line, no sentiment allowed near the numbers.

Item: Vane lives, held in the granary block, inner yard, reachable by no legitimate route available to Corvin Ashe. Correspondence goes out but — she'd checked with Peth, casually, professionally — almost never comes in for political prisoners. Dray's rule. Political prisoners don't get replies. Replies sustain hope; hope sustains resistance; the empire preferred its dangerous men starved of news until they were grateful for anything, even a confession.

Which meant she could not send him a letter. Even if she dared encode one — and she did not dare; Dray read flagged letters himself, and a man who liked reading letters was a man who'd learned their grammar — there was no reply channel. She could shout through a keyhole and hear nothing back.

Item: the code. Still sewn in her coat. Partially deciphered — she'd worked the early sections on the road, by candle, learning its shape: it used the old diplomatic grid, Vessany's peacetime cipher, which the empire had supposedly broken but plainly hadn't, or Dray's office would have burned it unread. She could read perhaps a third of it fluently. Enough to know it was long. Enough to know it was addressed not to Vane but to whoever Vane trusted — a testimony meant to travel onward after him. His testimony. The promise made in the orchard, in another country, in another life: *if war comes, carry my truth north.*

Item: the walls. Six hundred men, a commandant with fresh authority, a town too cowed to cough. Corvin Ashe could walk in and out of the civil office forever and never get one foot closer to the granary door. Petitioning scribes did not visit political prisoners. Nobody visited political prisoners.

She turned the items over. She found no route. Every line she drew hit a wall — the yard, the gate, the rules, the sheer stupid mass of armed boredom surrounding the one man in Kettermoor she needed to reach.

And underneath the inventory, quieter, the thing she refused to enter into the ledger: *he looked unbowed.* Hope, arriving hand in hand with impossibility, each making the other worse. If he had seemed broken, she could have grieved and planned around a corpse. If the walls had been soft, she could have planned like a person with a future. Instead she had a living man behind stone and a dead woman's skill set, and between them, nothing but time — and time, in occupied towns, was the one currency that always flowed toward the executioner.

She banked her candle and lay in the dark listening to the chandler's stock settle below, thinking of orchards, of a man who had once said *you're the only honest clerk I know*, meaning it as a joke neither of them laughed at properly.

* * *

On the fifth morning everything changed, and it changed the way disasters usually did: through a door.

She was at her desk in the civil office when Peth came in early, which he never did, moving quickly, which he never did, and spoke to the duty officer in a low voice she was not meant to overhear and had trained herself, over years, unable not to.

"The commandant wants the correspondence room cleared this afternoon. All trays, all registers, moved to his wing." A pause. "He's interviewing petitioners personally. Starting with the scribes."

"Interviews?" The duty officer sounded more tired than alarmed. "Since when does Dray interview scribes?"

"Since the Sennet Field anniversary filings came through from the capital," Peth said. "There's a list. Names connected to the old republic's diplomatic service are being pulled for questioning. Someone upstairs thinks documents are moving north." He lowered his voice further; Cyrus caught only fragments. "...commandant says he'll know them when he talks to them... wants the scribes brought to the garrison proper, one at a time..."

The duty officer grunted. Peth left. The office resumed its scratching.

Cyrus sat very still at her desk, pen moving, transcribing a wife's message about winter fodder, while inside her skull the inventory tore itself up and rewrote itself.

They were hunting her. Not specifically — not yet, not by name — but by species. *Names connected to the old diplomatic service.* Somewhere south, Odile Ferran's filed reports had ripened into suspicion; somewhere upstream, the remnant loyalists' alarm had become an empire-wide net. The category existed now. Corvin Ashe belonged to it the way a fish belongs to a net, invisibly, until the net closes.

And Commandant Dray wanted to look into the eyes of every scribe in Kettermoor, one at a time, in his own wing, behind his own walls.

She had one day. Perhaps less — Peth's *this afternoon* meant the summons could come by evening bell.

Cyrus finished the fodder letter. Stamped nothing; that was Peth's job. Tidied her desk with the thoroughness of a man expecting to return tomorrow, because a fleeing man tidies differently and tidying is legible.

Then she picked up her satchel, walked out of the civil office into the bright cold street, and stood in the square trying to think, really think, past fear, past hope, past the memory of a face at a barred window — when a boy touched her sleeve.

"Beg pardon, master. Message for the scribe Ashe." He held out a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "Gentleman said wait for you to read it before I go."

She broke the seal. Four words, in a small precise hand she did not know:

*"Dray knows what you carry."*

Across the square, unnoticed, the boy was already gone.
