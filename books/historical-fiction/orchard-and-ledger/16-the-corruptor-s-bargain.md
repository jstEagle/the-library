## 16. The Corruptor's Bargain

They came for her at the fourth bell of the morning, and they came politely, which in Kettermoor was worse than coming with irons.

Two soldiers, not the garrison's grey but the deeper slate of command staff, standing in the doorway of the scribe's loft she had rented above a cooper's yard. The taller one held a folded paper bearing the garrison seal.

"The Commandant requests the clerk Halloran," he said. "Bring your kit. You'll be writing."

Cyrus had been in Kettermoor nine days. Nine days of petitioning work in the administrative yard—copying grievances from farmers whose orchards had been stripped, drafting complaints that went into drawers no one opened. Nine days of walking the same circuit past the prison block at the same hour, because routine was armor and armor was the only thing between her and the screaming part of her own mind. Nine days since the morning she had looked up through the barred window on the third tier and seen Aldric Vane standing in the light like a man who had been waiting for it.

Older. Greyer at the temples. Unbowed—that word had come to her unbidden and stayed, because there was no other word for the set of his shoulders, the way he had turned his head when the light hit him, unhurried, as if the bars were his to open.

She had not wept. She had gone back to copying grievances.

Now she packed her ink, her pens, the flat case of nibs. She left everything else exactly where it always lay—the coat on its hook, the false papers in their drawer—and followed the soldiers down into streets still wet from the night's rain.

* * *

The commandant's office occupied the top floor of the old customs house, and it had been furnished by someone who understood what fear looks like when it is being sold back to you. The room was warm—too warm for the season—with a fire built high, and the windows behind the desk were shuttered so that the only light came from lamps and flame. A man could sit in this room all day and lose track of whether it was morning or night outside, which was perhaps the point.

Commandant Dray rose from behind the desk to greet her, which no commandant did for a clerk.

He was smaller than his reputation. Cyrus had heard his name spoken carefully even among the petitioners—a man who kept order in a town that should have been ungovernable, a man the occupation trusted with things it didn't trust to anyone else. He was broad through the chest, going soft, with the ruddy complexion of someone who drank well and slept soundly, and small quick eyes that did none of the sleeping.

"Clerk Halloran." He pronounced the name correctly, which she noted, and offered her a chair before the fire, which she noted more. "You've been doing good work for us. Clean hand. Careful with figures. My adjutant says your copies of the requisition ledgers have fewer errors than any scribe we've had."

"Accuracy is a habit," Cyrus said. "I'm glad it serves."

"It serves." He sat, folding his hands over his stomach, a comfortable man in a comfortable chair, and smiled at her with genuine warmth, or something engineered to be indistinguishable from it. "Which brings me to a matter requiring accuracy of a different kind."

He drew a document from the desk drawer—not the folded paper the soldier carried; something older, travel-worn, wrapped in oilcloth—and laid it on the desk between them without touching it further, the way a man lays down a card he has already counted.

"You know what this is?"

It was diplomatic format. Vessany crested, though the wax had been broken long ago and resealed with a forgery so competent she felt a professional flicker of admiration. Cipher columns ran down the right margin in a hand she recognized with a lurch she kept off her face: Vane's. His cramped, leftward-slanting script, the one she had spent three years learning to read in another life.

"A captured dispatch," she said evenly. "Republican cipher, by the look of it. Most such documents are worthless now. The republic is a memory with excellent stationery."

"Most are worthless." He leaned back. "This one isn't, because I'm told you're the best scribe in my administrative yard, and the best scribes notice things. So here is what will happen. You will work on this document. In this room. Alone, when I'm out, watched when I'm not. You'll tell me nothing unless I ask. When you can read it—if you can read it—you'll tell me that much and no more. Do you understand the arrangement?"

"I understand I don't understand it," Cyrus said. "Why a clerk? Why not your own cryptographers?"

"Because my cryptographers report to people I don't fully trust, and you—" he spread his hands, an honest man shrugging at the world's dishonesty "—report to no one. You arrived alone. You have no faction. No history anyone can find. A woman who came out of nowhere with clean hands and a careful pen." His eyes rested on her with a weight that had nothing casual in it. "In my experience, people with no history are either running from something or toward something. Either way, they want something very badly, which makes them the most reliable workers in the world."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the garrison drums sounded the changing of a watch.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

"Then you go back to copying grievances, and I find a worse use for this document and a worse fate for everyone in it." He picked up the oilcloth bundle again and held it out. "But you won't refuse. Look at it first, if you like. Then decide."

She took it. Under the forged seal, under the cipher columns, the outer leaf bore a routing mark she knew: the northern diplomatic line. Her line. The route she had memorized at nineteen years old, in a garden, while a man with dirt on his hands made her promise.

She looked up at Dray and gave him the face of a curious, cautious clerk.

"What's in it for me?" she asked.

"Ah." He smiled again, and this time the warmth reached his eyes, which was somehow colder. "That depends entirely on what you find inside."

* * *

She worked in his office for six days.

The first day, she did nothing but transcribe the cipher columns into fair copy, complaining audibly about the condition of the paper, the inconsistency of the ink, the slovenly habits of republican clerks—building, layer by layer, the persona of a technician absorbed in craft. Dray watched her for hours at a time, saying nothing, and she let him watch, because a watcher grows bored, and boredom is a door.

The second day, she began the real work, and discovered within two hours that the cipher was one she knew.

Not merely knew—had helped design fragments of, in the last spring of peace, in a borrowed office above a chandler's shop, arguing about column rotations with a young signals officer while Aldric Vane sketched in the margins and made them both laugh. The republic had assumed its ciphers would die with it. This one had survived in the only place that mattered: her memory.

By the third day she was reading fragments, and by the third evening she understood why Dray wanted a nobody with no faction to do this work, and why he wanted it done slowly, and why the shutters stayed closed.

The document was Vane's testimony. Not orders, not rescue plans—a confession, structured as testimony, naming names. And the third name decrypted cleanly, in full, in Vane's unmistakable prose:

*"…the dispositions at Sennet Field were known to the enemy before our army marched. The orders that placed the Second and Seventh Corps in the low ground were drafted not in the war ministry but transmitted from within, and the officer who carried them south was Colonel-Commandant Merec Dray, then attached to the general staff under credentials issued by this republic, which paid him, trusted him, and buried its dead accordingly…"*

She read the passage four times. Then she copied it into cipher—a different cipher, her own, one Dray would never recognize—and burned the draft in his brazier while he stood at the shuttered window with his back to her, and her hands did not shake until afterward, in the privy, where no one could see.

Dray hadn't captured the testimony. He'd intercepted it—or bought it, or tortured it loose from some courier whose name would never be recorded—and he had spent however long it took finding someone to read it for him. Not his cryptographers. Not the bureau. A nobody. Because whatever the document contained, it was worth more to him unread by anyone official, and worth least of all if it ever reached the light.

He wasn't hunting the code. He *was* the reason the code mattered.

And now he wanted Vane transferred—she had heard the adjutant mention papers moving between offices, a prisoner convoy requested, destinations left blank—and she finally understood the shape of the bargain he intended. The testimony named him. If it surfaced, he was a dead man regardless of which side won the war. Unless he controlled it. Unless he owned the only person who could read it, and owned the prisoner who wrote it, and moved both beyond the reach of every tribunal, every bureau, every reckoning.

Truth as currency. Lives as collateral. And herself—the last clerk of a dead republic—holding the coin.

* * *

On the seventh day, he made the offer.

He dismissed the guards himself. He poured wine—two cups, unprompted, setting hers within her reach—and stood at the fire with his back half-turned, a man rehearsing generosity.

"You're wondering why I haven't asked what you've found," he said.

"The thought crossed my mind."

"Because I already know what you found. I've watched you for seven days, Clerk. I've seen how you look at certain passages. There's a way a reader goes still when a text stops being text and becomes a mirror." He turned. "You know whose hand this is. Don't insult us both by denying it. You knew the cipher on sight, which means you were inside the republic's signal service, which means everything about you—the name, the trade, the traveling-clerk fiction—is costume. And I don't care. That's the beauty of my offer. Your secrets are safe with me precisely because I have no use for them."

Cyrus lifted the wine and did not drink it. "Go on."

"Vane dies in the spring tribunal. You know that. Everyone knows it. The new regime needs a traitor executed properly, and Vane is the traitor they've chosen—he commanded armies against them, he's famous enough to make the point, and conveniently, he's guilty of nothing except losing." Dray swirled his own cup. "But I can move him. There's a transfer protocol—prisoner exchange, medical repatriation, a dozen instruments. With my signature, Vane walks out of Kettermoor alive. North, maybe. Anywhere that isn't a scaffold."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you finish reading that document, you give me the complete plaintext, and the original burns." He nodded at the brazier, glowing and patient. "Every fragment of it. Everywhere it exists. Including"—his eyes came up, mild and terrible—"whatever you've already copied, wherever you've hidden it, which I assume exists, because a person like you always makes a copy. That's not an accusation. It's a compliment."

The fire ticked. Below them, the town went about its occupied day: carts, bells, a market opening under new flags.

"You're asking me to destroy his testimony," Cyrus said, "and save his life with it."

"I'm asking you to choose which matters more. The man or the words." Dray set down his cup and came around the desk, and for the first time he seemed tired, almost human, a man carrying a weight he'd carried too long. "I was at Sennet Field, Clerk. Before I was anything else, I was a staff officer who watched twenty thousand men walk into a killing ground because the republic needed someone to blame and couldn't afford to look in a mirror. Do you think I enjoy being the name in that document? Do you think a tribunal would weigh my treason against what I've kept functioning here—this town, these people, order out of everything falling apart?" He shook his head slowly. "History doesn't weigh, Clerk. History executes. And I would rather buy my life with a dead man's manuscript than hang for a war everyone has already decided to forget."

"Vane wrote it so no one would forget."

"Vane wrote it because dying men write letters." His voice hardened, the warmth draining out of it like water from cracked ground. "Here is what happens if you refuse. I take you downstairs tonight. My interrogators are not artists, but they are thorough, and by tomorrow noon you'll have given me the plaintext anyway, along with everything else you know—which, given who you clearly are, is considerable. Vane still dies. The document still burns. The only difference is what it costs you to arrive at the same place." He straightened his cuffs. "Whereas if you agree, one man lives who otherwise dies. That's arithmetic, Clerk. Even a republican should respect arithmetic."

She thought of the orchard at Mellowbrook, the trees going to army timber, the promise made in peacetime by two people who had believed the future was a place you could plant things. She thought of the sergeant at Halfway Bridge who had waved her through at dawn without knowing why, grace arriving from the enemy side of the ledger. She thought of Odile Ferran's pen scratching her name into an imperial ledger—being written down, being seen, being *counted*—and of how far she had come to deliver words she now held in her own two hands.

One man lives who otherwise dies. It was true arithmetic. It might even be mercy.

It was also exactly what a man guilty of Sennet Field would offer.

"I need time," she said.

"You have until tomorrow's fourth bell." Dray returned to his chair and became again the comfortable man, the host, the smile settling back into place like furniture. "Finish the transcription tonight. Leave it in the desk. Whatever you decide, decide as someone who has read the whole thing—because you haven't, Clerk. Not yet." His small eyes glittered across the lamplight. "There's a final section. Sealed separately. And I think, when you read who else is named in it, you may find my offer looks different than it does right now."

* * *

She worked until the candles guttered, alone in the warm shuttered room, and near midnight she broke the final section.

It was short. Vane's hand grew looser toward the end, the letters of a man writing fast against an ending. It named three officers of the old republic's remnant—the loyalists, the ones who had hunted her from Mellowbrook, who had executed a man in the square rather than risk exposure—and it described, in dates and sums, how they had sold the evacuation routes at Corren's Landing to save their own faction, trading other people's escape for their own impunity.

And beneath those names, in the last lines, a postscript she read twice, three times, her breath gone shallow in the overheated room:

*"To whoever carries this: you were promised it would save a life. It will not. It was never a rescue order. It is the truth, and truth saves no one—it only survives. Carry it north. Let it be read aloud where it cannot be denied. And forgive yourself, whoever you are, for what delivering it costs. The cost is the proof that it mattered."*

She sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls of the customs house, pipes knocked with the cold coming on.

The loyalists hadn't wanted the codes destroyed to protect the republic. They'd wanted them destroyed to protect themselves—and Vane had known it, had written it down, had sent it toward the north knowing what it would cost the one who carried it.

And Dray had just handed her the whole of it, sealed and sorted, because he believed she was a mercenary with no cause, and that everyone, given the right price, chooses life over truth.

She looked at the brazier. She looked at the finished transcription lying in the desk drawer, and beside it, invisible, the copy in her own cipher that Dray did not know existed, sewn where the diplomatic codes used to live, against her skin.

Tomorrow at fourth bell she would give him an answer.

She was still deciding what it would be when she heard, from the courtyard below, the sound she had learned to dread across a thousand miles of collapsing country: boots, many of them, moving out of rhythm—and then a voice she knew, raised in the cold air, asking the gate sergeant for *a clerk called Halloran*.
