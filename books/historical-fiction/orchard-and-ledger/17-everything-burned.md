## 17. Everything Burned

The trouble with a cipher is that it teaches you to read the world as if everything were written for you.

Cyrus had been working on the code for six days, an hour at a time, by the light of a shuttered lamp in the scriptorium's back alcove, where she had established herself as a scribe too dull to notice and too useful to question. The garrison at Kettermoor generated paper the way a wound generates blood—requisition orders, transfer manifests, execution warrants, all of it passing through her ink-stained hands—and somewhere inside that river of paper was the fragment she needed: the key that unlocked the last third of Aldric Vane's dispatch.

She had four of seven segments solved. The first had come easily, almost insultingly so—a rotating substitution keyed to the date of Sennet Field, which meant Vane had built his confession around the very day it described. The second and third had cost her two nights each. The fourth had cracked open this morning while she transcribed a laundry inventory, her hands moving through the routine work while the other part of her mind turned the cipher like a lockpick.

And what lay behind the fourth segment was a name.

Not Vane's. Not one of the ministers whose treason she had half-expected. A name she had seen three days ago on a requisition docket signed with a heavy, confident hand. A name attached to the man who had offered her a bargain over cold tea and smiled like a creditor.

*Dray.*

She sat very still after that, quill hovering, letting the knowledge settle through her the way water settles through cloth. Commandant Edran Dray—the architect of the occupation's smoothest sector, the officer whose signature opened doors, the man who had dangled Vane's transfer before her like bait on a hook—had sold the army at Sennet Field. Had sold eight thousand soldiers into the guns, among them, perhaps, a republican private named Ferran whose sister was even now somewhere south, chasing the same truth from the other direction.

The dispatch wasn't a rescue order. It had never been. It was evidence. And evidence was worth more than any prisoner, which meant the moment Dray understood what she held, he would not bargain. He would burn the world down around it.

She finished the laundry inventory. Her hand did not shake until she set the quill down.

* * *

He came for her at dusk, which told her he already knew.

If Dray had merely suspected, he would have sent soldiers at dawn, or summoned her through channels, wrapped in procedure. Dusk meant urgency wearing the mask of ordinary time—the hour when the garrison changed shifts, when the corridors filled with men who wouldn't remember faces, when a clerk could be walked out of a building and never walked back in.

She heard him before she saw him: the particular cadence of his boots in the scriptorium corridor, unhurried, accompanied. Two sets of footsteps. Then three.

Cyrus had perhaps ninety seconds. She had spent years learning what could be done with ninety seconds, and the first thing she did with these was nothing dramatic at all. She blotted the page in front of her, squared it, and slid it beneath the stack of completed inventories where it belonged. The cipher work—the actual fragments, the pencil notes, the strip of decoded text—lived in a oilcloth packet sewn into the lining of her coat, same as always, same as Mellowbrook, same as every road since. The coat went nowhere without her.

But the *cover*—that was another matter.

Her forged travel papers. The merchant's seal impressions. The letters of passage that said she was Marta Quill, licensed scribe of Harrow Fen, a person assembled out of candlelight and patience in a rented room while a real life waited outside the door like weather. They were in the false bottom of her writing case, because a scribe's case drew no suspicion and a body search might.

Dray rounded the corner with Captain Ives on one side and a sergeant she didn't know on the other, and his face wore the expression of a man arriving to collect a debt he'd already decided how to collect.

"Master Quill." His voice carried pleasantly, for the benefit of the scribes still bent over their desks. "A word in my office."

"Of course, Commandant." She closed the writing case. Latched it. Picked it up.

His eyes went to the case the way a hawk's eyes go to movement, and she knew—he knew—and the knowing was in the room now like smoke.

* * *

The office smelled of lamp oil and wet wool. Rain had started outside, drumming on the shutters, and Dray stood with his back to the window while Ives took up position by the door, and Cyrus set the writing case on his desk exactly as invited, because refusing would have been worse.

"You've been busy," Dray said.

"I keep the ledgers you pay me to keep."

"I mean elsewhere." He opened a drawer—not searching, performing—and produced a sheet of paper she recognized with a lurch she kept off her face. A requisition docket. The one with the name. "You transcribed this three days ago. You looked at my signature longer than the figures. Ives noticed."

Ives, by the door, nodded once, a small man enjoying a large moment.

"People look at signatures," Cyrus said. "It's called doing one's job."

"What I want to know," Dray said, coming around the desk, close enough that she could see the gray coming into his beard, the tiredness under the command, "is what else you're holding. Because a clerk doesn't sew documents into her coat. A clerk doesn't memorize shift changes. A clerk—" he tapped the writing case once, lightly, like knocking on a coffin lid, "—doesn't carry a second bottom in her writing box. Yes, we weighed it. Weighing things is also a job."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere across the yard, a guard called the hour.

Cyrus measured the distance to the brazier.

It was a modest iron thing near the hearth, kept fed because Dray liked his office warm, and it was the only fire in the room, and she had noticed it the first day she entered this office and had thought, in the idle way one notices exits, *that would do.*

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "I'm a scribe from Harrow Fen. Whatever you think I am—"

"Marta Quill is a fiction with excellent penmanship," Dray said. "I had Harrow Fen checked. There is no Marta Quill. There is no record, no family, no debt, no grave. Do you understand what that means? It means when you die tonight, no one will ask why. No one will come looking. You built yourself a person with nothing attached to her, and now that person is going to be a great convenience to both of us." He gestured to the case. "Open it."

She opened it. There was no percentage left in stalling; the only currency she had left was the next thirty seconds, and she intended to spend them precisely.

The false bottom lifted away under Ives's eager fingers. The papers came out—letters of passage, seal blanks, the merchant's commission, the whole paper skeleton of Marta Quill—and Ives spread them across the desk with the tenderness of a man uncovering treasure.

"There," Dray said softly. "There's our courier. Where are the dispatches?"

"Gone," Cyrus said. "Destroyed weeks ago."

"No." He picked up a letter of passage, studied the seal. "You don't destroy something you bled to carry across a country. You people never do. Sentiment is your whole religion." He looked up at her, and for a moment the commander's mask slipped and she saw the thing underneath: fear. A big man's fear, cold and specific. "What did Vane send you with? Tell me, and you walk out of here. I'll even give you the transfer. I'll put him on a cart north myself. That was always a real offer—it's more real now than ever."

And there it was, laid open: he needed to know what she had, because not knowing was worse than anything the document could say. She watched him need it, and she understood the shape of the trap he'd built for himself. He had spent years believing the confession died at Sennet Field with everyone who'd seen it. Then a clerk arrived asking after Vane, and the dead document grew a pulse.

"It names you," she said quietly.

The room went very still. Even the rain seemed to hold its breath.

"Does it," Dray said.

"Sennet Field. The eastern batteries stood down forty minutes before the assault. Someone gave the order and someone was paid. The dispatch carries names, amounts, dates." None of which she had fully decoded yet—but truth is mostly timing, and she had learned from masters how to spend a lie shaped like truth. "It's already out of my hands. Killing me buys you nothing but certainty that it surfaces anyway."

Something moved behind his eyes—the arithmetic of a cornered man—and she knew then that the offer was dead, that there would be no cart north, that the sergeant by the wall had been positioned near the hearth tools since they entered. But she had what she'd wanted: his full attention, fixed on her face, while Ives gloated over the papers and the rain covered sound.

"The dispatch," Dray said, "or we dig for it. Ives has done digging before. It's remarkable what the living will trade to stop being—"

She moved.

Not toward the door, not toward the coat with its sewn cargo—that was lost the moment she moved, and she had known it would be, and she let it go the way you cut a rope you've been hanging from. She went sideways, low, past the sergeant's grab, and her hand closed on the brazier's handle, and she swung the whole hot weight of it across the desk and into the spread of Marta Quill.

The papers caught like they had been waiting years for permission.

Seal blanks curled. Letters of passage bloomed orange and went black. The merchant's commission—three nights of candle-grease and a steady hand—lifted on the heat and burned through in a single breath, and Marta Quill of Harrow Fen, who had bought ink and mended ledgers and laughed at a neighbor's joke in a border town that never knew her, ceased to exist between one heartbeat and the next.

Ives shouted. The sergeant lunged. Cyrus took a backhand blow across the ear that rang her head like a bell and put her against the wall, and through the ringing she heard Dray roar at them to stop—to *stop*, because the burning papers were telling him something, and what they were telling him was that she valued the fiction less than he valued learning its secrets, and a woman who burns her own identity in front of you is a woman who has already decided what happens next.

"Get it out! Save the seals!" Ives beat at the flames with his bare hands and swore and snatched his fingers back. Too late. Ash and curl. The brazier sat in the wreckage of the desk strewn across the floor, coals scattered, small fires dying on wet wood, and Marta Quill was smoke.

Cyrus slid down the wall, ear roaring, and laughed—she couldn't help it, a short ugly sound that made all three men stare—and the laughter hurt her ribs and tasted like freedom, which is a taste most people never learn and can't mistake once they do.

They think I destroyed everything, she thought. They think the coat is empty now.

Let them search the coat. Let them find the oilcloth packet and spend their night on fragments and pencil notes and a cipher keyed to a date none of them would guess. Let them believe the worst of what she'd carried was ash. Every hour they spent believing it was an hour bought for what came next.

Dray crouched in front of her, breathing hard, holding one scorched corner of a letter of passage like a relic. "You just burned your own escape," he said. "Every road, every gate, every safe house—you were nothing without those papers, and now you're less than nothing. You're a corpse walking."

"No," Cyrus said. Her ear was bleeding; she could feel it cooling on her jaw. "Now I'm finally somebody."

He searched her face for the meaning of that and didn't find it, and the not-finding frightened him more than the fire had.

"Take her to the west cell," he said at last, standing, turning away, and his voice was steady again but his hands weren't. "Strip the coat. Burn the lining if you have to. And Ives—if she speaks to anyone, if anyone visits her, if a bird lands on her window—I want to know before it finishes landing."

They hauled her up. The coat went with them—her coat, with its precious sewn secret, marched off over a sergeant's arm toward whatever table they'd lay it open on—and Cyrus let them take it because there was nothing else to do, and because she had counted, in the long minutes before his boots came down the corridor, on exactly this.

The fragments would puzzle them. The substitution was keyed to Sennet Field's date, and only three people alive knew that date mattered: Vane, herself, and the man whose signature was all over the fourth segment. Dray would sit with the decoded strips all night and get nowhere, because the answer to his ruin was locked in a date he had every reason to avoid remembering.

Which bought her until morning.

The cell they threw her into was stone and straw and a slot of window showing rain. She sat with her back to the wall, ear throbbing, hands raw from the brazier's handle, and took stock the way she'd taken stock in every dark place since Vessany began to fall: what remained, what was lost, what was owed.

Marta Quill: gone, and good riddance. The coat: gone, temporarily—papers survived capture better than couriers did, and if they found nothing readable, they might grow careless with where they kept it. Odile Ferran: somewhere south, carrying the other half of the truth, the brother's name, the reason a census taker had crossed a line she couldn't uncross. Vane: thirty yards and two locked doors away, older and unbowed, waiting for a message that had taken a republic's death to deliver.

And the garrison's communication protocol—the signal tower, the nightly broadcast, the machine Dray used to run his perfect occupied sector—all of it operated by men who followed orders, and all of it useless to her unless someone on the inside turned a key.

She had no key. She had no allies inside these walls. She had a night, maybe less, before Dray's fear outran his caution.

Through the window slot, the rain eased. And across the yard, at the gate below the watchtower, torchlight flared against the wet dark as the evening post came through—riders, mud-spattered, travel-worn, one of them handing papers to the gate sergeant with a gesture Cyrus knew, a gesture she had invented variations of in a rented room by candlelight.

A small figure in a traveling cloak, hood back, face turned up toward the tower lights. Checking angles. Counting guards.

Cyrus pressed her forehead to the cold stone and breathed, and for the first time since Mellowbrook, the breathing felt like something other than endurance.

"Odile," she whispered to the empty cell. "You impossible, magnificent liar. What took you so long?"
