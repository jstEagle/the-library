## 18. The Reading of the Code

The key turned behind us with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Dray's signal room was smaller than I'd imagined—half a cellar, half a chapel. A long table scarred by candle wax. The garrison's cipher cabinet against the far wall, brass fittings green with neglect. And above it all the relay board: forty brass switches in four rows, each wired to a repeating station down the line—Harwick, Colm's Ford, the river forts, every occupying office between here and the capital. One message keyed into this room went out to all of them at once. Dray had built himself a throat that spoke to an empire.

And I had just locked us inside it.

"The bolt," Odile said behind me. Her voice was steady but her hands were not; she kept pressing them flat against her coat as if they might otherwise fly apart. "You locked it."

"Dray will have a key. We have minutes." I set the satchel on the table and opened it. Inside lay the dispatches—the last diplomatic codes of the Republic of Vessany, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of the orchard where they had been buried and dug up again. Beside them, my decoded fragments on loose sheets: three nights of work by candlelight, cross-checking the cipher against the garrison's own traffic patterns until the confession assembled itself like a body being stitched back together.

*At Sennet Field, the left flank was sold. The orders that withdrew the reserve came from within. Commandant Merek Dray accepted payment in land grants and a commission under the new flag. He signed his name in the enemy's ledger on the ninth day of Harvest Month.*

Odile crossed the room and put her ear to the door. "Boots," she said. "More than one pair. Coming down the corridor."

"Then we're done waiting for courage." I lifted the first switch from its cradle. It was heavier than it looked, worn smooth at the grip by years of Dray's thumb. "Help me with the cabinet. There should be a transmission log—a protocol sheet. Every garrison keeps one pinned inside the door."

She wrenched the cabinet open. Papers spilled out, and among them, exactly where tradition demanded, the protocol card: *For priority traffic, key the master sequence, hold ten seconds, transmit in clear or cipher as marked.* Clear, then. No cipher. Let every clerk and adjutant and junior officer on the line read it in plain letters they could not unlearn.

"Cyrus." Odile's voice caught on something. She was holding a folded page she'd pulled from the spill of papers—not the protocol, something else. A muster return. "This is the Fourth Vessany Line. My brother's unit. It lists—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It lists who surrendered the position at the ford. It's dated two days before Sennet Field. Dray's own signature authorizing the withdrawal order."

I took it from her. The ink was old but legible, and there it was: the same name, the same hand, the same betrayal written twice—once in secret, once in filing. Proof stacked on proof. "We'll send this too," I said. "All of it. Everything we have."

The boots reached the door. A fist hammered the wood, once, twice. "Open this room! In the name of the Provisional Authority!"

"Master sequence," I said, and began to key.

The switches were stiff. Each one required a full pull and release, and the machine answered each with a deep mechanical clatter that shook dust from the ceiling beams—there was no subtlety in this instrument, no quiet way to tell an empire what its commandant had done. The fist became shoulders against the door. Somewhere above us, a bell began to ring.

I keyed the date first. Then the header: *PRIORITY TRAFFIC — GARRISON KETTERMOOR — READ BY ALL STATIONS.* Then, letter by letter, the sentence I had carried across a broken country sewn into my coat lining:

*AT SENNET FIELD THE LEFT FLANK WAS SOLD.*

The door shuddered. Wood splintered near the hinge.

"Odile." I didn't look up. "When they come through, you get behind the relay board. They'll want to stop the transmission more than they'll want anything else. Don't let them touch the switches. Whatever happens—don't let them touch the switches."

"And you?"

"I'm going to keep talking."

*THE ORDERS THAT WITHDREW THE RESERVE CAME FROM WITHIN.*

The lock gave. The door burst inward and lamplight spilled across the floor along with three soldiers and Commandant Dray himself, still buttoning his coat over his nightshirt, his face carrying the particular fury of a man interrupted mid-escape. His eyes went straight to the relay board, then to me, and I watched him do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I'd watched him do across his desk two days ago when he'd offered me Vane's life for the code. He understood instantly what room he was standing in and what was leaving it.

"Cut the wire," he said. "Cut it NOW—"

A sergeant lunged for the junction box on the wall. Odile stepped out from behind the board and drove the iron poker from the hearth into the back of his knee. He went down hard, and the wire held, and the machine clattered on under my hands.

*COMMANDANT MEREK DRAY ACCEPTED PAYMENT IN LAND GRANTS.*

"Seize her!" Dray's voice cracked into something younger, something afraid. Two soldiers moved toward me and Odile swung the poker in a wide arc that bought three seconds, four. One soldier caught the shaft and wrenched it away; the other grabbed Odile by the collar and threw her against the wall, and I heard the breath leave her body, and I did not stop keying.

*HE SIGNED HIS NAME IN THE ENEMY'S LEDGER ON THE NINTH DAY OF HARVEST MONTH.*

"That is enough!" Dray crossed the room in three strides and closed his hand over mine on the switch, forcing it down, holding it dead against the plate. His face was close to mine, sweat-shining, and beneath the fury I saw the thing I'd seen in him from the beginning: not conviction. Calculation, drowning. "You understand what you've done? Not to me—to yourself. To the girl in the flour-barrel papers. To whatever name you were wearing before tonight. There's no road out of this for either of you now."

"There never was," I said. "That was the point of roads."

He struck me across the mouth with the back of his hand, and I tasted copper, and I laughed—actually laughed—because the pain meant I was still here, still present, still *Cyrus Halloran*, and the machine was still transmitting behind us, because Odile had crawled from the wall and thrown herself bodily over the junction box, arms wrapped around the conduit like a woman embracing a tree in a flood.

"The sergeant's wire is cut!" someone shouted. "The line's severed at the box—"

"It's NOT—" Odile's voice came out muffled against the metal. "The trunk line runs through the floor. Under the boards. I can feel it humming."

And she could. I could too, now that I listened—a low vibration rising through the soles of my boots, the whole apparatus alive, the message already gone out down the dark wires to Harwick, to Colm's Ford, to the river forts, to offices full of sleepy clerks who would read it over their morning bread and look up at their commanders with new eyes.

Dray heard it too. I felt the change come over his hand before I saw it leave his face—the grip loosening, the weight shifting, a man realizing he was holding a rope that had already been cut at the other end.

"What," he said quietly, "have you sent?"

"Everything," I told him. "Your signature. The muster return your own clerks filed. The land grant registry—you didn't think I'd stop at the confession, did you? I've had two days in your archive, Commandant. You gave me the run of it yourself when you thought you were buying me." I wiped blood from my lip. "Every occupying office between here and the capital is reading your name right now. By dawn, so will the reformist officers. By noon, so will your own sergeants."

He let go of my hand as if it had burned him.

And that was when the bell upstairs changed its rhythm—from alarm to something faster, something frantic—and the shouting started.

* * *

It began, as these things apparently always begin, with a rumor moving faster than any officer could outrun it.

The garrison had two hundred men in it, give or take the desertions, and most of them were Vessany-born conscripts serving under new colors because the old ones burned. They had marched under officers whose competence they trusted, and then at Sennet Field the reserve had been withdrawn and eleven thousand men had died in a killing ground that should have been a victory—and for two years the official word had been *miscommunication, fog of war, the fortunes of battle.* Now every telegraph clerk and signalman in Kettermoor was reading, in plain unambiguous letters, that their own commandant had sold the flank, and the word went through the barracks the way fire goes through dry grass.

From the signal room window—I'd dragged myself up to it, one arm around Odile—we watched it happen. Lanterns blooming in the yard. Men pouring out of the barracks blocks half-dressed, some armed, some not. A knot of them surrounding the duty officer, who stood on the steps gesturing for calm and achieving the opposite. Somewhere a shot cracked, then another, and then the particular ragged chorus of gunfire that means nobody is aiming and everybody is deciding.

"They're not rioting," Odile said. Her voice had a wondering quality, as if she were reading a census form and finding her own name in it. "Look. Look at the armory. They're going for the armory—but they're not firing on each other. They're firing *up*. At the officers' quarters. At his quarters."

She was right. The pattern was legible from above: chaos with a spine running through it. This wasn't a mob. This was an army remembering what it had lost and whom it had lost it to.

"He'll run," I said. "He has horses. He has the south gate—"

The south gate lights went out.

We looked at each other. Someone, or many someones, had gotten there first.

Down in the yard, a voice rose over the noise—not loud so much as *carried*, passed mouth to mouth along the ranks the way orders used to be passed before drums. I couldn't make out the words from the window, but I could see the effect: men turning, converging, flowing toward one building with a purpose that had nothing chaotic in it at all.

The prison block.

"Oh no," I said. "Oh no, no—they're going for Vane."

"Why is that bad?" Odile asked. "Isn't he—"

"If they reach him before I do, he dies in the first thirty seconds. Not because they hate him—because he's the most famous prisoner in the building and frightened men with rifles shoot famous prisoners first. Or worse—" I was already moving for the door, snatching Dray's dropped key ring off the floor as I went. "Worse, they drag him out and someone panics and puts a bullet in him as an act of enthusiasm. Martyrdom by accident. I did not carry those codes across three hundred miles of occupied country for him to die of friendly chaos."

The corridor outside was pandemonium—soldiers running both directions, an officer bellowing contradictory orders, smoke already threading down from somewhere above. Odile fell into step beside me without being asked, and I registered distantly that she had picked up the fallen sergeant's sidearm and was carrying it with the awkward but unmistakable grip of someone who had learned, at some point in her life, exactly how much a weapon weighed.

"You don't have to come," I said.

"My brother died at Sennet Field," she said. "Two days before, if the dating holds. Because this man signed a paper." She racked the slide badly, corrected it. "I'm coming."

We went down the service stair against the current of fleeing clerks, shoulder to shoulder in the narrow stone, and the noise swelled around us, and beneath it all I could still feel it—that hum in the floor, fainter now, fading as the last words of the message reached the farthest stations. Somewhere out in the dark, in a dozen towns that had never heard of Cyrus Halloran, machines were ticking out a dead republic's final testimony to audiences of enemies.

Let them read it. Let every one of them read it twice.

The yard doors were jammed open. Smoke rolled thicker here, lit orange from below, and through it we ran—past a barricade already forming, past a corporal who stared at us, decided we belonged to somebody's faction, and waved us through—toward the prison block where the crowd was thickest and the gunfire sharpest.

The outer gate of the cell wing hung half off its hinges. Inside, torchlight and confusion, men calling Vane's name in voices that ranged from reverence to threat.

And at the end of the corridor, through the smoke, a barred window—and behind it, calm as Sunday, a face I had carried across a country and half a decade.

Older. Bearded now. Unbowed.

"Aldric," I breathed—and pushed into the crush.
