## 19. Vane's Choice

The confession was still ringing out of the garrison's signal room when the first fire took hold.

Cyrus had triggered the protocol herself—keyed the drum-code relay, watched Odile feed the final fragment of the cipher into the transmitter's brass teeth—and now every occupying office from Kettermoor to the coast was reading the same words: *Dray sold Sennet Field.* The machine clattered on behind her like a loom weaving a shroud, and she left it to its work.

Smoke moved through the corridors in slow grey rivers. Somewhere below, men were shouting orders that no one obeyed anymore. The garrison's spine had been broken by a piece of paper, and the body was only now learning it was dead.

She ran.

*Prison block, east stair, third door past the cistern.* She had mapped it over four days of petitioning scribes' patience: measuring steps while pretending to count words, sketching the corridor in her memory the way she'd once sketched ledger columns. Her feet knew the way even as her eyes watered shut.

The east stair was choked with fleeing soldiers. She pressed against the wall and let them pour past her—young men in imperial grey throwing down their belts, an officer weeping openly with his sword still sheathed, two sergeants dragging a wounded comrade by the arms and not looking back. No one looked at a small clerk in a smoke-blackened coat. Invisibility had kept her alive for three years. It would keep her alive for ten more minutes.

The prison block smelled of wet stone and old straw and, beneath it, something sweeter—the oil they'd poured along the ground floor, she realized. Someone intended the evidence of this night to burn with the building.

Third door past the cistern. The lock was new iron; the wall around it was old brick. She had bought a pry-bar off the carter in Mellowbrook without knowing why, and her hands found it in her coat now as if it had always been waiting there.

"Stand away from the door," she said through the bars, voice pitched low.

A pause. Then a sound she hadn't heard in six years—a short, dry laugh, unhurried, as though the world were a slightly amusing document that had crossed a man's desk.

"Cyrus," said Aldric Vane.

Not *clerk*. Not *Halloran*. Just the name, spoken once, quietly, and Cyrus felt three years of careful nothing crack down the middle.

"How—" she began.

"You breathe like you're counting," he said. "You counted your breaths at my sister's wedding too, standing in the orchard, when you thought no one was watching." A rustle of movement behind the door. "Also, the whole garrison is broadcasting my testimony, so I assumed someone had come."

"I'm getting you out."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "I rather thought you might."

The lock fought her. The pry-bar screamed against iron. Behind her, up the stairwell, the smoke was thickening, turning from grey to the bruised yellow that meant flame nearby. On the fourth attempt something gave—not the lock but the hasp's mounting, rotten wood surrendering where iron wouldn't—and the door swung open on a man thinner than memory, bearded, in a prisoner's rough shirt, with eyes that were exactly, unbearably the same.

He looked at her for a long moment. She looked back.

"You came north," he said. "Through all of this."

"You made me promise."

"I made you promise years ago, when the war was theoretical." He touched her shoulder—one brief pressure, gone again. "People keep promises made in peacetime like they're ornaments. You carried yours across a country on fire."

"We can discuss my character flaws later," Cyrus said. "There's oil on the ground floor and Dray's men are regrouping somewhere, and the west gate—"

"The west gate is what you planned."

"The west gate is what I planned."

"And how many guards?"

"At last count, eleven. Now, who knows. That's why we go now, quickly, before—"

"No."

The word landed softly, and somehow that made it worse. Cyrus stared at him. Smoke curled between them, and Vane stood in it without moving, without coughing, a man planted like one of his orchard trees.

"Aldric—"

"There's a courtyard," he said. "Mustering yard, where they read the morning orders. I've listened to it through the wall for five months. Stone carries sound beautifully." He turned and walked toward the cell's narrow window, the barred slit high in the wall. "If I leave through your west gate, I am a fugitive. A rumor. A story told by loyalists to explain why their codes failed—*the traitor Vane escaped, helped by persons unknown, and everything he says is lies.* They will use my running as proof against every word I've written."

"That's not true—"

"It doesn't have to be true. It only has to be sayable." He put his hand flat against the cold wall. "But if I stand in that courtyard, in front of the garrison, and speak the confession aloud—here, tonight, while the whole occupation is reading it on paper—then I am not a fugitive. I'm a witness. There are officers down there right now learning that their commandant sold their comrades at Sennet Field. Some of them will want to kill me. Some of them will want to hear me. And the difference between those two groups is whether I come to them or run from them."

"They'll hang you."

"Perhaps." He smiled, and it was the same crooked smile from the orchard, from peacetime, from the world that had assumed tomorrow. "They'll certainly try. But Cyrus—if I flee with the truth, the truth stays mine. Mine dies with me, eventually; everyone's does. If I stand and give it away—freely, publicly, to anyone who will carry it—it stops belonging to any of us. It belongs to everyone. That's the only immortality testimony has."

Below them, something exploded—not large, a powder store or a barricade—and the floor shivered. Dust sifted down between them like flour.

"You promised me rescue," Cyrus said, and hated how her voice shook. "I buried duplicates of the code in an orchard. I bribed a carter. I spent a night sharing bread with an imperial sergeant so I could walk through his checkpoint. I burned my own name to get here. And you're telling me no."

"I'm telling you yes," Vane said gently. "Just not the yes you planned."

* * *

She argued with him for ninety seconds that felt like a season.

She argued logic—the tribunal could happen anywhere, safely, later, among reformist officers. He answered that tribunals convened by victors in quiet rooms become negotiations, and negotiations become erasure.

She argued fear—Dray's loyalists would cut him down in the yard before he finished a sentence. He answered that Dray's authority had died the moment the confession went out on the wire, and dead men's soldiers make poor executioners.

She argued, finally, with nothing—just stood there in the burning corridor with her fists clenched, a woman who had delivered a message across a collapsing country only to have its recipient decline delivery.

Vane watched her the whole time with terrible patience. Then he said, "Come with me, then. Stand with me. Two witnesses are better than one."

"I'm nobody," she said. "I'm a clerk who never existed."

"You were never nobody. You were hidden." He stepped closer. Through the cell's high window, the firelight caught half his face. "Which brings me to the question I've been saving. Six years ago, in the orchard, you told me a name and made me swear never to repeat it until the war ended. I've sworn well—I haven't said it to God, to prisoners, or to myself in the dark. But the war is ending, Cyrus. Right now, in this smoke, it is ending. So."

He waited.

She understood, distantly, what he was doing. He wasn't asking out of curiosity. He was asking because a witness needs a name; because testimony given by no one is testimony owned by no one; because the whole machinery of hiding that had kept her alive was also, she saw suddenly and clearly, the thing that would erase her. The census ledger had tried to record her as a lie. The loyalists had hunted a lie. Even her own mission had been built on the assumption that Cyrus-the-woman was worth less than Cyrus-the-vessel.

The fire was closer now. She could hear it eating.

"Sarel," she said.

The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh—painful, and then impossibly light.

"My name is Sarel Voss. I was born in Tarrow Mill. My father kept bees. I joined the foreign service at nineteen because I was good at languages and bad at staying home." Her voice steadied as she went, each sentence setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "I have been Halloran for three years and two months, and I am done. My name is Sarel Voss."

Vane closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does at a taste he'd nearly forgotten. "Sarel," he repeated, carefully, as if handling something breakable. "Well met. Again."

Then he picked up the pry-bar she'd dropped, tested its weight, and handed it back to her hilt-first—an old soldier's courtesy, a weapon returned to its owner.

"Shall we go testify?" he said.

* * *

The mustering courtyard of Kettermoor garrison was a rectangle of trampled mud ringed by barracks, lit on this night by fire instead of lamps, and filling fast with men who did not know what they were.

That was the thing Cyrus saw first, standing in the shadow of the cistern arch beside a man in a prisoner's shirt: the crowd had no shape yet. Three hundred soldiers, maybe more, pouring out of burning buildings and standing in loose, angry knots—some armed, some not, some shouting about Dray, some shouting about traitors, some just shouting because the world had changed under their boots and shouting was the only tool left. Officers tried to form lines and the lines dissolved. A lieutenant stood on a barrel waving his sword and was ignored.

Into this, Aldric Vane walked.

Not ran. Walked. Straight through the mud to the center of the yard, hands empty and visible, and Cyrus—because she had followed him, because she discovered in the last ten steps of the corridor that she could not do otherwise—walked three paces behind him with the pry-bar gripped in both hands and her heart going like a drum-code.

For a moment the yard simply confused itself. A prisoner? An escape? Men reached for weapons and didn't draw them. A sergeant yelled *seize him* and another sergeant yelled *wait*, and the two commands tangled and died.

Then someone near the front—a young officer, powder-burned, holding a printed sheet that was still warm from the signal room—said, in a voice that carried farther than he can have intended:

"That's him. That's Vane. The confession is his."

The yard went quiet in patches, like candles going out.

Vane stopped in the center and turned slowly, letting them all see him—the prison-thinness, the beard, the unbroken posture. When he spoke, he didn't shout. He used the voice of a man dictating a dispatch, level and clear, and the silence did the amplifying for him.

"I wrote the confession you are holding," he said. "I was at Sennet Field. I saw the supply lines opened and the flanks sold, and I spent five months in a cell writing down names, dates, and sums. Commandant Dray took imperial gold to fold our army in half. Everything on that page is true, and I will stand here while any man verifies it—line by line, if you wish. Kill me and the paper still speaks. Free me and I'll speak with it. Either way, you now know what your own officers did to your own dead."

Silence. Total, ringing, impossible silence in a burning fortress.

And then, from the back of the yard, a voice—old, gravelly, a quartermaster's voice by the sound of it:

"My brother died at Sennet Field."

Another voice. "My company held the left flank."

Another. "We were told reinforcements were coming. We were told—"

"They were coming," Vane said. "Until they were ordered not to."

The yard broke—not into violence, which Cyrus had braced for, but into motion: men turning toward the commandant's quarters, toward the signal room, toward each other, the crowd finally finding its shape, and its shape was a question with Dray's name on it. Somewhere a shot cracked and was swallowed. Somewhere a door went in.

Vane stood in the center of it all, unmoved, a tree in weather.

Cyrus stayed three paces behind him, as she had promised herself, watching the doors and the rooflines and the shadows—the clerk's work, the guardian's work, the work that had no name in any ledger. Once, a loyalist captain pushed through the crowd toward Vane with a drawn pistol, and Cyrus was already moving, already inside his reach, the pry-bar coming down across his wrist hard enough to drop the gun into the mud, and the captain looked at her—at the small smoke-blackened woman with murder in her face—and decided, wisely, to be elsewhere.

Vane glanced back at her. "Still counting breaths?" he asked.

"Not anymore," she said. And found that it was true. For the first time in three years, she was breathing like a person, not a ledger entry.

It was near dawn when they came for him—not Dray's men, whose power had burned with their master's quarters, but a column of reformist officers under a white banner and a colonel named, she would learn later, Ashe, who dismounted in the muddy yard, looked at Vane for a long moment, and said: "Secretary Vane. The Provisional Council requests your testimony. Under guarantee of tribunal. Public trial, public record, public verdict."

"Public," Vane repeated. "Good. That word has been scarce lately."

They took his wrists for the bindings and he let them—not as a prisoner, Cyrus saw, but as a man cooperating with a procedure he had chosen, which is a different thing entirely. As they led him toward the column, he turned his head and found her in the crowd without searching for her, as if he'd known exactly where she'd be standing.

"Sarel Voss," he said, loud enough for the officers to hear. "This woman carried the confession from the border to these walls alone. Write her name down. Make certain it gets written down correctly, this time."

Colonel Ashe looked at her—looked properly, the way almost no one had looked at her in three years—and said, "Name and service, for the record."

Cyrus opened her mouth.

And from across the courtyard, pushing through the smoke and the crowd with a leather folder hugged to her chest and soot striping her face, came Odile Ferran, census taker of Mellowbrook, walking like a woman who had crossed a country on fire of her own.

"Her name," Odile called out, "is already written down. I wrote it. And I've brought something else that belongs in the record too."

She stopped in front of them, breathing hard, and held up the folder, and Cyrus saw the military archive stamp on its cover and the name inked on its spine—a name from the confession, a name from Sennet Field—and understood that the night's accounting was not yet complete.

"He should hear it from you," Odile said, looking not at Vane but at Cyrus. "Both of you. Before the tribunal does."
