## 20. Aftermath in Ashfall

The ash came down like gray snow, settling on the shattered glass of the signal office, on the overturned brazier, on Cyrus Halloran's hands, which had not stopped shaking since dawn.

She stood in the doorway of the communications room and watched the occupying garrison of Kettermoor tear itself apart. Officers who had saluted Dray that morning were now reading his name off dispatch slips with the flat expressions of men doing arithmetic they didn't like. Somewhere below, a mob—half soldiers, half townsfolk—had breached the armoury and was burning requisition records in the courtyard. The smoke rose straight up into a windless sky and then fell back down again, coating everything, so that the whole fortress seemed to be snowing its own ruin.

*Dray sold Sennet Field.*

Fourteen words, broadcast to every imperial office within relay range because the protocol demanded it: any transmission flagged with the old republican authentication codes—the codes she had carried sewn into her coat lining for two years, through checkpoints and flour barrels and one census taker's careful pen—was to be copied verbatim to all stations. The empire's own machinery, turned against its own commander. Aldric Vane had designed that protocol years ago, back when he still believed systems could be trusted. He'd been right about this one.

"Cyrus."

Odile Ferran stood behind her in the corridor, holding a leather folio against her chest the way a soldier holds a wound. Her census bureau coat was streaked with ash. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry.

"You should sit down," Odile said.

"I've been sitting for two years."

* * *

Cyrus — three hours earlier, or a lifetime; she could no longer calibrate —

They had come for her in the stairwell, Dray's men, four of them with drawn sabers, and she had thought it was over, that the code would die in her coat and Vane would die in his cell and Sennet Field would stay a lie forever. Then the first bell rang—not an alarm, something older, the garrison's assembly bell—and the corridor filled with reformist officers whose faces she recognized from the proscription lists read aloud in occupied villages. Captain Ilse Marchand, formerly of the Third Vessani Rifles, now wearing imperial grey with the collar torn open. She had put a pistol to the lead saber-man's ear and said, very quietly, "Drop it or I'll write your name down too," which was, Cyrus thought later, the most bureaucratic threat ever delivered at gunpoint.

The fight in the commandant's office had lasted less than a minute. Dray had fought like a man who understood exactly what was waiting for him if he lost, which meant he fought well and then desperately and then not at all. They took him alive, which surprised everyone, Dray most of all. He sat on the floor of his own office with blood in his mouth and looked up at Cyrus with something almost like curiosity.

"The confession," he said. "That's what the code was. Not orders. Not a rescue." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "You carried a dead man's apology across half the country."

"It wasn't an apology," Cyrus said. "It was evidence. There's a difference. You're about to learn it."

Then the bells, the fire, the ash.

* * *

Odile — the courtyard, mid-morning —

She found her brother's name where she had known she would find it, in the third drawer of the records annex that the crowd had broken open and then, strangely, left intact. The Sennet Field casualty rolls. Imperial copies, meticulous, indifferent.

**FERRAN, TOBIAS. PVT. 2ND COY., 7TH VESSANI RIFLES. KIA 14 SEPT.**

No ceremony in it. Just ink. Just the fact of him, reduced to a line, filed under the army that had gotten him killed by selling him.

Odile stood there a long time while the ash drifted through the broken window and settled on the open page, softening the letters as though the record itself were trying to forget what it knew. She had spent eleven days digging through military archives, following the fragment Cyrus had let her see—one name, one date, one betrayal—and every thread had led here, to a field where seven hundred men had been fed into an ambush because Commandant Dray had sold their positions for a governorship and a guarantee.

Tobias had been twenty-two. He wrote letters home with terrible spelling and good jokes. The last one had arrived eight days before Sennet Field and asked whether the plum tree in the yard had fruited yet.

She closed the drawer. She did not cry. She suspected she would, later, somewhere without ash, but for now there was a strange clarity in her, the clarity of a form finally completed—all fields filled, nothing left blank.

Behind her, boots. Captain Marchand, ash-grey, stopping in the doorway.

"You're the census woman," Marchand said. "The one who let the clerk go."

"I'm the one who entered her name wrong on purpose," Odile said. "It's not quite the same thing."

"Isn't it?" Marchand glanced at the open drawer, the page, the name. Something moved across her face—recognition, maybe, or just the general grief of the week. "There'll be a tribunal. Vane insisted. Public hearings, full record, everything Dray buried dragged into the light. Your brother will be cleared posthumously. It won't bring him back, but—" She stopped herself, and Odile appreciated that. People kept trying to finish that sentence and there was no honest way to finish it.

"But it will be written down correctly," Odile said. "For once."

"Yes," said Marchand. "For once."

* * *

Cyrus — the cells, late afternoon —

Vane's cell door stood open. He hadn't left it.

He sat on the cot with his hands on his knees, an old man's posture, though he was not old—only made old, the way stone is made smooth, by weathering. Through the barred window behind him the ash fell in slow spirals. He looked up when she came in, and smiled, and it was the same smile from the orchard, ten years ago, crooked and unhurried, as if time were a thing that happened to other people.

"They tell me you brought it," he said. "Through Mellowbrook. Through the checkpoints. Through all of it."

"Someone had to carry your testimony north. You made me promise."

"I made you promise when we were both idiots standing under apple trees." He studied her face. "You look tired."

"You look imprisoned."

"Only since Tuesday." He stood, joints cracking, and crossed to the window, watching the courtyard fires. Below, Marchand's officers were loading Dray into a wagon under guard. The crowd shouted names at him—Sennet, Sennet—and threw clods of ash-damp earth. "They want me to run, you know. Marchand offered. A boat, papers, the coast. Disappear with the truth safe in my head and testify someday, somewhere, when it's convenient."

"What did you say?"

"That truth kept in a head dies with the head. Truth spoken in a square outlives the tongue." He turned back to her. "I'm going to stand in front of a tribunal and say all of it, out loud, in order, even the parts that shame me. Especially those. And then whatever happens happens."

"They might hang you anyway. Reformists make promises too."

"They do. And some of them keep them. That's the wager." His eyes went to the open door, to the corridor beyond, to freedom shaped like an exit. "Come with me. Not to escape—to witness. A tribunal needs witnesses who aren't officers. Someone who carried the document. Someone who can stand up and say *I held it in my hands, I read it, this is what it said.* Otherwise it's just soldiers grading soldiers."

Cyrus looked at the cell. At the cot with its thin blanket, the wall where he must have marked the days, the window with its view of burning paperwork.

"Aldric," she said. "There's something I have to tell you before we go anywhere. About my name."

He waited. He had always been good at waiting.

"My name isn't Halloran," she said. "It never was. Halloran was a dead clerk whose identity I borrowed in Year One and wore until it grew into me like a splinter. My name is—" and here her voice did something strange, caught on something, because the last time she had said it aloud she had been a different person in a country that no longer existed— "my name is Ceres Ambrose Vale. My mother called me Cerry. Nobody has called me anything real in six years."

Vane didn't laugh, didn't marvel, didn't do any of the things a man in a story might do. He just nodded slowly, the way one nods at a lock finally turning.

"Ambrose," he repeated. "Good orchard name."

And that—that absurd, small, perfect sentence—was what undid her. She sat down on the cot he'd vacated and put her ash-blackened hands over her face and cried for the first time since the war began, quietly, thoroughly, while outside the republic's betrayer was driven away in chains and the sky continued to snow its gray confessions.

* * *

Odile — the signal office, evening —

The room still smelled of scorched copper. Broadcast slips lay everywhere, the fourteen words repeated down every column, and Odile gathered them up because someone had to and because gathering was what she did. Filing was what she did. Records were what she did.

Marchand found her there again, which was beginning to feel less like coincidence than like appointment.

"The tribunal convenes in three weeks," Marchand said. "Kettermoor great hall. Open to the public—Dray's edicts forbade public assembly, so we're opening them out of spite. Witnesses will be deposed and recorded." She set a folded paper on the table. "I'm told you're thorough. I'm told you once tracked a missing soldier through eleven days of archives nobody else wanted to search. I need a registrar. Someone to keep the record of the record. Someone who understands that a ledger is only a weapon depending on who holds the pen."

Odile looked at the paper. It was a warrant of appointment, blank except for the title line, waiting for a name to be entered in it.

Her whole career she had written other people down—households, livestock, strangers passing through, a disguised courier whose name she had taken down knowing it might be a death sentence. Every entry a small act of power. Every power a temptation.

"And if I find discrepancies?" she said. "If the tribunal's own account conflicts with the imperial archive? If the empire's version and the truth disagree?"

"Then you enter the truth," Marchand said, "and initial the correction. That's the whole job. That's the entire revolution, honestly, if anyone asks you."

Odile picked up the pen. For a moment she thought of Tobias's terrible spelling, of the plum tree, of the casualty roll with its indifferent ink. Then she wrote her name—her full, true name, the one she had never had occasion to hide—in the space provided, and dated it, and signed beneath it in her smallest, most exact hand.

Outside, the ash had stopped falling. The sky over Kettermoor was clearing toward a hard, cold, honest blue, and in the west, faint as a watermark, the first stars were already being entered into the record.
