## 22. Orchard, Spring

The road into Mellowbrook had been widened since the war. Someone had cut back the hedgerows and laid gravel where the mud used to swallow cartwheels whole, and for a moment, standing at the top of the last hill with her pack on her shoulder and nothing hidden in it at all, Cyrus Halloran felt like a stranger arriving in a town she had invented.

That was close enough to the truth.

A year. She counted it the way she'd once counted cipher keys: one year since a grain cart carried her out before dawn while Odile Ferran watched from a window and chose silence. One year since Kettermoor burned, since Dray's confession went out over every garrison wire in three provinces, since Aldric Vane stood in the smoke of a prison yard with his hands empty and his voice steady and told the world what he was willing to die saying. One year since she had given her real name to a man in a burning yard and found that it still fit.

She walked down into Mellowbrook under that name now. No forged papers in her boot lining. No codes sewn anywhere against her skin. Just a woman of thirty-four, travel-worn, carrying two bundles of young apple stock wrapped in damp sacking and a letter of introduction from the provincial reconstruction office — signed by Odile Ferran, who had learned, somewhere along the way, that a ledger could be made to serve memory instead of power, and who wielded that discovery like a quiet weapon.

The gatekeeper at the town's edge — a boy of perhaps sixteen, too young to have fought in anything — glanced at her papers and waved her through without reading them.

"Here for the orchard land?" he asked. "You're the third this spring."

"The other two didn't stay?"

"Stayed a week apiece." He shrugged. "Ground's half stumps now. Army took what it wanted, and the rest of it's gone bitter."

"It'll come back," Cyrus said. "Apple ground remembers."

He looked at her as if she'd said something slightly mad, which was fine. She had been looked at worse.

* * *

The orchard was worse than she'd prepared for.

Half the trees were gone — felled in that first brutal winter of occupation, dragged off toward the rail line for trench timber and gun-carriage stock. The stumps stood in ragged rows like teeth knocked out of an old man's jaw. Of the survivors, some had been pollarded savagely, cut back past anything a careful pruner would allow, and two had died outright, their bark split by frost they no longer had the strength to resist.

But not all of them. That was the thing she hadn't let herself hope for until she saw it: a good dozen trees had come through. And down in the low corner where the ground stayed wet, where she had buried duplicates of the republic's final coded dispatch beneath the roots of a Blenheim Orange on the worst night of her life —

— there were saplings. Volunteers, self-sown from dropped fruit, a scatter of wild little whips shoulder-high, thriving on neglect.

Cyrus knelt among them for a long time without touching anything. The wind moved through the surviving branches with the sound she remembered, that dry paper rustle, the sound of a place that kept its own records.

She dug at the base of the Blenheim Orange with her fingers, gently, not really intending to find anything. But the trowel was in her pack, and after a while she used it, because some debts you verify even when you already know they're paid. Three feet down, wrapped in oilcloth inside a wax-sealed tin: nothing. She had come back in the autumn, quietly, on her way south from Kettermoor with Odile's evidence in her coat and Dray's name ringing in every occupied office in the province, and she had taken the duplicates out herself and burned them in a stove in Halfway Bridge with the innkeeper's blessing. The originals had done their work. The truth did not need copies anymore.

Still, she sat back on her heels over the empty hole and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. A year of carrying a weight that wasn't there anymore. The body keeps ledgers of its own.

* * *

Mrs. Tammett still kept the chandler's shop, and her eyes were still quick, though the hair above them had gone entirely white now, and she'd taken to wearing a black armband that Cyrus understood, from the way nobody commented on it, was permanent.

"You," Mrs. Tammett said, in the doorway, before Cyrus had said a word.

"Me."

"I told myself you wouldn't come back." She stepped aside to let Cyrus in. "I told myself that about a lot of people."

"How is the street?"

"Broke, mended, broke again. The requisitions stopped in the winter when Dray's trial started making noise up-country. Now it's just poverty, which is at least honest." She put the kettle on without asking. "They say the tribunal will run through summer. They say Vane speaks for four hours at a stretch and doesn't repeat himself. They say the reformist officers are terrified of him, which is the correct thing for them to be."

"They say a lot."

"They do." Mrs. Tammett set down two cups. "And I say you're the woman whose name was never in any census book I ever heard read out, and who bought ink from me eleven times under a clerk's name, and who laughed at Josper's joke about the tax assessor like she meant it." Her eyes were not unkind. They were simply accurate, which is sometimes harder to bear. "What do I call you now?"

"Cyrus Halloran."

"That's the false one."

Cyrus held her gaze. Outside, someone was leading a horse past the shop, and its hooves struck the cobbles in a rhythm like slow typing.

"My real name is Cyrus Halloran," she said. "It always was. The clerk was the fiction. The clerk was a coat I wore so long I forgot it wasn't my skin." She turned her cup once on its saucer. "The tribunal records have it right. There's testimony in the public file — mine, under seal until the tribunal closes. After that, anyone can read it. I'm done being unreadable."

Mrs. Tammett studied her the way she might study a ledger column that finally balanced.

"Cyrus Halloran," she repeated, tasting it. "Well. It suits you better than the other thing did. You never looked like a clerk. You looked like a woman doing arithmetic she hated."

"That's the most accurate description of my year anyone's ever given me."

The kettle sang. Mrs. Tammett poured, and pushed across the counter a small twist of paper — boiled sweets, the same kind she'd sold Cyrus on ordinary afternoons in another life. Some kindnesses don't change their form; they only change what they mean.

* * *

She planted for six days.

The ground needed more than she'd promised the gatekeeper it would give. She cleared suckers from the stump rows, hauled water from the stream because the pump handle had been stolen for scrap, and set the new stock — twenty-two whips of cider apple and pear, chosen from a nursery outside Halfway Bridge, paid for with the small pension the provisional government had grudgingly granted to people who could document what they'd done during the collapse. Her documentation ran to forty pages. Odile had written most of it.

On the fourth day, neighbors began to appear. That was how Mellowbrook worked, she remembered — nobody asked questions on day one, everybody showed up by day four. Josper came with a spade and a flask of something that was mostly spirit. Two boys she didn't know hauled stakes without being asked. An old woman named Grethe, whom Cyrus had only ever seen at market, stood at the fence for an hour watching the planting lines and then said, "Third row's crooked," and was right, and Cyrus redug it, and Grethe nodded once, satisfied, as if order restored to an orchard were a debt owed to the dead.

Maybe it was.

On the fifth evening, working alone in the failing light, Cyrus set the last tree in the corner where the volunteers grew — the wet corner, the Blenheim Orange's corner. She planted the newest whip beside the old stump, close enough that in ten years their canopies would touch.

"For the ones who didn't get a row," she said to it. She didn't say names. The names were in her, all of them, catalogued the way she'd once catalogued cipher keys: the contact who arrived burned and half-dead with instructions in ash, and who had died two days later in a room above the cooperage with her hand still closed around a message no one would ever decode. The suspected informer in the square, whose face Cyrus had been forced to watch and forced to remember. The sergeant at Halfway Bridge who despised the war and waved her through at dawn and never knew why, and who had died, Odile's letters reported, in the fighting after Sennet Field's truth came out — killed not by rebels but by his own command, for hesitating at an order. Grace from the enemy side of the ledger, repaid with a grave.

She tamped the soil down around the whip with her palms. Roots over flight. That had become the whole of her philosophy, and it had taken her twenty years, two identities, and one burning garrison to arrive at something a farmer could have told her in a sentence.

* * *

On the seventh morning she walked out of town to the graves.

There was no formal cemetery for the war dead yet — the province couldn't afford stone, so the dead lay where communities had managed to bury them, marked by whatever came to hand. The cooperage field held nine mounds under simple boards, each carved with a name and a date and nothing else, because whoever had cut the boards had understood that dates were the only part of a death that fit on wood.

Her contact's board read: **MERIN SOLASSE, COURIER. DIED CARRYING.**

Someone had chosen those words well. Cyrus stood in front of them for a long time.

"I finished it," she said. There was no one to hear, and she said it anyway, out loud, because she had spent too many years saying true things only in silence. "It cost what it cost. You'd want to know it worked. You'd want to know Dray is in irons and the transcript is public and schoolteachers in three provinces are already teaching Sennet Field as a caution, not a mystery." She crouched and pulled a weed from the base of the board. "And I use my own name now. You'd laugh. You always said I hid behind paperwork like it was armor. It wasn't armor. It was a wall, and walls keep things in as well as out."

Wind moved the grass. Somewhere behind her, a lark went up, climbing in that spiraling song that means open country and nothing hiding in it.

She visited the other graves she could reach — the sergeant's, far north, a day's ride she'd make before she left the province; the square, where she simply stood for a minute among the morning market stalls, because the living dead deserve standing-with too. Grief, she had learned, is not a country you leave. It's a country you learn to farm.

* * *

The tribunal news reached Mellowbrook weekly, in the gazette and in rumor, and the two versions rarely agreed.

Vane spoke, the gazette said, and the gazette was right. He had testified for eleven days and would testify for eleven more, walking the assembly through Sennet Field hour by hour, order by order, naming the officers who had sold the army's flank and the clerks who had laundered the orders and the census bureau that had taught a nation to see its own citizens as entries to be corrected. When the old guard tried to shout him down, he waited. Witnesses described the waiting itself as testimony — a man standing silent at a lectern until a room remembered why it existed.

They had offered him a ministry. Everyone knew it. Reformists loved a martyr with a pulse.

Rumor said what Vane would answer, and rumor said it with a grin: that he'd told the delegation he'd spent five years in Kettermoor learning the difference between power and witness, and he intended to spend whatever remained of his life on the correct side of that line.

Cyrus believed the rumor. She had seen him refuse a door once, in smoke, with soldiers coming, because a locked room was not the same as a free man. He had asked her name then, and she had given it — her real one, raw and strange in her mouth after years of disuse — and he had repeated it back to her like something worth keeping.

They wrote. Not often. His letters came on tribunal stationery, official in every particular except the postscripts, which were not official at all. The latest one lay in her coat pocket now as she stood in the orchard at dusk, the young trees silvered and still, and she took it out and read the postscript again for maybe the hundredth time:

*They ask me what I fought for, since the republic is gone. I tell them I fought for the record. Records outlive flags. Come north when the tribunal closes — or plant your trees and I'll come south. Either way, I know where to find you now. That's the whole victory, as far as I'm concerned. Yours under my own name too, A.V.*

She folded it away. Above her, in the oldest surviving tree, a pair of birds were building something out of the year's first cut grass, with the tireless, unimpressed industry of creatures who assume tomorrow.

Assume tomorrow. She smiled at them. Once, grief for that assumption had nearly broken her — the peacetime world that planted knowing it might be felled. But that was the lesson the year had taught, the one she could feel taking root in herself alongside the whips: the felling is not the argument against planting. It is the reason for it. Every stump in this field argued for flight, for erasure, for becoming no one in some distant town where no ledger knew her name. And every volunteer sapling in the wet corner argued otherwise, without rhetoric, without fear, on the strength of one dropped apple.

Memory chosen over invisibility. Roots over flight. She was done choosing the other thing.

* * *

She worked until full dark, and then she built a small fire at the orchard's edge — permitted now, the burn bans lifted with the army gone — and sat beside it eating bread and hard cheese and drinking the last of Josper's spirit, which was mostly spirit indeed.

Footsteps on the road. Unhurried. She knew them before she saw the figure, the way you know a voice on a wire.

Odile Ferran came up the track with a traveling bag and a lantern she didn't light, because the fire was enough. She looked older by exactly one hard year and no more. She wore bureau grey still, but the collar was different — the reformist cut, the one that meant the archives answered to her now.

"You didn't write that you were coming," Cyrus said.

"You didn't write that you were planting twenty-two trees. We've established we keep our own counsel." Odile lowered herself onto the ground across the fire, stiffly, the road in her knees. "I came because the tribunal adjourns Friday for the harvest recess. And because there's something in the amended census file I wanted to deliver in person rather than by post."

"The entry."

"The entry." Odile opened her bag and brought out a single sheet, copied fair in a hand Cyrus recognized — the same hand that had once recorded a false name with unsettling care. "Read it."

By firelight, Cyrus read her own life rendered in bureaucratic prose, and found she could bear it, and more than bear it:

**HALLORAN, CYRUS. Courier, Republic of Vessany (dissolved). Carried the final dispatch of the republican archive from Mellowbrook to Kettermoor garrison under conditions of extreme peril; instrument of the disclosure of the betrayal at Sennet Field. Presently resident, Mellowbrook district. Occupation: orchardist.**

Orchardist. She read the word twice. Twenty years of tradecraft, forgery, cipher, silence — summarized, correctly, as growing things.

"You gave me a trade," she said.

"I gave you the truth. It happened to have a trade attached." Odile fed a stick to the fire. "There's a second matter. The reason I traveled rather than wrote." She paused, and Cyrus saw — with the old courier's instinct for what a pause contains — that this was the difficult part. "When I went through the military archive for my brother's unit records, I found more than Sennet Field. I found a secondary file. Sealed. Cross-referenced to the tribunal but never opened, because no one surviving claimed it." Odile looked at her across the flames. "It concerns the northern route. The one that was compromised. The one your contact died carrying instructions about. It names who sold that route, Cyrus. And it isn't Dray."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark rows, the new trees stood in their straight lines, listening the way young things listen.

"There's a name in that file," Odile said quietly, "and the name is going to surface at the tribunal within the month whether we want it to or not. I thought you should hear it sitting down, in an orchard, with a friend. Because I suspect—" she hesitated, the first hesitation Cyrus had ever seen in her, "—because I suspect you already know it. And because I think it's going to hurt."

Cyrus looked at the fire, and at the dark rows of everything she had planted, and understood that the past, like an orchard, does not finish with you when you stop digging. It puts out shoots.

"Tell me the name," she said.

Odile told her.
