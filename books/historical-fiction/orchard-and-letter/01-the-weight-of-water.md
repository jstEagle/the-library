## 1. The Weight of Water

There is an art to sorting pearls by lamplight, and the art of it is this: you must not love them.

I learned that my first week in the Meridian Trading Company's sorting room, when I lingered too long over a white drop the size of a thumbnail and old Mr. Quill—no relation to any pen, despite what he believed—rapped my knuckles with his ruler and said, "Halloran, they're stock, not stars." I was fourteen. I had just been informed that my father's drowning had been entered into the ledger as a loss of equipment and labor, valued at eleven pounds, and so perhaps I can be forgiven for thinking a pearl might be something more than merchandise.

Five years on, I no longer think anything at all while I work. That is the trick of survival in Broome: you make your hands clever and your face blank, and you let the two of them conspire to keep the rest of you hidden away somewhere no clerk can reach it with a stamp.

The lamp above my bench hissed like a thing with opinions. Outside, Roebuck Bay breathed its evening breath—the slap of water on pylons, the groan of the lugger masts—and inside there was only the sound of shells turning over in my palms, one after another after another, until the pile became the sea and the sea became arithmetic.

Open. Tilt. Judge. Grade. Set down.

The good ones went left: round or near-round, clean skin, that deep inner light the Chinese graders called *water*—as though each pearl were holding a small ocean of its own, which, if you thought about it too long, was exactly what they were doing. The flawed ones went right: baroque lumps, pitted things, pearls with the look of having been interrupted mid-thought. And the rejects went into the tin pail with a sound like teeth being counted.

I had done this for five years, and in five years I had never once been caught looking.

That night, though, near the bottom of the tray, I found a pearl that stopped me.

It wasn't remarkable by any measure a ledger would respect. Slightly oval. A faint wrinkle at one pole where the nacre had laid down unevenly, like a scar. But the color—it held a green I recognized. The exact pale green of the shallows off Cable Beach in the hour before sunset, when the tide goes out far enough to show you the bones of the world and my father used to say, *Look, Osc, the sea's showing us her accounts.*

My hands stopped.

That was my first mistake. My second was lifting it toward the lamp.

"Halloran."

Mr. Quill's voice came from behind me, mild as milk going off. Mildness, from Quill, was worse than shouting. Shouting meant you'd annoyed him. Mildness meant he was about to enjoy himself.

"Sir."

"You've stopped."

"Inspecting, sir."

"Inspecting," Quill repeated, tasting the word. He came around the end of the bench with that rolling walk of his, the walk of a man who had never once hurried because hurrying implied someone might be worth hurrying for. He plucked the pearl from my fingers without asking, which was permitted, since technically the pearl did not exist and neither, in the company's way of accounting, did I.

He held it up to the lamp. Turned it. Held it further out, then close to his eye, performing the whole liturgy of appraisal with the leisure of a man who knew the value of making a boy wait.

"Middling," he pronounced at last. "Baroque-adjacent. Third grade, possibly fourth." He set it down on my side of the bench with exaggerated care. "And yet you looked at it for—" he consulted the clock on the wall, a brass-faced tyrant that ran four minutes fast to keep everyone honest "—eleven seconds longer than inspection requires. Eleven seconds, Halloran. At your rate, that is one-third of a pearl unsorted. One-third of a pearl is eightpence. Eightpence multiplied across a year is—" he did the sum aloud, because Quill believed all sums should be suffered publicly "—one pound four shillings and eightpence of company property, squandered on *looking*."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you know what we call a man who looks at what isn't his?"

I did, actually. I had a list. *Thief* was the charitable entry. But I said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole curriculum of my life by then, and I had graduated with honors.

Quill studied me. He had a face like a folded map—all creases and no territory—and when he smiled, which was rare, it was always the wrong kind of weather.

"Your father used to look," he said.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the yard a donkey complained about the heat, or the century, or existence generally.

"He'd hold them up to the sun on the jetty," Quill went on, conversational now, which was how I knew this was punishment rather than conversation. "Turned them in his fingers like a priest with relics. Said they were 'the sea's own thoughts.' Very poetical. Very unproductive. It took three men two years to train the poetry out of him." He leaned closer. His breath smelled of tea and pencil shavings. "We got there in the end. Mostly."

Mostly. As in: mostly trained, before the Lorelei went down with him in her ballast and the sea took delivery of whatever was left untrained. As in: mostly, except for the son, who apparently still carried a residue of looking about his person, undeclared, like contraband.

"I'll sort faster tonight, sir," I said. "Make up the time."

"Oh, you'll do more than that." He straightened, pleased, the way a man is pleased when a mechanism works exactly as designed. "Tomorrow you'll present yourself to Mr. Voss at the harbor office, nine o'clock sharp. There'll be an entry made in your file."

My file. Every indentured man in Broome had a file, thick as a Bible and read more often. Mine lived in the registry under assets, miscellaneous, labor—pearl-shell division. I had seen it once, by accident, when Mrs. Adeyemi left a drawer open: my name in a clerk's hand, my age, my term, and beneath it a running commentary in different inks, like marginalia in a scripture nobody prayed from. *Sullen. Competent. Watchful.* I had wondered for weeks who wrote *watchful*, and whether watchfulness was a virtue or a symptom.

"A notation, sir?" I asked carefully.

"A caution." Quill gathered his ruler and his dignity. "Recurring distraction. First documented instance." He paused at the end of the bench. "Or second, depending on what your father's file says. Files run in families here, Halloran. Like debts."

He rolled away toward his own bench, and I turned back to the tray, and my hands resumed their work—open, tilt, judge, grade, set down—with a smoothness I did not feel. Inside, some animal part of me paced a small cage. Not fear, exactly. Fear would have been simpler. This was the older, tireder thing: the knowledge that the company could reach into the smallest rooms of your life and leave its fingerprints there. That even eleven seconds of looking belonged to somebody else's books.

I finished the tray at half past ten. The last pearl I graded was a small brown thing, worthless, misshapen, the sort that ends up drilled and strung for tourists who want a piece of Broome without paying Broome's prices. I put it in the reject pail, and then—because the room was empty and Quill had gone home to count something—I took it back out.

I didn't keep it. Keeping it would have been theft, and theft was discovered, and discovery was ruin, and I had plans, such as they were, that required remaining undiscovered until they ripened. Instead I held it a moment under the lamp, this ugly little knot of shell-stuff, and I thought: *someone spent years making you, layer by layer, in the dark, wrapping grit in beauty, and the first dry land you touch is a tin pail.*

Then I dropped it in with the others and doused the lamp.

The walk home from the sorting sheds took twelve minutes if you took the road and nine if you took the mudflat path along the mangroves, and I always took the mudflat path, because the road passed the harbor master's office and I had developed, over five years, the habit of a man who arranges his life so as never to be seen arriving anywhere. Gideon Voss kept late hours. His window faced the track. I had no reason to avoid his window except that everything in Broome eventually passed through it, and I preferred my comings and goings to remain among the things the company had not yet catalogued. We were few, those uncatalogued things. I guarded them like eggs.

The moon was up, fat and indifferent. On the flats the mangroves stood knee-deep in shadow, breathing their rotten-sweet breath, and out beyond them the bay lay so still it looked poured. Somewhere out there, under forty feet of water and five years of silt, lay the Lorelei, and my father with her, and whatever it was the company had recorded as *misadventure, weather-related, regrettable*.

Regrettable. Eleven pounds. That was the arithmetic of my father's death: a boat, a diver, a storm, a salvage rushed against the coming blow, and a verdict written in a ledger by men who had never once been wet past their bootlaces. When I was fourteen I had believed the verdict, because verdicts come printed and grief does not, and a boy will trust almost anything that arrives in official ink. By seventeen I had begun to notice that official ink in Broome flowed through remarkably few pens. By nineteen—that is to say, tonight—I had noticed enough to keep my noticing strictly private, filed in the one ledger no auditor could open.

The tide was out. I walked the hard wet sand between the mangrove knees, and the mud took my boots with a sound like slow applause.

Here is what I did not permit myself to think about, in the ordinary course of a day: my mother. Mara Halloran, lost at sea in '89 when the packet she sailed on went missing between here and Singapore—another verdict, another line, another regrettable. I did not think about her because thinking about her was like pressing on a bruise to prove it still worked, and I needed everything I owned to work: my hands, my silence, my memory. Some nights the discipline failed. Tonight, walking the flats with the moon laying a silver road across the bay—a road going nowhere I could follow—it failed.

I let it fail for exactly as long as the walk lasted. That was the arrangement I had with myself. Grieve on the mudflats, where the tide erases everything twice daily; be a mechanism again by morning.

I remembered her hands. Not her face—faces go soft in memory, like things left in water—but her hands, absolutely: quick, salt-cracked, forever smelling of lime juice and rope. She had written letters once, before the writing stopped. Big looping hand, words crowded together like people sheltering from rain. *My Osc,* every letter began. *My Osc.* As though the O and the S were a spell she was keeping alive by repetition.

The last letter I had from her was dated March, '89. The company notice declaring her lost was dated October. Between those two dates there was a gap of seven months during which, according to every document I was ever allowed to see, my mother had been nowhere at all—not aboard, not ashore, not alive, not dead. Just a blank space in a column, and blank spaces, I had learned, were where Broome kept its secrets.

I reached the stilt-house at Chinatown's edge where I rented a corner off a Malay family who asked no questions, which I paid extra for. The Kettners' baby was crying two rooms over. A dog was conducting business of some importance in the lane. I climbed the outside stair, lit a stub of candle, and sat on my cot with my back to the wall, which was the position from which I conducted all my thinking.

Nine o'clock tomorrow. Voss. A notation in my file.

It should have frightened me more than it did. But I had been summoned before Voss twice in five years—once for the annual renewal of terms, once when a crate went missing and every diver's son within a mile of the sheds was interrogated with the delicacy of a boot—and both times I had survived by being exactly what the company wished its assets to be: dull, useful, transparent. A man made of glass has nothing to hide and therefore nothing to find. I had polished myself accordingly.

But tonight, sitting with the candle guttering and the dog finally settling its affairs, I found the polish wearing thin in places. Five years. Five years of pearls and ledgers and silences chosen with the care other men chose their words. Five years, and my term had three more to run unless something extraordinary happened, and extraordinary things did not happen in Broome; they happened elsewhere, to other people, in places with names that appeared on tickets.

And yet.

I want to record honestly what I felt that night, because later—when everything changed, when the flat gray surface of my days cracked like a tray of shells dropped on stone—I would need to remember that the longing was already there. Buried, yes. Packed down under five years of competent silence like a seed under clay. But there.

Because here is the truth I sorted pearls around every night: I did not want to stay in Broome. I wanted—God help me, I wanted—to stand on a deck going *away*. Any deck. Any away. To watch this town shrink to a smudge and then to nothing, and to feel the engine's beat come up through my boots instead of the counting house floor. To arrive somewhere my file did not exist, where my name was a word for a person instead of an entry under assets, and where a letter might reach me addressed in big looping handwriting that began *My Osc*.

That was the whole of my ambition, secreted in the deepest chamber of my silence: a ship going out, and me on it.

I blew out the candle and lay down. Through the wall, the Kettner baby had stopped crying. The tide was turning; I could hear the bay change its mind, that long low exhale of water deciding to return. In six hours the bell at the sheds would ring and I would rise and become a mechanism again, hands clever, face blank.

Nine o'clock, Voss. A caution, recurring distraction, first documented instance.

I fell asleep doing what I always did on the bad nights: reciting the silences. Which questions not to ask (where the Lorelei's cargo really went). Which observations not to share (that the harbor master's launch made night trips it had no logged reason to make). Which names not to say aloud in any building with a company sign on it (my own, sometimes, most of all).

Which silences keep you safe. I had the list by heart. I recited it like a rosary, and slept, and dreamed of water.

In the morning, walking to the harbor office along the road like an honest man, I noticed two things. The first was that the mail steamer's funnel stood above the breakwater, newly arrived, red and black against a sky the color of unpolished shell—which meant the fortnight's post was in, and the town's one connection to everywhere was temporarily, dangerously open.

The second was a stranger on the jetty.

He stood at the rail with his back to the town, watching the luggers come in, and there was nothing remarkable about him except that he was watching the wrong boats. The pearling fleet came in on the eastern moorings; the stranger watched the western channel, where nothing came in but tide and rubbish, and he watched it with the fixed patience of a man waiting for something specific, and I thought: *not cargo, then. Not trade. Waiting for a person, or news of one.*

He turned as I passed—turns like that cost money in Broome, attention like that drew Voss's window like a lamp draws moths—and for one stride our eyes met, and the stranger frowned. Not at me. At some arithmetic happening behind my face, some sum involving years and resemblance that he hadn't expected to be able to do on this jetty, this morning, in this town.

Then he touched his hat brim, courteous as a customs officer, and looked back at the empty channel, and I walked on to my nine o'clock appointment with my heart conducting itself disgracefully against my ribs.

I told myself it was nothing. Strangers arrived; strangers frowned; the tide came back in.

But my hands, those clever obedient hands, were shaking, and they would not stop until well after Voss had finished with me—and by then, I would have learned that the stranger had been asking, up and down Chinatown since dawn, a question no one had dared ask aloud in five years.

He was asking after the Hallorans.
