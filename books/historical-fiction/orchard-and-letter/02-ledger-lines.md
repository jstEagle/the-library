## 2. Ledger Lines

There is a particular smell to the counting house at Meridian Trading that I have never managed to describe without sounding like a man complaining, which is a dangerous thing to sound like in Broome. It is ink and salt and the ghost of tobacco, and underneath it all something faintly rotten — old shell meat, maybe, or old money, which amounts to much the same thing.

I was sent there on Tuesdays because I could count.

Not because anyone trusted me. Trust, at the Meridian Trading Company, was a currency reserved for men with surnames and soft hands. But I could look at a column of figures twice and tell you if they agreed with themselves, and I could do it without fidgeting, and I had learned — this was the important part, the part that kept me fed — to be useful without being memorable. A man who is memorable gets asked questions. A man who gets asked questions eventually answers one wrong. So I made myself into a piece of furniture with a talent for arithmetic, and for five years the furniture had served me well.

Tuesday mornings, Mr. Quill — the head clerk, a man whose name fit him the way a noose fits a neck — would drop the week's cargo manifests on the corner of my table without meeting my eyes. He never handed them to me. Handing things to me would have implied I had hands worth shaking, and Quill was very firm on the subject of what indentured divers were and were not. We were not, as far as I could establish, people. We were somewhere in the neighborhood of equipment.

I didn't mind. Or rather: I minded, enormously, every hour of every day, but I had gotten very good at not minding where anyone could see it. That is a skill too. In Broome it might be the most valuable one going.

So there I sat, at my table by the window that didn't open, working through manifests while the harbor did its glittering business outside like a postcard nobody had paid me to appear in.

* * *

The manifests themselves were dullness perfected. Lugs of shell, weights in tons, diver tallies, boat numbers, deductions — always deductions. The company deducted your rice, your water, your lamp oil, your tools, the wear on the company's ladder if you'd climbed one. By the time the arithmetic finished with a diver's season, he owed more than he'd earned, and the difference rolled forward into next season like debt rolling downhill, gathering weight. My father used to say the tide went out twice a day but a man's account only went out once, at the end, when they carried him up the beach. I was eleven when he said it and I thought it was a joke. It took me until I was fourteen to understand it was arithmetic.

That Tuesday, though, the third manifest in the stack was different. The paper was heavier stock, cream-colored instead of buff, and the hand that had filled it was not Quill's cramped spider-scrawl but something rounder, more patient. A ledger summary, quarterly, drawn from the master registry. Assets and liabilities, consolidated.

I almost passed over it. Almost. There is a version of Oscar Halloran who passes over things — who sees his own name in a column and slides the page beneath the next one and goes on breathing. That version survives longer than I have, probably. But he also dies smaller, and some mornings, tired down to the marrow of being careful, I choose the other fellow without meaning to.

I looked.

And there, under the heading **ASSETS — HUMAN**, in that round, patient hand, was a list. Boat crews mostly. Names I knew, names whose owners I'd drunk weak tea beside on the jetty of an evening, men who laughed with their whole chests when the whitefellas weren't listening. Kuta Jaya. Old Manu. The brothers Sali. Each name followed by a figure — a valuation, I understood after a moment's sick study. What each man was worth to the company against his remaining term. What he cost to keep. What it would cost to replace him.

And then, near the bottom, between a deckhand named Ferreira and a boy so young his entry read *apprentice, minor*, mine:

**Halloran, O. — diver, indenture 6 yrs rem. — valuation £38**

Thirty-eight pounds.

I want to tell you I felt outrage. Rage, even — clean and hot and righteous, the kind that makes men do great things in novels. What I actually felt was colder and stupider than that. I felt *corrected*. As though I had spent five years half-believing I was a person who happened to owe money, and here was a piece of cream-colored paper gently informing me that no, son, you are a thing that owes existence, and we've priced you, and honestly thirty-eight pounds stings a bit, doesn't it? Less than a good lugger. Less than a season's shell. Somewhere in the back of the arithmetic, a man had sat down and decided what Oscar Halloran was worth, and I hadn't even been consulted, which was somehow the worst of it. Not the number. The not-being-asked.

My father had been in that same column once, presumably. Diver, Halloran, T. — some figure against his remaining years. And then one October morning the sea closed over him off Cable Beach, and somewhere a clerk drew a single line through his entry, and the line cost nothing, and the sea was blamed, and the books balanced. That's what a line through a name costs. Nothing. That's the exchange rate.

I sat very still for a long moment. Outside the window, the harbor went on sparkling. A lugger came in low and fat with shell. Somebody laughed on the jetty, and the laugh carried across the water, bright and unpriced.

Then I heard footsteps behind me, and I did what five years had trained into my hands faster than any thought could move: I turned the page.

* * *

"Page forty-one," said a voice above me, "is the shell weights for the *Adelaide Mary*. You'll find page sixty-two considerably less restful reading."

Mrs. Adeyemi stood at my shoulder with a stack of folders against her hip and an expression I couldn't file anywhere. She was the company's second clerk — the one who actually ran the office, everyone knew, though the title belonged to Quill, the way the title of harbor master belonged to Gideon Voss while the actual running of it belonged to fear. Mrs. Adeyemi was a small woman, gray-streaked and precise, with hands that moved like she'd been born doing accounts and a face that gave away nothing except, occasionally, on its own schedule and to whom it chose, everything.

"I wasn't—" I began.

"You were." She set the folders down on my table, one by one, unhurried, squaring their edges to the wood. "You were reading the asset register, and you turned it face-down fast enough to take skin off the desk, and now you're about to tell me you weren't, and we'll both pretend the last ten seconds happened to someone else." She looked at me then, directly, which almost no one in that building ever did. Her eyes were the brown of strong tea and entirely unsentimental. "How much?"

I should have said nothing. I want that on the record, whoever's keeping it. The correct answer — the safe answer, the furniture's answer — was silence.

"How much?" I said.

"Your entry."

"Thirty-eight pounds."

She didn't flinch, which I respected, and didn't look away either, which unsettled me far more than flinching would have. "Mm," she said. "They valued Ferreira at fifty-one."

"Ferreira has four years left. And a bad lung."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one, maybe, some creature that had learned not to come out in daylight. "You read the terms as well as the figures, then."

"I read everything on the page. It's all on the page."

"Yes," Mrs. Adeyemi said softly. "It usually is. That's what nobody understands about ledgers — everybody thinks they're about hiding things. They're not. They're about *recording* things. Every cruelty this company has ever committed is written down somewhere in this building, in triplicate, initialled, and filed." She tapped one gray finger on the folder stack. "People think that's the crime. I've come to think it's the hope. Paper remembers, Mr. Halloran. Men forget, men die, men get promoted to Perth. Paper sits in a drawer and waits."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said nothing, which she appeared to find acceptable, possibly preferable.

She dealt out the folders like a card sharp. "Shell weights, *Adelaide Mary*. The *Coromandel*'s fuel accounts. And these—" her hand rested a moment on the thickest folder, "—are last quarter's diver tallies. Check them against the boat logs. If a figure disagrees with its source, mark it in red and say nothing to Quill. Bring disagreements to me."

"Why?" The word got out before I could stop it. Curiosity is another thing a man in my position isn't supposed to have, and I'd been starving it for years, but it had teeth, and sometimes it bit through.

Mrs. Adeyemi regarded me for a long moment. Outside, a winch groaned; somewhere below us the whole building shivered with the day's business.

"Because Quill rounds in the company's favor," she said at last, "every time, without exception, and a rounding error repeated four hundred times a quarter is not rounding, it is theft with good posture. Because the men those errors are stolen from can't read the columns they're named in. And because—" she paused, and I watched her decide how much of herself to spend on the next sentence, the way a careful woman counts coins, "—because twenty-two years ago I signed an indenture of my own, Mr. Halloran, in a port you've never heard of, and the man who held my paper is dead and forgotten, and I am still here, still counting. Somebody checked my figures once, early on. Just once. Just to see if they were right. It was the first time in my life anyone had bothered to ask whether I was right about anything." She squared the last folder to the table's edge with a small, exact snap. "Consider it repaid forward. Now work."

She walked away, gray dress and straight back, and left me sitting in the wreckage of my own composure.

* * *

I worked. Of course I worked. Whatever else that building made of us, it made us reliable, the way a mill makes flour.

But something had come loose in me, and I could feel it shifting around like a stone in a boot. I ran the *Adelaide Mary*'s shell weights twice and found them clean. The *Coromandel*'s fuel accounts had one discrepancy — eight gallons of kerosene entered twice, a small thing, eight gallons, hardly a hanging offense — but I marked it in red as instructed, and when I brought the folder to her desk Mrs. Adeyemi glanced at it, said "Good," and filed it in a drawer I noticed, without meaning to notice, locked.

It was the diver tallies that caught.

Boat log versus tally sheet, boat log versus tally sheet, all afternoon, the sun crawling across the window glass, and everything agreeing, agreeing, agreeing — right up until the *Lorelei*.

The *Lorelei*. Even the name of her put a taste in my mouth like copper. She was the boat my father had dived from, the last season, the year the storm came early and the company pushed the crews hard anyway because the shell prices were up and Voss wanted the hold full before the weather shut the beds. Everyone knew that story. It was practically scripture in Broome: bad season, brave boats, the sea takes what the sea wants. My father had gone down on the last dive of the last day and hadn't come up, and the verdict had been misadventure, and we'd buried an empty box because the sea keeps what it takes, and I had been fourteen, and within the month the company had taken his debts and folded them onto me like a man folding a flag, except flags get honored.

The *Lorelei*'s boat log, that final season — the one with my father's last entries in it — recorded her total shell takings at nineteen hundred lugs.

The quarterly tally sheet said two thousand one hundred.

Two hundred lugs of shell, gone between the boat and the books. Two hundred lugs, at that year's prices, was real money — wages, seasons, lives. And it wasn't an error. Errors go both directions. This went one way only, quietly, in the same round and patient hand that had priced me at thirty-eight pounds.

I sat looking at it for a long time. The light had gone amber. Down in the harbor the luggers were coming to anchor for the night, riding lights coming on one by one, little constellations pinned to black water.

I marked it in red. My hand was steady, which surprised me. Then I carried the folder to Mrs. Adeyemi's desk and put it down, and she opened it, and read, and went very still in a way I recognized, because I had been still the same way an hour earlier, holding a piece of paper that told me what I was worth.

"The *Lorelei*," she said quietly.

"My father's boat."

"I know." She closed the folder. For a moment she looked older, or perhaps just less armored. "Oscar." First time she'd used my name. It landed strangely, like a coin dropped in a quiet room. "This drawer locks. The discrepancies in it stay in it unless I put them elsewhere myself. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I saw nothing."

"That you saw nothing *yet*. There's a difference, and the difference is that someday — not today, I don't know when — there will be a right time to open this drawer, and when it comes, the paper will be waiting. Paper remembers." She locked the drawer. The key went somewhere into her bodice with the ease of long habit. "Until then, you are a boy who checks figures and finds them agreeable. Can you be that?"

Five years of practice said yes before I did.

"I can be that," I said.

"Good." She turned back to her own work, and I thought the audience was over, and I was halfway to the door when she spoke again, without looking up, her pen moving steadily. "Your mother sorted shell in the sheds her first season, you know. Before you were born. Fastest hands on the flats, they said. She used to check the shed tally sheets for the other women, unpaid, just so none of them got cheated blind."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. "You knew her."

"I knew of her," Mrs. Adeyemi said. The pen didn't stop. "In this town, knowing of Mara Halloran and knowing her were nearly the same thing. Good evening, Oscar. Mind the tide on your walk home."

I mind the tide. I mind it the way you mind a scar, automatically, without deciding to. But walking home along the wet sand with the lights of the town coming on behind me, I found I wasn't thinking about tides at all.

I was thinking about paper. About how it remembers, and who gets to read it, and what else might be sitting in locked drawers in that cream-colored handwriting — two hundred lugs missing from a drowned man's boat, a valuation against my name, and my mother's name, which I hadn't seen written down in six years, surfacing out of a stranger's memory like something rising slowly toward the light.

Behind me the counting house windows glowed, orderly and warm and full of secrets filed in triplicate.

I didn't know it yet, but three days later, a stranger would step off the mail boat onto the Broome jetty asking questions about the Halloran family — and everything I'd just learned about what paper remembers would stop being an abstraction forever.
