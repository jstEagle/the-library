## 3. A Stranger on the Jetty

The tide went out on a Thursday and left the jetty standing in mud like a man caught mid-stride, going nowhere.

I'd learned to read the tides the way other men read weather. Low water meant the luggers couldn't sail, which meant the divers came ashore, which meant the sorting sheds ran loud until dusk, which meant I could count on two hours at the counting house with nobody watching me do sums that weren't mine to do. Small freedoms. You learn to live inside them the way an oyster lives inside its shell—narrow, damp, and grateful for the walls.

I was hauling a crate of mother-of-pearl up the jetty planks when I saw him.

He came off the steamer tender like a man stepping into a room he'd been invited to years ago and had only now taken up the invitation. Not a tourist. Tourists arrived in Broome the way flies arrived at a butcher's shop—numerous, aimless, and quickly gone. This man carried one canvas bag and no luggage beyond it, and he walked the planks with the rolling gait of someone who'd spent more of his life on water than land and had made his peace with neither.

Weathered. That's the word my mind supplied first and refused to surrender. Weathered the way a fence post is weathered: grey, split in places, still standing. Fifty, perhaps older, though men who work the sea age unevenly, like houses in salt air. His shirt had been white once. His eyes were doing something I didn't like, which was moving.

They moved along the jetty, over the shell sheds, across the beach where the lugger crews mended nets in the shade of their hulls, and they did it with purpose. A man looking for a drink scans the town. A man looking for trouble scans the people. This one was scanning the people, and there was a patience to it that raised every hair I owned.

I turned my face to my crate and kept walking.

Here is a lesson they don't write in any ledger: in Broome, being noticed was a debt you paid for with interest. The Meridian Trading Company employed four hundred souls between the beach and the stockyards, and it knew each of them by number, weight, and output. What it paid less attention to was who we were when we weren't working—which suited me fine. Five years I'd been Oscar Halloran to nobody. To the company I was asset 4-1-9, pearl-shell division, and assets don't have mothers or drowned fathers or names that strangers might come asking after.

Asset 4-1-9 hauled his crate and thought about nothing at all, very loudly.

"Steady on," said a voice behind me, and I nearly dropped the lot.

It was the stranger. He'd crossed forty feet of jetty without my hearing him, which should have been impossible, and he was looking at me with his head cocked slightly, like a bird deciding whether a beetle was worth the effort.

"Heavy?" he asked.

"It's shell," I said. "It's always heavy."

That earned me half a smile. He had a face built for halves—a smile that stopped halfway, a squint that never committed fully to either friendliness or suspicion. Sun-cured leather for skin, a beard going iron-grey at the chin, hands like knotted rope. Somewhere under all that salt was a man who'd been young once, though you'd want a witness statement to prove it.

"You'd be the one to ask, then." He shifted his bag on his shoulder. "About shell."

"I sort it."

"Do you." He looked at my hands. I looked at his. Neither of us said anything clever.

A lugger's winch groaned somewhere behind us. The mudflats glittered, ugly and beautiful the way only tidal country manages—everything exposed, everything gleaming, nothing safe to stand on.

"Name's Reeve," he said. "Callum Reeve. Come in on the *Wattle*, out of—" he named a port I didn't catch, because I wasn't listening, because my blood had started doing arithmetic of its own. Reeve. Callum Reeve. I filed the name where I filed everything dangerous: nowhere at all, in a drawer that didn't exist.

"Oscar," I said. Just Oscar. First names cost nothing in this town; surnames were currency.

"Well, Oscar-just-Oscar." Another half-smile. "I'm looking for family. Hallorans."

The crate handle bit into my palm. I felt my face do what it had been trained to do over five long years, which was nothing. Voss himself had once told me I had the best blank face in the counting house, and he'd meant it as praise.

"Halloran," I repeated, as if tasting an unfamiliar dish. "Don't know it."

"No?"

"There are three thousand souls here and half of them pass through the sheds. Names go in one ear and out the other." I shifted the crate higher against my hip. "You might try the church registry. Or Mrs. Adeyemi in the office keeps records."

"Oh, aye, records." He said it lightly, but something moved behind his eyes—something that looked unpleasantly like recognition, though of what, I couldn't say. "Records. Very tidy, I expect."

"Tidier than most."

"And your own name? Just Oscar, is it? No family name attached?"

"Just Oscar."

He studied me then, properly, and I understood that whatever game we were playing, we were both playing it, and only one of us knew the stakes. There was a scar running through his left eyebrow that split it clean in two, and when he frowned—as he was frowning now—the halves pointed different directions, giving him the look of a man perpetually surprised and displeased about it.

"Just Oscar," he echoed. "Funny thing, names. My mother always said you can change what a man's called, but not what he is." He scratched his jaw. "She also said never trust a fellow who gives you his Christian name too quick. Says nothing about him except he wants something."

"What do you want?"

The question came out sharper than intended. His eyebrows—both halves—went up.

"Direct. Good. I like direct." He leaned in, just slightly, close enough that I could smell tar and old rope and beneath it something fainter, something almost like the smell of my childhood, which is a smell I can't describe and will never forget. "I want to find a woman called Mara Halloran. Or her kin. That's all. Kin."

The name went through me like a fishhook through soft flesh.

Mara. Nobody had spoken my mother's name aloud in five years—not since the parish priest had murmured it over the memorial service for the *Lorelei*'s lost, not since Aunt Ivy had drunk herself past the point of saying anyone's name but her own. It sat in my chest now, sudden and enormous, a bell rung in a dark room.

My face held. God help me, my face held.

"Don't know her either," I said.

"Don't know her." He nodded slowly. "Don't know Hallorans, don't know Mara. You're a hard man to help, Oscar-just-Oscar." He straightened, slung his bag again. "Well. I'll keep asking. Towns like this, somebody always remembers."

"Somebody usually does," I agreed, and heard my own voice come out steady as a clerk reading freight rates, and hated myself for the steadiness, and was proud of it too, both at once, which is a feeling I've grown expert in.

* * *

He didn't leave the jetty. That was the trouble.

I watched him from the counting house window while pretending to check invoices—Mrs. Adeyemi's territory, but she was at her midday meal, and the ledgers lay open under my hands like sleeping animals. Through the glass I saw Callum Reeve work his way down the beach, talking to net-menders, to the shell-sorters, to the Koepangers smoking by the boat ramp. Asking his questions. I saw heads shake. I saw shoulders shrug. And then I saw him stop at the sorting shed where Nell worked.

Nell. My sister, though she didn't know I counted her among my responsibilities, and wouldn't thank me if she did. She stood in the shed doorway with a shell knife in her hand and her chin up, fourteen years old and armored in scorn, and Callum Reeve said something to her, and she said something back that made him laugh—and then shake his head, slowly, like a man crossing a name off a list.

I should have felt relief. Instead I felt the particular sickness of a man watching a fire move toward a second building.

Because here's the thing about questions in Broome: they travel. A question asked on the jetty is a question asked in the pub by nightfall, and a question asked in the pub is a question laid on Voss's desk by breakfast. Gideon Voss missed nothing that happened within a mile of his harbor. Men had lost their berths for less than a stranger's curiosity. Men had lost more.

And if Reeve kept asking—if he said the name Halloran in enough ears—then sooner or later it would reach the one man in Broome who knew exactly what that name was worth, and what had been done to earn forgetting it.

I put down my pen. Picked it up. Put down my pen again.

"You'll wear a hole in the inkwell," said Mrs. Adeyemi, materializing beside me with her tea. She followed my gaze out the window, and her face did something complicated and unreadable. "Friend of yours?"

"Never seen him before."

"Mm." She sipped her tea. "He asked at the office this morning, before you were in. Wanted to see the shipping manifests for 'eighty-eight. The drowning year." My pen stopped moving entirely. "I told him manifests were confidential. He took it well. Charming fellow, in a battered sort of way." She looked at me over the rim of her cup. "He mentioned a name. Halloran."

The office clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a winch screamed.

"Common enough name," I managed.

"Is it?" said Mrs. Adeyemi, and there was something in her voice I couldn't read—kindness, maybe, or warning, or the strange third thing that is both at once. "In Broome, I find, no name is as common as it looks. Drink your tea, Oscar. You've gone grey around the mouth."

* * *

I finished the day the way I finished every day: invisibly. I returned the ledgers, swept the floor, banked the lamp, and walked home along the beach below the high-tide line, where the sand holds footprints only briefly and the company's eyes grow tired.

Reeve found me anyway.

Of course he did. I should have known—he'd tracked me once already across forty feet of jetty unheard, and a man with that talent doesn't lose a target who walks the same route home every evening because variety is a luxury assets can't afford. I heard his boots on the sand behind me and my whole body clenched before my mind caught up.

"Evening, Oscar-just-Oscar."

"Go away."

"Now, that's no way to treat—" He fell into step beside me, unbothered, hands in pockets. The tide was coming in behind us, patient as a creditor. "I'll be plain with you, since you're plainly a plain-spoken lad. I asked after Hallorans all day. Got shrugs, got lies, got one old Malay fellow who spat at my boots, which I respect. And then tonight I watched you walk this beach like a man carrying something breakable, and I thought: there's a fellow who knows the name and wishes he didn't."

"You thought wrong."

"Did I." He let it hang. We walked. The sun was going down over the water in that extravagant way it has here, spending color like a drunken heir. "Your hands shook on that jetty today."

"My hands did not shake."

"They shook. Only a little. Only a man watching would've seen it, and only a man who's watched men hear bad news for thirty years would know what it means." He stopped walking. I kept walking, then stopped too, because stopping was the lesser cowardice. "I knew a woman once," he said quietly. "Mara Halloran. Best hand with a ship's needle I ever saw—could patch a mainsail so neat you'd swear the tear had been a fashion choice. Shipmate of mine, years back. And she had a son she talked about the way sailors talk about home ports they can't get back to. Called him Osc."

The beach was empty. The water whispered its endless bookkeeping. My heart was making so much noise I was sure he could hear it.

"Lots of boys called Osc," I said.

"Aye. And lots of women called Mara." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than menace. "But how many of them had a husband dove for the Meridian Company and drowned in the storm of 'eighty-eight? How many of them vanished off the passenger rolls the same season? You see, Oscar, I've been doing sums too. Sailors do sums. We do them with lives instead of pounds."

"You're wrong."

"Likely. Likely I am." He spread his hands, palms open, an honest man's gesture, or a practiced one—I genuinely couldn't tell, and that frightened me more than anything else that day. "But here's what I'll do. I'll keep asking. Tomorrow, next day. The pub, the church, the office. Somebody in this town remembers the Hallorans, and I'll find them, because I promised a dying woman I would, and I don't break promises to dying women." He turned to go, then paused. "Unless somebody tells me to stop."

"Nobody's telling you anything."

"No," he said. "More's the pity."

He walked away down the beach, and I stood in the wet sand and watched him shrink against the burning horizon, and felt the two halves of myself pull in opposite directions like a tug-of-war over a rotten rope.

One half wanted to shout my name after him. *Halloran. I'm Oscar Halloran. My mother is alive.* Five years of silence pressed against the backs of my teeth like a tide against a door.

The other half—the half that had kept me fed and breathing since I was twelve—did its cold arithmetic instead. A stranger asking about Hallorans was a stranger painting a target. On me. On Nell. On whatever fragile secret he carried in that canvas bag. And if Voss heard the name—and Voss would hear the name, because questions in Broome travel faster than ships—then everything my silence had protected would be worth protecting still, only harder.

So I did what I had trained myself five years to do. I erased.

I walked home the long way, and by the time I reached my door I had done it thoroughly: Oscar Halloran folded up small and tucked away, asset 4-1-9 dusted off and set forward, the name Mara locked in the drawer that didn't exist. By morning I would be able to pass Reeve on the jetty and give him nothing but a nod and a stranger's blank politeness. I'd done it before, with grief. I could do it again.

What I couldn't account for—what I lay awake cataloguing like a bad audit—was the letter-shaped bulge I'd glimpsed in that canvas bag when the wind pressed it flat against his side. An envelope. Thick. Sealed. Carried close, the way a sailor carries the one thing on shore he can't afford to lose.

Or the way a courier carries mail that's six years late.

I stared at the ceiling until the moon moved, doing sums I didn't want to finish, arriving at answers I didn't dare sign my name to.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would avoid the jetty entirely.

Tomorrow, the tide would bring him to my door.
