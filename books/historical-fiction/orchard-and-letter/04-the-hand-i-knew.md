## 4. The Hand I Knew

There are three kinds of guilt a sailor carries, and I've had my belly full of all of them. There's the small kind — the shore you left too soon, the mate you didn't wake for his watch. Then there's the medium kind, the sort that sits in your chest like ballast and makes every drink taste of apology. And then there's the kind that comes with a dying woman gripping your wrist hard enough to leave bruises, saying *find him*, and you saying *aye* because you'd have said aye to anything she asked, and then spending four years finding every excuse under God's sky not to keep the promise.

I'm Callum Reeve, forty-one years old, and the third kind has been riding with me since Singapore.

I came into Broome on the *Kestrel's Fortune*, a tramp steamer with more rust than hull and a captain who prayed loudly to a god he clearly didn't believe in. Six weeks' stopover while they patched her plates. Six weeks. I want it noted, somewhere in the great ledger of things, that I did not choose six weeks. The ship chose them, or the shipwrights did, or possibly the rust. A man can only be responsible for so much.

The pearling town hit me like a wall of heat and brine and money changing hands. Roebuck Bay glittered like someone had spilled a jeweller's tray across the mudflats, and everywhere there were men — white men in good coats counting, brown men diving and dying, and everyone pretending this was simply how the world was arranged. I'd seen the arrangement before. I'd been part of the arrangement before, if I'm honest, back when I was young and thought a fair day's work was whatever the paymaster said it was.

But I wasn't here for pearls. I was here for a boy named Oscar Halloran.

* * *

Here is what Mara Halloran made me swear, on the night of the twelfth of March, four years past, in a boarding house room in Singapore that smelled of camphor and rain:

She made me swear I'd find her son. She made me swear I'd tell him the truth — all of it, even the parts that would make him hate her. And she made me swear I wouldn't post the letters, because posted letters could be intercepted, and intercepted letters could get people killed. She knew whose people might do the intercepting. She'd married into that knowledge.

"You'll know him," she said. Her voice was already thin as rice paper by then; the fever had been working through her for a month and the doctors had given up charging her for visits. "He'll have my eyes. Everyone else in that family has eyes like river water. He'll have mine."

"And what colour are yours?" I asked, because I am sometimes a fool at exactly the wrong moment.

She laughed — a proper laugh, the laugh I remembered from the *Lorelei*'s deck twenty years gone, when she was the harbormaster's daughter playing chess against half the crew and beating them all — and then the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that I poured her the last of the brandy I'd brought and hated myself for drinking most of it on the walk over.

"Grey," she said finally. "Grey like weather coming."

Then she gave me the packet. Oilcloth-wrapped, tied with tarred string, heavy as a sin. Letters. Nine of them, written across four years, addressed to "My Osc" in a hand that slanted right and pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. She'd written them knowing they might never be delivered. That's either hope or madness, and I've never decided which, and I don't think she had either.

"There's one at the bottom," she said. "The last one. Don't give him that one until he knows everything else. Not because it isn't true. Because it needs to be the last thing he learns, or none of the rest will make sense to him."

"What's in it?"

"That's between a mother and her son." Her grip found my wrist again. "Callum. Swear it properly. Not sailor's-swearing. Real swearing."

So I swore it properly, kneeling by a cot in a hot room while the monsoon hammered the shutters, and two days later I stood at her grave in the Protestant cemetery off Bukit Timah Road and felt the whole weight of the thing settle onto my shoulders like a wet sail.

And then — and here is where the guilt gets its hooks in — I went to sea for four years instead.

Not immediately. First I tried. That has to count for something, though I notice it counts for less every year. I wrote to Broome. Three letters over eighteen months, asking after a Halloran family, and got back nothing but silence. Later I understood why: you didn't ask questions about Hallorans in Broome unless you wanted Gideon Voss hearing about it, and Voss's Broome was a town where mail went missing and strangers got short shrift and accidents happened to the curious. But I didn't understand it then. I just took the silence as an answer. Boy grown and gone, probably. Or dead like his father. Or never existed, and I'd built a promise around a ghost.

That's what I told myself, anyway. The truth is simpler and worse: telling a man's mother you'll find him is easy. Finding him means arriving somewhere, and knocking on doors, and being seen, and being seen meant being *seen*, and Mara had filled my ears with enough about Gideon Voss that I'd developed a healthy terror of the man without ever laying eyes on him. So I let the trade winds decide, and the trade winds are cowards' allies, always blowing you somewhere else.

It was a barmaid in Batavia who shamed me straight, eventually. I'd had too much arrack and told her the story — sailors do, we're all walking ballads — and she looked at me with the flat contempt only a working woman can manage and said, "Four years? You rowed her across the Styx yourself, did you, and now you're resting your arms?"

I booked passage north within the week.

* * *

Broome at dusk, from the jetty, is beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. The tide goes out a mile and leaves the luggers leaning on the mud like tired horses. The shell sheds breathe their stink of rot and lime. Chinatown lights up, and the pearlers' masters' houses sit on the hill above it all like fat cats on a fence, and down on the flats the divers' camps huddle in the dark where nobody important has to look at them.

I spent three days watching before I spoke to anyone. Old habits — well, new habits, learned the hard way in ports where asking the wrong question cost teeth. I watched the company office, a solid stone building with MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY cut into the lintel and a flag flying out front that seemed to suggest the company considered itself a nation. I watched the harbor master come down to inspect a lading. Gideon Voss. I'd expected a brute. What I got was worse: a tall, silvering man with a scholar's stoop and a smile like a locked drawer, who shook hands with divers' masters and called each of them by name and laughed at the right moments, and I watched two young divers near him stand the way men stand near a snake they can't quite see.

Mara had described him once, in one of her lucid stretches near the end. *"Voss was my father's clerk when I was a girl. He used to bring me oranges. He's the most courteous man I ever met, and the only one I ever feared, and I grew up at sea among cutthroats."*

I saw enough in ten minutes to believe her.

On the fourth day I stopped watching and started asking, carefully, sideways, the way you test ice. A Malay boatman who shrugged. A publican who squinted. An old beachcomber on Dampier Creek who said, "Halloran? Drowned fella, years back. And the wife lost at sea, poor soul. No Hallorans now." He said it smoothly, easily, the way a man repeats something he's been given to repeat.

That was the moment the itch started at the back of my neck. I've been lied to by professionals in a dozen ports, and I know the sound of a lie that's been rehearsed by committee. *Lost at sea.* Said too fast, too complete, no grief in it at all.

The fifth day, I found the boy. Or rather, I found him and then lost my nerve, which I record here in the interests of honesty, since nobody else will vouch for me.

He was coming along the jetty road at dawn, ahead of the shell-sorting shift, and I knew him before I knew how I knew him. Small for his years — fifteen or sixteen, still growing into hands that were already a working man's hands — with a way of walking close to fences, close to walls, close to the edge of things, like a man perpetually stepping around some larger person's temper. Dark hair. Shoulders already curved toward the habit of looking down.

And when he passed me — close enough that I could have reached out and touched him — he glanced up, and I saw her eyes. Grey like weather coming.

I opened my mouth to say *Oscar* and what came out was nothing at all, because at that exact moment a company man stepped out of the office doorway behind us, and the boy flinched — a small, fast, practiced flinch, the flinch of a creature that has learned what attention costs — and something cold walked down my spine and closed my throat for me.

I let him walk past. Then I cursed myself roundly in three languages, bought a coffee I didn't want from a Chinese cookshop, and sat with my hands shaking around the cup like an old woman.

Because here's the thing I understood, sitting there: the boy wasn't hiding from me. He was hiding from everything. Five years in Voss's town will teach a child that silence is a coat you wear. And I was about to walk up to him on a public street and say *your mother is alive* — words that would travel through Broome faster than fire through a hay barn, straight back to the stone office and its courteous, smiling master.

Mara hadn't just asked me to find her son. She'd asked me to find him *without getting him killed*. That was the part of the swearing I'd been skipping over for four years, and now it stood up and introduced itself.

* * *

I spent two more days learning the shape of the town, and the shape was this: everything ran through the Meridian. The stores, the contracts, the berths on departing ships, the church warden's accounts, the police sergeant's drinking money. Voss didn't rule Broome the way a strongman rules; he ruled it the way a tide rules, patient and total, so that men born free lived as though freedom were simply something that happened elsewhere. Half the divers were indentured — debt-bonded, contract-bound, call it what you like, the chains had just gotten lighter and longer. And the name Halloran appeared nowhere in any conversation except as drowned-and-departed history, which told me the erasure had been thorough and deliberate.

Thorough and deliberate is expensive. Men don't spend that kind of effort on nobodies. Which meant the Hallorans had mattered to Voss, mattering usually means money, and money in Broome meant pearls.

I'm not a clever man, but I've spent thirty years around cargo manifests, and I know what it looks like when a ledger and reality disagree.

The sixth evening, the tide did me a kindness. Low water after dark, and I went out along the mudflats below the town because I couldn't bear another hour of the pub's chatter, and that's where the boy was — crouched at the tide line with a lantern, gathering bait or shells or maybe just air, the way lonely people gather air.

I came up slow, hands visible, talking before I was close enough to scare him. "Evening. Tide's generous tonight."

He went still the way wild things go still. Lantern tilted, weighing whether to run. Up close he looked younger and older at once — a face doing the work of a man on a boy's frame.

"I'm not company," I said. "I'm not police. My name's Reeve. Callum Reeve, late of the *Lorelei*, out of Fremantle and points east." I watched the name land. It landed nowhere — or rather, it landed somewhere deep, because his breathing changed, a hitch small as a fish turning under water. He'd heard the word *Lorelei* before. Somewhere. From someone. In a voice he'd spent years trying not to forget.

"You've got the wrong man," he said. Flat. Rehearsed. "Don't know any Lorelei."

"Aye," I said. "You do, though. You knew it before you finished saying you didn't."

Silence. Out on the flats, the luggers lay on their sides in the moonlight like sleeping cattle. Somewhere behind us, town noise, faint.

"I've come a long way," I said, "to find Oscar Halloran. And I'll say plainly, so there's no mistake: I mean him no harm, and I'll not speak his name loud, and if he tells me to walk away, I'll walk away. But I'd be failing a dead woman — a woman who is *not* dead, which is the whole point — if I walked away without showing him one thing first."

I saw the sentence hit him. All of it at once, like a wave over a low deck. His lips moved. Nothing came out.

I unwrapped my bundle slowly, letting him see every movement, and took out the top letter — oilcloth worn soft from four years of my handling, tarred string retied a hundred times. I held it out at arm's length, paper catching the moonlight, and turned it so he could read the direction written across the front in that hard-slanting hand:

*For my Osc, when he is found.*

"My Osc," I said quietly. "That's you, lad. That's been you all along. Your mother wrote this four years after the sea was supposed to have taken her. She wrote it in a room in Singapore with me sitting beside her, and the last thing she did on this earth was make me promise to put it in your hands."

His hand came up — shaking, I could see it shake even at arm's length — and stopped an inch from the paper, hovering, the way you hover over a stove you've been burned by.

"She's alive," he said. Not a question. Not a statement either. Something between, raw as an open wound.

"She was alive in March, four years past, and sharp-tongued to the end, and she beat me at chess the week before she died, and she made me swear on holy ground I'd find you." I kept my voice steady. Inside, four years of cowardice were dissolving into something hotter. "I'm late, lad. I know I'm late. Hate me for it if you need to — I've earned it. But read the letter. Read it tonight, somewhere private, and then find me tomorrow, and I'll tell you everything else. Everything. Including the parts about this town that you deserve to hear from a friend before you hear them from an enemy."

He took the letter.

He held it like it was live coral, like it might sting, like it was the most fragile and the heaviest object ever carried by human hands. And then he looked up at me with his mother's grey storm-coming eyes, and I watched five years of practiced silence crack down the middle like ice in spring thaw.

"The jetty stairs," he whispered. "Tomorrow. After the sorting bell. There's a gap in the pilings where nobody—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. Even now, even shattered, he was checking who might hear. God help us both, Voss had trained him well. "Sunset," he amended. "Come at sunset."

"Aye," I said. "Sunset."

He tucked the letter inside his shirt, against his skin, and walked away fast along the tide line, and I stood alone on the flats with the mud sucking gently at my boots and the whole town of Broome glittering its lying glitter behind me, and I thought: *Mara, old friend, I've done it. He has your hand in his keeping now.*

And then, because I'm a sailor and therefore constitutionally incapable of unclouded joy, I thought: *Now the hard part begins.* Because a boy with a letter is a boy with a secret, and secrets in Gideon Voss's town have a way of surfacing — and I'd seen the harbor master smile, and I'd seen what stood near him, and I knew with a diver's certainty that somewhere in that polite stone office, the water was already beginning to move.

I just didn't yet know how fast it would rise.
