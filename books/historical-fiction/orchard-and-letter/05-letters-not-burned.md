## 5. Letters Not Burned

The tide goes out in Broome like a man clearing his throat before saying something difficult. It takes the water with it for a mile or more, leaving the mudflats shining and exposed, and every soul in town knows you don't walk out there unless you have business with the crabs or the oysters or your own conscience. The flats are honest that way. They show you everything the sea has been hiding.

I had business of all three kinds, though I'd have told you only about the oysters.

It was a Thursday, and I'd taken to walking the low-tide line after my shift because it was the one hour of the day nobody owned. Not Meridian, not the sheds, not the barracks where the other indentured men snored like a tide of their own. Just me, the mud sucking at my boots, and the gulls arguing overhead about whose fault everything was. I'd learned to count my life in these hours the way Mrs. Adeyemi counted hers in ledger lines—one honest column against all the false ones.

So when I heard footsteps behind me on the wet sand, out where no respectable person walked and no careless one survived, I knew before I turned that it was him.

"You walk like a man being followed," Callum Reeve said.

"I walk like a man who's been taught what happens to men who stop."

He came around to face me, and up close he was worse than on the jetty—older, salt-cured, with a face like a map of somewhere violent. His eyes had that particular patience sailors get when they've waited out weather worse than anything a boy can threaten them with.

"You denied knowing me," he said. "On the jetty. Loud enough for three men unloading wool to hear."

"Loud enough for more than three."

"Aye." He didn't smile, but something in his face shifted, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to break. "That's why I'm here and not at your door. I watched you deny me, Oscar Halloran, and I thought: there's a lad who's learned his lessons well. There's a lad somebody trained like a dog learns not to bark at the wrong knock."

"Don't call me that name."

"Which? Oscar?"

"The other one."

"Halloran?" He said it softly, testing it, like a man pressing on a bruise to see how deep it went. "Your mother's name. Your father's name. The name on your christening record, if records still exist for Hallorans in this town, which we'll come to."

I looked past him at the horizon, where the tide was already beginning its slow, patient return. "You should go back to whatever ship brought you. Broome eats strangers slower than it eats the rest of us, but it gets there."

"It ate your father whole," he said. "And spat out a verdict of misadventure."

The mud seemed to go cold under my feet. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"No," he agreed, terribly reasonable. "I know exactly what I'm talking about. That's the problem with me. Always has been." He reached inside his coat, slow, deliberate, the way you move around a frightened animal—and God help me, I was the frightened animal, five years of silence sitting on my chest like ballast. "I'm going to show you something now, lad, and you're going to want to throw it in the sea. Don't. It's been in enough water."

* * *

It was oilcloth-wrapped, the way sailors wrap things they mean to survive, and tied with twine gone soft from handling. He peeled it open with fingers that were steadier than mine would ever be again, and what lay inside was paper. Letters. A bundle of them, thin as a dead man's hair, bound together with the kind of care that costs the binder something.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked.

"Paper."

"Papers that weren't burned." He held the bundle out across the space between us, and the wind off the mudflats stirred the topmost page like it wanted to read it first. "There was a time when letters to you and your family started disappearing. You remember that. Or maybe you don't—you were young, and grief makes a fog of everything. But your mother wrote. From Singapore, she wrote, year after year, and year after year nothing came. Because someone in this town was intercepting them. Someone decided the Halloran family ought to be a closed account."

"That's a lie." My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "She died. The *Sylph* went down off Rottnest. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they were told." He let that sit between us while a gull screamed about something. "Mara Halloran didn't drown, lad. She never set foot on the Sylph. And these—" he shook the bundle gently, "—are four years of her asking why her son doesn't write back."

I don't remember taking the letters. I remember the oilcloth being cold and damp against my palms, and I remember thinking, distantly, that my hands had stopped belonging to me. They were shaking so hard the pages chattered like teeth.

The handwriting hit me before the words did. That forward lean, the tall ascenders, the little hook she put on her capitals—I'd have known it in the dark, in my sleep, ten years from now. I used to practice it as a boy, copying her grocery lists into the dirt until she caught me and laughed and said I'd be a clerk yet, God help the company. Her hand. Unmistakable. Impossible. Dated—my eyes went to the dates and stuck there like flies to paper—the eighth of March, 1889. Two years after the sea was supposed to have taken her.

"*My Osc,*" the letter began, and the world tilted sideways on its axis and did not right itself.

I couldn't read fast. My eyes kept snagging and sliding, the way they do when the thing you're reading is too large for the space behind them. *My Osc. Four letters now returned to me, and I begin to think the sea between us has grown teeth. If you receive this, write to me at the address below, or better, tell me plainly if you do not wish to, and I will trouble you no more—but say it in your own hand, for I could not bear to learn it second-hand.*

"She thinks I didn't answer," I heard myself say. "She thinks I read them and chose not to—"

"She thinks the worst because silence is the worst," Callum said quietly. "Four years, lad. She wrote forty-some letters by my count. I carry the ones she gave me when I promised. The rest went where the others went."

"Where did the others go?" I asked, and then answered myself, because I'd spent five years learning which silences kept me safe, and suddenly every one of those silences rearranged itself into a shape I recognized. "Voss."

Callum didn't confirm it. He didn't need to. Confirmation was written all over my face, and he watched it arrive there like a man watching weather come in.

"You suspected," he said.

"I suspected nothing. Suspecting is a luxury." I looked down at the letter again, at *my Osc*, and felt something crack in my chest—not cleanly, not like breaking bone, but the way a dam cracks, with water already moving through the fault. Five years I had built my survival on a simple arithmetic: my mother was dead, the sea took her, the company was indifferent but not malicious, and my job was to be useful and quiet until my indenture ran out and I could become some smaller, safer version of myself. Every piece of that arithmetic had just been entered into evidence and found fraudulent.

"Why you?" I managed. "Why does a stranger carry my mother's letters across two seas?"

"Because a dying woman made me promise." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Singapore. Fever ward. She wasn't dying, mind—she got better, which she'll tell you herself with more humor than I can manage—but she thought she was, and fever makes honest men of everyone. She grabbed my sleeve and said: Callum Reeve, you're a worthless man but you keep your word, find my Osc and tell him I never stopped writing. Find my Osc and tell him none of this was abandonment."

"And did you tell her you'd wait four years to sail here?"

"I told her I'd come when I could afford the passage." Something moved behind his eyes—guilt, old and well-fed. "Worthless men are also poor men, lad. But I'm here now. And there's more than letters in this bundle."

* * *

There was, and it was the more that undid me.

Beneath the letters was a photograph, stiff and foxed at the corners: a woman standing outside a shopfront with painted signage, holding a child on her hip. The woman was thinner than the mother in my memory, harder around the mouth, but she stood the same way—weight on one hip, chin up, daring the camera to make something of it. Mara Halloran, defying photography itself.

And the child. The child had my father's dark hair and my mother's stubborn chin, and she was perhaps three years old, and she was looking directly into the lens as if she already distrusted it.

"Nell," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. The name came out of me like something escaping a locked room.

"You know her."

"My sister." The word felt foreign and enormous. "Half-sister. She'd have been a baby when—" When our mother fled, I almost said, and caught myself on the edge of that word, *fled*, because it implied things. Choices. Reasons. A Sylph that sailed without her. "She works in the shell sheds. In Broome. Now."

Callum went very still, the way men do when they realize they've stepped onto ground that might not hold. "Then your mother believes both her children are with her aunt. She writes to Nell too—or tried. Those letters went the same way yours went."

The tide was coming back in. I could see it far out on the flats, a gray seam of water widening with terrible patience, and I knew we had maybe twenty minutes before the channel filled and walking became swimming. I should have cared. Some part of me noted it the way you note a clock ticking in another room.

I sat down on the mud. Right down, boots and all, because my legs had resigned their commission without informing me. Callum, to his credit, sat down beside me like it was a thing men did, and we sat there in the enormous wet silence with four years of my mother's voice in my lap.

"She asks about me," I said finally. "In every one."

"In every one. 'Has my Osc grown.' 'Is my Osc eating.' 'Tell my Osc the mangoes here are nothing like home.'" He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "She talks about mangoes the way other women talk about scripture."

"She always wanted an orchard." The memory surfaced whole, intact, untouched by five years of careful drowning: my mother under the fig tree behind our first house, driving a stake into the ground and telling me, *A tree is a promise you make to people who aren't born yet.* I'd forgotten I remembered that. Grief makes a fog of everything; apparently it keeps a few things in glass jars too. "She said she'd plant one when we had land of our own. Somewhere the company couldn't take it back."

"Well," Callum said, "there's land in Singapore. Small plot, behind a rooming house she keeps with another widow. She plants what she can in pots. Mangoes won't take in pots. She'll tell you that at tedious length."

I laughed. It came out of me ragged and cracked, half sob, entirely involuntary, and once it started I couldn't stop it—laughing at mangoes in pots, laughing at the sheer obscene size of it, my mother alive and opinionated and planting things in containers on the far side of an ocean while I sorted pearls for the men who had erased her. I laughed until my eyes stung, and Callum sat beside me and let me, watching the tide come in with the patience of a man who had carried a promise across two seas and could carry it a while longer.

When it was out of me, I wiped my face with a muddy sleeve, which my mother would have scolded me for, which made my eyes sting again.

"There's a ship," Callum said, when he judged me capable of hearing it. "The Sarawak Star. Mail steamer, out of Fremantle, calls here in six weeks. There's a berth aboard—assisted passage, subsidized, for verified kin of residents abroad. One berth." He paused, and I felt him choosing his next words with care. "Proving kinship means papers, lad. Parish records. Company registry. And we both know whose thumb sits on those."

Six weeks. Papers. Voss's thumb. Even cracked open and half-drowned in hope, some cold surviving part of me did the sums immediately and didn't like any of them. But that cold part sounded very far away just then, because the letter was still in my hands, and my mother's handwriting was still leaning forward off the page, and she was still asking, in ink two years dry, whether her Osc was eating.

"Read me the last one," I said.

Callum hesitated. "It's long."

"Read me the last one."

He took it, unfolded it with a reverence he hadn't shown the others, and read. And near the end, in a paragraph about tides and mango trees and a daughter growing too fast, my mother had written a line that Callum's rough voice stumbled over:

*"And if my Osc cannot come to me, tell him the orchard waits anyway. Trees keep. Mothers keep longer."*

The water was lapping at our boots now. Out on the flats, the crabs were making their decisions about higher ground.

"Six weeks," I said.

"Six weeks."

I stood, and my legs held, mostly, and I wrapped those letters back in their oilcloth with hands that had almost stopped shaking—almost, because some trembling you don't want to stop; it's proof the thing in your chest is alive and not just beating out of habit.

"Keep them safe," I said. "Not on you. Voss has eyes everywhere, and if he learns what you carry, he'll burn them for certain this time. Bury them somewhere only you and I know, and tell me where when this is over. And Callum—" I made myself look at him, made myself say the true thing instead of the safe one. "Thank you. For keeping your word. Even if it took four years and cost you whatever peace you had."

"Worthless men keep their word," he said. "It's the only currency we've got."

We walked back toward the town across the filling flats, two silhouettes against a rising sea, and I carried my mother's last letter folded against my chest where the oilcloth had warmed it. Six weeks. One berth. A harbor master with a ledger full of lies and a registry with my name filed under assets.

But my mother kept longer than trees. And for the first time in five years, I intended to test just how long a silenced son could keep too.
