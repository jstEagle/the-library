## 6. What the Company Owns

There is a particular pleasure in the first hour of the morning at the harbor office, before the clerks arrive and the day begins its usual assault on one's patience. I take my tea at the window that overlooks the jetty, and I watch the water do what water does—nothing, beautifully, for hours at a stretch—and I remind myself that all of this is mine to keep orderly. Not mine by deed; the Meridian Trading Company holds the deeds. Mine by function. A man who keeps a ledger balanced owns, in every way that matters, whatever the ledger describes.

I had been harbor master of Broome for eleven years by that October, and I flatter myself that I knew the town the way a surgeon knows a body: where it was sound, where it was rotten, and precisely which incisions could be made without waking the patient.

The patient woke that morning at half past nine, when Tom Brack came up the stairs with his hat in his hands and his face arranged into the expression he believed passed for neutrality. Brack has many qualities I find useful—discretion, physicality, an imagination so limited it might charitably be called loyalty—but subtlety is not among them. When Brack is worried, he looks like a man carrying a full chamber pot across a ballroom.

"Morning, Mr. Voss," he said.

"Brack." I did not look up from the manifest I was annotating. There is a technique to making a subordinate wait, and the technique consists chiefly of having something genuinely interesting to read. The manifest was not interesting—it was the usual cargo of sandalwood and mother-of-pearl shell—but I gave it my fullest attention regardless. "You've brought me your hat."

"Sir?"

"You're holding it like a petition." I set down my pen. "Sit, if you're going to loom. Looming is for churches."

He sat. He turned the hat in his hands twice around, which for Brack constituted visible distress.

"There's a stranger in town," he said. "Came in on the *Kestrel* Tuesday. Big fellow, weathered, hands like rope. Been asking round the sheds and the pubs after a family name."

"What name?"

"Halloran."

I will confess here, since no one is reading over my shoulder and the tea has long gone cold, that there was a moment—a single, unlovely heartbeat—in which the morning's pleasant order tilted beneath me. It passed quickly. It always passes quickly. That is the great advantage of practice: fear becomes a thing one notes and files, like a discrepancy in a column, to be addressed in due course rather than suffered in the moment.

"Halloran," I repeated, as though tasting an unfamiliar wine. "Diver family, isn't it? The father drowned some years back. Misadventure, if I recall the verdict."

"That's the one. And there's a boy still on the books—Oscar Halloran, indentured, five years served. Works the sorting room." Brack leaned forward. "But that's not what's strange, sir. This fellow isn't asking about the boy. He's asking about the *mother*. Mara Halloran. Whether anyone remembers her, whether she had people here, whether—" he consulted a scrap of paper he'd written nothing useful on— "whether she 'ever spoke of Singapore.'"

I let the silence sit between us for the length of time it takes a slow man to count to five.

"Well," I said at last, pleasantly, "that *is* curious. A woman dead these six years, and a stranger arrives to ask after her opinions on tropical ports. Either he is a sentimentalist of unusual dedication, or he is something else entirely."

"You think she's alive, then?"

"I think," I said, rising to refill my cup, "that the question is not whether Mara Halloran breathes, but who wants to know, and why now, and what they imagine knowing will buy them. Sentiment does not cross an ocean, Brack. Men cross oceans for money, for revenge, or for leverage. Occasionally for love, but love, in my experience, rarely asks after shipping schedules."

I said that last part lightly, watching him. Brack knows nothing of the Lorelei. No one living knows everything of the Lorelei—that is rather the point of how I arranged matters—but a man in my position learns to test the walls of his own house regularly, tapping them for hollows.

"The stranger give a name?" I asked.

"Reeve. Callum Reeve. Says he's a salvager, out of Thursday Island. But the *Kestrel*'s manifest lists no passenger of that name under any trade but 'supercargo,' and supercargoes don't generally go trawling public houses for a dead woman's gossip."

"No," I agreed. "They go trawling for cargo. Which raises the interesting possibility that our Mr. Reeve considers a woman to be cargo." I returned to the window. Below, the tide was making, sliding green and patient over the flats, and the divers' luggers rode their moorings like patient animals. Everything in order. Everything described, accounted, owned. "Where is he lodging?"

"The Continental. Pays cash. Keeps odd hours."

"They always do. Brack, I want two things, and I want them done quietly, because quiet is the entire art of this. First: eyes on him. Where he walks, whom he speaks to, how long he speaks to them, and whether he drinks enough to loosen anything worth hearing. Do not approach him. Do not let him see you twice in the same place wearing the same face. You have several faces; deploy them."

"And second?"

"Second, I want to know if he's spoken to the boy. Oscar Halloran." I said the name the way one reads a line item. "If a stranger comes asking about a family, sooner or later he'll come to the surviving member. If he reaches the boy before we understand his purpose, I want to know within the hour. Within the half-hour, if you can manage it without running, because running attracts attention, and attention, Brack, is the one commodity in this town I refuse to let circulate freely."

He nodded, relieved to have instructions, and stood to go.

"Brack." He paused at the door. "One more thing. The boy himself. Is he behaving?"

"Behaving, sir?"

"Is he dull? Obedient? Does he sort his pearls and keep his mouth shut and thank God or whoever for his keep?" I smiled at the window glass, at my own reflection superimposed on the harbor. "I ask because dull men are the foundation of any well-run enterprise, and I should hate to discover a crack in the foundation."

"Dull as ditchwater, far as anyone says. Never gives trouble. Mrs. Adeyemi has him doing the manifests sometimes—says he's got a head for figures."

Ah. Mrs. Adeyemi. I made a small note of that, mentally, in the ledger I keep behind my eyes. Adeyemi is competent past all reason and loyal past all accounting, and I have never entirely determined what holds her loyalty together, which means I have never entirely trusted it. Competence without comprehensible motive is like a tide chart drawn by someone else: accurate, perhaps, but whose interests does the accuracy serve?

"Good," I said aloud. "Keep it that way. And Brack—if our Mr. Reeve should happen to speak to the boy, I want the substance of it, word for word if possible. Not rumor. Substance."

He went. I finished my tea, which had gone bitter, and considered the day.

* * *

Let me say something in my own defense, since history, if it ever bothers to record me at all, will certainly not bother to be fair. People imagine that a man who does what I have done must be a monster, all appetite and cruelty, some panting villain from a stage melodrama twirling his mustache over the drowning of divers. Nothing could be further from the truth. I am a reasonable man. Every decision I have ever made seemed, at the moment of making it, the only decision available to a person in my circumstances—which is, I suspect, the universal experience of everyone who has ever done anything, honorable or otherwise.

Consider the arithmetic honestly. In 1888 the Lorelei came up from the deep-water grounds with a catch that would have remade the company's year, and the company's year, I will remind you, is not an abstraction—it is wages, it is the Sydney office's confidence, it is my own position and pension and the whole architecture of a life built carefully over twenty years. The catch was extraordinary. Extraordinary catches attract extraordinary attention: auditors, partners, rivals, questions. Questions are the termites of commerce. One cannot answer them all, and so one builds differently—one builds so that no one thinks to ask.

Did men die? Yes. Divers die. That is the trade. The sea takes them, storms take them, the bends take them, greed takes them—the coroner writes *misadventure* in every case because the coroner understands, as all sensible men in company towns understand, whose bread feeds his table. I did not invent the arrangement. I merely conducted it, competently, as I conduct everything.

And the woman—Mara Halloran. People will say I drove her out, hunted her, threatened her children. What I did was present her with a clear-eyed account of her situation, which is more honesty than most people receive in a lifetime. She had seen too much and mattered too little, and I offered her passage and silence and a future in which her children grew up unmarked by their parents' knowledge. She took it. Mothers take such bargains every day, all over the world, and no one calls *them* monsters. They call them practical.

I have never lost sleep over it. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own skull: I have never once lost sleep. Sleeplessness is for men who doubt their own bookkeeping.

* * *

By afternoon the reports began arriving, as reports do when one has set a machine in motion—small, fragmentary, each one useless alone and useful in aggregate, like grains of shell ground to powder and pressed into something with a price.

Reeve had spoken to the publican at the Continental, who remembered nothing, which meant the publican had been paid to remember nothing, which meant Reeve understood the local economy better than I'd credited. He had walked the length of the sorting sheds at closing time. He had bought a drink for one of the old beachcombers who camps past the creek, and the beachcomber—talkative as driftwood usually isn't—had told him, according to Brack's second face (a wharf laborer named Dunn, deployed with more competence than I expected), "everything about the old diving families, and a fair bit of rubbish besides."

And then, at four o'clock, the report I had been waiting for with the patience of a man who has learned that waiting is simply another form of ownership.

"He found the boy," Brack said. "Jetty end, low tide. Talked to him maybe ten minutes. Boy walked off quick—didn't run, but quick."

"Did you hear what was said?"

"Too far. Wind was wrong. But—" Brack hesitated, and I confess the hesitation interested me more than anything he'd yet reported. Brack does not hesitate; hesitation requires imagination. "The stranger touched his arm, sir. Like he knew him. Or knew somebody he knew."

I looked out at the harbor. The light was going amber and long, and the mudflats were giving back the sky, and somewhere below us in the sorting room a young man with his father's hands was putting pearls into graded trays, unaware—or nearly unaware—that the entire machinery of his life belonged, bolt and rivet, to the building he stood in.

A stranger touches the boy's arm like family. A stranger asks after a dead woman's opinions on Singapore. Six years of settled accounts, and here was a finger running down the column, checking my sums.

"Mr. Voss?" Brack said.

"I'm thinking," I told him, and I was. Thinking is, in my experience, greatly underrated by men of action, who mistake it for inaction right up until the moment it finishes with them.

Here is what I concluded, walking home that evening along the cliff path with my collar up against the sea wind: a stranger who asks careful questions is a problem. A stranger who asks careful questions and touches the Halloran boy like kin is a problem with a schedule. And problems with schedules can be met on one's own terms, provided one moves first—not crudely, never crudely, but with the courtesy that is the finest disguise menace ever wears. I would invite the boy in for a chat, perhaps. I would ask after his health, his work, his ambitions, in the warm voice I keep for exactly such occasions. I would remind him, gently, without ever saying a word that could be quoted, of everything he owed and everything he might lose.

And if the stranger persisted—if he kept pulling threads—I would simply have to determine what thread he held, and cut it, and mend the fabric before anyone noticed the fray.

The lamps of Broome were coming alight below me, one by one, obedient little flames in their ordered rows. From up here the town looked peaceful as a ledger page. All those lives, entered neatly. All that property, sleeping soundly.

I confess I smiled. It had been years since anything had made the work interesting.

Down in the dark, the tide was turning.
