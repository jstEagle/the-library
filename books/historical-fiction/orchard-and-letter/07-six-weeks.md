## 7. Six Weeks

The tide was out so far that morning it seemed the sea had lost its nerve. Mudflats stretched to the horizon, silver and stinking, and I stood among them like a man surveying all that remained of his fortune.

Callum Reeve had asked me to come at first light, before the town woke, before the sheds filled, before any of Voss's eyes had their morning tea. So there I was, boots sunk to the ankle in Roebuck mud, watching a stranger dig a pipe out of his coat pocket and light it with hands steady as a surgeon's.

"You came," he said.

"You said low tide. You said alone."

"And you came anyway." He drew on the pipe, studying me through the smoke. "That's either trust or curiosity, lad, and I've known men drowned by both."

"I came to tell you to stop asking questions in this town. Whatever you want from a Halloran, there isn't one here. There's an Oscar who works for the Meridian Company, and he's nobody's son."

"Is that what they call you? Oscar?" He smiled, and there was something in it—something worn soft with years, like sea glass—that made my chest hurt without my permission. "She called you Osc. In every letter. 'Ask after my Osc,' she wrote. 'Tell him his mother keeps the orchard gate open.'"

The mud seemed to tilt beneath me. I kept my feet because keeping my feet was the one skill I had never surrendered to anyone.

"She's dead," I said. "Lost at sea. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows what they were told." Callum crouched, unhurried, and turned over a piece of driftwood as if the conversation were happening elsewhere, in some other man's life. "I knew Mara Halloran eleven years, boy. I carried her sea chest when she married your father. I fished her out of a squall off Thursday Island when she was six months gone with you and laughing about it. Dead women don't write letters." He looked up at me then, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "Dead women especially don't write letters dated three years past their drowning."

* * *

We walked while he talked, out along the flats where the water had abandoned, our voices safe under the cry of gulls and the wide indifference of the morning. It was the kind of place a man could speak freely—not because no one could hear, but because anyone watching would see only two figures idling where no secrets could possibly live.

"The Sarawak Star," Callum said. "Mail steamer, Singapore line, calls here on the twenty-third. Six weeks tomorrow."

"What of her?"

"She carries assisted berths. Government scheme for kin of settlers—one passage subsidized for a verified family member, someone to keep the colonies peopled. One berth on this sailing is held against a Halloran claim." He stopped walking and faced me fully. "Filed in Singapore, at her petition. Your mother has spent four years pulling strings and saving coin to buy that berth, Oscar. Four years. She thinks she's buying your freedom."

The word landed in me like a hook set deep. Freedom. I turned it over and found it rusted through with complications.

"There's a condition," I said, because there is always a condition; I had learned that before I learned my letters.

"There's always a condition." He nodded, approving perhaps, or pitying—I couldn't tell which was worse. "Verified kin. The shipping agent won't issue a berth on sentiment. You'll need papers. Proof of identity, proof of relationship. Birth record from the parish, registry entry from the company showing your parentage, and a magistrate's endorsement that you're free to travel."

Free to travel.

I almost laughed. The sound died somewhere behind my teeth.

"Mr. Reeve," I said carefully, "do you know how the Meridian Company records its people?"

"I know how most companies do."

"Not like most." I watched a crab make its sideways, determined way across the mud, and envied it nothing but its lack of ledger lines. "There's a registry in the company office. Every indentured worker entered in it. And do you know under what heading my name appears?"

He waited.

"*Assets*," I said. "Under assets. Between the pearl shell lots and the company boats. I've seen the page myself—I audit the manifests. Oscar Halloran, male, nineteen years, valued at—" I heard my own voice go flat, which was the only way I could say it, "—valued at thirty-one pounds against debt."

Callum said nothing for a long moment. The tide whispered somewhere far off, deciding whether to come back.

"Then we get you out of that column," he said at last.

"It isn't a column. It's a wall." I kicked at the mud, hating myself for the childishness of it and doing it anyway. "You don't understand what you're asking. To prove I'm kin to Mara Halloran, I have to go to the parish register—and Father Dunne keeps it locked, and Father Dunne dines at Voss's table twice a month. To show I'm free to travel, I need the company's own endorsement—the company that owns me. Every document that proves who I am lives in a drawer controlled by the man who decides whether I'm allowed to be anyone."

"And Voss knows you're a Halloran?"

I hesitated. That was the question, wasn't it—the hinge everything swung on.

"He knows I'm Oscar," I said slowly. "The quiet one who sorts pearls and doesn't ask questions. Whether he's ever connected me to the diver who drowned... I don't know. Men like Voss file people away. But a Halloran asking for a berth—a verified kin claim—that would land on his desk. It would have to pass his inspection. His signature."

"So we keep it from passing his desk at all."

"How?"

"That," said Callum, "is the interesting part."

* * *

He laid it out as we walked back toward the town's edge, talking low and fast now, the sailor's habit of cramming work into whatever weather allowed.

Mrs. Adeyemi, he'd learned already—he'd been three days in Broome and knew more than I'd managed in five years, or perhaps he simply asked questions I'd trained myself never to think. The clerk who kept the registry copies. The one person in that office who had once looked at me like a person rather than a line item.

"She can pull the registry pages. Copy them, even. A duplicate extract, properly stamped—agents accept those."

"If she's willing. If she isn't afraid."

"Everyone's afraid," Callum said. "The trick is finding what they're more afraid of than Voss." He didn't elaborate, and I didn't ask, though something in his tone suggested Mrs. Adeyemi carried debts older than my indenture.

"And the parish?"

"Nell Halloran," he said, and I stopped walking.

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. My half-sister. The child Mara had been carrying when she vanished—or so I'd believed until an hour ago; now the arithmetic rearranged itself, ugly and undeniable. If my mother fled instead of drowned, she'd been pregnant. And the girl in the shell-sorting shed, the sharp-tongued one the other workers gave a wide berth—

"You know about Nell," I said.

"I know your mother asks after her too. 'My Nell.' Same letters." Callum's eyes were shrewd. "Do you know her?"

"I know of her."

"Then you'll need to know more than of her. The parish birth record will list both children under Mara Halloran. One document proves you both. She gets it from the church side—you're known there, aren't you? You attend?"

"I attend," I said, which was true the way a cage bird attends to the weather.

"Good. Then that part costs you nothing but a Sunday and a steady hand."

Nothing but a Sunday and a steady hand. As if the parish were not Voss's second parlor. As if Father Dunne did not take his brandy where Voss took his. As if a Halloran name spoken aloud in that church wouldn't carry to the harbor master's office by Monday bells.

"Mr. Reeve." I turned to face him square. "Let me tell you what happens to questions in this town. Five years ago my father asked a question about cargo weights. Three days later he was diving the Lorelei wreck in weather no captain should have allowed, and the verdict was misadventure, and the company paid for a funeral that cost less than a good pearl. Since then I have learned exactly one trade: silence. I sort pearls. I keep my eyes down. I am useful, and usefulness is armor. You're asking me to take the armor off in front of the man who ordered the sword."

Callum took a slow draw on his pipe, and I watched grief move through his face—not fresh grief, old grief, the kind that has lived so long indoors it knows all the furniture.

"I promised a dying woman," he said quietly. "Not that I'd save you. She didn't ask for that—she's stronger than any of us. I promised I'd find her son and put the choice in his hands. The choice is in your hands, Oscar. Take it or leave it. But understand this—" he leaned close, and the smoke curled between us like a question mark, "—the Sarawak Star sails on the twenty-third whether you're aboard or not. Berths don't wait. Ships don't wait. And your mother—" his voice caught, just barely, a rope fraying under load, "—your mother has waited four years already. Six weeks, boy. All you have to do for six weeks is be careful, and be quick, and be lucky. And if you're none of those things—" he shrugged, "—well. You'll be no worse off than you are now."

"No worse off," I repeated.

It wasn't true, of course. It was never true. A man who reaches for freedom and misses does not return to his former life unchanged; the reaching itself marks him. Voss would know. Voss always knew. The surveillance would tighten, the small cruelties would sharpen, the ledger would acquire new annotations against my name.

But standing there on the flats with the tide beginning its long crawl home, I felt something crack open in my chest that five years of careful silence had kept sealed. Hope. Ugly, inconvenient hope, with its fingers in everything.

Six weeks. Forty-two days. I had survived five years by measuring time in tides and paydays and the slow rotation of pearl lots through my sorting trays. Now time had a different shape entirely: a countdown, a fuse, a ship's horn growing louder in my imagination.

"All right," I said.

Callum raised an eyebrow.

"I'll do it. The papers, the parish, the registry—all of it. But we do it my way. Quietly. No names spoken above a whisper, no questions asked in daylight, nothing that leaves a trail a clever man could follow backward. If Voss learns what we're about before that ship sails, we're finished—worse than finished. Do you understand me?"

For the first time that morning, Callum Reeve grinned, and it transformed his whole weathered face into something younger, something that might have sailed beside my mother laughing through squalls.

"Aye," he said. "Mara said you'd say that. Said her Osc was the careful one. Said you got your father's shoulders and her caution, and God help whoever mistook the caution for weakness." He spat into the mud, ceremonially, like sealing a contract. "Six weeks, then. We start today."

We started today.

What neither of us said—what neither of us could say without breaking the fragile thing we'd just built—was that we were not the only ones counting. Somewhere up the hill, in an office smelling of ink and camphor, a man had already noticed a stranger asking questions, and had already decided to watch. The machinery was turning. It had been turning since before Callum's ship made anchor.

And I had five years of evidence for what happened to people who stood in its way.

But that evening, sorting pearls under lamplight with my hands steady and my mind anywhere else, I made myself a promise no ledger could record: in forty-two days, one way or another, the Sarawak Star would sail carrying a piece of me out of this place—whether it was my body or merely my hope, I hadn't yet decided.

The lamp flickered. Outside, the tide was coming in.

Somewhere across that darkening water, my mother was keeping an orchard gate open.
