## 8. The Drowned Year

There is a trick to remembering a drowning. You don't summon it all at once. You let it come in pieces, the way the sea gives things back—first a shoe, then a spar, then, if it's feeling generous and cruel in equal measure, the man himself.

I was eleven the year my father drowned, and for five years I have carried that day around like a stone sewn into my coat. Most days I forget its weight entirely. But since Callum Reeve put that letter in my hands, the stone has been shifting, working loose, asking to be looked at properly. And so I have looked.

Here is what I remember, in the order the sea returned it.

* * *

First: the light.

March of '89, and the wet season had been sulking over Broome for a week, building itself up out of the northwest like something with a grudge. On the morning of the last dive the sky had that greenish bruise-color I have since learned means trouble, though at eleven you don't learn weather, you just feel it in your teeth.

My father stood on the jetty at dawn checking his gear with the particular patience he reserved for the sea. He was not a large man—Halloran men ran to lean and long—but he moved like someone who had made an agreement with the water years ago and kept to his side of it. He'd been diving the beds off the Lorelei for the Meridian Trading Company going on nine seasons. Nine seasons of holding his breath down in the cold green dark while the company's ledgers fattened upstairs in offices where the ink never got wet.

"You'll mind your mother," he said that morning. Not a question. He said it every time he went out, the way other men crossed themselves, and I answered every time the same way—"Yes, Da"—because that's what the ritual required. Neither of us knowing it would be the last time the words were exchanged, or perhaps knowing it in that way families know things without letting them surface, the way divers know the bottom is always there under them.

He cuffed my ear gently. "And keep Nell from eating the shell grit. She'll try it."

Nell was four. I remember her less from that year than from afterward, which is its own kind of grief—grieving a sister who was there and then wasn't, hauled off to an aunt in the town while I was folded into the company's care like a mislaid parcel. But that morning she was just a small loud thing asleep in the house on Dampier Terrace, and my father loved her with the unembarrassed ferocity of a man who had waited late in life for daughters.

The lugger was called the Lorelei. Remember that name. It will matter more than any of us knew it would.

* * *

Second: the sound.

I wasn't supposed to be anywhere near the jetty when they came back, because mothers have instincts about storms the way dogs have instincts about earthquakes, and mine had planted me firmly at home with instructions and a list of chores long enough to occupy three boys. I did half of one chore and then did what children do, which is disobey in the direction of the thing they fear most.

By midday the storm had arrived properly—not the greenish threat of dawn but the full black performance, wind flattening the palms along the foreshore, rain coming sideways in sheets so thick the town dissolved into it. And through it, cutting across everything, came a sound I can still produce on demand inside my own head: the company's salvage bell. A quick, impatient clangor, nothing ceremonial about it, the sound of men being summoned to work rather than mourned.

They were ringing it for the Lorelei.

She'd gone down off the beds in the worst of it—turned by a squall, some said later; swamped taking shell aboard too fast, said others. The crew had gotten clear mostly, but the dive tender reported my father had been down when it hit, down on the bottom with his air hose running back to a boat that no longer floated above him. Which meant, by the arithmetic of hoses and lungs, that he was either drowned already or trapped somewhere with a pocket of air, waiting for someone to come down after him.

The salvage crews went out because Voss—the harbor master even then, polished and pleasant as lacquered wood—had ordered it, and the order had come with a timetable attached. The storm was still blowing. Every old diver in town said the same thing, which I heard secondhand in fragments over the following weeks: *You don't go down in that. You wait six hours, eight, till it settles. The dead are patient. The living who go down after them aren't.* But Voss wanted the shell recovered before it spoiled and the gear salvaged before it scattered, and the Meridian's timetable, as everyone in Broome understood without ever being told, outranked the sea's.

So men went down into green murk stirred to the consistency of milk, visibility measured in inches, current dragging the lines sideways. They found the Lorelei's mast. They found crates of shell. What they did not find, that day or the next two, was my father.

On the fourth day the search was called off. "Misadventure," the verdict read, in a hand I would later recognize as belonging to a clerk paid to be brief. *Lost at sea during salvage operations; death by misadventure.* Fourteen words to close the account of Thomas Halloran. The company sent a form letter expressing regret and noting that, per the terms of his contract, certain deductions against his final wages would apply for equipment lost with the vessel.

They billed my father for drowning. I want that understood. They weighed his life, subtracted a diving helmet and a length of air hose, and sent my mother a figure owing.

* * *

Third: the silence.

My mother received the letter standing in the doorway, and I watched her read it, and what I remember most—more than her tears, which came later and privately—is how still she became. Stillness in Mara Halloran was not calm. It was arithmetic. I know that now, though I didn't know it then. I thought she was grieving. She was calculating.

Within a month she was gone. There was a service—a small one, at the church, with a wooden marker in the sand because there was no body—and the story settled over the town like fine silt: poor Mrs. Halloran, lost her husband to the sea and then taken herself, fever on a coastal steamer, such a tragedy, those Hallorans and the water. People said it to me in the soft voices people use on orphans, and I learned to nod and lower my eyes, because grief that asks questions makes people uncomfortable, and uncomfortable people make life difficult for orphan boys in their debt.

Because that was the other thing. My mother's passage—such as it was—her farewells, the settling of whatever small accounts remained: someone had arranged it. Someone had paid. I was twelve before I understood that our household had no money for steamers, and thirteen before I stopped wondering aloud who might have helped us, because the one time I asked, an aunt on my father's side went pale and told me never to ask again, in the voice you use to tell a child not to touch a snake.

I stopped asking. That was the first silence I learned to keep, and I have kept so many since that they've begun to run together, one silence laid over another like shells in a sack, until I sometimes wonder whether there's an original Oscar underneath them at all or just the shape he left behind.

* * *

Now, the reframing—which is the whole point of this chapter, if a chapter can be said to have a point, and I've decided mine does.

For five years I filed that day under *weather*. Storms happen. Luggers turn over. Men who breathe other men's air for a living die of it eventually, and the sea doesn't sign contracts and the company only enforces the ones that favor it. This is the story I told myself because it was livable. A drowned father is a tragedy. A murdered one is a problem, and problems require action, and action requires power, and I had none.

But here is what I know now, standing at thirty-some-odd years old with a letter in my mother's handwriting dated three years after her funeral:

My father went down that morning knowing the weather. Nine seasons. He could read the northwest sky the way Mrs. Adeyemi reads a ledger—fluently, suspiciously, catching the errors. He would not have dived into a building squall unless someone above him insisted the shell had to come up *that day*, or the berth was lost, or the bonus forfeited. And who set the timetables? Who signed the orders? Whose polite, immaculate signature sits at the bottom of every work directive that came out of the harbor office for nine years?

Gideon Voss's.

And here is the other thing, the thing I've turned over maybe four hundred times in the past week, examining it from angles like a pearl with a flaw you're trying to locate: they searched four days and found crates of shell but no body. Fine. The sea keeps what it keeps. But the salvage went down *fast*, faster than prudence allowed, and fast salvage isn't grief—it's retrieval. You don't risk men's lives in a storm-churned bay to recover shell worth some hundreds of pounds unless the shell—or something traveling with the shell—is worth the risk.

Unless something came up that night that was never entered in any book.

I didn't understand that at eleven. At eleven I understood the bell, and the rain, and my mother's stillness, and the form letter with its deductions. At eleven I filed it all under *the sea takes and the company bills.*

At this age, with the stone finally loose in my coat, I file it differently. Now it reads: a man who could read weather was sent down into it. Men were sent down after him before it was safe, in a hurry, for cargo. His family was billed for his gear. His widow was quietly funded onto a steamer and erased. And every record of it passed across the desk of one Gideon Voss, who has, this very week, set a man named Brack to follow a stranger asking questions about my family—as if questions were cargo he could seize at the jetty.

Weather doesn't arrange passages. Weather doesn't destroy files.

Men do.

* * *

Last night I sat up with the lamp after the counting house went quiet and let myself do the arithmetic I've avoided since Callum Reeve walked up the jetty. If my father was murdered—if that word is allowed, and I find that once spoken it fits the facts like a key fits a lock—then my mother didn't abandon us. She fled. And if she fled, she fled from something specific, and the something specific has a name, and the name signs things in beautiful copperplate at the top of Roebuck Bay.

Which means the day I've spent five years mourning as an act of God may have been, all along, an act of Gideon Voss.

I put out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the tide, and I thought about my father's last morning, the cuff on my ear, *keep Nell from eating the shell grit.* Small instructions from a careful man. He checked his gear twice. He always checked twice. A careful man, sent down careless, by design.

Tomorrow Callum wants to talk about the Lorelei—what she was carrying, what came up with the shell that night, why the salvage couldn't wait for the storm to pass. I told him I'd listen. I told him I was ready.

That was a lie, of course. But it's the first lie I've ever told on purpose in the direction of the truth, and somehow that felt like the beginning of something.
