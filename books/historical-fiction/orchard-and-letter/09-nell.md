## 9. Nell

The trick to sorting mother-of-pearl is that you never let the shells know you're in a hurry. They can tell. Same as people. You pick up a shell and you turn it slow, like you've got all morning, like the iridescence on its lip is a matter of state, and meanwhile your thumb has already told you everything — thickness, flaw, whether it's worth a shilling or a curse. My hands learned this before I did. I was eleven when I started in the sheds and my hands were smarter than me then and they still are.

"Oi, Nell," said Dolly Marsh across the bench, "you've got a face on you."

"I've always got a face on you, Dolly. It's called my face."

"You know what I mean. Storm-face."

"It's called Tuesday." It was Wednesday, but she wouldn't check.

Dolly laughed the way she does everything, too loud for indoors, and Mrs. Quill two benches down shot us the look that means one more word out of either of you and I'll have you sweeping shell dust with your eyelashes. Mrs. Quill has run the sorting floor since before I was born and will run it after we're all dead, sorting something, I don't doubt. She once made a grown man cry over a misgraded tektite. I respect her enormously and would never say so where she could hear.

I turned another shell. Slow hands. The light through the shed's high windows came down grey-green, sea-colored, the way it always is in Broome by mid-morning, and the dust hung in it like something the air couldn't quite decide to say.

Here is what nobody tells you about shell-sorting: it is not boring. Boring would be a mercy. It's the opposite of boring — it's the same thing forever, which is worse. Every day the tides bring in the luggers, every day the luggers bring in shell, every day the shell comes to my bench and I grade it and the company pays me four-and-six a week minus what I owe the company for the privilege of being paid by it, and every night I go home to Aunt Bridie's house where the gin bottle sits on the dresser like a member of the family with seniority.

Four years I've done this. Since I was eleven. Which means I am fifteen now, though if you ask Mrs. Quill she'll say sixteen going on forty, and if you ask Aunt Bridie she'll say "Nell? Is Nell here?" because by evening she generally can't find herself, let alone me.

The shell didn't know I was in a hurry today. Good. Because I was.

* * *

Let me explain about the money, since everyone in this town eventually explains about the money, usually to me, usually as a reason I should be more grateful or less mouthy.

Aunt Bridie gets the guardianship allowance from the parish — nine shillings a fortnight for keeping me, which works out to roughly nothing per meal once you account for the gin, and I do account for the gin; I keep accounts the way other girls keep diaries. She also owes Mr. Brackish at the store, and owes the woman who does her washing, and owes, in some arrangement I've never fully understood but have heard shouted about through the wall, a man named Coyle who comes round on Fridays and speaks very quietly, which frightens me more than shouting ever has. Shouting spends itself. Quiet saves up.

So I earn my own keep and then some. The trouble is the some goes into the household pot, because I'm fifteen and female and therefore legally a kind of weather — something that happens to you rather than something you are. I hand over my wage envelope on Saturdays and Bridie gives me back sixpence "for sweets" as though I were eight, and I take the sixpence because taking it keeps the peace, and I put it in a tin under a loose board in my room, and the tin now holds one pound, three shillings, and a button I'm fond of.

One pound three. At that rate I'll be able to leave Broome in approximately 1911, destination anywhere, fare third class, dignity included free of charge.

I don't tell anyone about the tin. That's the whole art of having anything in this world: don't tell.

* * *

At the midday bell I took my bread and dripping out to the jetty end, past the lugger crews, past the boys diving off the piles — showing off, half drowning, delighted — and sat with my feet hanging over water the color of strong tea.

This is my office. Nobody bothers me here because I've cultivated an expression that suggests I bite.

From there you can see the whole harbor doing its business: the luggers coming in on the tide with their shells, the smoke of the pearling fleet, the Meridian Trading Company offices standing white and smug above the beach like a church that worships something else entirely. And sometimes — this is the part I've never said out loud to anyone, not even Dolly, especially not Dolly — sometimes there's a young man who comes down the far stairs from the counting house and stands at the rail a long way off, and doesn't come near, and doesn't look away either.

Oscar Halloran. Though I didn't learn the name until last year, and only then by accident, overhearing Mrs. Quill say to the paymaster "that Halloran boy, the diver's son" and feeling the name land somewhere under my ribs like a dropped coin.

Halloran. My aunt says my mother's name was Mara. Mara what, she's never managed to say sober, and drunk she says other things entirely, things about ships and running and someone paying, and then she cries and calls me by my mother's name, which I hate so much I can't even describe it. Being loved wrongly is worse than being ignored. Ask any cat.

So: Oscar Halloran, diver's son, indentured man, five years in the company's books. He stands at his end of the jetty and I sit at mine, and between us is sixty feet of sun-bleached timber and the entire unsayable history of our family, and neither of us crosses it.

He thinks I don't notice him. I notice everything; it's my one marketable skill besides thumbs. He watches me the way you watch a fire you're not sure you lit. And I watch him back, and what I feel isn't warm, exactly. It's more like hunger with a grudge attached. Because here's the arithmetic nobody taught him either: he got kept. Whatever happened — whatever storm took my mother and drowned whoever Oscar's father was — Oscar stayed in Broome, in sight, in a life with edges, while I got handed to Bridie like a parcel nobody claimed at the counter. He had a father's grave, probably. A story. I had a smell of gin and a surname I wasn't sure belonged to anyone.

I know it isn't fair. Fairness is for people who can afford it. But knowing a thing and feeling it are different departments, and they don't correspond.

That Wednesday he was there again, at his rail, and a shell-dust wind came off the water and he pushed his hair back with the heel of his hand — a gesture so exactly like something I do, something I caught myself doing a hundred times, that I bit the inside of my cheek until it hurt.

Stop looking like me, I thought at him, viciously. Find your own gestures.

* * *

Afternoon shift. The shells came and the shells went. Dolly talked about a boy named Ernie who plays the concertina badly, and I said "badly" is generous, and Mrs. Quill said "Marsh, O'Halloran—" 

Wait. No. She said "Marsh, Nell—" because that's how she says my name, just Nell, no surname, and I'd never wondered why until that exact moment, standing there with a shell in my hand and the whole shed suddenly too quiet in my ears.

"Nell," Mrs. Quill said, "daydreaming costs the same as theft in my book."

"Yes, Mrs. Quill."

"And stop biting your cheek. You'll ruin your looks and then what'll you have?"

"A personality," I said, and Dolly snorted, and Mrs. Quill almost smiled, which from Mrs. Quill is like a solar eclipse — noted, dangerous, not to be looked at directly.

But the thing had gotten into me. Surname-less. Why would the shed rolls carry me without a surname? Everyone else was So-and-so this and Mac-something that. Even the Malay sorters had names written proper in the ledger. Just Nell. As if I'd been entered into the record of the world by someone who couldn't finish the line.

That night I asked Bridie, casual as I could make it, which wasn't very. Casual and I have an arrangement whereby I attempt it and it declines.

"Bridie. What was Mum's full name?"

She was peeling potatoes with the concentration of a woman defusing something. "Mara," she said.

"Mara *what*, Bridie."

Pause. Knife pause. "Halloran," she said finally, so quietly I nearly missed it, and then, louder, with the defensive brightness she deploys when cornered, "and a fine mess she made of it, that's what. Now leave the spuds be."

Halloran. So it was true, or mostly true, or true enough to hurt. Oscar Halloran at the end of the jetty, watching me with my own gestures, was my brother. Half-brother. Something-brother. And had known it, presumably, for years — years of him standing sixty feet away saying nothing, while I grew up thinking I was the only person in the world related to nobody.

I went to my room and lifted the loose board and counted the tin again, though I'd counted it that morning. One pound, three shillings, one button. It hadn't grown. Money never does when you need it to.

* * *

You want to know what loneliness is? Not silence. Silence is fine; I've made my peace with silence, we're old friends, we don't speak. Loneliness is being surrounded — a shed full of women, a town full of men, an aunt full of gin — and knowing that not one soul in it knows the actual shape of you. Dolly knows I like boiled lollies and dislike Ernie's concertina. Bridie knows I exist on alternating days. Nobody knows that I read the shipping news in the paper every Thursday and pick a port I'd sail to if the tin ever filled — Thursday it was Colombo, Friday Batavia, Sunday I settled on Singapore for no reason except the word sounded like a door opening.

Nobody knows that when I sort shell I imagine each perfect piece is a ticket somewhere, and grade them accordingly, and that I've been saving the flawless ones in a matchbox — not stealing, exactly, more like collecting evidence — twelve perfect buttons of nacre that prove beauty can come out of muck and survive the grading.

Nobody knows that I talk to my mother. Out loud, some nights, walking home along the beach where the sand holds the day's heat like a grudge. I tell her things. *Your sister drinks, Mum. Your daughter sorts shells. Your son stands on a jetty and doesn't cross it.* Whoever your son is. *If you're dead, say so. If you're alive, say that instead.*

She never answers. Dead mothers are terrible correspondents.

Or so I believed, Wednesday nights, walking the wet sand with my boots in my hand and the town's lamps behind me and the water ahead doing its endless indifferent shining.

Because here's where the week turned. Here's the hook the day set in me and didn't take out.

Friday. Payday, and Coyle-day, and I'd stayed late at the shed finishing a batch Mrs. Quill wanted graded before Monday, so it was dusk when I cut down behind the company offices to reach the beach path — and stopped, because voices, low ones, the saved-up kind.

Two men stood by the seaward wall. One was big and moved like hired muscle, hat brim low; I knew him by silhouette — Brack, the one who comes about Bridie's debts, the quiet collector. The other was older, silvering, dressed like money pretending to be modest, and I knew him the way everyone in Broome knows him: Harbor Master Voss. The company's smooth face. He was speaking and Brack was listening, and I pressed myself into the shadow of the rain tank with my heart doing something undignified.

"—the Halloran question," Voss was saying, and my blood went cold and bright at once. "Handle it quietly. No scenes. And find out who the girl talks to."

The girl. Me. Brack nodded once.

They separated. Voss walked toward his office like a man strolling through a garden he owned. Brack went the other way, past the rain tank, close enough that I held my breath and smelled tobacco and salt on him as he passed.

I stood in the dark a long time after, doing sums I didn't want to do. The Halloran question. There was a question about Hallorans, and it had answers worth handling quietly, and I was part of the arithmetic.

And the worst part — the part I'll admit to no living soul, the part that burned worse than fear — was this: for one black second, hearing *the girl* in Voss's mouth, I felt almost glad. Someone was asking after Hallorans. Someone, somewhere, had made my family a question important enough for a harbor master to lose sleep over.

Whatever was coming, it had found me before I could find it.

I picked up my boots and walked home the long way, past the jetty, where a lamp still burned at the far rail and a figure stood against it — my brother, though I'd have died before saying the word aloud — and I lifted my hand, just slightly, halfway, an inch of greeting I'd deny under oath.

He lifted his back.

Sixty feet of timber, five years of silence, one inch each. It was, I decided, a start.
