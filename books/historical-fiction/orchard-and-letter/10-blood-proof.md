## 10. Blood Proof

There is a particular cruelty in paper. A man can survive a cyclone, a shark, a season of bad luck and worse wages, but hand him a form and watch him drown in it.

I learned this on the Tuesday after Callum Reeve put his mother's letter into my hands, when I stood at the counter of the parish office in Chinatown with my cap in my fists like a schoolboy asking for sweets.

The office smelled of dust, candle wax, and the particular staleness of ledgers that have been handled by too many hopeful people. Behind the desk sat Mr. Ellery Hobbes, parish registrar, a man built like a filing cabinet and about as warm. He had a way of looking at you over his spectacles as though you were a stain he hadn't yet decided how to treat.

"Name," he said.

"Oscar Halloran."

The pen paused. Not long — half a heartbeat — but I'd spent five years learning to read pauses the way other men read weather, and this one had teeth in it.

"Halloran," he repeated, writing it down with great care, as though the name might bite him if he rushed. "Purpose?"

"Family records. Births. I'm needing proof of kinship."

"For what purpose?" he said again, slower, the way you'd speak to a man who might be simple or might be lying, and hadn't decided which was more convenient.

"A berth," I said. "On the *Sarawak Star*. Assisted passage for verified kin."

Something moved behind his eyes. It wasn't surprise. That was the thing I noticed most, standing there sweating in my good shirt: nothing I said surprised him. He had been told I would come, or he had been told to expect someone like me, and either way the machinery was already oiled and waiting.

"Kin of whom?"

"Mara Halloran. My mother." The words felt strange in my mouth, saying them aloud in front of a stranger. Alive. In Singapore. Asking after her Osc. "Reported lost at sea, some years back."

"Reported," said Hobbes, and made a small mark in his ledger. "And you're claiming she lives."

"I'm not claiming anything, sir. I'm asking after records. Birth certificate. Baptismal entry, if there's one. Anything in your books with my name on it and hers underneath."

He closed the ledger with a soft, deliberate sound, like a door being shut on a conversation.

"The parish records," he said, "are not freely available to — " and here he paused again, choosing his word with a jeweler's care — "to persons without standing."

"I work for the Meridian Trading Company."

"Yes," said Hobbes, and something flickered across his face that might have been pity in a better man, or its opposite. "That you do."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because it took me years to understand these things and I'd hate for anyone reading this to think I was slow on the uptake. Nothing rude occurred. No doors slammed, no voices raised. Hobbes simply informed me that requests for certified extracts required written application, that applications required a reference from one's employer, that references required verification, and that verification was conducted by the registrar's discretion. His discretion. And his discretion, he added, regretfully, was presently engaged elsewhere.

"When will it be engaged here?" I asked.

"These matters take time, Halloran. Six weeks is a short span for such things." He almost smiled. "You'll want to begin early."

*Six weeks,* spoken back to me like a coin tossed to a beggar. He knew. Of course he knew. The whole coast probably knew inside two days that the Halloran boy had gone strange in the manner, asking questions, and Voss's men knew before the coast did.

I thanked him. That's the part that still sits wrong in my chest — I thanked him, cap in hand, because five years of indenture teaches you that gratitude is cheaper than trouble and just as heavy to carry home.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi found me that evening sitting on the shell piles behind the sorting sheds, which is where I go when I need to think somewhere the walls can't hear me. She came down the path with her skirts gathered up out of the wet sand and no lantern, because she knows the ground as well as I do, and she sat herself on the pile beside mine without asking, the way people do when they've already decided the answer to every question you might raise.

"You went to see Hobbes," she said.

Word travels fast in Broome when it wants to. Faster when somebody pays for the postage.

"He says I need a company reference."

"You need three things." She held up fingers, counting, and in the dusk her hands looked like a teacher's. "A certified extract from the parish register showing your birth and your mother's name. An attestation from the company registry confirming your identity and standing. And the assisted-passage claim form itself, signed and witnessed. The first comes from Hobbes. The second comes from this office. The third needs both of the others attached before the shipping agent will even look at it."

"And all three roads run through Gideon Voss."

"Not through him." She smoothed her skirt over her knees. "Through men who eat from his hand. Which is worse. Voss at least has to pretend to be fair in public. His creatures don't bother pretending to each other."

"Why are you telling me this?"

She was quiet a moment. Out past the jetty the tide was coming in, making its small patient noises against the pilings, the same noises it had made the night my father didn't come up.

"Because you looked at the manifests," she said finally. "Most men look at their wage line and stop. You read the whole page. A man who reads the whole page is either going to be trouble or he's going to be useful, and I've decided to find out which before somebody else decides for you."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. But it's the only one you're getting tonight." She stood, brushing sand from her skirt, and paused. "The registry copy you need — I keep those books. If it were a matter of copying a page, that page could be copied. But certification requires Mr. Vane's signature, and Mr. Vane takes his dinner with the harbor master every Thursday, and signs nothing without asking himself first what Gideon would want signed."

"So I'm locked in a room where every door has Voss's name on the handle."

"You're locked in a room," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "where every door has always had Voss's name on the handle. You've only just noticed." She looked down at me, and for a moment her voice softened into something almost gentle. "Go home, Oscar. Sleep. Men make their worst mistakes when they're tired, and you can't afford a single one."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in my cot above the boat shed listening to the water and doing sums, because sums are what I have instead of prayers.

Here is the arithmetic of my situation, laid out plain:

To board the *Sarawak Star*, I must prove I am Mara Halloran's son. To prove it, I need the parish record. The parish record is held by Hobbes, whose salary supplement arrives, I would wager my last year of indenture, in an envelope without a return address. To obtain the extract, I need a company reference. The company reference requires the signature of Mr. Vane, registry officer, who dines Thursdays with the man who may have arranged my father's drowning. And beneath all of it, under everything, like ballast in a hull: I am entered in the company's own books as an asset. Assets do not apply for passage abroad. Assets are applied *for*.

There was a time — say, ten days ago — when the whole of my ambition was to remain unnoticed until my indenture ran out and then live small and quiet somewhere the company's shadow didn't reach. I had gotten good at it. I knew which silences kept me safe the way my father knew which currents ran under Roebuck Bay. Keep your head down. Sort your pearls. Sign your wage chit and don't read the fine print.

But a letter changes a man. A letter in your dead mother's handwriting, dated four years after her death, asking after *my Osc* — that is not a document. That is a lit match dropped into a dry paddock, and everything I'd spent five years keeping damp has caught, and I cannot put it out, and I find I don't especially want to.

The trouble is that wanting is free and everything else costs. Freedom in Broome is gated twice over: once by the law, once by paper, and the paper-keepers all drink from the same well. A man could die of old age in the queue.

Unless.

I turned the thought over carefully, the way I'd turn a suspect pearl under lamplight, checking for flaws.

Hobbes wanted a written application with a company reference. Fine. Applications could be written by anyone; the reference was the lock. Vane wouldn't sign without Voss's blessing. But Voss — and here my blood went cold and hot at once — Voss had already shown interest in me. He had summoned Callum's shadow to his office; his men had been seen asking after strangers at the jetties. A man like Voss did not spend attention without expecting return. Which meant that sooner or later, he would send for me. And a summons from the harbor master was a door opening, even if it opened onto a knife.

Every gate they'd built around me had Voss's name on the handle. But handles turn from both sides.

I got up in the dark and found my pencil stub and the back of a cargo sheet, and I wrote out the list: parish extract, registry attestation, claim form. Under each item, the man who held it. Under each man, the thing he wanted — because every man wants something, even if it's only to be left alone, and Hobbes's spectacles had gone glassy with fear when I said the word *berth*, fear of having to choose between his master and his conscience, which meant he still had a conscience, which meant he was a door that might yet open.

Under Vane I wrote *Thursday dinners* and left it there, because I didn't yet know what a man like that wanted besides his seat at the table.

Under Mrs. Adeyemi's name I wrote nothing at all, because some entries you don't commit to paper.

Then I folded the sheet small and put it in the lining of my coat, next to where the letter lived, and lay back down, and listened to the tide come in over the flats, patient as arithmetic.

Somewhere out there, six weeks were already burning down like a lamp with a short wick. Somewhere out there, a woman who was supposed to be dead was waiting to learn whether her son was coming, or whether her son was another name the sea had taken.

And somewhere in this town, Gideon Voss was doing his own sums, and I would have bet the pearls off the ocean floor that his columns balanced cleaner than mine — for now.

Because the next morning, when I reported to the sorting room, there was a slip on my bench in a hand I recognized: neat, black, unhurried.

It read: *Mr. Halloran. The harbor master requests your presence at his convenience.*

His convenience. Not mine. Never mine.

But a summons, I reminded myself, folding it away, is a door opening.

Even onto a knife. Especially onto a knife.
