## 11. Mrs. Adeyemi's Price

There are three kinds of men who come to the registry office after hours, and I have learned to sort them the way I sort cargo manifests: by what they want, by what they fear, and by how badly they lie about either.

The first kind comes for money. They want a figure adjusted, a shipment rerouted on paper, a debt quietly dissolved. I name a price high enough that they flinch and go elsewhere.

The second kind comes for revenge. They want a rival's name blackened in the ledgers, an entry falsified, a signature forged. I tell them the ink is company property and my conscience is not for sale, which is only half true—the other half is that Gideon Voss pays better than any grudge does.

The third kind comes for the truth. They are the rarest and the most dangerous, because they have usually already decided to do something foolish, and they want me to make it possible.

Oscar Halloran stood in my doorway at half past seven on Thursday evening, holding his cap like a man holding his own head, and I knew within four seconds that he was the third kind.

* * *

Let me explain something about this office, because it matters to what happened next.

The Meridian Trading Company's registry is not merely a room with cabinets. It is the memory of Broome—every indenture signed, every wage docked, every diver entered as "human asset, depreciating," every death recorded as "loss of equipment, incidental." The company keeps its books immaculate because the books are its alibi. Whatever happened in the world—the storms, the drownings, the pearls that vanished between the luggers and the counting house—happened somewhere else. In here, everything is orderly. Everything balances.

I have kept those books for eleven years. I came to Broome from Freetown by way of London and three ports whose names I decline to remember, and when I arrived I was told, in the polite way of polite men, that a woman of my complexion could expect exactly one kind of employment in this town, and that the registry clerk's position had been held open for precisely such a person, because no white man would sit at that desk and no one would question the figures kept by a woman they preferred not to see at all.

Invisibility, I was informed, was a benefit of the post.

I took it. I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not perform regret for you now. A woman alone in the 1880s learns that survival is not a moral position; it is a precondition for having one. I copied what I was told to copy. I balanced what I was told to balance. And I watched, year after year, the way you watch water rise around your ankles—taking note, saying nothing, telling myself that the day would come when my notes mattered.

I am fifty-one years old. I had begun to suspect the day would never come.

Then Oscar Halloran closed my door behind him and said, without preamble, "Mrs. Adeyemi. I need to see the registry entries for the Lorelei."

* * *

I did not look up immediately. This is not affectation; it is habit. In eleven years I have learned that the first three seconds of any dangerous conversation are spent deciding whether the speaker knows how dangerous it is. If he doesn't, you can still steer him out. If he does—if he has come anyway—then the conversation belongs to both of you, and you had best know what you're agreeing to before your mouth commits you.

So I finished the line I was writing. I blotted it. I set down the pen and aligned it with the edge of the blotter, which is a small vanity of mine, and then I looked up at him.

He had changed since the drowning year. Of course he had—he'd been fourteen—but the change was more than age. The boy who had sat outside this office for two days after his father's lugger came back short one man, waiting for someone to tell him something true, had been replaced by a young man who had learned that no one in Broome tells anyone anything true, and who had arranged himself accordingly: shoulders down, eyes modest, voice never above a working volume. Five years of being owned teaches a particular posture. I recognized it. I wore a version of it myself every time Voss walked through my office humming his little hymns.

"The Lorelei," I repeated. "That's an old entry."

"1891," he said. "The salvage manifest. And the dive logs, if they exist."

"They exist," I said, "in the sense that the sea exists in a painting."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile, but the recognition of a fellow translator. Good. I had feared I would have to conduct this conversation entirely in the company's dialect, and I am tired of the company's dialect.

"Sit down, Mr. Halloran," I said, "before someone sees you standing there looking like a petition."

* * *

Now, I must be honest with you, since honesty is the currency of this chapter: I did not decide to help him in that moment. What I did in that moment was decide to find out how much trouble he intended, so that I could measure my own exposure against it. These are different decisions, whatever the romantics say. I have survived this long by keeping them separate.

"What do you need from the Lorelei's records?" I asked.

"I need to know what she was carrying the night she went down." He paused, and I watched him weigh something. "And whether the weight matches what was reported."

I let the silence sit. Silence, in a registry office, is a form of interrogation; most men cannot bear it longer than ten seconds and will confess to anything just to fill it. Oscar Halloran lasted forty. I made a note of that too.

"You understand," I said finally, "that if the weights don't match, the discrepancy isn't clerical error. My figures are correct. They are always correct. So if there is a gap between what the Lorelei carried and what the Lorelei reported—" I folded my hands. "—then someone made that gap on purpose, and that someone signs Mr. Voss's name under mine."

"Yes," he said.

"And you intend to prove it."

"I intend to try."

"On what evidence? Your father drowned. The lugger's log burned—or so the report says. The divers who worked that year have scattered or been paid off or been frightened into silence. You have a feeling, Mr. Halloran, and feelings make very poor exhibits before a magistrate."

"I have more than a feeling," he said quietly. "But I'm not free to say what."

I should have sent him out then. I want that on the record, wherever the record of my life ends up—which, knowing me, will be a drawer in this office, misfiled on purpose. He had just told me, in effect, *I know something I won't share, but trust me.* No sensible clerk trusts a man who says that. Sensible clerks keep their heads down for thirty years and retire to a small house where nobody asks questions.

I looked at him sitting there with his cap in his hands, and I thought about his mother.

* * *

Mara Halloran came into this office perhaps forty times in her years in Broome. Not for herself—for the others. She was one of the few people in this town who understood that the registry was not a building but a weapon, pointed whichever way its keeper chose. She'd come to check that the wages owed to the Malay divers were entered correctly, and when they weren't—and they never were—she'd stand at my desk with her arms folded and say, "Mrs. Adeyemi, I believe you know these figures are wrong, and I believe you dislike knowing it," and she was right on both counts, and she knew she was right on both counts, and she never once raised her voice, which made it worse for everyone concerned, including me.

She disappeared the same season her husband drowned, and the story given out was that she'd taken ship and been lost at sea, and grief makes people accept arithmetic they'd otherwise question. But I processed the departure manifests myself, in those days, and I will tell you what I told no one: there was no passenger record for Mara Halloran on any vessel leaving Broome that month. There was a berth, though—a berth paid in cash, by an account with no name attached, and cash payments for assisted passages were so rare that the entry might as well have been written in blood.

I asked Voss about it once. Only once. He smiled at me with all the warmth of a well-lit shop window and said, "Mrs. Adeyemi, the registry reflects transactions. It does not interrogate them. That is why the arrangement works."

I never asked again. I entered the berth in the book, I balanced the column, and I added Mara Halloran to the list I keep—not in any ledger, you understand; in my head, which is the only ledger in Broome that has never been audited.

The list has nine names on it. It has had nine names for five years. I had begun to think it was finished.

Oscar Halloran sat in my office asking after the Lorelei, and I found myself thinking: *ten.*

* * *

"Mr. Halloran," I said, "I'm going to ask you a question, and your answer will determine what happens next. Why now? You've worked in this company for five years. You've walked past these cabinets every day of your indenture. What happened this month that made the Lorelei matter?"

He hesitated. Then he said, "If I tell you, you're in it. Fully. There's no halfway."

"I've been in it fully for eleven years," I said. "I simply haven't been consulted. Consider this my consultation."

He looked at me for a long moment, doing his sums—I could see him doing them, this boy who sorts pearls and reads manifests, weighing what I was against what I might cost him. And then he told me. About the stranger on the jetty. About the letter in his mother's hand, dated three years after her sea-burial. About Singapore. About the Sarawak Star and the single assisted berth and the six weeks that had already begun to run.

He told me everything except the nature of Callum Reeve's business, and I noted the omission without offense. A man is entitled to one locked drawer.

When he finished, the lamp had burned lower and neither of us had moved.

"Your mother is alive," I said.

"So it appears."

"And Voss believes her dead."

"He believes whatever is convenient."

I turned my chair toward the window. Below, the town was settling into its evening noises—dogs, drunks, the shell sheds locking up, the tide going out with that long wet sigh it makes. Somewhere out there was a woman I had half-believed dead for five years, alive, writing letters, asking after her son. And somewhere out there was Gideon Voss's house, where that same man was at this hour probably taking port and reviewing accounts, secure in the belief that every loose thread from 1891 had been cut, tied off, or buried.

"Well," I said. "You've certainly chosen an ambitious week for it."

* * *

Here is what I gave him, and here is what I refused.

I refused to simply open the cabinets. Understand: if Voss ever audits my access—and he audits nothing else, so of course he'd audit that—a record of the Lorelei files being pulled by the registry clerk would hang us both by Friday. Whatever we did, we did as copies. Originals stay in place, untouched, their dust unbroken. Dust is an excellent security system; no one suspects a document that has visibly not moved in years.

What I offered instead was this: over the coming nights, I would copy the relevant pages—the salvage manifest, the crew roster, the wage disbursements for the final voyage—into my own hand, onto my own paper, and pass them to him a sheet at a time, folded inside routine correspondence. Nothing leaves this office that could be traced to a missing file. If we are caught, we are caught with paper that proves nothing except that a clerk writes a great many letters.

He accepted this. He had the sense to accept it without arguing for more, which raised him considerably in my estimation.

And then I told him the thing he needed to hear, the thing I had been circling since he walked in, because it was the price of my help—not payment, exactly, but honesty, which in Broome costs more than money:

"Before you build your hopes on the dive logs, Mr. Halloran, you should know that they may not exist anymore. Any of them."

He went still. "The logs?"

"After your father drowned—in the weeks after—there was an inventory review. Ordered personally by Voss. Very unusual; reviews of that kind come from Perth or London, never from the harbor master's own pen. Every file touching the Lorelei's final season was removed 'for verification.' Some were returned." I looked at him steadily. "Many were not. I watched the ash cart come twice that month. Officially, it was old correspondence being destroyed per retention policy. Officially."

"You're saying he burned them."

"I'm saying that a man who drowns a diver and steals a pearl cache does not leave receipts. What remains is whatever was too ordinary to bother destroying—wage lists, supply orders, the manifest of the salvage itself, which was filed under a lugger's name rather than the Lorelei's, a filing error I have never corrected because correcting it would have drawn attention." I allowed myself the smallest satisfaction. "Clerks survive by noticing what powerful men hope will be overlooked. Sometimes we survive by declining to fix their mistakes."

Oscar absorbed this the way he seems to absorb everything—silently, completely, with some internal process that takes longer than most people's but produces harder conclusions. Then he said, "The salvage manifest survives."

"It survives."

"Then the weights can be compared."

"If someone has the true figures," I said. "Which, unless your sailor friend has been diving in places he shouldn't—" I stopped. I watched his face do something complicated. "Ah," I said. "He has, hasn't he. Well. Don't tell me. Locked drawers, Mr. Halloran. We each keep ours."

* * *

One more thing happened that night, and I record it because it explains why I am writing this account at all, and why my loyalty—which Voss assumes is purchased, and which the town assumes is nonexistent—actually has a history, and histories are the only collateral I deal in.

As Oscar rose to leave, he stopped at the door, and instead of thanking me—which I would have waved away—he said:

"My father used to say the company keeps two sets of books. The paper ones and the real ones. He said the paper ones were for the company, and the real ones were for whoever was brave enough to read them." He put on his cap. "He'd have liked you, Mrs. Adeyemi."

He left before I could compose a reply, which was tactful of him, because I had none.

I sat for a while after he'd gone. The lamp guttered. Down at the jetty, the Sarawak Star was not yet even a rumor on the water, but six weeks is a short time when you are copying history by lamplight, and I am an old woman with slow hands and a fast conscience, and I have waited eleven years for someone brave enough to read the real books.

I took out a fresh sheet of my own paper. I dipped my pen.

Tomorrow night, I begin with the salvage manifest.

Tonight, I add a tenth name to my list—and for the first time since I started keeping it, I write one down intending, God willing and tides permitting, to cross it back off again.
