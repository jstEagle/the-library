## 12. The Pearl That Wasn't

There's a particular kind of silence a man learns at sea, and it isn't the absence of noise. It's the presence of everything unsaid. I've carried silences across three oceans, and the heaviest of them I'd been carrying for eleven years, folded up in my chest like a chart nobody was meant to read.

The boy—Oscar Halloran, though he wouldn't give me the name—had walked away from me on the jetty two days past with his shoulders set like a man walking into weather. I'd seen that walk before. I'd worn that walk. It's what a body does when it's spent its whole life learning which doors stay shut.

So I did what any sensible man does when the front door won't open. I went round the back.

* * *

The shell-sorting sheds sat at the edge of the company compound like a row of tired animals, long-roofed and low-shouldered, breathing out the smell of brine and mother-of-pearl. Inside, a hundred pairs of hands moved over the day's take, sorting shells by size and lustre with a speed that made the work look easy, which is the surest sign it wasn't. The noise in there was tremendous—shells rattling into bins, women calling measurements to one another over the din—and I thought, standing in the doorway, that if you wanted to say something dangerous, this was as good a place as any. Nobody could overhear. Everybody was listening anyway, in the way of people who sort pearls for a living: eyes down, ears open.

I wasn't there for the shells. I was there because the boy worked the pearl room, not the sheds, but the sheds were where the talk lived, and I needed talk. Specifically I needed a woman named Dolly Fenn, who had shucked luggers alongside Tom Halloran back when Broome was half the size it is now and twice as honest, or so she claimed, which is how you know a place has grown dishonest since.

Dolly spotted me before I'd finished crossing the threshold. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a bollard, with forearms that could crack a coconut and a face that suggested she'd already decided what she thought of me. She wiped her hands on her apron and came toward me with the unhurried gait of someone who has never once been late and never once hurried.

"You're the one asking after Hallorans," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the one," I said.

"Voss's men been asking after you." She said it flat, the way you'd report the tide. "Brack and another fellow. Been through here twice yesterday, showing your description round. Tall stranger, grey in the beard, walks like he's got a bad ankle."

"I have got a bad ankle."

"Well, they got that right at least." She jerked her chin toward the yard behind the sheds. "Come on, then. If they're going to arrest somebody, I'd rather it weren't in front of my girls. Bad for the sorting."

* * *

The yard behind the shell sheds was all broken shell and wiregrass, fenced by palings that had given up any pretense of keeping anything in or out. A couple of goats had taken possession of it, and regarded us with the flat contempt goats reserve for all humanity. Dolly sat herself down on an upturned crate with the sigh of a woman settling in for something, and I leaned against the fence, which creaked a warning I chose to ignore.

"Tom Halloran," she said. "You knew him."

"Dived with him. Off Thursday Island first, then here. Best diver I ever shared a deck with, and I'll hear no argument on it. He could hold his breath longer than sense itself, and he always knew where the shells were before the shells knew themselves." I turned my hat in my hands. "He pulled me out of a rip once, off the Ribbon Reefs. Carried me up from forty feet like I was a sack of flour. I owed him my life from that day on, and I've been trying to pay it back ever since, mostly to the wrong people."

Dolly watched me with eyes the color of shallow water over sand.

"And Mara," I said. "His wife. She was the finest letter-writer I ever knew. Kept half the fleet supplied with words home. When she went in the water—" I stopped, because my throat had closed around the old grief like a fist. Eleven years, and it still did that. "When she drowned, or when they said she drowned, I lost more than a friend. I lost the person who taught me that a man can be more than his trade."

"They said," Dolly repeated. She picked a piece of straw off her apron and considered it. "That's an interesting way to put it."

"It's the only way I can put it and keep my hands steady."

For a while there was just the sound of the goats and, faintly, the rattle of shells through the shed wall. Then Dolly looked up at me, and whatever she saw in my face must have satisfied her, because she nodded once, slow, like a door unlocking.

"Sit down, Callum Reeve. You're going to want to be sitting."

I sat.

"The night Tom died," she began, and stopped, and started again, and I understood she was choosing her words the way you choose footholds on a cliff. "It was the Lorelei that went out that day. Voss's own boat, though he'd leased her to the Meridian for salvage survey. Storm coming out of the northwest, black as a preacher's coat, and every skipper in the harbor was tying down. But Voss sent her out anyway. Said the beds couldn't wait."

"He sent divers down ahead of a storm?"

"He sent them down *rushed*. That's different and worse. No proper line tenders, no second boat standing off, half the crew green as grass. Tom went down on the last drift of the day because the tender above him panicked and hauled him fast when the squall hit. Fast hauling kills divers, Mr. Reeve. Every man in this town who knows anything about pearls knows it, and every man in this town says misadventure, because saying anything else is bad for continued employment."

My hands had gone still on my knees. "I heard the verdict," I managed. "Misadventure. Act of God."

"Act of God," Dolly agreed. "Funny thing about acts of God. They always seem to happen near Gideon Voss."

A goat wandered over and chewed thoughtfully at the fence post beside my knee. I let it. My mind was moving too fast for my body to bother with small movements.

"There's more," Dolly said. "There's always more, in this town. More is what we're rich in." She lowered her voice, though we were alone with nothing but goats and wiregrass, and somehow that made it worse. "The last drift Tom made that day—the drift that killed him—he wasn't working the survey beds. The tender's log said survey beds. But the Lorelei anchored four miles east of where the log put her. Four miles. There's a hole out there, a gut in the reef, deep water, the kind of ground nobody surveys because nobody can work it profitably. Except Tom Halloran could, because Tom Halloran was the only man alive who could hold his breath down into that dark and come back smiling."

"Why would the log lie about the anchorage?"

"Because of what was in the gut." Dolly's voice had gone very quiet now, quiet as the bottom of the sea. "They found shell down there, Mr. Reeve. A whole bed of it, untouched, virgin ground, and in among the shell—" She paused. In the shed behind us, the rattle of sorting rose and fell like surf. "In among the shell was a pearl. A big one. The biggest anyone in this town had ever seen or heard tell of. Big enough that the man who held it would never work again. Big enough that a certain harbor master might do a great many things to make sure it never reached the company's books under anyone's name but his own."

The world tilted slightly. I gripped the fence rail and the fence complained and I didn't care.

"They weighed it?" I asked. My voice sounded far away to me.

"Nobody weighed it. Nobody official. It went straight from Tom's hand to the Lorelei's strongbox, and the strongbox went ashore under guard, and the manifest that night listed cargo worth maybe forty pounds. Forty pounds. For a full day's diving by the best crew on the coast, in storm conditions, on virgin ground." Dolly spat, with great precision, between the palings. "Forty pounds."

"Where is it now?"

"Ah." She spread her hands. "Now that's the question, isn't it? The pearl vanished. The bed's location vanished. Tom vanished, into the water. And a year later Mara vanished, into the sea, same as her husband, and everybody said what terrible luck the Halloran family had with water." Her mouth twisted. "Terrible luck. Or else a man named Voss with a strongbox and a ledger and no witnesses he hadn't arranged."

I stood up. The fence groaned; a goat objected. I paced the length of the yard and back, because my legs wanted to move even if my mind wanted to run in circles.

Eleven years I'd carried the guilt of the surviving shipmate. Eleven years I'd told myself the sea took Tom Halloran, the sea being blameless in the way the sea always gets to be blameless, and Mara's drowning was grief or accident or the cruelty of weather, and none of it was anybody's doing, and therefore none of it was mine to fix. It was a comfortable arrangement, guilt without address. You could post your letters to the ocean and the ocean never wrote back.

But greed has an address. Greed has an office with a brass plate on it and a chair with its name worn into the leather.

"That's rumor," I said finally, turning back to her. "You've said it yourself—nobody weighed it, nobody official. Rumor's a dangerous cargo. It shifts in transit."

"Rumor is all we've got," Dolly said, unoffended. "This town runs on rumor the way the luggers run on wind. But I'll tell you what isn't rumor." She leaned forward, and the light caught the deep lines of her face. "Old Manny Chakrabarti, rest him, tended line on the Lorelei that day. Drank himself dead inside three years, but before he went, he told me—and only me—that the anchor bearings in the log were written in a different hand than the rest of the page. Same ink, different hand. Somebody else filled in where the Lorelei had been. And Manny said the last thing Tom said when he came up that final time, before the squall took the boat's stern away, was that he'd found something that would buy Mara the farm she always dreamed of. Something that would set his family free."

"*Free,*" I repeated. The word tasted strange. Everything in this town was owned—the boats, the beds, the boys in the counting house. Freedom was a word people used the way sailors use mermaids: beautiful, and not for touching.

"That's what he said." Dolly shrugged. "And then the storm came, and the tender hauled him wrong, and the harbor master wrote a verdict, and a family went into the water one piece at a time."

I stood very still. Somewhere beyond the sheds, the tide was turning; you could feel it in the air the way you feel a change in pressure behind the eyes.

"Dolly," I said slowly. "If what you're telling me is true—if that pearl exists, if it's hidden somewhere, unrecorded—it's proof. Proof the books are false. Proof the salvage was stolen. Proof that a man died for a strongbox."

"And proof," Dolly said, "that whoever holds that secret holds power over Gideon Voss. Which is why, Mr. Reeve, in eleven years, nobody in this town has spoken that story above a whisper, and why the whisper keeps getting shorter." She rose from her crate, joints cracking like rigging in a swell. "And why you ought to think very carefully about who you tell next. Because the last person who asked these questions loudly was Mara Halloran, and she ended up on a ship out of this port with her children scattered and her name in the death column of a shipping notice she probably read herself, laughing, in some port where the sun shines."

"Mara's alive." I said it plainly, because I was done whispering. Dolly's eyebrows climbed. "I carry her letters. Written after her supposed drowning. She's in Singapore. She sent me to find her son."

For a long moment Dolly Fenn looked at me the way you look at a chart that contradicts everything you know about the coastline. Then she laughed—one short, hard bark, more surprise than mirth.

"Well," she said. "Well. The old girl always was too clever for this town." She stuck out a forearm like a spar and I shook it, and her grip nearly took my fingers off. "Find that boy. Tell him what his father found. And Callum—" Her grip tightened. "Tell him his father didn't die of weather. He died of arithmetic. There's a difference, and the boy deserves to know which killed him."

* * *

I found Oscar at low tide, as I'd found him before, down where the mudflats gave onto the channel and the town's noise fell away behind you. He was gathering shell off the flats—company work, even unpaid, even off-shift, because a boy indentured to the Meridian learned early that usefulness was the only currency that bought safety. He heard my boots on the wet sand and stiffened, but didn't run, which I counted as progress.

"You shouldn't be here," he said, not turning. "People are watching me. Which means people are watching you."

"People have been watching me since I came off the boat. I've been followed by professionals in worse places than Broome." I came round to face him. Up close, in the low slanting light, I could see the resemblance to his mother—not in the features exactly, but in the way his eyes held yours a moment longer than was safe. "I need to tell you something, and then you're going to wish I hadn't, and then you're going to need to hear it anyway. That's generally how truth works."

He set down his basket. Slowly. Like a man setting down something that might go off.

"My father's dead," he said. "Whatever you're selling, I've heard the verdict."

"You've heard the verdict," I agreed. "Verdicts aren't the same as truths. Verdicts are written by the survivors, and in this town, survivor means employed." I took a breath, and let eleven years of carried silence start to come apart. "I dived with your father, Oscar. Thursday Island, and here. I owe him my life. And I've spent eleven years believing the sea took him, because the sea is easier to forgive than a man in a good coat. But the sea didn't take him. Sit down, lad. This will go easier sitting down."

He didn't sit. So I told him standing, watching his face go through weather of its own as I laid it out—the Lorelei, the storm, the false anchorage, the virgin bed in the deep gut four miles east of the logged position, the pearl. The pearl most of all. His face when I described it—big as a pigeon egg, some said bigger, lustrous as a moonrise—was the face of a man hearing a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

"They never entered it in the books," I said. "Forty pounds on the manifest for a fortune off the beds. Your father found it, Oscar. Found the thing that would have bought your mother her farm, bought your freedom before you were old enough to owe it to anyone. And it never reached the company's ledgers because it reached Gideon Voss's strongbox instead, and the man who found it was sent down rushed, in a squall, by a panicked tender, and the verdict said act of God." I let that sit in the salt air between us. "Your father didn't die of weather. He died of arithmetic. Someone added up what that pearl was worth and subtracted what your father was worth, and the answer came out the way answers come out in this town."

Oscar's hands had begun to shake. He looked down at them as though they belonged to someone else, and I remembered being young and learning that the ground under your feet was not the ground you'd been promised.

"The log," he said at last. His voice came out scraped thin. "The Lorelei's log. Where is it?"

"In the company registry, or what's left of it. Though I'm told—" and here I watched him carefully, because this was the hinge— "that pages have gone missing from that registry before. After the drowning year. Pages have a way of going missing when a harbor master wants them to."

Something moved behind his eyes—something quick and dark and calculating, five years of sorting silences suddenly reorganizing themselves around a new fact. He knew the registry. Of course he did. He worked among its papers. He was, as far as the Meridian was concerned, a line item in it.

"You said my mother sent letters," he said quietly. "Letters dated after she drowned."

"I did."

"And now you're telling me my father was murdered for a pearl."

"Not murdered for a pearl exactly. Murdered for a *secret*. The pearl's location. The bed in the gut. A secret like that is worth more than the stone itself, because whoever holds it can go back for more. I think Voss wanted the location out of your father and couldn't get it, and I think your mother knew what Voss was, better and earlier than anyone, and I think—" I stopped myself, because the rest was speculation, and speculation is a leaky boat. "I think there's a great deal we don't know yet. But the shape of it, lad. Look at the shape of it. A father drowned on falsified logs. A mother fled under a fake death. Two children scattered into company hands. And one berth on a steamer six weeks out, and a harbor master who's already asking questions about strangers."

The tide was coming in around our boots. Oscar stared out at the channel where the water was reclaiming the flats inch by inch, patient as a creditor.

"If it's true," he said, "if the logs are false—then there's paper somewhere that proves it. Paper doesn't drown. Paper doesn't disappear unless someone burns it, and burning leaves ash, and ash leaves traces." He turned to look at me, and for the first time since I'd landed in Broome I saw not the careful, erased company boy, but Mara Halloran's son entire—something fierce banked down behind those eyes like a fire built to burn slow and long. "My father found something that would have freed us. And they took it. And they took her. And they wrote *misadventure* in a book and called it God."

"Aye," I said. "That's the shape of it."

"Then we need the log," Oscar said. "The real one, or the copy, or the ash. Whatever exists. Because a verdict written by the guilty man isn't evidence against him. It's evidence *of* him—but only if we can prove the book lies."

I felt the old sailor's caution rise in me—slow, lad, slow, you're poking a sleeping thing—but I swallowed it, because looking at him just then I understood something that rearranged the whole voyage of my heart: this boy was not going to spend his life being useful and silent. The silence was ending. I could hear it ending, like ice going out in spring.

"There's a clerk," Oscar said, almost to himself. "Mrs. Adeyemi. She keeps the manifests. She was decent to me when decency cost something." He bent and picked up his basket, and when he straightened, the company boy was back in his posture, smooth and obedient, but his eyes had not come back from wherever they'd gone. "Low tide's short, Mr. Reeve. And I've just realized I've been sorting pearls for five years in the house that robbed my father."

He walked back toward the compound with the basket balanced on his hip, and I stood alone on the flats watching him go, feeling the weight of what we'd just lifted shift onto younger shoulders than I liked.

Behind me, the water kept coming in, covering the flats, covering the tracks, patient and indifferent as a ledger.

Six weeks, I thought. One berth. One murdered diver's son. And somewhere in the company registry—or the ashes of it—a lie waiting to be read aloud.

I only hoped we'd find it before Voss found us.
