## 13. Voss's Ledger

There is a particular pleasure in a ledger that balances to the penny, and I have spent thirty years cultivating it the way other men cultivate orchards. A ledger does not lie. A ledger does not weep at funerals or ask inconvenient questions about where its father went. A ledger is the only honest companion a man of affairs can rely upon, which is why I trust mine more than I have ever trusted any person, including—especially—the ones who share my table.

So you will appreciate that when I sat down on the evening of the fourteenth to reconcile the quarterly accounts and found myself unable to concentrate, it was not the arithmetic that troubled me. The arithmetic was flawless. It is always flawless; I employ no one in this office who would dare make it otherwise.

It was the stranger.

Callum Reeve. The name had come to me twice now—once from Brack, who reported that the man had been asking after Hallorans at the Mermaid's Rest, and once from Mrs. Adeyemi's registry log, where his signature appeared as a witness to nothing in particular, a man covering his movements with the thin paper of legitimate business. He had signed himself "agent, marine salvage." Marine salvage. In Broome, that phrase means one of two things: a man who pulls drowned ships apart for their brass, or a man who pulls drowned secrets out of them for considerably more.

I poured myself two fingers of the good whisky—the one I keep behind the false spine of the third ledger volume, because even a man's vices should be filed correctly—and I considered the matter the way I consider all matters: as an entry awaiting classification.

* * *

Let me tell you something about Gideon Voss that no biographer will ever write, chiefly because there will be no biographer: I did not begin as a harbor master. I began as a wharf boy in Fremantle, fourteen years old, counting bales in the rain for men who never learned my name. I learned early that the world keeps two ledgers. The public one, where your labor is recorded and underpaid. And the private one, kept by whoever is clever enough to keep it, where the true value of everything is written down and shown to no one.

I resolved, at fourteen, to be the keeper of the private ledger rather than a line item in someone else's.

It has taken me twenty-five years to build what I hold here. The Meridian Trading Company thinks it owns Broome's pearl trade, and in the way of large dull institutions everywhere, it believes its own paperwork. But paperwork is only ever a shadow of the real thing. The real thing is knowledge. Who found what. Who was paid what. What went over the side of a lugger at midnight and into whose boat. The company owns the jetties and the sheds and the indentures. I own the truth beneath them, and truth, properly managed, appreciates like land.

The Lorelei was my finest entry. Eleven years ago, and I can still recite it line by line, the way a bishop recites his catechism.

Thomas Halloran came up off the bottom with the find of a decade—a shell holding pearls the divers still speak of in whispers, though they speak them carefully, the way men speak near a sleeping dog. The storm was coming in. The Lorelei's master wanted to run for shelter; Halloran argued they should push through to Broome first, cargo secure, before weather closed the passage. There were words on deck. There was always going to be a decision, and I had arranged, with the modest foresight that distinguishes prudence from paranoia—I want to be very clear about that distinction, because history tends to confuse them—that the decision would be made by the man I'd paid to make it.

The ship went down in the outer channel. Three men drowned, Halloran among them. The verdict was misadventure. The company paid out the customary condolence sums, small enough to insult and large enough to close mouths. And eleven days later, when the salvage crew I had personally selected brought up what could be brought up, the manifest recorded forty-one shells recovered.

The true number was fifty-three.

Twelve shells. Do you know what twelve shells of that quality are worth, even after the buyer's cut and the Singapore passage and the silence? Enough to buy the harbor master's position outright. Enough to stop being a line item forever. I did not steal those shells. Let us be precise with our language, because precision is the whole art of respectability. I *reallocated* them. They had surfaced from the sea, which belongs to no one, and the sea has never once asked me to account for my takings. It seemed fitting that I should extend her bookkeeping methods onto dry land.

The only flaw in the arrangement—and I record flaws honestly, even in my own work—is Thomas Halloran's widow. Mara. She asked questions. Not loudly, not publicly; she was nobody's fool, and fools survive longer in Broome than questioners. But she asked, quietly, in the way of a woman doing her own arithmetic: why her husband had sailed into a storm he'd have sailed around if left to his own judgment, why the salvage crew was changed at the last hour, why the manifest figures didn't sit right against what the divers said had come up in that final shell.

She was asking the wrong man the right questions, and a woman who asks the right questions is worth ten times any stranger's inquiries, because she knows where the bodies—in this case, quite nearly her husband's—are buried.

I arranged her passage to Singapore. Generously, some might say. I prefer to say: efficiently. A grieving widow with a son newly indentured to the company and a daughter on the way—one doesn't need to know all the details of a household to manage it—one-way passage, a small settlement, and a letter of introduction that placed her in respectable employment far from anyone who might corroborate her suspicions. The story put about was drowning at sea, which had the elegant economy of being almost true. She had gone to sea. She had, in every sense that mattered to Broome, drowned.

Her son Oscar took the news the way I expected: silently. That boy has been the easiest asset on my books for five years. Never complains, never asks, works until the lamplight fails. Some men mistake that sort of quietness for contentment. I do not mistake anything, but I confess I mistook its durability.

And now a salvage agent walks into my town asking after Hallorans.

* * *

I worked late that night, which is to say I sat in my office above the harbor with the lamp turned low, listening to the water slap the pilings, and I conducted an audit—not of the accounts, which needed no auditing, but of my exposure.

Item: the Lorelei incident. Closed eleven years. Verdict accepted, families compensated, witnesses dispersed or dead. The salvage foreman, Dawes, drank himself into the cemetery three years back, taking whatever he remembered with him. Residual risk: minimal.

Item: Mara Halloran. Alive, presumably, in Singapore, but Singapore is a large pond and she is a small fish who has shown no appetite for swimming home these many years. If she wished to raise a scandal, she has had a decade of Sundays in which to do it. Residual risk: negligible.

Item: the letters—if letters exist. And here my pen hovered over the page, because this was the entry I did not like the shape of.

A stranger does not cross half an ocean to ask after a dead woman's family out of sentiment. Sentiment is cheap and stays where it is bred. Reeve had come with purpose, and purpose in my experience wears one of three coats: revenge, blackmail, or trade. Revenge requires a grievance, and I could not place him in any grievance of mine. Trade I could handle; everything is tradeable at the right figure. Which left blackmail, and blackmail means he already knows something, and knowing something means someone talked, and talking means my careful ledger has a loose page somewhere fluttering in the wind.

Or—he knew nothing yet, and had come looking. A treasure hunter following rumor the way sharks follow offal. Rumors about the Lorelei have circulated for years; drowned ships grow richer in the telling, the way fish do. If that were all he carried, then he was dangerous not for what he knew but for what he might learn, and there was exactly one person in Broome positioned to teach him.

Oscar Halloran.

You see the geometry of it now, I hope. A stranger asking after Hallorans, and in this town there are precisely two Hallorans left: a girl sorting shells in the sheds, and a young man in my keeping who spent his childhood aboard the Lorelei and his adolescence swallowing its grief. Ask either of them the wrong question—or the right one—and eleven years of careful bookkeeping begins to read aloud.

I am not a cruel man. I want to say that plainly, because the magistrate, should it ever come to that—and it will not—will hear otherwise from people with grievances. Cruelty is inefficient. It generates resentment, and resentment generates testimony. Everything I have done in Broome has been aimed at the opposite: generating silence. I have fed widows, clothed orphans, endowed the church roof, and settled more debts quietly than the bank has recorded openly. The town calls me fair. Fairness, maintained consistently over decades, is simply cruelty with better accounting.

But I will not be sentimental about the boy, either. Sentiment, in a man of my position, is a leak in the hull. Oscar Halloran is an asset. Assets exist to serve the ledger. If his silence serves it, excellent; it has served it well for five years. If his silence begins instead to serve a stranger from Singapore, then he ceases to be an asset and becomes a liability, and the difference between those two categories is, in my experience, the entire content of wisdom.

I finished the whisky. I did not pour another. Clarity is best taken neat.

* * *

Brack came at my summons, smelling of the jetty and his usual breakfast cigar. He stood in the doorway the way he does, hat in hand, deference worn like a borrowed coat.

"You wanted me, Mr. Voss."

"Sit. No—stand, you'll wrinkle the chair." I let him stand. "Our visitor. Reeve. Where does he lodge?"

"The Mermaid's Rest. Takes his meals alone. Talks to the fishermen, buys rounds, laughs in the right places." Brack shrugged. "He's friendly."

"Friendly men frighten me more than surly ones. Surly men announce themselves. Friendly ones get invited inside." I looked out at the harbor, where the luggers rode their moorings like patient animals. "What has he actually asked?"

"About the Halloran family. Whether any kin remain. Where they're to be found. He mentioned the mother—asked if she'd truly been lost at sea. Asked it casual, like a man making conversation."

"Nothing else?"

"He asked about the Lorelei wreck. Said salvage was his trade, wondered if anything was worth raising from her. Old mate of mine told him she'd been picked clean years ago."

"Did he believe that?"

Brack considered. "He thanked him and bought him another beer."

Which is precisely what a man does when he intends to verify a claim independently. I turned the information over. A salvage agent, genuinely in trade, might dive the wreck—there was brass and fittings enough to justify the expense to a small operator. A salvage agent in search of something larger would also dive the wreck. Both men end up in the same water. Only what they bring up differs.

"Watch him," I said. "Quietly. Not following—watching. I want to know who he speaks to, how long he speaks to them, and whether he so much as looks at the company office. Especially—" I paused, weighing how much to say even to Brack, who is loyal the way a dog is loyal, which is to say entirely but without judgment, "—especially whether he goes anywhere near the shell-sorting sheds, or the counting house yard, or any Halloran."

"You think he's trouble, sir?"

"I think he's a question," I said. "And questions in this town have historically been answered one of two ways. We'll see which kind this one turns out to be."

Brack nodded and made for the door, relieved to have simple instructions. I stopped him with one finger raised.

"And Brack. Nothing heavy-handed. This man is a guest in port under the company's flag. If he comes to harm while flying that flag, there are authorities in Perth who write letters. Whatever handling is required must look like nothing at all. Do you understand the standard?"

"Looks like nothing at all," Brack repeated, the way he repeats things, which is to say without comprehension but with commitment.

"Good. Off you go."

* * *

Alone again, I did something I rarely permit myself: I opened the old volume. Bottom shelf, behind the company histories nobody reads, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. My private ledger—not the metaphorical one I've been describing to you, but an actual book, slim, unmarked on its cover, containing eleven years of entries in my own hand. Receipts that appear nowhere else. Figures that reconcile with nothing official. Names of men paid for services the company never contracted.

I turned to the Lorelei pages and read them the way a priest rereads scripture, hunting not new meaning but confirmation that the old meaning holds.

Halloran, T. — final dive, shell of unusual yield. Confirmed by diver's testimony (Mbeki, R.), witnessed on deck.
Salvage recovery: 53 shells actual; 41 recorded. Disposition of remainder: [entry redacted in my own careful fashion].
Storm forecast: verified with telegraph office, Fremantle, prior date.
Lorelei master: instructed re: schedule. Instructed by whom — see separate memorandum, not retained.

That last line is the closest I ever came to writing a confession. *Not retained.* I burned the memorandum within the week, of course. A man who keeps incriminating documents is a man planning to be caught. But the phrase remains, a little tombstone in the margin, marking the grave of the version of Gideon Voss who thought he might someday need to explain himself.

I had not opened this book in two years. Opening it now felt less like caution than like superstition, and I dislike superstition in myself almost as much as I dislike it in others. But there it is: a stranger arrives asking after Hallorans, and the harbor master reaches for a book he hasn't touched since the last stranger who asked after Hallorans. Draw from that whatever conclusion you like. I draw the practical one: when the past knocks, you check that the door is locked, and then you go out and meet the knocker wearing your best face.

Because that is the other lesson of thirty years, and it may be the most important one: surveillance is defensive, and defense cedes initiative. Brack would watch. Watching would tell me what Reeve did. But what I needed was to know what Reeve *wanted*, and the fastest route to that knowledge runs directly through the man himself. A dinner. An introduction. The famous Voss courtesy—every man in Broome knows I receive visitors generously; it is half my armor. Let him sit at my table. Let him drink my wine and tell me, in the loosened language of a guest, precisely which rumor carried him across the water. Men confess to hosts what they would never confess to interrogators. Hospitality is the oldest intelligence instrument in the world, and unlike most instruments, it improves the host's reputation for using it.

If he was a mere treasure hunter, I would feed him a false chart and a modest find and send him on his way grateful. If he was something worse—if he carried letters, if he had spoken to Mara Halloran in Singapore, if somewhere across that water a woman had finally decided to spend her decade of silence—then I would know soon enough, at my own table, over my own wine.

And if it came to that, there were arrangements available. Not crude ones. Crude arrangements create martyrs, and martyrs generate the very questions they were meant to bury. No—the elegant solution, should one be required, is always the same: control the paperwork. If Reeve arrived carrying proof, then proof could be discredited. Documents age badly; memory ages worse; and a magistrate presented with contradictory papers will side, nine times in ten, with the party who owns the printing. I had spent years positioning myself as the owner of Broome's printing, in every sense.

I closed the private ledger and returned it to its shelf, and I noted—with the mild self-observation I permit myself at the end of long evenings—that my hands were perfectly steady and had been throughout. Good. Fear that shows in the hands is fear that ends up in testimony.

Still, when I blew out the lamp, I stood a moment in the dark listening to the harbor. Water on wood, the creak of moored boats, the ordinary night sounds of an enterprise running exactly as designed. Somewhere out there, a stranger slept with salt in his clothes and questions in his pocket.

I composed the invitation in my head before sleep took me. Wednesday next. Dinner. The good claret, not the excellent one—you save the excellent claret for men who've already agreed to help you.

A question, I thought, settling into bed. Very well. I had answered harder ones with less warning. The Lorelei herself had tried to bury my finest transaction at the bottom of the channel, and I had simply sent better divers down after it.

Whatever Callum Reeve intended to raise from that water—

—I intended to reach it first.

On Tuesday morning, before I had sent the invitation, Brack brought me the day's report, and one line in it turned my blood to harbor ice: the stranger had been seen, the evening previous, standing at the fence of the counting house yard, watching the clerks' quarters.

Watching, Brack said, the window of one particular room.

Mine were not the rooms he meant.
