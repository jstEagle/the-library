## 14. Sunday Bells

There are three places in Broome a man can be seen without anyone thinking twice about it: the jetty at dawn, the pub after dark, and the church on Sunday. The trouble with the church is that it's the only one of the three where you're expected to be *seen* — where the whole architecture of the building exists to turn every face in the room toward yours.

I hadn't been inside St. Augustine's since I was eleven years old, which was the year they buried my father under a slab of local stone that cost more than he'd earned in his last two seasons. I remembered the sermon being about the sea and its mercies. I remembered thinking that the sea had shown precious little mercy, and that whoever wrote sermons had clearly never been asked to dive for their supper.

I went back now because of paper.

The parish kept its records in a vestry cabinet — baptisms, marriages, deaths, all of it entered in a copperplate hand by men who believed God was watching their penmanship. Somewhere in that cabinet was proof that Mara Halloran had given birth to an Oscar, son of Thomas Halloran, diver, drowned 1889. That was the document Callum said I needed, or something like it, if I was ever going to convince the shipping office that I was kin to a woman alive in Singapore rather than simply property of the Meridian Trading Company.

The problem was that the parish answered to the town, and the town answered to Gideon Voss the way a tide answers the moon. You didn't walk into a vestry and ask for your own history unless you wanted that history reported upstairs by suppertime.

So I did what any sensible man does when he needs to case a church. I attended the service.

* * *

I'll say this for Sunday in Broome: it was the one morning the town put on clothes. Pearlers came ashore scrubbed pink, their collars stiff as hull plating. The Japanese divers sat on the left side, the Malay crews on the right, the Europeans up front where the fans could reach them, and everyone pretended this arrangement was geography rather than arithmetic. Mrs. Adeyemi would have called it a ledger written in seating. She had a way of saying things like that, flat as a ruled line, that made you see them forever after.

I arrived late on purpose. Late is invisible; early is memorable. I slid into the back pew behind a family of shell-graders whose hats formed a kind of hedge, and I let the liturgy wash over me like warm water over a man who hasn't bathed properly in a week. There is something in the old words — the ones about the valley of the shadow, the ones about being led beside still waters — that gets into a body whether you believe them or not. My mother used to mouth them along with the curate, her lips moving like she was tasting each phrase. I caught myself doing it and stopped, embarrassed, though there was no one behind me to see.

The curate was new since my childhood, a thin nervous man named Mr. Pellow who read his sermon off cards and pronounced "providence" as though it were a fish he wasn't sure was edible. But halfway through, when he announced that the records office would be open after the service for "baptismal inquiries and certificate matters," my heart performed a small treacherous leap. Certificate matters. Four words, and suddenly the whole dusty cabinet in the vestry was within reach of a man with a plausible face and a shilling for fees.

I began composing my story right there between the collects. A man applying for work down south needed proof of age. Simple. Men lied to churches all the time; churches were built for it, in a way — half the congregation confessing sins they intended to repeat by Tuesday.

Then the hymn started, and the hedge of hats shifted, and I saw her.

* * *

She was sitting four rows up on the aisle, and I knew her before I knew how I knew her — the way you know a wave from your own beach. Nell. Eleanor Halloran, though nobody in Broome had called her anything but Nell since the day Aunt Bridget signed for her keep. Fifteen now, maybe sixteen. She'd grown into the Halloran stubbornness so completely that it seemed less like a trait than an occupation. Her dress was clean but mended at the cuffs in the particular diagonal stitch of someone who mends fast and doesn't care who notices. Her chin was set at the angle our father used to set his when a paymaster tried to short him.

She sang. That undid me a little. She sang the hymn with her whole chest, slightly ahead of the organ, loud enough that a woman in front of her turned around, and Nell stared the woman back into her prayer book without missing a note. I thought: *that's Mara's voice.* Not the sound of it — I couldn't remember the sound anymore, God forgive me — but the manner of it. The refusal to be quieted.

I should have stayed behind my hedge of hats. Every sensible instinct I owned, five years' worth of instincts trained by the Meridian Trading Company, told me to stay small, stay unseen, stay safe. But the letter was folded against my ribs inside my shirt, and it had made reckless creatures of us both, that letter — me for carrying it, her for existing on the far end of it — and before I could argue myself out of it, I was moving up the aisle through the crowd spilling out of the pews.

"Nell."

She turned with the wariness of a girl who has learned that men saying her name usually want something. Up close she was thinner than I'd imagined, with a smudge of shell-dust still in the crease of her ear that no Saturday scrubbing had reached. Her eyes went over my face the way a sorter goes over a tray of pearls — quick, dismissive, then back again, slower.

"I know you," she said. "You're the one who—" She stopped. Recalculated. Whatever she knew of me, she'd decided not to spend it in public. "You're Oscar."

"Hello, Nell."

"That's a funny thing," she said, "you knowing my name."

"It's not such a mystery. We've got the same one, near enough."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softness exactly, more like a door opening onto a dark room where something valuable was stored. She glanced past me at the dispersing congregation, at Mr. Pellow shaking hands at the door like a man greeting passengers, at the two Voss men who always materialized wherever anything interesting happened, standing by the lych-gate smoking and pretending not to watch.

"Not here," she said quietly. It wasn't a question and it wasn't agreement either; it was tactics. Our mother's daughter down to the bone. "Walk me as far as the shell sheds. Nobody thinks anything of a sorter walking with a tally-man."

"I'm not a tally-man."

"You are today." And she stepped out into the white glare of the morning, leaving me to follow, which was, I was beginning to understand, simply how things would go between us.

* * *

We walked the length of Dampier Terrace with a careful yard of air between us, two people who happened to be going the same direction, nothing more. She smelled of soap and brine and, faintly, of the kerosene they burned in the sorting sheds. She walked fast, the way people walk when stopping feels dangerous.

"Aunt Bridget says you're company property," she said, eyes forward. "That you sold yourself after your da drowned. Is that true?"

"Indentured. Five years served, one to run."

"That's a long word for the same thing." She kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary violence. "She says it like it's a lesson. *See what comes of diving, Nell. See what comes of Halloran blood.* She says it with gin in her tea, mind, so I take her lessons at a discount."

Despite everything, I nearly laughed. "How is she?"

"Drunk by noon on Sundays, sober enough Mondays to remember she was drunk, and generous with both versions of herself." Nell shrugged. "She loves me the way weather loves a coast. Irregularly, and mostly destructive."

"She raised you, though. After—"

"After Ma died. Yes." Something in her tone closed like a ledger line drawn firm. "And you? What do they tell me about you? Nothing. Five years, Oscar. You worked the same beach as me. I've watched you cross the compound yard a thousand times, head down, hands full, never once looking sideways. I used to make up stories about why. That you were proud. That you were simple. That you were punished for something." She finally turned and looked at me straight, and her eyes were brown, our mother's brown, and furious. "What was the truth?"

"The truth," I said slowly, "is that looking sideways is expensive in Broome. I learned the price young and paid it in silence."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's a down payment."

She huffed — almost a laugh, strangled at birth — and we came to the corner where the track bent toward the sheds and the smell of the shell heaps rose up, sweet and rotten together, like the whole town distilled. She stopped in the shade of a casuarina and faced me, arms crossed, and I understood that whatever I gave her next would be weighed to the grain.

"Why now?" she said. "Five years of head-down, and today you walk across a church to say my name. What do you want?"

And there it was, the fork in the road. I could give her the safe lie — that I'd felt fraternal, that church does that to a man — and keep the letter, the berth, the whole burning secret buttoned under my shirt where it had lived for ten days. Or I could start spending the truth, here in the shade, with Voss's men a hundred yards behind us and the sheds full of ears ahead.

I thought about what Callum had said, that night on the jetty with the letters spread between us like contraband: *Secrets sink ships, lad. Not because they're heavy. Because everybody aboard is bailing in a different direction.*

"Nell," I said, "how would you feel about having a mother?"

The question landed on her like a dropped anchor. I watched it go through her — surprise, then anger at the surprise, then something rawer underneath that she fought down the way she fought everything, jaw first.

"Don't," she said. "Don't you dare play games with that."

"It's not a game. It's the opposite of a game. It's the most serious thing I've ever held in my hands." I touched my chest, where the letter lay. "But I can't say more here, and I can't say it fast, because there are men in this town who will do anything — *anything*, Nell — to keep what I know from getting out. So I'm asking you for three days and a place to talk. Somewhere Bridget won't be, somewhere Brack isn't watching."

"Brack." She said the name like spitting a pip. "He came round Thursday. Asking about you, as it happens. Whether you'd been strange lately. Whether strangers had come round." Her eyes narrowed. "I told him you were the dullest man in Broome. He seemed to believe it. Was I wrong to believe it too?"

"Ask me again in three days."

"I hate riddles."

"I know," I said. "You get that from her."

That stopped her cold. I saw her catch it — the pronoun, the *her*, hanging in the salt air between us — and I saw her decide, with visible effort, not to grab at it in the open street. Instead she stuck out her hand, formal as a contract, which was so absurd and so entirely her that my throat went tight.

"Thursday," she said. "Low tide, the rocks past the old Chinese garden. Bridget drinks herself insensible by six. And Oscar—" Her hand gripped mine, hard, calloused, a child's bones with a worker's skin. "If this is cruelty dressed up as kindness, I'll never forgive you. I don't have enough left over for forgiving."

"It's not cruelty," I said. "It's the best news anyone in this family has had in nine years. That's a promise."

She let go, turned, and walked toward the sheds with her spine straight as a mast. At the door she paused — just a moment, just long enough to look back — and whatever passed across her face in that instant was the closest thing to hope I'd seen on a Halloran since before the Lorelei went down.

I stood under the casuarina a while after she'd gone, listening to the bells finish their fading, and told myself the plan was holding. Three days. One conversation on the rocks. Then everything.

I didn't yet know that Nell would go home that afternoon and put the question to Bridget over the gin bottle — *what really happened to our mother* — or that the answer, when it finally came loose, would crack open a truth neither of us was braced for: that Mara Halloran hadn't died at sea at all, that she had fled Broome pregnant and afraid, and that somebody in this town had paid for her passage.

Somebody whose name, when Bridget finally slurred it out, would make the blood in my veins go cold as ballast water.
