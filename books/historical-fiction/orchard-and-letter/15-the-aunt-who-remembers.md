## 15. The Aunt Who Remembers

My aunt Bridie had two states of being: sober and vicious, or drunk and honest. Most people preferred the first, because at least then you knew where the knife was pointed. I'd learned to prefer the second, in a grim sort of way, because drunk Bridie told the truth, and the truth was the only thing in that house that ever came free.

That Thursday she came home from the Continental with her hat on backwards and her opinion of the world already drafted. I was mending a net by lamplight because mending nets paid threepence more than mending shirts, and threepence was threepence when your aunt drank your wages faster than you could count them.

"You," she announced from the doorway, "have your mother's chin."

I didn't look up. Looking up encouraged her. "You've got the publican's chin, by the look of you."

"Don't be clever." She dropped into the chair opposite me with the grace of a sack of flour going over a wharf rail. The chair complained. So did she. "Cleverness never fed anyone."

"Neither has sentimentality, and yet here we are, drowning in it."

She squinted at me across the lamp. In that light, with the drink on her, she looked older than her years and younger than her sins, which is a trick I've never managed to work out. Her hand went to the bottle she'd smuggled home inside her coat — she thought I didn't know about the coat trick; I'd known since I was nine — and she poured with the concentration of a woman defusing something.

"Your mother had that same mouth," she said. "Always answering back. Drove your father mad."

"Which father?" I said, before my brain could stop my tongue. It's a question I'd asked maybe twice in my life, both times sober, both times met with a silence so complete you could hear the sea turning over outside. Drunk Bridie was different territory. Drunk Bridie answered things.

She didn't answer this time either. But she did something worse. She laughed.

It wasn't a big laugh. Just a huff of it, bitter as barksap, escaping through her nose while she looked into her glass like it owed her money. And it sat there between us, that laugh, doing damage.

"What's funny?" I asked.

"Nothing." She drank. "Nothing's funny. Nothing's been funny since 1886."

I put down the netting needle. That was the year. Everyone in this town had a year they didn't talk about — the cyclone year, the fever year, the year the Lorelei went down with half its crew. For our family it was simpler and crueller: 1886 was the year my mother drowned.

Or so I'd been told, in the flat way people tell children terrible things so they'll stop asking. *The ship went down off Timor. There were no survivors. God rest her.* Aunt Bridie had delivered it once, aged eleven, and never again, and I'd built my whole understanding of myself on top of that single sentence like a shack on a sandbar.

"Bridie." I used her Christian name, which I saved for emergencies, like a flare. "What happened in 1886?"

"The ship sank." She said it automatically, the catechism version, and then her face did something complicated — guilt trying to climb over drink and losing badly. "The ship sank," she repeated, softer, "and your mother wasn't on it."

The lamp hissed. Outside, some drunk was singing near the jetty, badly, the way men sing when they're grieving something they won't name.

"Say that again," I said.

"I didn't say anything." She was already retreating, pulling the drink around her like a shawl. "You're putting words in an old woman's mouth."

"You're forty-three and you said what you said." My heart was going hard against my ribs, the way it does when you're frightened and furious at the same time and can't split the difference. "Mum wasn't on the ship."

"Nell—"

"*Bridie.*"

And there it was, the flare, burning through all her defences. She looked at me then properly, and I saw her decide — I actually watched it happen, the moment the drink tipped the scales and the truth came up like a diver too long underwater, gasping, bloated, alive.

"She wasn't on it," Bridie said. "I helped her pack."

* * *

I want to tell you I reacted well. I want to tell you I sat there with dignity and asked sensible questions in a level voice. What I actually did was stand up so fast the lamp rocked and shadows swung round the room like startled birds, and I heard my own voice come out of me thin and strange:

"You helped her *pack?*"

"Keep your voice down."

"You told me she drowned! You stood in a church and let them say a mass over an empty sea! You took her wages — you took *my* wages — and every year on the anniversary you cried into your gin like—" I couldn't finish. The sentences kept collapsing under their own weight.

"Sit down," Bridie said, and for once there was no venom in it, just exhaustion, just an old tired woman holding a glass in a hot little room. "Sit down and I'll tell it, since apparently I'm telling it tonight whether I like it or not."

I sat. Not because she told me to. Because my legs had opinions of their own.

She looked into her glass a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — flattened out, gone somewhere else, back thirteen years to wherever she kept it buried.

"It was winter. You'd have been barely walking. Your brother—" she stopped, corrected herself with visible effort, "—Oscar was four, nearly five. Your mother came to me at night, no warning, with a bag half-packed and a look on her face I'd only seen once before, the night your father's boat came in without him." She meant Oscar's father. She always made that distinction, even drunk, even now. Halloran's ghost had its own wing in Bridie's memory; mine, whoever he'd been, didn't rate a mention.

"What look?" I asked, because if I didn't ask small questions I'd start screaming large ones.

"Fright," she said simply. "Not the fright of weather. The fright of *men*. She said — God forgive me, Nell, I've said this to no living soul — she said, 'They know what Tom found, and they'll take it out of the children if I don't go.'"

Tom. Thomas Halloran, the diver, the drowned man, the saint whose death had been explained to me in one sentence along with hers. *Misadventure,* the coroner wrote. *The storm took him.*

"And you believed her?"

"I didn't believe anything. I was twenty-nine and stupid and scared. She was my sister-in-law and she was crying and she said, 'Bridie, if anyone asks, I'm dead. Let me be dead. Dead women's daughters get left alone.'" Bridie drained the glass and refilled it, hands not steady. "So I let her be dead. I packed her bag. I walked her down to the beach at low tide where a lugger was waiting, and a man I'd never seen before or since handed me an envelope and said, 'This covers her passage and a bit besides. Tell no one.' And I took it." Her jaw worked. "God help me, I took it."

"How much?"

"Enough to keep us a year. More than a year." She wouldn't meet my eyes. "I told everyone she'd sailed on the *Kestrel* and the *Kestrel* went down, and after a while everyone stopped asking, because grief bores people eventually, and I drank the envelope, Nell. Year by year, I drank it. Is that what you wanted to hear? Is that the shape of your aunt?"

It wasn't. The shape of my aunt was something I hadn't finished seeing yet. Because my mind had snagged on a detail and wouldn't let go, worrying it like a dog with a bone.

"Pregnant," I said.

Bridie blinked. "What?"

"You said she fled. You said she was crying and frightened and running from men who wanted what Tom found." I leaned forward. "Was she carrying, Bridie? When she ran?"

The silence stretched so long the singing man outside gave up mid-verse. Bridie set down her glass very carefully, the way drunks do when they're trying to prove to themselves they're still in command of their own hands.

"How would you know to ask that?"

"Because I do sums," I said. "Because I've done them my whole life without knowing why. Because you never once told me who my father was, not drunk, not sober, not dying, and because you just called Oscar 'your brother' and then corrected yourself like the word burned your tongue." My voice was shaking now and I let it. "When did she come to you, exactly? What month?"

"August." Barely a whisper. "Late August."

I was born in May. Do the arithmetic yourself; I've done it enough for both of us. Late August to May is nine months, give or take a week, and a woman doesn't flee across water in the middle of the night with a bag unless she's fleeing for two.

"So she was pregnant," I said. "With me. And someone paid her passage. Someone with money, someone connected, someone who knew what Tom Halloran found down there in the dark." I stood again, slower this time, because the floor felt less reliable than it had. "Who, Bridie? Who paid?"

"I don't know." For once she sounded like she meant it. "A gentleman. Gloves. Town clothes but salt-stained boots — I remember the boots, don't ask me why, I remember the boots. He didn't give a name. He gave an envelope and he looked at me like I was furniture and he said the family would be provided for, and I was young and greedy and I believed 'provided for' meant more than one envelope." She laughed again, that same bitter huff. "Provided for. Thirteen years of shell-sorting wages and your aunt's gin. Some providing."

I turned away from her. I needed a wall to look at instead of her face, and our wall had precious little to recommend it except that it hadn't lied to me for thirteen years.

Here's what I knew, standing there with my palms flat against the boards: my mother was alive. Somewhere past the horizon, past Timor, past every map I'd ever memorised in the mission school, Mara Halloran was breathing and walking and presumably remembering, and she had let us believe she was at the bottom of the sea. She had chosen it. Chosen the empty mass, the false grave, the two children growing up on other people's charity and their own stubbornness.

And someone in Broome — someone with gloves and salt-stained boots and access to luggers at midnight — had helped her disappear, and that same someone's town had swallowed her husband whole the year before and called it weather.

I thought of Oscar then. I don't know why him first, except that he was the other half of everything, the quiet boy grown into a quieter man, sorting pearls for the company that owned him, and I suddenly understood — the way you understand a thing all at once, whole, like cold water — that whatever my mother had run from, it had never stopped running after the ones she left behind. It had taken Tom. It had taken Oscar's freedom. It had spent thirteen years taking small pieces of me, threepence at a time.

"Nell." Bridie's voice behind me, wretched. "Nellie, love—"

"Don't." I picked up my shawl. "Don't 'love' me tonight. Tonight you're just the woman who drank my mother's fare and called it keeping us safe."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed, opening the door onto the warm dark and the smell of the tide flats. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. That appears to be the family trade."

I walked out without my hat, which for me constitutes hysteria. The moon was up over the mangroves and the mud smelled rich and rotten, and I walked fast, past the shell sheds, past the church with its locked records and its comfortable lies, down toward the water where the town runs out of excuses.

Because here's the thing I hadn't said out loud, even to myself, until my feet found the sand: if my mother was alive, then somebody in this town might know it besides Bridie. Somebody with gloves. Somebody whose boots remembered the beach at low tide. And if the letter of the law said Mara Halloran was dead, then the parish register said it too, and the company registry said it, and every document that could prove otherwise was locked in a drawer owned by a man named Voss.

I stood at the waterline letting the shallows pull at my ankles, and I made myself a promise with the moon for witness: I was done being a girl who did sums she didn't understand. Whatever it cost, whatever drawer it lived in, I was going to find the paper that proved my mother had a pulse.

I was still standing there, soaked to the shin and shaking with purpose, when I heard footsteps on the sand behind me — unhurried, deliberate, familiar in a way that made the hair rise on my arms before my brain caught up.

I turned. Oscar stood at the edge of the lamplight, holding something folded in his hand, and his face told me he'd come looking for me, and that what he carried was going to change the size of the world.
