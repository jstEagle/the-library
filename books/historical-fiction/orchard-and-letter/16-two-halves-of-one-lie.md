## 16. Two Halves of One Lie

I had rehearsed it eleven times on the walk from the jetty to the shell-sorting sheds, and every version was a lie.

That's the thing about telling the truth: you spend all your preparation deciding which parts of it to soften. *Your mother is alive* — too blunt, said at speed. *I have news about Mara Halloran* — cowardly, and she'd see through it in a breath. *Sit down first* — no one in the history of the world has ever sat down calmly after being asked to sit down.

In the end I used none of them, because Nell saw me coming across the yard and folded her arms like a customs officer.

"You've been hovering," she said. "Three days you've been hovering. If you've got something to say, say it, or go hover somewhere the tide can have you."

There it was. The aunt's tongue, sharpened on a whetstone of gin. I'd forgotten — or told myself I'd forgotten — how much she looked like our mother when she was angry. Same chin lifted at exactly the angle that dares you. Same eyes going flat and bright at once, like water over a pearl.

"Walk with me," I said.

"I'm working."

"The shells will keep."

"They always keep. That's the whole trick of them." But she wiped her hands on her apron and came anyway, because underneath the armor Nell was curious the way other people were hungry — constantly, and against their better judgment.

We walked out past the sheds toward the mangroves, where the mud sucked at your boots and nobody lingered unless they had business being unobserved. Gulls argued over something dead. The air smelled of salt and rot and green things fighting for footing, which is Broome's whole character in a sentence.

"Nell," I said. "Do you remember much about Mum?"

She stopped walking so abruptly I nearly went past her.

"That's not a fair question," she said.

"It's the only question there is."

"She died." Her voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavy across ice. "At sea. When I was four. Auntie keeps the card somewhere, the memorial card, though mostly she uses it to prop up the table leg that wobbles. Why are you asking me this? Oscar, why are you asking me this?"

Because I'd carried the letter for six days like a coal in my chest, burning a hole shaped exactly like my own cowardice. Because Callum Reeve had said *tell her or don't, lad, but a secret kept from family rots faster than fish.* Because Mrs. Adeyemi had looked at me over her ledger and said, in that dry voice that made kindness sound like arithmetic, *"You cannot build anything on half a foundation, Mr. Halloran. It will fall on whoever is standing closest when it goes."*

And because Nell was standing closest.

"Come and sit down," I said.

"No."

"All right." I sat down myself, on a fallen mangrove trunk furred with salt-crust, and looked up at her. "Then stand. But I'm going to tell you something, and I'd rather you heard it sitting."

"Oscar—"

"Mum is alive."

The gulls went on arguing. The tide went on doing whatever the tide does regardless of human catastrophe. Nell stood absolutely still for the space of three heartbeats — I counted them, God help me, the way I count everything now, pearls and ledgers and heartbeats — and then she laughed.

It was a horrible laugh. Short and hard, with nothing in it but disbelief wearing anger's coat.

"No," she said. "No, you don't get to — Auntie tells that joke when she's drunk. *Your mum's probably living it up in Singapore, girl, eating off plates.* She thinks it's funny. You think it's funny?"

"I'm not joking."

"You're standing in the mud making jokes about our dead mother, and you want me to laugh—"

"Nell." I took the letter out of my shirt. Callum's oilcloth wrapping, tied with the tarred string he used for everything, because Callum trusted tarred string more than he trusted most governments. "Read it."

She didn't move. So I unfolded it myself and held it out, and after a moment — a long moment, long enough for the wind to change and come at us off the water carrying the smell of the drying pans — she snatched it out of my hand the way you snatch something you're afraid will be taken back.

I watched her read. I know the letter by heart now; I could recite it in the dark. *They tell me Oscar is alive and indentured to the Company, and my heart breaks twice over, once for what was done and once for what I could not prevent. Ask him if he remembers the orchard behind the old house, and the mangoes we could never afford to let ripen. Tell him his mother counts the years by his birthdays.*

Nell read it once fast, then again slow, her lips moving on the second pass like a child sounding out scripture. Then she lowered it and looked at me, and her face was doing several things at once, none of them settled, like weather over the bay.

"This is her hand," she said quietly. "I know her hand. She wrote me — before. Little notes. *Mind the pot, Nelly-bird.* This is her hand."

"Yes."

"She's alive."

"Yes."

"In Singapore."

"Yes."

"And you've had this—" and here the weather turned, all of it, every unsettled thing in her face collapsing into one hard black squall, "—for how long?"

The question I'd been dreading for six days. There was no honest answer that wasn't damning, so I gave the honest one.

"Nine days."

"Nine." She said it the way you'd say a number in a debt collection. "Nine days. Nine days you've known our mother is alive, and you've spent them hovering. Watching me sort shells. Asking me how Auntie is, like a man checking on a horse he means to buy. Nine days, Oscar, while I went to church and lit a candle for a woman who is eating breakfast on the other side of the water!"

"Nell—"

"Don't you *Nell* me!" Her voice cracked out over the mudflat and a gull lifted off, offended. "You don't know what it's been. You got five years with her. Five years and an orchard and mangoes you couldn't afford to let ripen — I got four years and then a card propping up a table leg! And you knew. You stood in the sorting shed watching me work and you *knew*, and you let me go on grieving her. Do you know what I did last Sunday? I prayed for her soul. Out loud. In front of Father Doherty. I asked God to be kind to a woman who is *alive*, and He knew, presumably, because God knows everything, and everyone in that church knew but me!"

"I was trying to find out if it was true."

"It's in her *hand!*"

"It's dated four years after they buried her, yes, and I am not a man who can afford to believe things just because they're written down. I needed to know who wrote it. I needed to know why. I needed—" My voice did something humiliating, cracked right down the middle like bad rope under load, and I hated it, and I kept going. "I needed to know it wasn't bait."

That stopped her. Not gently — nothing about Nell was gentle — but it stopped her.

"Bait," she repeated.

"There are men in this town, Nell, who would very much like to know what I know and who I've been talking to. Men who sign papers. Men whose clerks enter my name in ledgers under *assets*. If I'd run to you on day one waving a letter, we'd both be in Voss's office by now, and not as visitors."

"Gideon Voss." She spat the name with real accuracy, which surprised me until I remembered she'd grown up on the aunt's gossip, and the aunt's gossip ran to harbor men and their sins the way rivers run to the sea. "What's Voss got to do with Mum?"

And there it was. The second lie I'd been rehearsing around. Because the full truth was: we don't know yet, but everything points at him, and the man who arranged your father's drowning may well be the man who paid your mother's passage, and I have spent nine nights staring at the ceiling assembling those two facts into shapes I don't want them to make.

"Auntie knows something," I said instead. "Callum thinks so. Something about who paid for Mum's ticket."

"Callum." Her eyes narrowed. "Who's Callum?"

"A friend of Mum's. From the ships. He brought the letter."

"A stranger turns up asking questions, hands you a letter saying our mother's alive, and you believe him enough to hide it from me for nine days, but not enough to tell me?" She flung the letter back at my chest — not threw, flung, there's a difference and the difference is contempt — and wrapped her arms around herself. "You're just like her, you know that? Keeping things. Deciding what people can bear. Well, I'm not a child, Oscar. I haven't been a child since the day they told me she was dead and Auntie poured herself the first of about four thousand drinks over it."

"I know."

"You don't know anything. You were gone. You went into the Company's house and became one of their — their *assets* — and I stayed here with her ghost and her bottles, and you want credit for protecting me now? From what? From knowing our mother loves us? That's the terrible thing you were guarding me against?"

The mud stank. The gulls resumed their argument over the dead thing. Somewhere behind the sheds a bell rang the shift change, and neither of us moved.

"I was afraid," I said finally. It came out smaller than I meant it to. Most true things do. "Not of Voss. Of this. Of your face right now. I've spent five years learning which silences keep me safe, Nell, and I forgot how to tell the difference between a silence that protects and one that just — sits on people. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I let you pray for a living woman. That was mine to carry and I made you carry it blind."

She stared at me. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, the way her eyes always were, even at four years old, even crying she looked like she was filing a complaint about the crying.

"Say the rest," she said. "All of it. Every piece. If you leave one thing out I'll know, and I'll never forgive it, and I mean that, Oscar, I will take it to my grave and spit on it there."

So I told her. All of it, standing in the mangrove mud with the tide coming in behind us like a slow witness. Callum Reeve and his promise to a dying woman — no, not dying, I corrected myself, a *determined* woman, a woman who had hired a sailor the way other people hire lawyers. The Sarawak Star, six weeks out, one assisted berth for verified kin. The parish records and the company registry standing between us and it like two locked doors with Voss's men leaning on them. The drowned year. The Lorelei. The rumor Callum was chasing about a pearl that never reached the books. Mrs. Adeyemi, copying pages by lamplight, risking prison for reasons she hadn't fully given me and I hadn't dared ask twice.

Nell listened without interrupting, which frightened me more than the shouting. When I finished she was quiet a long time, looking out at the water, and when she spoke her voice had changed — flattened, concentrated, the voice I imagined she used on the shell shed floor when someone tried to short-weight her pile.

"Our mother left us," she said. "Deliberately. Got on a ship and let us grow up thinking she was at the bottom of the sea."

"We don't know why yet."

"We know she *did* it." She turned on me. "Don't you dare start defending her already. Don't you dare do that thing men do where they start building excuses before they've finished hearing the crime. She let me light candles for her. She let Auntie drink herself stupid on grief that wasn't even — it was a *lie*, Oscar, the whole funeral of it, the whole—"

She stopped. Her hand had gone to her mouth, and above it her eyes had gone wide, and I watched the thought arrive in her the way you watch a wave arrive — you see it far out, building, and you know exactly where it's going to break.

"The berth," she said. "One berth. Two of us."

I said nothing. Which, with Nell, was the same as confessing.

"No." She stepped back from me, actually stepped back, boots sucking in the mud. "No, I can see it. I can see it forming behind your eyes, all noble and heavy, like a headstone. You're going to give it to me."

"Nell—"

"You're going to give it to me and stay here. Stay here with the ledgers and the assets and the man who maybe killed our father, and put me on that ship alone, and call it love." Her voice broke on the last word and she hated that too; I watched her hate it. "Well, I won't have it. Do you hear me? I won't be saved like a — like a shell in somebody's good pile. I didn't ask for the berth. I didn't ask for any of it. I asked for a sister's honesty and I got nine days of hovering."

"You'd be with Mum. You'd be free."

"Free." She laughed that horrible laugh again, but it caught halfway and came out as something else, something wetter. "And what would I be free *to*, Oscar? Singapore, a mother I don't remember except her handwriting, and a brother at the bottom of the world who gave away his one chance because he's decided he's the one who pays for everything. That's not freedom. That's just a different kind of indenture, and you'd be signing yourself into it."

The tide had reached our boots. Cold through the stitching. Neither of us moved.

"I haven't decided anything," I lied.

"You decided nine days ago." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, like punishing it. "You've been deciding it every day since, watching me in the shed, doing your arithmetic. Well, here's mine: two halves of one lie, that's what we are. They told each of us half the story and let us fill in the rest with grief. Mum's lie. Voss's lie. Auntie's lie. Everyone's lie but ours." She picked the letter up off the ground where it had fallen — I hadn't seen it drop — and smoothed it against her knee with fierce tenderness, and something in my chest turned over. "So here's what happens now. No more decisions made for me. No more silences chosen on my behalf. Whatever you know, I know. Whatever comes next, we choose together, or not at all."

"And if what comes next is dangerous?"

"Then it's dangerous for both of us, and you'll just have to bear it." For the first time all afternoon, something flickered at the corner of her mouth that was almost — almost — a smile. It lasted less than a second and cost her visibly. "Five years I've watched you watch over me from across yards and sheds, thinking I didn't notice. I noticed, Oscar. I noticed everything. I just thought you were ashamed of us."

The words went in deeper than any blow she'd landed all afternoon. Because she'd been right, in a way, in the way that hurts — there had been mornings I'd crossed the road to avoid being seen near the shell sheds, mornings I'd told myself it was prudence, tradecraft, keeping the family invisible to Voss. Some of it had even been true. But not all of it. Never quite all of it.

"I'm not ashamed," I said. "I never—"

"I know that now." She cut me off, not cruelly. Almost kindly, which was worse. "Now I know it. Before today I had a dead mother, a drunk aunt, and a brother who wouldn't look at me. Today I've got a living mother, a liar for a brother, and a boat ticket with one name on it that we're going to fight about properly, later, when I've decided whether to forgive you." She tucked the letter into her bodice, over her heart, with a decisiveness that made it clear the negotiation over its custody was closed. "This stays with me tonight. And tomorrow you'll take me to meet this Callum, and I'll ask my own questions, and you'll stand there and let me ask them. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Oscar." She paused at the edge of the mangroves, silhouetted against the grey water, small and straight-backed and entirely our mother's daughter. "The thing about the orchard. The mangoes you couldn't afford to let ripen. Did she really write that?"

"She really wrote that."

Nell nodded slowly, and pressed her hand flat against her bodice, over the letter, and I understood that some part of the rage had already begun its long conversion into something else — not forgiveness, not yet, but its raw material.

"Then she remembers us," she said. "Whatever else she is, whatever she did — she remembers us. Mind the pot, Nelly-bird." She said it softly, testing the words like a tongue finding an old scar. Then she turned and walked back toward the sheds, and the mud took her bootprints behind her, filling slowly with seawater, erasing the evidence that anyone had stood there at all.

I stayed until the tide had come all the way in, turning over the one thing I hadn't told her. Not the pearl, not Voss, not the letters — those she had now, all of it, every piece as promised.

It was the arithmetic. The cold little sum I'd been carrying since the jetty: *one berth, two children.* Because I'd watched her face when she worked it out, and I'd seen what I expected — fury at the idea of being given it.

But underneath the fury, for half a second, before she killed it — hope. She wanted it. She wanted Singapore and our mother and out, wanted it with her whole furious heart, and would rather bite through her own tongue than say so while I stood there planning my martyrdom.

Which meant the only kind thing left to do was the one she'd forbidden.

Some gifts you have to give in secret, and ask forgiveness for afterward. I'd had nine days' practice at that.

Tomorrow I'd take her to Callum, as agreed. Tomorrow we'd plan together, as agreed. And somewhere inside all that agreeing, quiet as a clerk entering a name under *assets*, I would find the way to put *Nell Halloran* on that manifest — and make sure that when the Sarawak Star sailed, my sister was on it, and my name was nowhere in the book.

The tide covered the last of her footprints, and I went home in the dark to practice lying to the person I loved best in the world.
