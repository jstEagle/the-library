## 17. One Berth

There is a particular arithmetic practiced in Broome that no clerk ever teaches you, though every one of us learns it by heart before we're grown. It goes like this: one berth, two children of Mara Halloran, and a ship that sails whether your heart is ready or not.

I sat on the end of the jetty with my boots off and my feet in the water, doing sums.

The Sarawak Star departed in twenty-six days. The assisted passage required proof of kinship — a parish baptismal record, an entry from the company registry, and the signature of a magistrate's officer confirming identity — all of which had to be lodged with the shipping agent no later than ten days before sailing, because bureaucracy, unlike grief, keeps perfect time. That gave us sixteen days to gather papers for a woman who was legally dead, from offices run by men who answered to Gideon Voss, without Gideon Voss noticing.

And when those papers were gathered — here was the sum I kept arriving at no matter how I shuffled the figures — they would bear one name.

One.

* * *

You'd think the mathematics would be simpler than this. You'd think a man could look at a problem of two souls and one seat and arrive at some clean conclusion, like a pearl falling through a sieve. But I'd spent five years learning that nothing about the company's arithmetic was clean. Everything came out with remainder. Everything left something over — a man overboard, a name under *assets*, a mother declared drowned so her silence couldn't testify.

My father used to say the sea keeps honest books. He said it the way men say things they want to believe, standing on the deck of the Lorelei with his gear half-laced, squinting at weather he already knew was turning. The sea keeps honest books. And then the company kept dishonest ones, and between the two ledgers my father went down like a stone with a verdict attached — *misadventure*, written in a hand so tidy it might have been Mrs. Adeyemi's own.

I thought about that verdict now, watching the tide come in over my ankles. Misadventure. As if the sea had acted alone. As if there weren't always hands on the rope.

Nell deserved better arithmetic than that.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my sister, gathered the way I gathered everything in this town: sideways, patiently, pretending not to look.

She was seventeen. She worked the shell-sorting sheds on the far side of the harbour, where the mother-of-pearl came in stinking and crusted and left polished as communion. She'd been raised since our mother's disappearance by Aunt Bridget Halloran, whose relationship with drink was less a habit than a vocation. Nell paid the rent, cooked the meals, hid the bottles, and had developed, by all accounts, a tongue that could strip barnacles off a piling at forty feet.

She didn't know I watched over her. I want that understood, because it sounds like charity when it shouldn't. It wasn't kindness. It was the only penance available to me. Every week I made sure the shed foreman owed someone a favour, and that someone mentioned Nell's quickness with the finer shells. Every month a little extra appeared in her aunt's rent account, entered under repairs, unremarked. When the fever went through the sheds three years back, it was my saved wages that bought the quinine, laundered through Callum's ship chandler cousin until it smelled of legitimate commerce.

None of it was anonymous, exactly. Nothing in Broome is anonymous; the town has four streets and eleven thousand eyes. But it was deniable, and deniable was the best I could offer, because the day Gideon Voss learned that Oscar Halloran loved anybody, that somebody became leverage. I'd seen how leverage worked. My whole family had been leverage once, and the interest had been collected in full.

So I'd loved her at a distance, the way you love a light across water — aware of it, steering by it, never presuming to touch.

Now the letter in my shirt pocket changed all the distances at once.

* * *

I took it out and read it again, though I knew every word by then, could recite it against the sound of the tide the way other men recite psalms.

*Ask after my Osc,* she had written. *Tell him the mangoes still ripen where we planted them. Tell him I count the years wrong on purpose, so there are fewer of them.*

Mara Halloran, alive in Singapore, asking after a son who had spent five years being furniture so thoroughly that the company had entered him in its books between the whale oil and the copra. She'd been told he was safe. Safe, in the language of people who flee, means breathing. It doesn't mean found.

Callum Reeve had carried that letter eleven thousand miles inside his coat, through monsoons and customs houses, on the strength of a promise made to a dying friend. He'd earned the right to see it delivered. What he hadn't earned, what nobody had earned, was what I was contemplating now — which was to take the promise and bend it, quietly, without telling him, into a shape that suited me.

Because here was the thing I'd figured out lying awake after Nell stormed off down the beach, after she'd said *don't you dare decide for me*, after she'd forbidden me — *forbidden me*, a girl of seventeen forbidding a man of twenty-three, and God help me, part of me had wanted to laugh and the rest of me had wanted to weep —

Here was the thing. Nell would refuse the berth if I offered it straight. She'd refuse it out of pride, or fairness, or sheer cussedness, and she'd be right to, and we'd stand there on the sand arguing ethics while the mail steamer's deadline walked past us both like a magistrate who doesn't hear the poor.

But if the papers were simply... arranged. If the manifest simply showed *Nell Halloran, verified kin, assisted passage, one berth*. If the decision arrived pre-made, sealed in official ink, too late to argue with —

Well. Then she'd have to forgive me afterward. Or not forgive me. Either way she'd be in Singapore by Christmas, eating mangoes with a mother who counted years wrong on purpose.

Some gifts you give openly. Some you have to smuggle past the person they're meant for.

* * *

I went to see Callum the next morning, and I'm ashamed to say I rehearsed my face on the way.

He kept rooms above the chandler's shop on Dampier Street — one room and a view of masts, furnished in the manner of a man prepared to leave any place within the hour. A seabag packed in the corner. A photograph of a woman I took to be long-dead, propped facing the wall so it wouldn't watch him sleep. Sailors' housekeeping.

"Oscar." He poured tea without asking, into cups mismatched like a crew pressed from four nations. "Your sister calmed down yet?"

"She was never uncalm. She was accurate."

He grunted, which in Callum's language was applause. "Accurate's rarer. Sit."

I sat. I let him talk first — about the berth, about the documents, about the parish priest Father Dunne who owed Mara's memory a debt and might be moved to produce a baptismal record for a girl whose christening he'd performed himself, ink faded but genuine. Callum talked with his hands the way sailors do, mapping the town in gestures: the registry here, the agent's office there, Voss's shadow over all of it like a net.

"And the indenture," he said finally, and set down his cup. "They'll need your master's release to even consider transferring anything. You're company property, lad. Papers don't move unless Voss lets them."

"I know."

"You've got a plan?"

I looked at the teacup. Wry, Oscar. Keep it wry. "I've got an intention," I said. "Plans are just intentions that have survived contact with Voss."

Callum laughed, short and rough, and didn't press. That was the thing about him — he'd carried enough secrets across enough oceans to know when a man's pockets were full. He thought I was frightened of Voss. Everyone did. It was useful, being underestimated; it was practically my profession.

What I actually needed from Callum was smaller and meaner. I needed to know the mechanics of substitution. If the parish record named both children — and it would, Mara had been a Catholic the way storms are weather, she'd have baptised us both — then either Halloran child could claim the kinship. One record. Two names. One berth.

The paperwork wouldn't care which of us boarded.

Only the paperwork's author would have to care, and I intended to do my caring in advance, alone, where nobody could veto it.

* * *

That afternoon I walked past the shell sheds without stopping, which is the hardest way to walk anywhere.

Through the open doors I could see the sorters at their benches, and there she was — Nell, third bench from the window, her sleeves rolled, her hands moving through the shell like a pianist's. She'd inherited Mother's hands. I'd inherited Father's shoulders and Mother's stubbornness and somehow none of the grace. The universe distributes fairly in the end; it just spreads each family's allotment thin.

A boy ran messages along the benches, and I saw Nell catch his sleeve, speak to him, send him off laughing. Even at distance I could read the shape of it: she was managing something. Fixing some small injustice with the efficiency of someone who'd been fixing bigger ones since she was nine years old.

She didn't know anyone was watching. That was the difference between us, maybe. I had built my whole survival on knowing when eyes were on me. She had built hers on assuming none were, and daring the world to prove otherwise.

Voss would prove otherwise soon enough, I thought. Voss noticed everything eventually. Which meant whatever I built for her had to be finished before he looked this direction — and it had to look, when it broke, like nobody's plan at all.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi found me at the counting-house ledger that evening, which surprised me, because finding people was supposed to be my trick.

"You've been writing the same figure twice," she observed, nodding at my page. "Line forty-one. Copra. Unless the copra has doubled, which the copra has not."

I blotted it. "Long day."

"They're all long days. That's why they invented nights." She drew up a stool, unhurried, and lowered her voice to the register she reserved for things that could hang a person. "The shipping agent received an inquiry today. From the harbour office. Standard verification, they called it. Names of persons making assisted-passage applications this quarter."

My pen stopped.

"Nothing yet on the list but merchant families," she went on, mild as milk. "No Hallorans. Not yet." Her eyes came up, dark and steady and entirely without mercy. "Mr. Halloran. Whatever you are building — build faster. Voss does not ask questions because he is curious. He asks them the way a fisherman casts. To learn what moves beneath."

"How long?" I managed.

"If history teaches, ten days before the cast comes up full. Perhaps fewer." She rose, smoothed her skirt, and became again the impeccable clerk whom no one suspected of anything, least of all loyalty. "Correct line forty-one. And Mr. Halloran —"

"Yes?"

"Whatever you intend to sacrifice, make certain it's yours to sacrifice. I've watched men spend other people's futures to buy their own consciences. The exchange rate is ruinous."

She left me with the ledgers and the lamp and the taste of copper in my mouth, which is what conscience feels like when it's been bitten.

* * *

Ten days. Sixteen until lodgement closed. Twenty-six until the ship.

I walked home the long way, along the tideline, and did the brutal arithmetic one final time, laying the numbers out like pearls on black velvet, looking for the flaw in the string.

One berth. Two children. A mother waiting on the far side of an ocean, growing older at the rate of one day per day, same as everyone. A father at the bottom of the channel with an honest book the sea never got to keep. A harbor master who had murdered for pearls and papered the murder with a verdict, and who was now, right now, casting his line over the shipping lists.

If I boarded, Nell stayed. Ten more years in the sheds, another decade of hiding bottles and paying rents and daring the world to notice her, with Voss's successors holding her indenture papers and her aunt's debts like reins. And if Voss decided, somewhere in those ten years, that loose Hallorans were bad for business —

If she boarded, I stayed. Five years left on my indenture, perhaps commuted for good service, perhaps not. A town I knew how to survive in. A counting-house where I could become invisible again, which was the one trade I'd mastered. And the knowledge — the permanent, salt-water knowledge — that my mother's mangoes were ripening somewhere without me.

Both options cost everything. That was the nature of the company's arithmetic; it never sold anything at fair value. But only one option put a Halloran on that gangway, and only one of us could be spared, and between the two of us it wasn't even a question, it was barely a sum.

Nell was seventeen. She had decades she hadn't lived and a mother who thought she was safe and a fury that deserved somewhere larger than four streets to burn in.

I had five years of practice at being left behind. I was, if the truth were told, rather good at it.

The flaw in the string, of course, was her. She'd fight it. She'd fight it like a cat in a sack, and she'd be magnificent, and I'd have to lie to her face for sixteen days while I forged her future behind my back. Mrs. Adeyemi's warning rang in my ears — *make certain it's yours to sacrifice* — and I turned it over honestly, the way you're supposed to.

It was mine. My berth, my release, my freedom. Nobody else's future spent to buy my conscience. Only mine, spent the other direction, which was either nobility or arrogance depending on how angry she got, and I already knew how angry she'd get.

Fine. Let her be angry in Singapore. Anger travels well; it keeps better than most cargo.

I stood at the high-tide line and made it formal, the way my father used to seal a bargain with the sea — spit in your palm, grip the air, no witnesses needed because the water remembers everything.

"Nell Halloran," I said to the dark, to the channel, to whatever was listening down there besides my father, "you are getting on that ship if I have to carry the paperwork myself."

The tide came in and erased my footprints, obliging fellow.

* * *

I was halfway home, rehearsing lies — *the agent needs both our records for verification, standard procedure, don't look at me like that* — when I saw the lantern.

Not on the street. On the water. A skiff riding low past the end of the jetty, oars shipped, sitting quiet as a trap. And standing in it, unmistakable even by that guttering light, the breadth of him, the set of the shoulders — Callum Reeve, talking to someone I couldn't see.

Negotiating, from the angle of his head. Or refusing to negotiate. His hand came up, flat, final.

Then the lantern swung toward shore, and its light crossed the jetty piles, and for half a second it caught the face of the man sitting opposite him in the stern.

Brack.

Voss's Brack, sitting calm as Sunday in Callum's boat, and Callum letting him sit there, jaw working, saying nothing loud enough to carry across the water — which meant the conversation had started before the lantern, and was going badly, and had an audience neither of them knew was watching.

I stepped back into the shadow of the net racks, heart going like a diver's pulse at depth, and did the only sum left that mattered:

How much did Voss know tonight that he hadn't known yesterday?

The skiff pulled away toward the harbour office light, one lantern shrinking on the black water. Callum's boat rode empty at its mooring.

Sixteen days had just gotten shorter.
