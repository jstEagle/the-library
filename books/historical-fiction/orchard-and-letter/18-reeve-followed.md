## 18. Reeve Followed

A sailor learns early that the sea has two moods and only one of them is honest. The storm tells you plainly it means to kill you. It's the calm you have to watch—the flat glass morning that lies to your face while it stacks a swell over the horizon. Men trust calm. Men die of it.

I'd been in Broome nine days, and every one of them had been too calm.

It started as a itch between my shoulder blades, the kind I'd learned to read off the coast of a dozen ports where a man with the wrong questions was worth money to somebody. I'd felt it faintly since the jetty—that prickle of being counted. But counting wasn't following. A man could be counted from a distance, catalogued and dismissed. What I felt now, walking out of Chinatown with a paper of fried fish and no destination worth naming, was something more deliberate. Something with boots.

I didn't look back. That's the first rule, and the rule most men break because pride is cheaper than sense. You look back, you confirm what your neck already knows: somebody is there. Instead I stopped at a fruit stall, made a show of squeezing mangoes I had no intention of buying, and used the reflection in the brass scales.

He was good. I'll give him that. Not standing still like an amateur pretending interest in durians, not drifting aimless like a man whose handler told him to be invisible and he'd interpreted that as *lost*. He moved with the crowd the way oil moves on water—at home in it, part of its surface tension. Big fellow, broad through the shoulders, sunburnt neck, hat brim pulled to do its work properly. He was examining a bolt of calico at the draper's across the way with such concentrated boredom that I knew two things about him immediately: he'd done this before, and he hated it.

The second thing made me like him slightly. The first thing made me plan his ruin.

*Brack,* I thought, though I couldn't yet have said how I knew the name. Later I'd learn it proper—from Oscar, white-faced, telling me the harbor master kept a man named Brack the way other men kept a dog, except dogs can be called off. But in that moment all I had was the shape of him and the certainty that Gideon Voss did not spend wages on a man like this to watch a stranger buy fish.

Because that was the arithmetic, see. Voss knew I'd been asking after Hallorans. I'd been careful—careful as a man can be who's spent thirty years shouting orders over engines—but careful isn't invisible, and Broome is a small town wearing a big town's hat. The question was what Voss knew beyond that. Did he know about the letters? Did he know I'd found the boy?

The letters. God help me, the letters.

They were against my chest at that moment, wrapped in oilskin inside my coat, four pages in Mara Halloran's hand that I'd carried from Singapore through every port between there and here like a man carrying lit powder through a gunnery drill. A dying woman's request. *Find my Osc. Tell him I never—* well. She wrote what she wrote, and it wasn't mine to paraphrase, even in my own head. The point was this: if Brack took those letters off my body, Voss would know everything, and everything included a boy of nineteen with an indenture and no protection and a mother alive on the far side of the world. Voss wouldn't need to hurt me much. He'd just need to make sure I never got near Oscar again, and then wait for the tide of ordinary cruelty to do the rest.

So the letters needed to not be on my body. And they needed to get that way without Brack seeing them go.

Here's the trouble with losing a tail in a town like Broome: there aren't enough places to lose one. Singapore you can vanish in—streets folding into streets, a hundred doorways per yard of pavement, ten thousand faces that have their own reasons for not remembering yours. Broome has three streets, a foreshore, a Chinatown built like a rabbit warren by people who understood hiding better than any architect alive, and a great deal of open country where a lone white man stands out like a lighthouse. The town is a labyrinth with the walls painted glass.

But a labyrinth is a labyrinth, and I'd been hunted before—not always the hunter—and there are tricks older than either of us.

First trick: give the tail a pattern. Men follow patterns; it's how they convince themselves they're doing work. So I did what I'd half-done already these past days—I walked my circuit. Jetty, Chinatown, the shell sheds' outer fence, back past the company office with my eyes carefully elsewhere. Let Brack settle into the rhythm of me. Let him build his little map: *Reeve walks here at this hour, looks at nothing, buys nothing.* A bored man follows a boring target badly, and a badly-followed target leaves seams.

Second trick: break the pattern at the seam.

The seam I'd chosen was the passage behind the cookhouses in Short Street—a slot barely wide enough for two men shoulder to shoulder, running between a Chinese eating-house and the back wall of a pearl buyer's store, opening at the far end onto the yard where the shell carts queued. In the middle of that passage stood the cookhouse's wash-line, strung high with drying shirts, and beneath it a drain-grate that smelled like every drain-grate in the tropics, which is to say like the town's whole supper coming back up to say hello.

I went in fast. Not running—running announces itself, the crowd parts around it like water around a prow, and Brack would've seen the wake even if he missed the boat. But quick, purposeful, the walk of a man late for something, and I turned into that passage on the turn of the crowd's movement so that a cart, a woman with baskets, and the shadow of the awning each bought me a second apiece.

Three seconds. In three seconds I did the following: shrugged my coat off one shoulder, worked the oilskin packet out of its inner pocket—sweat-slick, my hands steadier than they had any right to be—and dropped it down behind the loose brick I'd noted two days earlier, third course up, where the cookhouse wall met the buyer's store and the mortar had gone soft as bread. The brick sat proud by half an inch. Any man who looked would think the wall was simply falling down, as most of Broome was. I seated the brick back with the heel of my palm, dragged my sleeve across the damp patch to rough it up, shrugged the coat on, and stepped out the far end into the cart yard looking exactly like a man who had cut through to save himself the walk around.

Total cost: perhaps four seconds. Total risk: my heart trying to climb out through my teeth.

I didn't go straight out the far end, mind. Straight lines are for dead men. I doubled—through the yard, past the shell carts, out the gate onto Dampier Terrace heading the wrong way along my circuit, then ducked into the billiard room, ordered a beer I didn't want, and watched the street through the fly-specked window while I drank it warm and thought unkind thoughts.

Brack came through the yard ninety seconds later. I watched him do it well, I'll say that—he didn't hurry, didn't check the passage mouth with his eyes, gave the whole scene the casual once-over of a man killing time. But he paused at the gate. Just a beat. Just long enough to look back at the passage and decide whether a man could have shed anything in it. Then he came out onto the terrace and stood in the sun like a fence post with opinions, turning his head slowly along the street.

Looking for a man who had, as far as the street could tell him, evaporated.

I finished the beer. I even enjoyed it, in the way you enjoy things when your pulse is doing ninety and you want to prove to your body that nothing whatsoever is happening.

* * *

The trouble with doubling away from your tail is that eventually you have to become yourself again, and yourself lives somewhere. I was lodging above the boatshed on the creek—paid cash, registered nowhere, the kind of arrangement that keeps a man fed and keeps no records. But Brack had had days. If he'd found the boatshed already, then my little theater in the passage had been wasted effort, and if he hadn't found it, leading him to it now would be the stupidest thing I'd done since I signed aboard a pearling lugger at fifteen.

So I didn't go home. Home, for the next few hours, was wherever the crowd was thickest.

I'll tell you what nobody admits about being followed: it does something to time. Every minute stretches like wet rope, and every face in the street becomes a possible second pair of eyes—because a professional like Brack doesn't work alone if he can help it, and Voss struck me as a man who helped himself to whatever resources lay to hand. By mid-afternoon I'd mentally recruited half of Broome into Voss's employ: the ticket-of-leave man sweeping outside the hotel, the two Malay divers sharing a smoke by the sea wall, a child who looked at me a half-second too long. Paranoia, a shipmate of mine used to say, is just situational awareness that's stopped getting invited places.

I walked. I let the heat have its way with me. And I thought about Mara Halloran, which is what I did instead of praying.

She'd been the toughest soul aboard the *Coromandel*, tougher than men twice her size, and she'd needed to be, being a woman alone on a working ship with a child and no husband the company recognized. I'd been her friend in the way sailors are friends—badly, intermittently, but truly. When she fled Broome I'd believed the official story same as everyone: lost overboard, taken by the sea, poor woman, poor boys. It was easier to believe. Believing cost nothing.

Then, eleven years later, a dying woman in a Singapore rooming-house had gripped my wrist with what strength the fever left her and said, *Callum, they'll tell you I'm dead. I need you to carry the truth to my son, because I couldn't carry it myself.* And believing had started costing plenty.

She'd paid my passage to Broome with money she'd saved scrubbing and sewing for years, and I had sworn on the last coherent thing she said to me that those letters would reach Oscar Halloran or I'd die trying, and I meant to keep that oath, but I confess that oath-keeping sits differently in a man's chest when there's a large quiet fellow with government boots reading the street behind him.

Which brought me to the question underneath the question: what did Voss actually know?

If he knew about the letters, he'd not have sent Brack to *follow* me. He'd have sent him to take me. Quietly, at night, somewhere the mud would drink what was left. No—following meant uncertainty. Following meant Voss knew a stranger was sniffing at old wounds and wanted to know how deep the sniffing went. That was survivable. Uncertain men hesitate, and hesitation is a country I know how to fight in.

What I couldn't allow was for uncertain to become certain. And the fastest road to certain ran through one person: the boy.

* * *

I found Oscar—or rather, arranged to find him—near the shell sheds at the end of the sorting shift, when the workers spill out like sand from a torn bag and no one's trajectory means anything. We'd established the fiction by then: Callum Reeve, trader, occasionally buys specimens from the boys on the foreshore, harmless as driftwood. He'd shown me a shell that morning, sold me a piece of trash, taken my coin with hands that shook until our fingers touched and then went still.

"You're being watched," I said, low, not looking at him, both of us facing the water where a lugger was beating in on the evening tide.

"I know." His voice was flat as slate. "Brack. He's been on me since Sunday."

"Since—" I bit down on the word. Since before I'd spotted my own shadow. Which meant Voss had set his dog on the boy first and on me second, which meant the boy was the fixed point and I was the variable, which meant Voss understood something important: you don't follow the letter, you follow the address it wants to reach.

"Does he know about the letter?" I asked. "Your mother's letter. Does he know it exists?"

Oscar was quiet long enough that a whole wave went out and came back. "He knows someone asked about Hallorans. He doesn't know why. Mrs. Adeyemi says the registry shows nothing—she checked. As far as the company's paper goes, my mother died at sea in 'eighty-three and I have no kin living."

"Then we keep it that way." I finally turned and looked at him, this thin serious scrap of a man with his father's drowned eyes. "Listen to me, lad, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to be loud and foolish about shells for the benefit of anyone listening. The letters are safe. They're not on me and won't be again until the day they change hands. You'll not come looking for them, you'll not ask where they are, and if anyone ever asks you about papers in my keeping, you'll look at them the way you look at me right now—like a stranger selling rubbish. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Good. Because here's the foolish part." I picked up a shell from his basket, held it to the light, made my voice carry the particular boredom of commerce. "Poor specimen. Cracked nacre. I'll give you sixpence and we'll call it charity."

"Done," he said, matching my tone exactly, and I thought: *Mara, your boy has survived this place by becoming exactly what it needed him to be, and one day soon that survival skill is going to break a courtroom wide open.*

We parted. I watched him walk toward the compound road with his sixpence and his empty face, and I watched, further back, Brack detach from a doorway and fall in behind him at forty yards, patient as tide.

And that was when the cold went through me proper, colder than any southern sea. Because I'd solved my problem—letters hidden, tail shaken, boy briefed—but I'd confirmed the shape of the trap entire. Voss wasn't watching me. Voss was watching *both ends of the rope*, me and Oscar, waiting for the rope to pull tight so he could see which direction the pulling came from. Every contact we made was a signal flare. Every kindness was evidence.

Six weeks to the *Sarawak Star*. One berth. Two Halloran children. One harbor master with a ledger full of sins and a memory like a fishhook.

I walked back toward the creek in the last of the light, past the boatshed, past my own door without stopping, and on to the foreshore where the mudflats breathed their low-tide breath and the mosquitoes rose in congregations. There, sitting on a mooring post with a pipe I didn't light, I let myself feel it—the fear, clean and total, the old salt taste of it. Fear's not the enemy. Fear's the cargo manifest of a man's life; it tells you exactly what you're carrying and exactly what it will cost to lose it.

I was carrying four letters, one oath, and the knowledge that a man had drowned in that mud twenty years ago so that another man's books could balance.

Tomorrow I'd need to move the letters again—Voss's people would sweep that passage within a week, mark me. Tomorrow I'd need to start learning Brack's rhythms, because a tail you understand is a tail you can use. A man who knows he's followed can feed his follower anything he likes, and I had a head full of false courses I was itching to lay.

But tonight, tonight I sat on the mooring post and watched the lights of Chinatown come on one by one, and I thought about a woman's handwriting saying *my Osc*, hidden behind a soft brick smelling of drains and frying oil, holding its breath in the dark the same as the rest of us.

It was nearly full dark when I noticed the second man.

Not Brack—wrong build, lighter, quicker. He'd appeared at the top of the beach track as if he'd grown there, and he stood looking out over the mudflats with the studied idleness of a man who has been placed rather than drawn. Placed, I'd have bet my last shilling, between my route and the boatshed. Not following. *Covering.* Brack trailed; this one waited.

Which meant Voss had stopped wondering where I slept.

I stayed on the post until my legs ached, smoking my unlit pipe down to ash that wasn't there, doing sums. Then I rose, stretched like an old man—which I am, mostly—and walked not toward the boatshed but toward the one place in Broome where a stranger might plausibly go at night and where Voss's men would hesitate to put hands on me: the billiard room, and after it the hotel veranda, and after that anywhere public and lit until the town's lamps burned low.

Let them watch. I'd spent thirty years sleeping in hammocks in howling weather; I could sleep sitting up in a public house with one eye open, and tomorrow—

Tomorrow I'd dive the Lorelei.

Because there was one thing those watchers couldn't follow me to, one place their lanterns didn't reach, and it lay under forty feet of cold green water where a good man had died keeping Voss's secret. The letters were words. Words could be argued with, forged, denied.

The ballast of the *Lorelei* was proof.

I turned up my collar against the night wind coming off the water, and behind me, at the top of the beach track, the waiting man shifted his weight, settling in for a long, useless night.

*Sleep well, mate,* I thought. *You're guarding a ghost. The real cargo's already gone to ground.*
