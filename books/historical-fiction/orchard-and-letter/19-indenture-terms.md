## 19. Indenture Terms

The summons came the way weather comes to Broome: without apology and ahead of any warning you might have wanted.

I was sorting shells in the counting house annex—third tray of the morning, my fingers doing their work while my mind did another entirely—when Mrs. Adeyemi appeared in the doorway with a slip of paper she did not hand me so much as aim at me.

"Mr. Voss will see you at eleven," she said. Her voice was level. Her eyes were not. They held something I'd learned to read the way sailors read a horizon: not the storm itself, but the particular green light that comes before one.

"Eleven," I repeated, like a man confirming his own execution time.

"Eleven." She lingered a half-second longer than the message required. Then, quieter, almost under her breath: "Wear your clean shirt, Oscar. It matters how you look when they decide what you're worth."

And she was gone before I could ask whether that was advice or a warning.

* * *

I had been inside Voss's office exactly twice in five years. The first time, aged seventeen and newly indentured, I'd stood on a floor mosaic of the company crest while a man whose name I've since forgotten read out the terms of my father's debt in a voice better suited to livestock auctions. The second time was to report a discrepancy in a shell consignment, which Voss corrected with such swift, surgical courtesy that I understood, even then, that discrepancies were permitted only when he discovered them himself.

The office had not changed. The ceiling fan turned with the slow patience of something paid by the hour. The windows looked out over the harbor, where the masts of the pearling luggers stood in their forest like a congregation waiting for a sermon. On the wall hung charts, framed certificates, and—the piece de résistance—a single pearl mounted in glass, the size of a pigeon's egg, labeled in copperplate: *Meridian Trading Company. Founded on Excellence.*

Founded on excellence. I'd had five years to consider what else it might have been founded on.

Voss sat behind a desk of jarrah wood so highly polished it seemed less furniture than water you could drown in. He was reading when I entered—or appearing to read—and he let me stand there for the length of ten heartbeats, which I understood was not rudeness but measurement. He was timing me. Everything about Gideon Voss was timed. His courtesy arrived precisely when it would be most effective; his silences lasted exactly as long as your nerve.

"Oscar Halloran." He said my name like a man testing a coin between his teeth. "Sit."

There was one chair. There is always one chair in such rooms, positioned so that you must cross the full length of the carpet to reach it, feeling all the while like a specimen being conveyed from shelf to shelf.

"You've done good work this season," he began, setting down his pen. "The March consignment. The Kuri Bay lots. Mr. Fenn remarks that your eye for grading has sharpened considerably." He smiled—not widely; Voss's smiles were issued in denominations, and this was the small change. "A young man who can distinguish a sixteen-millimeter silver from a common blister at a glance is worth keeping."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me yet." He folded his hands. "I'm going to offer you something. But first—" and here his voice dropped into a register I can only describe as conversational menace, the tone of a man discussing the price of tea while holding a knife below the table's edge, "—first I want to talk about a stranger."

The fan turned. Somewhere out on the harbor a winch groaned through its cargo.

"A stranger," I said.

"Callum Reeve." He pronounced it carefully, having clearly enjoyed learning it. "Arrived on the *Adelaide Lass* three weeks past. No cargo interests. No family here—at least none he'll admit to. Has taken lodging at Mrs. Quill's boarding house, pays cash, and spends his days asking questions." Voss tilted his head, birdlike. "Questions about the Halloran family, Oscar. About *your* family."

I have spent five years learning which silences keep me safe. I knew them the way other men know prayers: the silence of the obedient, the silence of the stupid, the silence of the man too tired to care. I reached for the stupid one, because it was closest.

"I don't know any Callum Reeve, sir."

"No?" Voss opened a drawer—I heard it slide, smooth as an eel—and produced a sheet of paper. "My man observed him at the jetty Tuesday last. Talking to you. At some length. You were seen to walk away quickly." He laid the paper flat, though I couldn't read it upside down and he knew it. "You walked away quickly, Oscar. Men walk away quickly from conversations that frighten them. Or conversations that interest them. Which was yours?"

"The jetty, sir? There's a deal of traffic on the jetty. Shell buyers, deckhands—"

"It was low tide," Voss said softly. "Half the town was mudflats and the other half was asleep. And yet there you were, our most reliable sorter, deep in conversation with a man who asks after dead women."

Dead women. The words went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. My mother's name was in this room. Not spoken—*weighed*, set down on the desk between us like a pearl he intended to grade.

"He asked directions," I said. "He was lost."

"Nobody is lost in Broome, Oscar. Broome is four streets and a beach." Voss rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the harbor with his hands clasped behind his back, a portrait of a man surveying his kingdom. "Let me tell you what I know about strangers. A stranger arrives. A stranger asks questions. And questions, in a town like this, are never idle. They are hooks. And where there are hooks, Oscar, there is always someone fishing—and the fish they're after is rarely the fish they catch."

I said nothing. The stupid silence again, but stretched thinner now.

He turned back from the window. "Here is what I am prepared to do." He returned to the desk but did not sit, standing over it instead, hands flat on the jarrah, so that I had to look up at him like a boy called before the schoolmaster—which, I suppose, was the geometry he preferred. "Your indenture runs three more years. Three years of trays and lamplight and Mr. Fenn's opinions. But I have the authority, as harbor master and company agent, to sign an early release. Full discharge. Clean papers. Your name struck from the registry as a liability and restored to you as a free man." He paused, letting each word land like a diver's weight. "Effective within the week."

The room tilted. That is the only honest way to describe it. Five years I had counted days the way misers count coins—secretly, obsessively, with a shame attached to the counting itself. One thousand eight hundred-odd mornings of waking to the bell and belonging, bell to bell, to the Meridian Trading Company. And here was a man offering to cut the chain with a signature.

"Free papers," I managed. "In exchange for—"

"For nothing so crude as information." Voss smiled again, and this smile had more denomination to it. "I would simply like you to *talk* to me. As one reasonable man to another. Tell me what Reeve wants. What he showed you. What he promised you." He straightened. "Men like Reeve come to towns like this carrying stories, Oscar. Stories about pearls that were never booked. About drowned divers. About women who died at sea and yet, somehow, write letters." 

The floor did not merely tilt; it opened.

He saw it. God help me, he saw it—the flicker in my face, the half-second where my whole body forgot its training—and he leaned forward slightly, the way a fisherman leans when the line goes taut.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Letters. You know something about letters, don't you?"

I built the wall back up brick by brick, the way I'd built it every day for five years, and I said, in a voice I barely recognized as my own: "I don't know what you mean, sir."

"Of course you don't." He resumed his seat, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world because all the time in the world was mortgaged to him. "Then let us speak hypothetically. Hypothetically—if a stranger brought a young man letters claiming to be from a dead mother, that young man should consider very carefully who benefits. Forgers exist, Oscar. Conmen exist. Men arrive in pearling ports with sad tales and false handwriting, and they take money from fools, and sometimes worse things happen to them than losing money." He steepled his fingers. "Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," I echoed.

"But if that young man came to me—came to me *first*, mind, before anything unfortunate occurred—and told me everything, honestly, as a loyal employee should"—he drew a document from the drawer and turned it to face me, and there it was, the release, already drafted, already dated, my own name sitting in it like bait on a hook—"then that young man would be free. Free to go anywhere. Free to board any ship. Free to sail to Singapore, if Singapore took his fancy."

Singapore. He said it watching my face, and I knew—he *knew*. He didn't know how much, or exactly what, but he knew the shape of it, the way you know a shark from the shadow of it passing under your boat. Callum had been followed. Nell might be next. And here sat the spider, offering the fly its choice of webs.

I looked at the paper. My name. *Oscar James Halloran, discharged of all obligations to the Meridian Trading Company.* The words I had imagined on a thousand sleepless nights, arranged in official ink, real enough to touch.

"May I hold it, sir?"

"Of course." He slid it across with the magnanimity of a man tossing scraps to a dog. "Read it. Take your time."

I read it. Every line. The date left blank. The signature space empty. It was perfect. It was everything.

And underneath the wanting—underneath the great black tide of it that rose in my chest and nearly drowned the rest—I heard my father's voice, not saying anything wise, just saying *mind the current, Osc, the current's stronger than it looks,* which is what he said every morning before a dive, and which turns out to be true of more than water.

Because here was the arithmetic no one teaches you: freedom offered by Gideon Voss is not freedom. It is a purchase. And whatever he bought with it—my silence, my testimony, my word against Callum Reeve's—would be worth more to him than three years of my labor. Men don't pay above market rate unless they're buying something dangerous. And once I'd sold it, once I'd told him everything, he would not need me free. He would need me gone, or quiet, or both—and a signed discharge makes a very tidy instrument for either. Free men can't be searched for. Free men can disappear without anyone obliged to ask why.

He wasn't offering me a door. He was offering me a window over deep water.

I set the paper down. Carefully. The way you put down something you want so badly your hands aren't to be trusted with it.

"It's a generous offer, sir."

"It is," he agreed pleasantly. "Generosity is a habit of mine. Ask anyone." Nobody had ever asked anyone; we all knew what happened to people who asked.

"I'd want time to consider."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—disappointment, perhaps, or its well-dressed cousin, recalibration. "Time," he repeated.

"A decision of this weight, sir. A man doesn't sign away—doesn't accept—" I stumbled, caught myself. "I'd want to think it proper. Pray on it, maybe. Sunday's coming."

"Sunday," said Voss, and the word came out with a faint edge, like a blade drawn an inch. "Yes. Churches do fill up on Sundays. With all sorts. Even strangers, I'm told." He reclaimed the document, squared it, and returned it to the drawer with the tenderness of a man racking a rifle. "Very well. You have until Friday. Come to me Friday evening with your answer, and your—observations. If they please me, you'll have your papers before the tide turns Saturday."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me." He picked up his pen, the audience concluded. "Thank yourself, if it goes well. And Oscar—"

I stopped at the door.

"—the tide in this town comes in fast, and it doesn't announce itself. Whatever Reeve has told you, whatever paper he's waved under your nose—papers can be lies. Ink is cheap. Loyalty, properly placed, is expensive." He glanced up, and his face was all warmth and reasonableness, and beneath it, plain as the drowned thing under still water, was the other face. "*Be* expensive, Oscar."

I said, "Yes, sir," because five years of survival is mostly saying yes, sir, and I walked out across that drowning-polished floor with my legs working through sheer institutional habit, and I closed the door behind me with a click soft as a trap springing shut on empty air.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi was at her desk in the outer office, entering figures with her pen moving in quick, economical strokes. She did not look up as I passed. But as I reached the door she said, without pausing her writing:

"Well?"

"He offered me my papers. Free discharge. In exchange for telling him everything about Callum."

The pen stopped. Just for a moment—one beat, two—then resumed. "And what did you tell him?"

"That I'd pray on it."

Her mouth twitched. It might have been a smile; with Mrs. Adeyemi, smiles were filed under miscellaneous and rarely itemized. "Friday evening," she said. "That gives you four days."

"Four days for what?"

She set down her pen at last and looked at me directly, and I saw in her face something I had not seen there before—not kindness this time, but kinship. The look of a woman recognizing, in a younger face, the expression she must once have worn herself: the look of a person deciding what they are willing to lose.

"Four days to become faster than he is," she said. "Or four days to bury whatever Reeve gave you somewhere he'll never find it. Because understand this, Oscar Halloran, and understand it now: Gideon Voss does not make offers. He makes *investments*. And when an investment fails to return"—she tapped the ledger before her, once, lightly—"he writes off the principal. However much it cost him."

"And if I take his offer?"

"Then you'll be free," she said. "Free the way a released bird is free over open ocean, with the nearest land a hundred miles behind and a hundred ahead, and the man who opened the cage watching from the cliff to see how far you get." She picked her pen back up. "Friday, Oscar. Don't come to this office Friday unless you already know your answer. By Friday, knowing it won't be safe to still be finding it."

I went out into the white heat of the street, the harbor glittering ahead of me like a lie told beautifully, and I thought about my father saying *the current's stronger than it looks*, and I thought about Nell in the shell sheds with her sharp tongue and her borrowed loneliness, and I thought about a letter in a hiding place that smelled of bilge and tar, written in a hand I'd know blindfolded, asking after *my Osc*.

Friday, he'd given me.

Which meant, whatever else was true, that Gideon Voss was afraid. Men who are not afraid give you until Sunday.

And a man afraid enough to buy my silence today would be willing, by inspection day, to pay a great deal more to make sure I never spoke at all.

I turned toward the mudflats, where at low tide a certain weathered stranger would be waiting—and I walked fast, before the current could close over my head.
