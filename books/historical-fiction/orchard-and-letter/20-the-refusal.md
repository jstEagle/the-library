## 20. The Refusal

Voss's office smelled of cedar and camphor, the way a coffin might if the undertaker had ambitions.

I had been summoned by a boy who didn't meet my eyes—always a bad sign. In five years of indenture I had learned to read summons the way sailors read weather. A summons from Mrs. Adeyemi meant work. A summons from the paymaster meant money, or the absence of it. But a summons delivered by a boy who looked anywhere but at your face, with the harbor master's own card pressed into your palm as though it might burn him—that meant you were already being weighed for something, and the scales had been oiled that morning.

The office sat above the counting house, with windows angled to catch both the jetty and the road. I understood the geometry of it. From that desk a man could watch everything come and go, and everything coming and going could feel, without knowing why, watched. It was a room built to make visitors stand slightly wrong. I stood wrong accordingly.

"Halloran." Voss did not look up from his papers. "Sit."

I sat. There is a particular quality to waiting while a powerful man finishes a page he has already finished, and I had long ago stopped resenting it. Resentment is a luxury of men whose time belongs to themselves. My time belonged to Meridian Trading, per the registry, line forty-one, entered under *assets* in Mrs. Adeyemi's careful hand—the one kindness in that book, that she had written my name as though it were a name rather than a number.

Voss set down his pen. He was not a large man, but he was a *finished* man, the way a knife is finished—everything unnecessary ground away. His grey suit fit like an argument won. When he smiled, which he did now, it arrived precisely on schedule, like the mail steamer.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"No, sir."

"You do know." He folded his hands. "You've always known more than you let on. That's not an accusation. In fact—" he leaned back, and the chair accepted him like a throne accepts a modest king, "—it's the reason we're speaking at all. Useful men are quiet men, Halloran. You have been the quietest man in Broome for five years. I have noticed."

There it was. The thing I had spent half a decade building—my silence, brick by patient brick—held up to the light and appraised like a pearl of dubious luster. I kept my face in the shape it knew best: attentive, dull, faintly anxious. The face of a man who has never had a thought worth hiding.

"A stranger came off the Lorelei last week," Voss continued. "Weathered fellow. Asks questions in the taverns about old families, old wrecks. Callum Reeve, he calls himself." He said the name slowly, tasting it, watching me over the top of nothing at all. "He has been asking after Hallorans."

My heart performed a single enormous beat and then, obedient to five years' training, went quiet as a diver holding air. I let my brow crease. I let confusion arrive a half-second late, the way it does in honest stupid men.

"Halloran," I repeated. "That's—"

"It's your name," Voss said mildly.

"Yes, sir. Only no one's called us anything but assets since I signed on." I produced a small, apologetic shrug. "Sorry, sir. Habit."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not belief, not yet disbelief. Assessment. He was a ledger-keeper of souls, this man; I could feel myself being entered into a column, and I could not tell which column. So I gave him what every auditor hopes for: figures that balance and reveal nothing.

"He asked after your father," Voss said. "And your mother."

"I don't remember much of either, sir." This was true in the way a broken clock tells time twice a day. "Father drowned when I was eleven. Mother went before that. The aunt raised—well. She tried." I let a shadow cross my face, and it was no effort at all, because the shadow lived there permanently beneath everything, and all I did was open the door to it briefly.

"Mm." Voss rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind him, surveying his harbor. "Do you know what I admire about this town, Halloran? Everyone in it wants something. The divers want pearls. The shopkeepers want coin. The priests want souls. Wanting makes people legible. You can read a wanting man like a manifest." He turned. "But you—you've been nearly illegible. Five years and I couldn't tell you what you want. Until recently."

"Sir?"

"Until a stranger started asking questions, and certain documents began to interest people who had ignored them for years." He came back to the desk but did not sit. He stood over me, and I noticed his hands—clean, unscarred, hands that had never once been in the water they sent other men into. "Parish records. Registry pages. Old dive logs. Curious reading material for a man of your reputation."

*Brack,* I thought. *Brack has seen something, or thinks he has.* My skin went cold and then hot, and I did the only thing that has ever saved me: I made myself smaller.

"I can't read much, sir," I said. "Letters mostly. Numbers, if they're written plain. Mrs. Adeyemi's been teaching me the manifests because I'm quick with sorting." A pause, then, carefully, the bait: "Was I doing something wrong? With the papers?"

And there—there it was—the almost imperceptible softening. A man hunting guilt relaxes when the quarry shows him fear instead of cunning. Voss studied me for three full seconds, and I counted them by my pulse, and I kept my eyes where an honest frightened man keeps them: near his, never quite meeting, dropping to the desk, to my own hands.

"No," he said at last. "No, you weren't doing anything wrong." He sat down again, and the whole temperature of the room changed, the way water changes over a drop-off. "In fact, I'm here to offer you something. An opportunity."

"Yes, sir."

"Your indenture runs four years yet. That's a long time to be young and owned." He smiled at his own phrasing, pleased with its honesty. "I can shorten it. Release papers, properly signed, endorsed by the company. You could walk out of this office free. Tomorrow, if you liked."

The words went through me like cold water through cloth. Free. The word I had not permitted myself to say aloud since I was thirteen years old, spoken now across a cedar desk by the man who owned my next four years, offered up like a plate of fruit.

I thought of the letter in its oilcloth wrapping, sewn into the lining of my jacket, pressing against my ribs like a second heart. I thought of six weeks. I thought of a berth on the Sarawak Star with one name on it, and a woman in Singapore who had once called me *my Osc* in ink that outlived her reported death.

And I thought: *he knows. He knows about the ship. He knows what freedom costs, and he is pricing it.*

"What would I need to do, sir?" I asked. Flatly. Not eager—eagerness would be suspicious—but with the weary practicality of a man who has learned that gifts from owners come with invoices attached.

"Just talk," Voss said comfortably. "Tell me about the stranger. What he wants. Where he sleeps. What he carries. Whether he's shown you any papers—letters, documents, anything of that nature." He opened a drawer and drew out a sheet of paper, laying it square before me. Blank. "Nothing onerous. A few names. Perhaps a signature, later, if matters should ever reach a magistrate—which I doubt they will. Matters rarely do, in Broome."

*I doubt they will.* Delivered like a weather report. Like a promise.

I looked at the blank paper. On it, I understood, he wanted me to write my own sentence: to inform on the last living link to my mother, to hand Callum Reeve to whatever fate the Lorelei's old cargo demanded, to sign away the only chance two Halloran children would ever have of standing in the same room as the woman who bore us. And in exchange, four years of my life returned to me—minus, of course, the life itself, minus the self that would remain standing in this office having done it.

I thought of my father going down into green dark water for a company that recorded his death as misadventure between entries about copra prices. I thought of my mother's handwriting, which I had not seen in nine years and which had crossed an ocean to find me anyway. I thought of Nell, sharp-eyed in the shell sheds, whom I could not save and would not sell.

"Sir," I said, and heard my voice come out exactly right—apologetic, slow, a laborer's voice, "I don't know anything about him. Truly. He asked me some questions on the jetty about my family and I told him I didn't know the name. Thought he was after money or a debt. People come through asking after dead men's debts." I shrugged the shrug of a man embarrassed by his own insignificance. "If I'd known he'd cause trouble, I'd have mentioned it. But I haven't seen him since. He'll have gone on with the tide."

Voss regarded me for a long moment. Outside, a winch groaned; somewhere a gull argued with the morning. Inside, the harbor master of Broome decided whether I was a dull asset or a liar, and whether it mattered which.

"You understand," he said pleasantly, "that if you *were* concealing something—if this stranger had approached you, shown you anything, promised you anything—it would go very badly for you. Indentured men who lie to the company find their terms extended. Find their rations shortened. Find that accidents happen to men who are inconvenient." He said it all with the warmth of a host describing the amenities. "Whereas a man who helps me—a man who is *useful*—can expect great things. Freedom, even."

"Yes, sir. I understand." And then, because terror without stupidity is just clarity wearing a disguise, I added the touch that sealed it: "Only—I'd want it in writing, sir. If I'm to say anything. About anyone. I'd want it witnessed proper. Because if I say a name and it turns out wrong, that's my neck, isn't it. I'd want the release papers first. Signed. Then I'd say what I know."

A pause. His fingers tapped once on the desk.

"That's unusually shrewd of you, Halloran."

"Mrs. Adeyemi says I'm quick with numbers, sir. Same thing, isn't it. Knowing what you're owed."

Something moved across his face—irritation, perhaps, or the recognition of a mirror. He had offered me corruption dressed as mercy; I had answered with caution dressed as stupidity, and underneath both, we each knew exactly what the other was selling. But he could not put release papers in my hand first, because the papers were the payment and payment precedes betrayal in every arrangement he had ever made, and I had just refused to buy on credit.

"We'll leave the details for another day," he said smoothly. "Think it over. Talk comes easy. Papers come easier—for friends."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir."

I rose. My legs held, which surprised me. At the door I paused, and turned back with the expression of a man remembering a small domestic worry.

"The ship, sir. The Sarawak Star. I heard the shell-buyers talking. Is it true there's berths going cheap? Only—my sister. She's got no one but the aunt, and the aunt..." I trailed off delicately. "If there was honest work in Singapore for a girl, I'd want her gone from here. Away from all this." I gestured vaguely—at the office, the harbor, the whole apparatus of ownership—and watched him absorb the fact that I had named the very ship, the very destination, with a fool's artless honesty.

"There may be provisions made," Voss said, after a moment too long. "For deserving cases."

"Thank you, sir. She's a good worker. Deserving."

I walked out.

* * *

The stairs down from that office are twenty-two steps. I know because I counted them that day, gripping the rail, one step at a time, while my body finally permitted itself the shaking it had postponed for forty minutes. By the twelfth step my knees were unreliable. By the fifteenth my vision had gone silver at the edges. I stood on the landing beside a stack of shell crates and breathed until the world resumed its ordinary colors.

Then I walked out into the light like a man with nothing on his mind, bought a meat pie I could not taste, and ate it on the seawall in full view of the counting house, because a watched man must be boring in public if he is to be interesting in private.

Let me tell you what I understood, sitting there with grease on my fingers and gulls eyeing my crust.

He will never let me board that ship.

It was not a suspicion anymore. Suspicion is a fog; this was landfall. Gideon Voss had offered me my freedom priced at a betrayal, and when I declined to pay, he had filed me—not as harmless, but as *unresolved*. A ledger entry pending. And a man like Voss does not permit unresolved entries to walk onto a steamer bound for Singapore, where letters might be posted, magistrates consulted, mothers found. Whatever happened in the next six weeks, the Sarawak Star would sail with Nell's name confirmed or not at all, and I would be lucky to see the gangway except from a distance, from a hold, from a grave.

Some men, learning this, would run. I considered it, honestly—considered the night beach, the luggers, Callum's tales of ports where a strong back could vanish into a crowd. But running solves nothing that staying cannot solve better, and it abandons everything that staying alone can protect. My mother ran, and it cost her nine years and a lie about drowning. I was done letting Voss write the story of where Hallorans go.

So I sat on the seawall and let the terror finish its work in me. Terror is a fine raw material if you're patient. Boil it long enough and what skims to the top is clarity: they think I am slow, so my slowness is armor. They think I am alone, so my allies are invisible. They think I can be bought, so every day I stay unsold is a day they underestimate me. And they believe the paperwork is theirs—all the registries, the logs, the ledgers, the whole paper empire of Broome—when in truth the paper is where a man like Voss is most vulnerable, because paper remembers what its keepers wish forgotten.

Mrs. Adeyemi kept the files. Callum carried the sea in his bones and could dive where my father died. Nell could get into places no one guarded because no one bothered to guard what a girl might want. And I had a face that lied beautifully by telling the truth slowly.

Six weeks. One berth. One harbor master who had mistaken ownership for power, and one asset who had just, quietly, declined the purchase.

I brushed the crumbs to the gulls, stood, and touched my cap to the counting house as I passed—as a good dull asset would—already composing in my head the message I would carry at low tide to the jetty, where a weathered stranger waited to learn that the trap had sprung shut around all of us, and that we had six weeks to cut ourselves out of it.

Behind me, in an upstairs window, a curtain shifted.

Someone had been watching the seawall after all.

And whoever it was had seen me smile.
