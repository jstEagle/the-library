## 21. Brack's Question

There is a particular way men look at you when they want something and are too proud to ask straight out. My aunt's suitors had it. The debt collectors at the door had it. And now this man on the veranda of the shell-sorting sheds had it, wearing it like a hat he'd borrowed and couldn't quite pull off.

"You'd be Eleanor Halloran," he said.

"Nell," I said. "Eleanor makes me feel like I'm being fined."

He smiled the way a knife smiles, if knives could be bothered. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with hands like cured hams and eyes that did all the working while the rest of him stood around looking decorative. Brack. I knew the name before he gave it. Everyone in Broome knew which men belonged to Voss the way you know which dogs belong to which yard—you hear them at night.

"I've been asking after your brother," he said.

"Have you." I went on tying the bundle of shell in front of me. Mother-of-pearl doesn't care who's watching; it catches light the same for a magistrate as for a thief. That's one thing it taught me young. "Oscar's not hard to find. He's wherever there's work nobody wants."

"When did you last see him?"

"Sunday. Church." I pulled the twine tight with my teeth. "He sits three rows back. Sings like a man being punished for someone else's crimes. You can't miss him."

Brack laughed. It was a good laugh, warm even, and that was the frightening part. Men who lie badly are no trouble at all. Men who lie well—men whose lies come wrapped in warmth—that's when you check where your money is and where the exits are.

"Family's a fine thing," he said.

"It has its moments."

"He ever mention a stranger? Sailor type. Weathered. Goes by Reeve."

I let the silence sit just long enough to be honest, then said, "Strangers come through Broome every tide. Half of them call themselves captains and the other half call themselves something better."

"This one's been asking about Hallorans."

"Then he's either a debt collector or a romantic," I said, "and I can't help either of them. We're not an interesting family, Mr. Brack. We're the kind people step over."

He looked at me for a long moment. I kept my face doing what it always does in the sheds—mild boredom with a suggestion of resentment, the expression of a girl thinking about her dinner. It's not a mask I put on. It's more like a coat I've worn so long I've forgotten I'm wearing it, except on days like this one, when some part of me underneath stood very still and listened to itself lie.

Because here was the thing: I didn't know anything. Not truly. Oscar had been strange lately—quiet even by his standards, and my brother's standards were set by clams—but I hadn't asked why. Asking questions in Broome was like poking a sleeping crocodile to see if it's asleep. But I knew *of* things. I knew a weathered sailor had been seen at the jetty. I knew Oscar had gone down to the flats at low tide twice this week when there was no reason on God's green ocean to go. And I knew, with the animal certainty of someone raised by a drunk who lied professionally, that when a man from Voss's office asks about your family, your family is already in danger, and every true word you give him is a brick you hand him for building your cage.

So I lied. Smoothly. Easily. With a little shrug at the end like a ribbon on a parcel.

And I hated it. Not because lying is wrong—I'd stopped believing that around the age of nine—but because of how *good* it felt. How natural. There's a version of me that lives just under the skin, the one my aunt's drinking built and Broome polished, and she is quick and smooth and entirely without scruples, and she comes out when men like Brack lean on the fence, and afterward she goes back inside and I have to live with her.

"Your aunt," Brack said then, conversationally, examining his fingernails. "Mrs. Kettle. She keeps accounts at the Meridian store, doesn't she? Running a tab?"

My hands went still over the shell. Just for a half-second. I made sure it was only a half-second.

"Everyone runs a tab at the Meridian store," I said. "That's rather the point of a company town. You pay us in scrip and we pay you back in labor until one of us dies of it."

"She's carrying a fair bit," he went on, as if I hadn't spoken. "Eleven pounds, or near enough. Old debts do get reviewed, come inspection season. New management likes tidy books."

"There's no new management."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But there's new *questions*, and questions make old debts interesting."

There it was. He hadn't asked me anything outright since the stranger. He didn't need to. He'd laid the arithmetic on the table between us like cards: my aunt's debt, eleven pounds, called in whenever Voss felt like calling it; and the price, which was whatever I knew about Oscar and the man named Reeve. He thought he was threatening me. What he didn't understand—what none of them understood, these men who thought possession was the same as power—was that I had nothing left that fear could reach. When you grow up watching a woman drink herself from respectable to ruinous, you learn exactly how much worse things can get, and after a while the distance between here and rock bottom gets so short that threats lose their altitude. Eleven pounds? I'd watched my aunt owe four times that to men with less patience than Brack and survive it by outliving their interest.

"I'll tell Oscar you asked after him," I said brightly. "He'll be touched. Nobody asks after him."

"That's not necessary."

"Oh, but I insist." I stacked the last shell and stood, brushing off my skirt. "Is that everything, Mr. Brack? Only the afternoon shift won't sort itself, and unlike some people I don't get paid to stand on verandas looking ominous."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Something more tired than that. Almost like recognition, though I couldn't think why a company man would recognize anything in a shell-sorter with salt-cracked hands and a drunk for a guardian.

"For now," he said, and touched his hat brim, and went.

* * *

I gave it an hour. Long enough to seem unconnected, short enough to matter. I told the shed supervisor I had cramps—the one ailment no man will interrogate—and cut along the back lanes toward the counting house, moving at the speed of a girl with somewhere dull to be.

The trouble with Broome is that it has no secrets, only delays. By the time I reached the lane behind the offices I'd already passed two people who'd want to know later where I was going, and I filed their faces away for future lying-to. The lane smelled of tar and hot ironwood, and the counting house windows threw long bars of light across the sand, and I found Oscar exactly where I'd guessed he'd be: stacking ledger crates under the eaves, alone, looking like a man rehearsing how to be furniture.

"We need to talk," I said.

He didn't jump. Oscar never jumps. Things land on him and he absorbs them the way sand absorbs rain. "Nell. Shouldn't you be—"

"Sorting shells. Yes. I invented a medical condition. It's very serious and entirely fictional." I stepped into the shade beside him. "A man came to see me today. Brack. Voss's dog, the big one with the hands."

Something moved behind Oscar's eyes then, quick as a fish under a boat. "What did he want?"

"What do you think he wanted? You, Oscar. Or whoever it is you've become lately, because I'll tell you now, my brother, you've been stranger than usual, and I count as an expert on strange." I crossed my arms. "He asked about a sailor named Reeve. Then he mentioned Aunt's tab at the store. Eleven pounds. Called up at inspection season, he says, if certain questions stay unanswered."

The fish behind his eyes went still. That frightened me more than anything else, because Oscar going still wasn't calm—it was the stillness of a man standing very quietly on ice and listening to it talk.

"What did you tell him?" he asked.

"Nothing. Less than nothing. I told him you sing badly at church and our family bores everyone." I lowered my voice. "Which brings me to the question I've been chewing on since he walked off. What have you done, Oscar? Or what's been done to you? Because men like Brack don't spend an afternoon on a shed veranda for small reasons."

For a moment I thought he'd give me the furniture act again—the dull nod, the nothing-to-see-here shrug that had served him five years inside the company's walls. I could see it assembling itself on his face, brick by obedient brick. And something in me rose up against it, hot and sudden, the same fury that had carried me through the whole conversation with Brack without cracking once. Five years I'd watched my brother fold himself smaller and smaller so the company wouldn't notice him. Five years of watching him survive by disappearing, and I'd told myself it was wisdom, and today I discovered it looked from the outside like dying politely.

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare do the quiet thing with me. I just lied to a professional liar on your behalf. I earned the truth. Hand it over."

Oscar looked at me then—really looked, the way you look at something you've been keeping in a drawer and have suddenly to decide about. And I saw the decision move through him like weather.

"Low tide tomorrow," he said quietly. "There's a flat past the second channel marker. Come at dusk. Bring nothing and tell no one, not even the way you tell no one, which I know is its own art form."

"Is that a yes to the truth?"

"It's a promise of one." He glanced down the lane, both directions, the habit of a lifetime. "It's not mine alone to give, Nell. There are others involved. But you'll have all of it—all of it—tomorrow."

"Others," I repeated. "What others? Oscar. *What others?*"

But he was already turning back to his crates, already becoming furniture again, though this time I could see the seams of it, the effort of it, and somehow that was worse and better at once.

"Dusk," he said. "Second marker. Trust me one more day."

I walked back to the sheds the long way, past the church and the dried-up well and the house where my aunt would presently be pouring her first glass of the evening and asking me, in her wounded way, why the world was against her specifically. And the whole way I turned over what I'd done on that veranda—how easily the lie had come, how smoothly, how I'd even enjoyed it a little, the fencing of it, the craft—and I hated Brack for making me show myself that skill, and hated my aunt for training it into me, and hated Broome for needing it.

But underneath the hate, steady as a keel, sat the other thing. The real thing. My brother was in something deep enough that Voss's own hound had been sent sniffing at me, and debts were being weighed like ammunition, and Oscar—who had spent five years teaching himself to be invisible—had promised me truth at dusk by a channel marker.

Whatever was coming, it was coming fast. I could feel it the way you feel a storm two days out, in the flatness of the water and the wrongness of the light.

I got home late. My aunt was asleep in her chair with the bottle tipped against her knee like a faithful dog, and on the floor beneath the table, half-tucked under the rug where she'd shoved it, lay a letter with the Meridian seal broken open—and my name written on it in her wobbling, drink-ruined hand.

They'd written to *her* about me. About my debts, or hers, or ours, tangled together like rope.

I picked it up and read it by candlelight, standing, because some things you shouldn't read sitting down.

Then I folded it small, hid it in the lining of my skirt where I kept everything that mattered, and began, quietly, to plan.
