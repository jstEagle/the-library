## 22. The Wreck of the Lorelei

The sea off Broome does its grieving quietly. That's the thing nobody tells you about drownings — the water doesn't hold a grudge, doesn't keep a marker, doesn't so much as ripple where a man went down. Five years on, the tide over the Lorelei's grave was the same flat, glittering indifference it had been the day Thomas Halloran's lungs filled up. The ocean is a bookkeeper with no conscience. It balances every account and remembers none of them.

I remembered enough for both of us.

I'd borrowed a lugger from a Malay diver named Salleh who owed me a favor from Thursday Island and didn't ask why I wanted it anchored over a spot marked on no chart. Smart of him not to ask. Men who ask questions in Broome have a way of stopping asking them permanently.

The morning was glass-calm, which I took as an insult. A day like that should've been reserved for weddings or christenings, not for diving down to shake hands with the dead. Instead the sun came up pink and generous over Roebuck Bay like God had nothing better to do than make the water pretty for me.

"You sure about this?" Salleh asked, chewing the end of a rope he was splicing. "Water's clear, but she's deep past thirty feet, and there's iron down there. Iron shifts."

"I'm sure."

"Your funeral." He said it cheerfully, the way divers say such things — as a professional courtesy, not a curse.

I got into my suit. It wasn't mine — I'd hired it along with the lugger, canvas and copper that smelled of another man's sweat and old rubber, the kind of rig that fits nobody and fits everybody, which is how most things in this world fit. The helmet went on heavy, the world going quiet and brass-rimmed, and then Salleh's face at the little port, mouthing something that was either "good luck" or "don't die," and honestly on Thursday Island those two sentiments were interchangeable.

Then the water took me.

* * *

Descent is a strange kind of falling. Slow, deliberate, a controlled surrender to weight. The light went from gold to green to a bruised twilight, and my ears popped and complained, and the air hissed in that steady rhythm that sounds, if you let your mind wander, exactly like breathing through somebody else's lungs. Which, in a manner of speaking, I was.

Below twenty feet the bottom announced itself: pale sand, ribbed like a sleeping animal's flank, scattered with shell rubble. And there she was.

The Lorelei lay on her starboard side, half-buried already, the sand reclaiming her the way sand reclaims everything up here — patiently, without malice, without hurry. Her hull was furred with weed and barnacle, her masts gone, her deck cargo long since salvaged or scattered. She hadn't been a grand vessel. A pearling lugger, forty-odd feet of working timber, the kind of boat you'd pass in any harbor without a second look. But boats aren't just boats when men die aboard them. They become something else. Monuments, maybe. Or evidence.

I hung in the water above her and let the current move me gently, and I thought about the night she went down. Not the official version — I'd read that in the company records Mrs. Adeyemi had risked her neck letting me glimpse, all neat columns and tidy phrases: *sudden squall, gear failure, misadventure, three souls lost.* Official versions are written by the survivors, and the best survivors are always the ones signing the ledger.

No. I meant the other version. The one Mara Halloran used to tell me about in fragments, back in Singapore, before she stopped talking about Australia altogether — the one where her husband came up out of the water with his hands shaking because of what he'd found, not what he'd lost. The one where a lugger loaded heavier than her manifest said she should be, riding low at the mooring while the barometer fell, and a harbor master too impatient to wait out weather that every other master waited out.

Three souls. Thomas Halloran. Two others whose names I never learned, because grief keeps poor records and Voss kept excellent ones.

I swam down along the hull, trailing my hand along the planking the way you'd trail fingers along a coffin lid. Politeness costs nothing. Somewhere under that weed were the timbers that had held him, or near enough. The current pushed at me, gentle but insistent, like a host trying to move a guest along, and I let it take me aft toward where the ballast would be.

Ballast. That was the word that had been turning over in my head for two weeks, ever since the pearl question first surfaced between me and Oscar — though "surfaced" is generous; more accurately it had been dragged up, dripping, by the look on the boy's face when Callum Reeve says too much, which happens to me regularly. The rumor I'd carried out of Singapore: Thomas Halloran found something the night he died. Something big. Something that never appeared in the Meridian Trading Company's books.

Pearls don't sink, you might think. Pearls get pocketed, sold, smuggled, worn. But a cache — a real cache, mother-of-pearl shells packed with seed pearls and a few stones worth a stationmaster's annual salary — that gets *hidden*. And where do you hide a cache you can't declare? You don't put it ashore, where it becomes inventory and inventory gets audited. You don't leave it on a lugger that's due for inspection. You hide it where only you know to look.

And if the hiding place goes down with a ship — well. Then the hiding place is perfect, isn't it? Because dead men don't file reports, and drowned witnesses don't talk, and the sea keeps everything exactly where you left it.

Unless somebody comes looking.

* * *

The ballast was amidships, exposed where the hull had split along a seam — a spill of granite boulders furred green, settled into the sand like a cairn some drunken giant had built. I worked my way along it, gloved hands moving stone after stone, and the work was slow and cold and my breath rasped loud inside the helmet, and twice I startled small fish that shot away like thrown coins.

Stone. Stone. Stone. Sand. Stone.

My arms were burning by the time I admitted I might have the wrong idea entirely. Maybe the cache was fiction. Maybe it was the sort of story that grows around every drowning — treasure and treachery, because "the sea took him" is unbearable and "a greedy man let him drown" gives the grief somewhere to go. Maybe I'd come five thousand miles and thirty feet down to chase a widow's tale.

Then my hand found a stone that wasn't a stone.

It was shaped like one, sized like one, weighted like one — but wrong. Too regular. Too smooth on two faces. I hauled it up out of the sand and turned it in the green murk, and even filtered through seawater and five years of silt, I knew the shape of a sealed jar when I held one. Stoneware, waxed cloth over the mouth, wired shut. The kind of container Chinese divers used for keeping pearls safe in their belts, except this one was the size of a man's head and had spent five years pretending to be ballast.

I nearly laughed inside the helmet, which is a good way to drown, so I didn't.

Instead I did the arithmetic every diver knows. One jar. How many more?

I went back to the cairn and stopped guessing and started measuring. The false stone had been wedged third from the end, low, in shadow. If you were Gideon Voss — careful, patient, a man who dressed his greed in ledgers — you wouldn't scatter them. You'd line them up. Countable. Recoverable.

Fourth stone from the end: jar. Seventh: jar. Eleventh, twelfth, thirteenth in a run of three, tucked tight together. Fourteen jars in all, each one heavier than honest granite has any right to feel, each one lifted out of the sand with hands that shook worse the further I went, until by the last one I wasn't shaking from the cold or the depth.

Fourteen jars. I brought up eleven on the lines Salleh had rigged — three trips, the last one made with my air running thin and my heart going like a drum in the shell of my ears. Three stayed below, wedged deeper than I could safely reach with the light dying overhead, because a dead Callum Reeve proves nothing and helps nobody, and I've been reckless exactly once per decade and had already used up this decade's allowance.

Salleh helped haul me aboard the second time like a landed fish, and when my helmet came off I sat on the deck dripping and gasping in the outrageous sunshine, eleven stoneware jars ranked in front of me, seawater pooling out of them onto clean planking.

"Well?" Salleh said.

I pried the waxed cloth off the nearest jar with a knife tip. Inside, packed in rice husks gone gray and sodden, mother-of-pearl shell gleamed like the inside of a chapel dome, and among the shell, loose, a scatter of pearls — small ones mostly, seed and button, but there, sitting in the hollow of a shell like an eye that had been waiting five years to open, one stone the size of a thumbnail. Round. Cream-pink. The kind of pearl that gets named after queens and starts wars in drawing rooms.

Salleh looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"That's not ballast money," he said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

He spat over the side, a sailor's benediction. "Whose?"

"A dead man's," I said. "Stolen from him by a live one."

We covered the jars with sailcloth and I sat beside them for the run back in, one boot on the fabric like a man guarding a sleeping dog, and I did my own accounting, which is the only kind I trust anymore.

Here is what fourteen jars means, laid out plain. Here is what I now held in sailcloth on a borrowed lugger: proof that the Lorelei rode low that night because she was carrying cargo that was never entered anywhere — cargo Voss had hidden in her ballast precisely because a storm was coming and a wreck explains everything. Cargo worth, conservatively, more than every wage the Meridian company ever paid Thomas Halloran, multiplied till the figures ran off the page.

Voss hadn't merely concealed a find. He'd *staged* a disaster. Loaded the lugger overweight with hidden wealth, sent her out into weather he knew was coming, and let the sea do the murdering for him — then written "misadventure" into the record with a steady hand and gone home to his dinner. Three men drowned so that a theft could retire, safe and sunken, until such time as Gideon Voss chose to fish his fortune back up.

Except he never did. Why? Fear, maybe — the wreck was watched, talked about, a graveyard. Or arithmetic: the pearls could wait, Voss couldn't afford questions. Either way, the sea had kept his secret at interest, and now I'd broken into the vault.

And here was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed anchor-chain, cold and unmovable: Oscar. Oscar sorting pearls by lamplight for the company that killed his father, memorizing which silences kept him safe, his whole life bent around a verdict of misadventure that was a lie with a signature on it. Every hungry year that boy had endured, every silence he'd bitten down on, traced straight back to that cairn of false stones. I'd promised a dying woman I'd find her son. I hadn't known I'd also be digging up the reason she had to send that promise across an ocean in the first place.

Vindication, they tell you, feels like triumph. Nobody tells you it feels like this: sitting on wet planking in the sun, guarding jars of a dead man's stolen wages, wanting to shout and wanting to weep and doing neither, because the man who deserved the shouting and the weeping was thirty feet down under a cairn of rocks, and no verdict on earth brings him up.

The bay opened ahead of us, glittering, indifferent, gorgeous. Somewhere on that shore, Gideon Voss was presumably polishing his courtesy and adjusting his accounts, unaware that his retirement fund was coming into harbor under sailcloth.

I touched the sailcloth once, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder.

"Hold on, Tommy," I said, to a man I'd never met. "You're coming ashore proper this time."

And behind us, three jars still slept in the dark under the Lorelei's bones — and ahead, on the jetty, a figure in company whites stood watching our lugger come in, very still, for much longer than idle curiosity takes.
