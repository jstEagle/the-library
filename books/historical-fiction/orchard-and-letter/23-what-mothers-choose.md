## 23. What Mothers Choose

The letter had been in my pocket all day, folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. I'd carried it through the shell sheds, through supper I didn't taste, through the long grey hour after lamps-out when Broome settles into itself like a dog circling twice before lying down. Now I sat on the end of the jetty with my back against a bollard and my boots dangling over black water, and I unfolded it for what I told myself would be the last time before morning.

It was the last letter. Callum had given me them one by one over the past week — *you read these in order, lad, or you'll hate her wrong* — and I'd obeyed like a diver obeys his rope, hand over hand, trusting the line more than the dark.

The first letters were easy. Easy to read, I mean; not easy in any way that mattered. They were full of Singapore — the smell of the quays, the rain coming down like a curtain drawn across the world, a boarding house run by a widow who salted everything including, apparently, her opinions. She wrote about work at a chandler's, mending nets she said were older than she was. She wrote small jokes. She wrote *I hope you are eating, Osc, you never ate, you pushed food about your plate like a man negotiating with it.*

She did not write why she'd gone. Not in those letters. There was a paragraph in the second one where the ink changed colour halfway through a sentence — a different pen, or a hand pressed harder — and then nothing, a blank space the length of three lines, as though she'd written something and scraped it off the page with a knife. I put my thumb against that bare patch and felt nothing, because paper keeps no ghosts you can touch. But I looked at it a long while.

Tonight was the last letter. Dated eleven months ago. The paper was thinner than the others, as if she'd been saving the good stock and this was what remained when saving ran out.

I read it by feel more than light, the moon doing what it could.

---

My Osc,

You will be angry with me. Good. Anger is honest, and I have had enough of love that lies politely.

* * *

I stopped there the first time, out on the water the words kept pulling me toward. A launch went by far off with a lamp swinging, and I watched it until my eyes remembered how to see close things.

*You will be angry with me.* I had been angry for years — since I was twelve years old and a harbourmaster's clerk had come to our door with his hat in his hands and his voice arranged into sorrow, telling us the Lorelei was lost with all hands, telling my mother she was a widow now, telling me — though he addressed this to her, not me — that the company would look after its people.

They had looked after us exactly as long as it took to draw up an indenture contract for a fatherless boy of fourteen, and then they had looked after the ledger instead.

Five years I had carried that anger like a stone sewn into my shirt. It had shaped itself to my body. I knew its weight better than my own heartbeat. And here was the woman who'd left me holding it, writing from across an ocean: *Good.*

I read on.

* * *

You think I abandoned you. I know how it must look from inside Broome, which is a town built on looking away from the water. But I need you to hear this whole letter before you decide what I am, because there are men in this world who count on people deciding quickly.

Your father found something, the year he died. You were too young to be told, and I was too proud of him to keep quiet, and between us we made the mistake every poor family makes: we believed a good thing could stay ours.

He brought it home wrapped in his shirt. A pearl, Oscar. Not a pearl such as they sell in the shops. A pearl like a moon that had got tired of the sky and come down to be held. Your father laughed the way he did when he'd been diving too deep — that giddy, gasping laugh — and he said, *Mara, we're rich, and worse than that, we're known.* He understood already, do you see. He understood what being known would cost.

He took it to Gideon Voss himself, because your father was an honest man and honest men believe honesty is a language everyone speaks. Voss weighed it, praised it, entered it in his book — and paid him half of what it was worth, and promised the rest at season's end. And then Voss did not enter it in any book at all, or if he did, it was a book kept somewhere darker than the office.

I learned later what your father suspected in his last weeks: that Voss had sold it privately, that there were others like it in that ground, and that the only loose thread left was a diver who knew what came up out of it.

Then the Lorelei went down, and your father with her, and the company called it weather. The same storm, they said, that had sunk two other boats — though those boats were wrecked on the far side of the bay, and yours went down in water your father had dived a hundred times.

I did not believe in storms, Oscar. I believed in Gideon Voss.

* * *

My hands had gone still in my lap. Out past the jetty the tide made its slow argument with the shore, and I let it talk, because I couldn't yet.

*I did not believe in storms. I believed in Gideon Voss.*

Every man in Broome says the company is hard but fair. Every man knows someone the company ruined and finds a way to blame the ruin on the man, or the drink, or the luck. We are taught from childhood that the water takes people — the water is a beast, the water has moods, the water is God's own ledger and no one audits it. It is the perfect crime, drowning. Nobody prosecutes the sea.

But my mother had grown up on this coast, daughter of a diver herself, and she had watched the company's boats go out in weather other boats stayed ashore in, and she had counted who came back. So she did the arithmetic nobody wanted done, and she arrived at Gideon Voss.

And then — this is the part I had to read four times — she wrote:

For a year I kept silent, because silence kept you fed. Then Voss came to the house. Not the office — the house. He sat in your father's chair without asking, which told me everything about him before he opened his mouth. He said the pearl grounds near the Lorelei site had shown promise, and that your father had been a careful man, and careful men sometimes marked their grounds. He asked if your father had left papers. A chart. Any record of where he worked.

I said no. He smiled at me the way a man smiles at a door he intends to open eventually.

After that visit, things began to happen to us. Small things. Our rent rose. My washing work dried up — ladies who had employed me for years suddenly had no linen. Someone spoke to the priest about the state of my soul, and the priest grew cold. And one afternoon a man followed you home from the sheds — you were thirteen, Oscar, you had a limp that week from a dropped shell case — and when I asked him his business he showed me a company badge and said he was concerned for the safety of children playing near the water.

Concerned for the safety of children. That is how they say it here. That is how they have always said it.

I understood then that I was not a witness to be ignored. I was leverage. A widow with two children — yes, two; Nell was born seven months after the Lorelei went down, and before you do the sums, Osc, know that your father knew, and married me knowing, and loved her before ever he saw her — a widow with two children and no protection is worth more to a man like Voss than any chart. Because a mother can be made to give up anything by threatening what she loves, and your father's friends might hold out against threats to themselves, but no one holds out against threats delivered gently to a child.

So I chose. Voss wanted the location of the grounds, and he believed I knew it, and he was tightening the screws one turn a month to make me tell. And I looked at you — at you limping home in the dusk with a man behind you measuring your walk — and I understood that if I stayed, one day one of my children would be hurt, or taken, or drowned, and it would be called misadventure, and I would trade what he wanted anyway, having bought nothing with the price.

There was a ship leaving. A woman on it owed me. And I had one asset Voss did not know about: your father, bless him, had written down the grounds after all — a little chart, hidden where I knew he'd hide it, because I knew his hiding places better than he knew them himself. I did not take the chart with me. I left it where it was, and I told Voss nothing, and I got myself aboard that ship with a story about a sister in Singapore, and I left my children behind me like a trail of bread crumbs leading nowhere, because a mother gone is worthless as leverage, but a mother present is gold.

Do you understand? I did not leave to escape you. I left so that there would be nothing left to threaten. I made myself worthless, Osc. I spent five years making myself worthless — no name, no history, a woman mending nets in a boarding house that smells of salt and cabbage — and it has worked. No one has come. No one has followed. Whatever your father hid is still hidden, and I am nobody, and nobody is safe.

But I am dying of it, son. Being nobody is a kind of drowning, and I have been under a long time.

If you are reading this, then Callum found you, and Callum is the only honest man I ever met on a deck, and I have paid him the only currency I have left, which is the truth. If you are reading this, then perhaps the chart matters again, or perhaps it doesn't, or perhaps you simply want to know your mother did not leave because her heart was small. It wasn't. It was so full of you it leaked for five years onto every page I wrote and burned.

Come if you can. Or hate me if you must. Both are welcome; only write.

Your mother,
Mara

* * *

I sat on the jetty until the moon moved a hand's width, and the anger did not so much leave me as change shape.

That was the thing nobody tells you about fury: it has a skeleton, and if you pull the flesh away you find a structure underneath, and the structure of mine had always been a question. *Why did you go? Why me? Why us?* Five years I'd asked it of a woman who wasn't there to answer, and the question had hardened into an answer of its own: *because you could. Because leaving was easier. Because I wasn't worth staying for.*

And now here was the answer, arriving late and in her handwriting, and it was worse than the answer I'd invented, because the answer I'd invented had been simple. Simple hurts clean, like a cut. This hurt the way drowning hurts — everywhere at once, in places you didn't know you had.

She hadn't left because I was worth little. She had left because I was worth everything — literally everything, priced and leveraged, a hostage with a limp and a hunger. Voss had walked into our house and sat in my dead father's chair and smiled at a door, and the door had been my mother, and the hinges had been me and Nell.

Nell. Seven months after. Married knowing. Loved before ever he saw her. I did the sums anyway, sitting there, because grief makes you do sums even when you've been told not to, and the sums came out fine — came out better than fine, came out to a father who'd chosen us both. Which meant Voss had spent years hunting the chart of a dead honest man by squeezing the widow of the man he'd likely murdered, and the whole time there'd been a child in the house Voss never troubled to count, because girls don't count, in ledgers or anywhere else.

Something in my chest went cold and steady at that. Cold and steady is not a feeling I'm familiar with. My feelings usually arrive loud and get managed quietly. This one just settled in, like a tool laid on a bench within reach.

Because here was what I understood, out on the black water with the letter soft as cloth in my hands: Voss had never stopped wanting what my father found. He thought my mother knew. He thought the knowledge had gone down with her or across the sea with her. But the chart was still where she'd left it — *hidden where I knew he'd hide it, because I knew his hiding places better than he knew them himself* — and she had never told me where, because telling me would have been hanging it round my neck like the pearl itself.

Except she'd also written: *whatever your father hid is still hidden.*

Still hidden. In Broome. Under Voss's nose, or in some crack of the house we'd lost, or buried in the ground itself where a diver marks his grounds the way a spider marks a web — and I was the only person alive who might read that chart, because I had his hands and his patience and his habit of remembering water.

I folded the letter along its worn creases and put it back in my shirt, against my skin, where it had lived all day.

The anger wasn't gone. Don't mistake me — I intend to be angry for a very long time, the way you intend to breathe. But it had turned from a stone I carried into a keel, something that ran beneath me and gave me way through water instead of dragging me down.

Somewhere behind me, on the dark street above the beach, a match flared and died — quick, practised, the kind of light a man strikes when he wants to see without being seen. I didn't turn around. I sat very still and listened to the tide and thought: *Brack, or one of Brack's sort. Voss knows about the letters now. Which means Voss knows what I know.*

Which means the chart isn't a secret anymore. It's a countdown.

I stood up slowly, an obedient asset stretching stiff legs after a long shift, and I walked home the long way, past the church, past the sheds, past the aunt's house where a lamp still burned in Nell's window — and I began, for the first time in five years, to plan instead of endure.
