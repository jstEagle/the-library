## 24. Midnight Copying

There is a particular hour in Broome when the town stops pretending to be awake. The pearling luggers knock against their moorings like restless children, the dogs have exhausted their opinions, and even the wind off the water seems to take its boots off and settle in for the night. It is the hour I was built for, I sometimes think. Adunola Adeyemi, keeper of the Meridian Trading Company's ledgers, most honest woman in the building between the hours of eleven and three.

The rest of the day is another matter.

I sat at my desk with the lamp turned low — not out; a dark office invites questions, and questions are the one commodity I cannot afford to trade in — and I listened to the building breathe. Old timber does that. It groans and ticks as it cools, and if you've worked in it long enough you learn its language. Floorboard above the strongroom: settling, not footsteps. Rafter near the window: wind, not Brack. I had spent five years learning this building's honesty precisely because the men who ran it had none.

On my blotter lay the key to the records room, which I had lifted from Mr. Pell's hook at half past nine, while he drank his nightly brandy with the audacity of a man who believes himself unobserved. Everyone in Broome believes themselves unobserved. It is the town's great collective delusion, and I have made a quiet profession of not correcting it.

I did not go to the records room at once. That is the trick of courage, if anyone asks me, and no one ever asks me: you do not charge at it like Voss charging at an error in his favor. You sit with it first. You let it look at you and you look back, and you make certain that whatever happens after, you will be able to live inside the woman who did it.

So I sat. And I thought about debts.

* * *

People imagine a clerk's life is ink and tedium. They are wrong only in proportion. A clerk's life is memory. Every entry in every ledger is a small act of remembering, done by proxy, because men with money prefer to forget. My father used to say — he said many things, mostly in Yoruba, mostly to himself — that the white man's greatest invention was not the steamship or the telegraph but the ledger book, because it allowed him to sin once and account for it forever as though the sin were merely arithmetic.

I came to Broome eight years ago with a husband whose lungs the sea took inside two seasons, and I took the position at Meridian because grief requires wages like anything else. I expected to copy manifests until my eyes went and then die respectably somewhere with a view of water I had never learned to love.

What I did not expect was the drowning year.

Everyone in Broome remembers the drowning year the way you remember a bad tooth — not the pain itself but the weeks around it, the dread, the careful chewing. Three divers lost off the Lorelei in a single season, Halloran among them, and the company held its inquiry and pronounced misadventure with such smooth efficiency that you could almost believe the sea had simply reached up and helped itself. Almost. I was the one who entered the verdict into the record. My hand did not shake. I remember being proud of that at the time, and I remember being ashamed of that pride ever since.

Because I had seen the cargo sheets.

Not the ones bound and presented to the magistrate — those were works of art, tidy as hymnals. I mean the working sheets, the rough tallies the lugger masters brought ashore before Voss's people got their hands on them. A shell tally from the Lorelei's last dive that did not match the official manifest by a margin no storm could explain. A weight discrepancy. And a line item, in Pell's own cramped hand, that had been struck through so violently the pen had torn the paper: a single word, *special*, followed by nothing.

I asked about it once. Early days, still foolish enough to think a question was a question rather than a confession.

Pell looked at me over his spectacles with something that was almost pity. "Mrs. Adeyemi," he said, "the company pays you to enter figures, not to have opinions about them." And then, softer, in the tone of a man passing along a kindness the only way his nature permitted: "Some columns are better left blank."

I left them blank. For five years I left them blank, and I told myself it was prudence, and prudence is what cowardice calls itself when it wants to keep its job.

But here is what prudence never accounted for: Oscar Halloran.

I watched him, these past years. One watches everyone in this office; watching is survival. But Oscar was different to watch. He sorted pearls by lamplight with the stillness of a man practicing invisibility until it became a craft. He spoke only when spoken to, and less than that when possible. And yet — this is the thing — his work was flawless. Not obedient-flawless, the way a beaten dog retrieves flawlessly. Attentive-flawless. As though every pearl he weighed were a letter he was reading from someone far away.

And I knew that look. God help me, I knew it from the inside. It is the look of a person keeping faith with someone the world has declared dead.

When Callum Reeve came asking after Hallorans, I marked it the way I mark everything: quietly, in the ledger behind my eyes. When Oscar began leaving the office at odd hours with his collar up and his stride too long for a man merely walking, I marked that too. And when Voss summoned Pell into the strongroom last Tuesday and emerged wearing the expression he reserves for problems that require either money or graves — I marked that most of all.

I am fifty-one years old. I buried a husband and, before him, most of my illusions. I have no children, unless you count the ledgers, and they are bastards all of them. The sensible arithmetic of my life says: keep your head down, collect your wage, die warm.

But arithmetic, I have come to understand, is exactly how monsters do their sums.

* * *

At midnight I rose, took the lamp, and went down the corridor to the records room, and my heart behaved disgracefully the entire way, hammering like a debt collector at the door. The lock turned without complaint. Pell oils it faithfully; he is meticulous about everything except honesty.

The Lorelei files lived in the third cabinet, second drawer, under the general heading *Vessels — Lost*. I had rehearsed the geography so thoroughly in my head that finding them felt almost anticlimactic, the way a feared meeting often turns out smaller than its anticipation. There they were: the dive logs, the manifest, the salvage report, the inquiry transcript. Five years of dust on the folder edges, dust that had been doing more honest work than the paper beneath it.

I carried them back to my desk and laid them out, and I began.

Copying is a discipline, and I will confess to a certain vanity about mine. I can reproduce a hand — any hand — given sufficient example. It is a clerk's parlor trick, taught to me by a mission schoolmistress who believed neatness next to godliness and forgery adjacent to both. Tonight I did not imitate Pell's hand. That would serve nothing; the point was not to counterfeit the documents but to duplicate them, exactly, plainly, in a fair round script that could never be mistaken for anything but what it was: evidence, transcribed by a witness.

The original logs stayed where they lay. If Voss discovered the folder disturbed, let him discover copies missing instead — copies that could be explained, however lamely, as clerical overflow, personal reference material, the harmless hoarding of a fussy old woman. But the originals must stay in the cabinet, untouched, because a document removed is a document denied, and Voss denied things professionally.

So I copied. Page by page.

The dive log for the Lorelei's final week: depths recorded, times recorded, air estimated — and there, in the margins, small annotations in a different ink, later additions, the kind of thing a careful eye catches and a careless magistrate never would. *Shell taken up — see tally.* *Holding ground shifted, master advised delay.* *Master advised delay again.* *Company instruction: proceed.* Company instruction, proceed. Four words, and under them a drowned man's name.

My pen moved steadily. I had expected my hands to shake — I am fifty-one, not stone — but they did not, and I found this moving beyond all reason. As though some steadier version of me had been waiting eight years for this assignment and stepped forward now without being asked.

The manifest comparison took longest. I ruled columns, entered the official figures, then the working tallies beside them, and drew the differences out in a third column so that no magistrate, however sleepy, could miss the arithmetic. The Lorelei's last haul, officially recorded: modest. Actually taken aboard, per the lugger's own tally sheet: nearly triple. And the difference — the beautiful, damning, murdered difference — entered nowhere at all. No column. No line item. Just Pell's torn word, *special*, struck through in the copy I had seen five years ago, and absent entirely from the version bound for the inquiry.

They had erased it twice. Once in ink and once in law.

I set down my pen at some point past two and pressed my fingers flat against the desk, and I will tell you honestly what passed through me, because a woman my age has earned the right to her own testimony. What passed through me was my husband. Not his face — the sea kept that — but his hands, which I remembered perfectly, cracked and salted, counting shells into a company scale on a jetty very much like this town's, trusting the number the clerk wrote down because what else does a diver have to trust? The figure is the contract. The figure is the promise. Take it away and you have not cheated a man of wages; you have cheated him of having existed.

Halloran died for a number that was never written down. So tonight I wrote it down.

That is the whole of it. That is the theology of it, if you want one. Somewhere in Singapore a woman named Mara Halloran believes her husband's death was weather. Somewhere in this town a boy sorts pearls believing the same. Neither of them knows that the storm was a man named Gideon Voss, and that the rain was arithmetic, and that the flood was a struck-through line in a ledger. Well. Rain can be documented too. Everything can, if someone is willing to sit up past midnight with cold fingers and a steady pen.

I finished near three. Eleven pages, copied true, folded once, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and placed — after considerable deliberation — inside the false bottom of my hatbox, beneath the grey hat nobody has ever seen me wear. A hatbox is the last place a man like Voss looks, because a hat implies a woman has interests beyond his ledger, and he has never been able to imagine such a creature.

I returned the files to their drawer. I wiped the desk. I banked the lamp. And then, because I am thorough and because thoroughness is the only prayer I know, I checked the corridor.

Which is when I heard the front door.

Not opened — tried. Handle turning slow, the particular patient pressure of a man who expects locks to be his enemies. Then a pause, long enough for me to count four of my own heartbeats, and the scrape of a key being tested in a lock it did not fit.

I stood absolutely still in the dark of my office and did the arithmetic I am best at. Pell's keys were on Pell's hook, where I had left them, one hook short until dawn. Which meant the man at the door carried his own key, or believed he did — and only two men had keys to the Meridian office, and one of them was asleep in a house on the hill with his brandy.

Brack.

He tried the handle once more, unhurried, almost gentle, the way he does everything. Then his boots moved along the verandah boards — toward the window, I realized, toward the shutters — and I heard him stop directly outside the glass, close enough that I could hear him breathing through the gap in the slats, listening to the dark inside for a sound that should not be there.

I had left the lamp warm. Any fool could touch the chimney and know.

I put my hand on the hatbox lid and waited, and out on the verandah, Brack breathed, and the building ticked around us like a clock deciding whether to strike.
