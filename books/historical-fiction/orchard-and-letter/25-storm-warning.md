## 25. Storm Warning

There is a particular quality to a Broome morning that tells you the weather is about to turn. The air goes thick and sweet, the water lies flat as poured tin, and the frigatebirds climb higher than they have any business climbing, as though they've heard something we haven't. The old shellers call it the hush before the blow, and they read it the way priests read scripture—reverently, and with an eye toward what it's going to cost them.

I should have read it myself on Thursday last. Instead I was reading Mrs. Adeyemi's copied pages for the eleventh time, tucked inside my shirt against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and feeling—for the first time in five years—that the machinery of this town had finally begun to turn in my favor.

That is the trouble with hope. It makes a man stupid in exactly the ways despair never does.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes in Broome do, at the counting house.

I was at my sorting table by seven, doing nothing more remarkable than pretending to sort pearls while my mind ran through the plan like a man rehearsing his own funeral. Callum had the pearl cache secured aboard the *Kestrel*, wrapped in oilskin beneath his bunk where no customs man would think to look twice. Nell had the parish record—the baptismal entry proving Mara Halloran bore two children, not one—copied in Father Doyle's own careful hand and signed. Adeyemi's false logs were already in motion, destined for the magistrate's desk on inspection day, twelve days distant. And I had the berth registration half-forged in my kit, needing only Voss's countersignature, which he would apply himself when he processed the assisted-passage papers, because vanity is a wonderful thing in a bureaucrat—he liked his name on everything, even other people's escapes.

Twelve days. For the first time since the letter had arrived, twelve days felt like a distance I could actually cross rather than a wall I'd be beaten against.

Then Brack came down the jetty road at a pace that meant trouble.

You have to understand Brack to understand why I noticed. He was not a man who hurried. Hurrying admitted urgency, and urgency admitted caring, and Brack's entire profession consisted of appearing to care about nothing except his pay and his dinners, in reverse order. He walked through Broome the way a tide walks through rock pools—slowly, inevitably, ruining things without seeming to notice them. So when I saw him coming along the front at nearly a jog, coat unbuttoned, hat pushed back, I felt the hush settle over me the way the old shellers said it settled before a storm, and I knew.

He went into the counting house. He did not knock.

Through the window I watched him speak to Voss—or rather, I watched Voss listen, which was worse. Voss stood very still behind his desk with his hands folded, the picture of a patient man receiving mildly inconvenient news about freight rates. But his thumbs were moving. Little circles, one against the other, the only tell I had ever caught on him, and I had spent five years learning his tells the way other men learned psalms.

His thumbs were circling like propellers.

Mrs. Adeyemi appeared in the doorway of the records room with a ledger under her arm, and I saw Voss turn to her, and I saw her face do something I had never seen it do in all my time at Meridian House: nothing at all. Perfect blankness. A woman closing every shutter in the house at once.

Voss said something short. She bowed her head and went back into the records room, and Brack went with her.

I sorted pearls. Or rather, my hands sorted pearls while the rest of me sat in the corner of my skull doing arithmetic. The copied pages were missing from the registry drawer. That much was obvious from fifty yards. Which meant Voss knew pages *existed*, knew they'd been copied, and knew roughly when. What he didn't know—and here was the whole of my fortune balanced on a knife's edge—was who had taken them, or where they'd gone, or how many hands they'd passed through between the drawer and the dark.

Adeyemi had taken them herself, of course. That had been the arrangement; she'd insisted. "If a clerk's drawer is light," she'd told me, "the clerk answers for it. If a diver is seen leaving with documents, the diver hangs. Let me carry what is mine to carry." She had been gone two days now—sent, officially, to attend her sister's confinement in Cossack, which was true the way a coin rubbed on paper is silver.

Brack emerged from the records room alone. Voss followed him out onto the veranda, and the two of them stood talking with their heads close, and then Voss did the thing that turned my stomach to ballast.

He smiled.

Not the smile he used on pearling masters and magistrates. This was smaller, private, the smile of a man confirming that a tooth still aches exactly where he thought it did. Then he went back inside and rang the bell that summoned the company's foremen, and I understood that the twelve days I had been counting on had just become a rumor.

* * *

The lockdown announced itself the way such things always announce themselves in a company town: through small permissions revoked.

By ten o'clock, the divers' quarters were closed to visitors. By eleven, the shell-sorting sheds had been told that no worker would leave the beach camps without a written pass, effective immediately, owing to—here the foreman paused, savoring the official language—"irregularities in the cargo manifests pending inspection." By noon, the mail boat from the outer grounds had been held at anchor rather than allowed alongside, and the men aboard it were sitting in their own smell, cursing, watching Broome sit behind its locked doors like a oyster slammed shut.

And by one o'clock, a notice had gone up on the counting-house door, written out fair in Voss's own hand, informing all concerned parties that the annual departure inspection of the steamship *Sarawak Star*—previously scheduled for the twenty-sixth—would now be conducted **at the company's earliest convenience**, and that all applications for assisted passage must be lodged and verified **before** that date.

"Before," I said aloud, to nobody, standing in front of the notice like a man reading his own death warrant to check the spelling.

The mathematics were simple and brutal. The *Sarawak Star* sailed six weeks after the letter's arrival, which gave us inspection day, berth verification, and sailing day stacked neatly end to end like dominoes. Every part of our plan had assumed the inspection fell where it had always fallen, because bureaucracy is the one force in nature more reliable than gravity. Move the inspection forward—even three days, even two—and the chain broke everywhere at once. The magistrate wouldn't have Adeyemi's logs until she returned from Cossack. Callum couldn't produce the pearls without knowing when to produce them. And the berth itself, the single subsidized berth for verified kin, required Voss's signature on papers Voss now controlled absolutely, on a timeline Voss had just shortened at will.

He hadn't found us. Not yet. But he had picked up the board we were playing on and shaken it, to see what rattled.

I found Nell at the edge of the sorting sheds, where the shell heaps threw up their stink and glitter, and told her without preamble, because she was not a woman you could feed information in courses.

"The inspection's moved."

Her hands stopped in the shells. She didn't look up. Around us the other sorters worked on, brown arms and quick fingers, and the sound of the sheds covered everything, which was why I'd chosen it. "Moved where?"

"Soon. No date given. 'Earliest convenience.'" I let the words sit in the air between us, souring. "Which means whenever he judges us least ready."

Nell looked up then. She had sand dust in her eyebrows and a cut on her knuckle she was ignoring the way she ignored all injuries below the rank of severed limb, and her eyes went around the shed once—checking sightlines, checking ears—before she spoke. Five years younger than me and she checked sightlines like a quartermaster.

"How soon could it be?"

"A week. Three days. Tomorrow." I made myself say it. "He controls the clock now, Nell. That's what the lockdown means. It's not about the pages. It's about making sure whatever he doesn't know yet, he'll know before we can use it."

"And Adeyemi?"

"On the road to Cossack, or pretending to be. If Brack rides after her—" I stopped. Neither of us wanted to finish the sentence. If Brack rode after her, he would find either a clerk visiting a fictitious sister or a courier carrying proof that Gideon Voss had falsified dive logs the year Thomas Halloran drowned, and only one of those discoveries ended with the courier alive.

"She's cleverer than Brack," Nell said. It wasn't comfort. It was assessment, delivered flat, the way she delivered everything. "But clever loses to fast on an open road."

* * *

I went looking for Callum at low tide, because low tide was when a stranger with no business in Broome could walk the mudflats beyond the jetty and be reasonably certain the only thing following him was a heron with opinions.

He was there, boots black to the knee, poking through the exposed wreck timbers of some vessel old enough that nobody living remembered her name. He heard me coming—I made sure he did; startling Callum Reeve was a good way to get hit—and straightened up slowly, the way he did everything, conserving motion the way other men conserved money.

"You've seen the notice," he said.

"You too?"

"I can read. It's one of my failings." He wiped his hands on his trousers and studied me, and whatever he saw in my face made him stop studying and start listening. "Tell me."

I told him. All of it—the missing pages, the lockdown, the passes, the held mail boat, the inspection moved to 'earliest convenience.' I watched the news land on him the way ballast lands on a hull: no drama, just the waterline quietly rising.

"So he knows the drawer was light," Callum said at last. "But not who emptied it."

"He suspects everyone. That's the point of locking everything down. Suspicion is cheaper than knowledge and covers more ground."

Callum grunted. Out on the flats, the heron stabbed at something and missed. "Then we've one asset left that he can't lock down, lad, and it's the one he's least expecting."

"The pearls."

"The pearls." He crouched, drew a line in the wet mud with one finger—a coastline, a harbor shape I recognized as Broome's, then a longer line running northwest, out to sea. "Everyone's thinking like clerks. Papers, signatures, dates, who signs what and when. Voss thinks like a clerk too—it's why he wins. But a magistrate doesn't hang a man for a forged ledger. He hangs him for treasure." The finger stopped on open water. "*Lorelei* sits in eleven fathoms, four miles off the ground, and she's holding enough pearl to make a bishop weep. That evidence doesn't live in a drawer. It lives where he can't reach it, on a ship he doesn't control, in the keeping of a man he thinks is a drunk." He stood, knees cracking. "We don't wait for his inspection. We choose the hour ourselves."

"With what? He holds the passes, the berth, the timeline—"

"Lad." Callum put a hand on my shoulder, and it was heavy as an anchor stock. "I've been chased by better men than Gideon Voss across worse coasts than this one. Here's what I know about being hunted: the moment your hunter sets the pace, you're dead. You've got to break his rhythm. Make him react. A reacting man isn't thinking." His eyes went past me, up the beach, toward the counting house with its shutters drawn, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the easy weathered calm, but something harder underneath, something that remembered other harbors and other men who'd owned them. "And I happen to know exactly how to make that man react."

"What?"

"Give him back a ghost." He smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile. "He thinks the letters are ash and the past is buried. Time someone knocked on the lid."

* * *

That evening the storm arrived early, as storms in Broome will, arriving before the warning finished being posted. The wind came off the water in long gray gusts, slamming shutters up and down Dampier Terrace, driving the pearling luggers to double anchors in the roadstead. In the divers' quarters the men played cards and argued about whether the season was done, and I sat on my bunk with the copied pages against my ribs and listened to the building groan, and did the arithmetic again, and again, hoping a different answer would fall out if I asked politely enough.

It didn't. The truth was this: we had planned for a war of positions, and Voss had just declared a war of speed. Every piece of our plan—logs, record, pearls, berth—had been built to arrive together, at the inspection, before the magistrate, in order. Compress the timeline and the pieces arrived separately, piecemeal, each one deniable, each one destroyable on its own. A log page alone proves nothing. A baptismal record alone proves nothing. Even the pearls alone prove only theft, and theft, in a company town, is merely ambition.

Together, they proved murder. Apart, they proved only that somebody had been meddling.

Somewhere below, thunder rolled in off the water like furniture being dragged across heaven, and I thought about Mrs. Adeyemi somewhere on the Cossack road with Brack possibly behind her and a storm certainly above her, carrying five years of quiet courage in a leather satchel. I thought about Nell in the aunt's house on Napier Terrace, where the wind would be getting in through the same gaps it always got through, and whether she'd bar the door tonight or simply sit facing it, watchful, the way she faced everything.

And I thought about Voss, in his fine dry counting house with his thumbs circling, waiting to see what rattled loose.

The knock came near midnight, when the storm was at its worst and no sane person was abroad. Three knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried—the knock of a man who wanted it known he was not afraid of weather.

I went down the stairs in the dark and opened the door on Brack, streaming rain, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man delivering condolences he privately enjoyed.

"Mr. Halloran." My blood went cold at the name—my name, spoken plainly, in the open doorway—but his face gave away nothing except rain. "Harbor master's compliments. He'd have a word with you at the counting house. First thing tomorrow." A pause, precisely measured. "Eight sharp. Don't be late. He's got a full morning, what with the inspection being moved up."

"Moved up to when?" I asked, and hated how steady my voice came out.

Brack put his hat back on, tipping the brim so the rain sheeted off into the night behind him.

"Tomorrow afternoon," he said. "Right after he speaks with you."

And he turned up his collar and walked out into the storm, whistling.
