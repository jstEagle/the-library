## 26. The Bargain Reversed

There is a particular pleasure in arranging one's office before a difficult conversation, and I will not pretend I am above it. I have always believed that power is mostly furniture — where you sit, where they sit, what sits between you. A man who understands this can conduct an inquisition with the air of a man discussing the price of wool.

So that morning I moved things about.

The inspection was now set for Thursday — four days hence instead of ten, because Brack had found Mrs. Adeyemi's cabinet standing open and certain pages conspicuously absent from the Lorelei file, and because I have survived thirty years in the pearl trade by knowing that when a document walks out of your office, it is already walking toward someone who will read it aloud. I had sent word to the magistrate myself, all courtesy, all reason: *given the irregularities in the cargo accounts, Mr. Magistrate, perhaps an earlier audit would spare us all embarrassment.* He agreed readily enough. Magistrates are like tide charts; they respond to pressure in predictable ways.

Which left me with the boy.

Oscar Halloran was due at my office at nine o'clock. It was 8:40, and I stood at my window watching him come up the jetty road, because of course he came early. He had been coming early for five years. Indentured men learn to arrive before they're expected; it is the one small country they are permitted to own, the territory of not-giving-offence. I watched him pause at the bottom of my steps — just a breath's pause, no more — square his shoulders in that way of his, and climb.

I confess I have always liked him. This is not sentiment; it is professional appreciation. In five years the boy has never once given me trouble, never asked for anything, never looked at me longer than propriety allows. He sorts pearls like a monk transcribing scripture, and he keeps his mouth shut, and in Broome a young man who does those two things is worth his weight in shell. I had marked him years ago as material for advancement — a clerk, perhaps, eventually a trusted hand in the counting house. There is a version of events in which Oscar Halloran spends his whole life grateful to me, and I intend, this morning, to make that version available to him.

The trouble, of course, is the stranger. Callum Reeve. A sailor with salt in his voice and letters in his pocket, asking after Hallorans in every public house between here and the churchyard. And behind Reeve, somewhere on the water, the wreck of the Lorelei, which I had hoped was a closed account and which was evidently being reopened by persons with diving experience and too much curiosity.

And behind all of it — I will admit this even to myself, in the privacy of my own skull, where no magistrate can subpoena me — the pearl. The great grey monster Tom Halloran pulled out of the dark in '89, big as a dove's egg, worth more than everything else the Lorelei carried put together. The pearl that never entered my books. The pearl that bought Mara Halloran her passage and bought her husband a hole in the water, and bought me, if I am honest, this office, this chair, this view of the harbour where I have spent three decades becoming indispensable to men who barely remember my name.

I sat down at my desk. I arranged two chairs.

One I placed opposite me, at conversational distance. The other I placed — and this took some thought — slightly apart, angled toward the window, so that whoever sat in it would be looking at the harbour while I spoke. I did not yet know which conversation would happen in which chair. That was rather the point of the exercise. A good negotiator prepares both rooms before the guest arrives.

* * *

He knocked at two minutes to nine. Of course he did.

"Sit down, Halloran."

He sat in the near chair without being directed to it, which surprised me slightly, and then I understood: he had been in this office often enough to know where the punished men sit. Good. I could work with a boy who knew the geography of his own subjection.

"You've been reliable," I said, opening the ledger in front of me though I had no need of it. "Five years. No absences, no complaints, no drinking, no debts at the company store beyond what's reasonable. Do you know how rare that is?"

"No, sir."

"Rare," I said. "It is very rare." I turned a page I hadn't read. "Most of them, you give them wages and they turn them into ruin inside a month. Rum, cards, a woman with expensive tastes. But not you. You've been saving. Don't look alarmed — I sign off on the passbook entries. Forty-one pounds, eleven shillings. More than some free men manage."

He said nothing. His hands were folded in his lap like a schoolboy's, but I saw the knuckles, and knuckles don't lie. A man can make his face do anything; his hands are honest.

"Here is my difficulty, Halloran." I closed the ledger and gave him the full weight of my attention, which is a considerable weight; I have been told so by better men than either of us. "A stranger has arrived in this town. He is going about asking questions concerning your family. Your father. Your mother. He has been seen speaking to shell-sorters, to publicans, to — I'm told — a clerk in my own office." I let that last item hang there just long enough. Adeyemi. If she thought her midnight copying was invisible, she was mistaken, and I wanted the boy to carry that knowledge home like a stone in his shoe. "Now. An ordinary man might think: what business is that of mine? But you and I both know that nothing that touches the name Halloran is ordinary. Not since '89."

His face did something very small. Not a flinch. A settling, like silt after a boat passes.

"I don't know any stranger, sir."

"Of course you don't," I said warmly. "I never supposed you did. You are the most discreet young man in Broome. That is precisely why I'm speaking to you." I rose, went to the window, looked out at the water with the expression of a man bearing the cares of commerce. Behind me I heard him breathe. "But discretion can be tested from outside, Halloran, whether or not it exists within. If a magistrate asks questions on Thursday, and it emerges that a Halloran has been consorting with a stranger who claims — absurdly — that documents exist in the handwriting of a drowned woman, then questions begin to attach themselves to people who have done nothing wrong. People whose only crime is their surname. Do you follow me?"

"Yes, sir."

"Mm." I turned back. "You don't, quite. But you will."

I returned to my desk and did not sit. Standing over a seated man is a vulgar trick and I use it sparingly, only when it matters.

"Let me tell you what I know about your mother," I said, "since apparently everyone else in this town has been telling you things lately."

That landed. I watched it land. His stillness acquired a different quality — the stillness of a man underwater listening for something on the surface.

"She was a fine woman," I said. "Better than this town deserved, which is a low bar, but true. And when she went — when the *Kestrel* went down with all hands, or nearly all — I grieved her, as I grieved your father. Two losses in eighteen months. Terrible luck for one family. Terrible." I shook my head slowly. "Unless one asks the question that sensible men avoid asking, which is: what were the odds? And what was the cargo?"

Silence. The harbour ticked against the piles below us.

"I am not a sentimental man, Halloran, but I am a fair one, and fairness requires me to say: whatever that stranger has told you, whatever papers he waves about, understand what they are. They are a trap. Not for you — for me, and through me, for this company, and through the company, for every wage-packet in this port. A man arrives with letters in a dead woman's hand and a story about stolen pearls, and what does he want? Sympathy? No. He wants leverage. He wants a magistrate, a hearing, a scandal. And the raw material of his scandal — do you know what it is?" I leaned forward slightly. "It's you. Your grief. Your hope. He found a young man who has spent five years wanting his mother back, and he is going to spend that longing like coin. Because that is what such men do. They fish with other people's hearts."

I let him sit with that. It is a good line. I have used variants of it in three negotiations over the years and it has never once failed to make the listener feel simultaneously flattered and filthy.

Then I opened the drawer.

* * *

Every bargain has its moment of transfer — the instant when the thing offered crosses the space between two hands, real or implied — and a wise bargainer stages that moment carefully. Too early and the offer seems cheap. Too late and it reads as desperation. I had calculated that the correct moment was now, roughly four minutes into the drawer-opening portion of our interview, and so I drew out the paper I had prepared the night before, and laid it on the desk between us, face up.

An assisted-passage certificate. Berth class two, steamship *Sarawak*, sailing — well, the date hardly mattered; the ship would sail when the inspections cleared, and I controlled the inspections. The passenger line read, in my own fair copperplate: *HALLORAN, OSCAR J.*

I watched him read it. I wish I could describe to you what happened in that room during the six or seven seconds he stared at that piece of paper, because it was the most eloquent silence I have witnessed since old Meridian himself died mid-audit with a pen still moving. The boy did not move. His breathing stopped entirely, resumed, stopped again. His eyes went once — only once — to the window, toward the harbour, toward the sea, and I understood in that glance that I had guessed correctly, that the stranger had indeed told him about Singapore, about his mother, about the berth with one seat in it. Brack's reports had been suggestive but incomplete; this filled them in. The boy knew. He had known for days. And he had told no one, applied for nothing, moved not one inch — which meant he was planning something, and planning it alone, and planning it quietly, exactly as I would have planned it in his place.

Which made what I did next both crueler and kinder than it appeared. Crueler, because I was taking from him the secret he had built his hopes around. Kinder, because I was about to offer him that hope intact, wrapped, delivered — at a price he could pay without staining his soul past recognition.

"The *Sarawak* carries one subsidized berth," I said, "for verified kin of company families lost in service. Your father qualifies. Your mother's disappearance complicates the paperwork — dead women cannot vouch for sons — but I hold the registry, Halloran. I am, in the relevant legal sense, the only man in this colony who can confirm that Oscar Halloran is who he says he is. Which means—" I tapped the certificate once, lightly, "—that this berth is mine to grant. To you. Today. Signed before you leave this office."

His voice, when it came, was careful as a man crossing ice: "What would I need to do?"

"There it is," I said, almost fondly. "You see? You're a natural. Most men would have snatched at the paper first and asked after." I sat down at last, bringing us level, eye to eye, conspirator to conspirator — because that is the register in which the rest of the interview must be conducted, and register is everything. "Three things. Small things. Honest things, in fact — that's the beauty of it. Nothing you'd need to invent. Only confirm."

I held up one finger.

"First: the letters. Whatever the stranger showed you — and we both know he showed you something — you will surrender it to me. All of it. Every page in that woman's handwriting. For verification purposes. If there truly are letters from Mara Halloran, then they are evidence, and evidence belongs with the authorities, not circulating in public houses among sailors with grudges."

Second finger.

"Second: you will tell the magistrate, on Thursday, plainly and in your own words, that this man approached you, pressed these documents upon you, refused to leave you in peace — that you, a loyal servant of the company, were coerced by a stranger trading on your family's grief. That is not a lie, Halloran. That is a description. Coercion wears many coats; sometimes it calls itself kindness. A man who finds a grieving boy and fills his head with impossible hopes — what would you call that, if not coercion?"

Third finger.

"And third: you board that ship. You go to Singapore. You find your mother — if she lives, and I am assured by parties I trust that she does — and you live the life that letter promised you. Orchard weather. Your mother's table. Nell grown and beside you." I paused. "Everything the stranger dangled in front of you, I am handing you whole. He offered you a smuggled hope with conditions attached and a magistrate waiting at the end of it. I offer you the same hope, certified, stamped, and paid for — with three sentences of truth."

I leaned back. Outside, a lugger came about in the morning light, sails filling, and I confess the sight pleased me; there is no better omen for a negotiation than a vessel catching wind at the right moment.

"Do you understand what I am doing, Halloran? I am reversing his bargain. He came here to buy you with your own longing. I am buying you with it instead — and unlike him, I can actually deliver. He has a letter. I have a berth. He has a story. I have a stamp. When that ship sails, you will be on it, and the difference between us is that my version comes with papers."

* * *

For a long moment he said nothing, and I allowed him the silence, because a man deciding is a man already halfway bought.

I knew what was moving through him. I have sat across from indentured men for thirty years; I know the interior weather of the unfree better than most clergymen. He was weighing it — genuinely weighing it, and this is the part of my profession I do best, the part I am not ashamed of: I had constructed an offer so nearly innocent that refusing it would cost him more than accepting. That is the devil's arithmetic, if you like, though I prefer to think of it simply as arithmetic. One berth. One mother alive at the end of a voyage. Three sentences spoken to a magistrate, sentences that were — I want this understood — substantially true. The stranger *had* pressed papers on him. The stranger *had* traded on grief. Where precisely coercion begins and compassion ends is a question for philosophers, and magistrates are not philosophers; they are busy men who take a witness at his word when the witness is clean-faced and frightened and the alternative witness is a salt-crusted wanderer with no fixed address.

He would say yes. Watching him, I was quite sure he would say yes. His eyes kept returning to the certificate the way a thirsty man's return to a well — the name written there, *HALLORAN, OSCAR J.*, in ink that was already dry, already official, already more real than any promise a stranger could make. Five years of sorting pearls under lamplight, five years of being entered in ledgers under *assets*, five years of arriving early and keeping silent and owning nothing in the world but forty-one pounds eleven shillings and a dead father's name — and here, at last, on my desk, in my handwriting, was the door out. All he had to do was walk through it saying three sentences I had written for him.

"Take tonight," I said generously, "if you need it. Though I'll tell you frankly — the berth won't keep. There are others with claims, distant cousins, company men with favours owed. Thursday the magistrate convenes, Friday the manifests close. After that, Halloran, the arithmetic changes." I smiled, and I made sure the smile reached my eyes, because a smile that doesn't reach the eyes is wasted on the young; they can smell theatre. "But I don't think you need the night. I think you're a decisive young man. I've watched you decide things silently for five years. You decide quickly and completely and then you never look back. It's your best quality."

He looked at the certificate a long time. Then he looked at me, and for one unguarded half-second I saw past the careful blankness into the machinery underneath, and what I saw there was not gratitude and not fear but calculation — quick, cold, thorough — and it gave me a flicker of unease, gone as soon as it came, the way a shadow under clear water is gone before you can point to it. Because for that half-second the boy looked less like an asset considering an offer than like a man considering *me*, measuring, pricing — and I have been priced before, in rooms I didn't know I was sitting in, and I did not care for the memory of it.

"May I ask one question, sir?"

"Certainly."

"If I do this." His voice stayed level, deferential, dull — the voice he used for reciting manifest figures. "The letters. If they're genuine. What happens to them?"

"Burned," I said pleasantly. "Verified, catalogued, and destroyed. Standard practice for compromised correspondence. Sentiment is a luxury, Halloran; the company deals in certainty."

He nodded slowly, as though this were reasonable, as though burning a dead woman's last words to her son were a formality like any other, and I felt the transaction settling around us like ballast — the satisfying heaviness of a deal nearly done.

"I'll need the evening," he said. "To bring the papers. They're — not all in one place."

Something in the phrasing snagged at me. *Not all in one place.* A small phrase. An honest-sounding phrase. But I have spent thirty years listening to small phrases, and I know that the dangerous ones are never the large ones. Large phrases announce themselves. Small phrases slip past you carrying luggage.

"Naturally," I said. "Bring them tomorrow morning. Eight o'clock. We'll complete the certificate together — you'll sign the testimony draft, I'll countersign the passage order, and by Friday you'll be a free man on a ship to Singapore. Does that suit?"

"That suits, sir."

He rose. He thanked me — quietly, correctly, with exactly the right degree of a subordinate's gratitude, neither grovelling nor stiff. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and asked, in the tone of a man closing a ledger:

"And Nell, sir. My sister. If the berth's mine—"

"The berth is yours," I said. "Yours alone. Kinship clauses cover the named recipient only, I'm afraid. Company rules." I spread my hands, regretful. "One seat, Halloran. The sea is full of them."

He went. I heard his boots on the stairs, measured and unhurried, and then the outer door, and then the street sounds swallowing him, and I stood at the window and watched him walk back down the jetty road, not hurrying, not looking back, a young man carrying a piece of paper's promise in his breast pocket like coal in a stove.

Brack emerged from the doorway opposite, where he had been pretending to inspect crates, and raised an eyebrow at me across the distance. I shook my head once: *not yet*. The boy had taken the bait whole — or seemed to. Tomorrow at eight he would bring the letters, or he would bring a story about why he couldn't, and either answer would tell me everything I needed to know. That is the elegance of the reversed bargain: it wins twice. If he complies, the scandal dies unborn and the pearl stays buried and I sail comfortably through another inspection. If he refuses — if that careful blank face of his has been a mask all along, if the stranger's poison runs deeper than I judged — then tomorrow morning I will know it, and Thursday I will destroy him with his own hesitation, and the berth will go to some cousin of nobody, and the *Sarawak* will sail without a Halloran aboard, and the account books of 1889 will sleep on undisturbed for another thirty years.

Either way, I win. That is what I told myself, standing there in the morning light with the harbour glittering below like spilled coin.

Either way, I win.

It was only later — much later, when the lamps were lit and the tide had turned and I was reviewing the day's entries in the quiet way I have — that I remembered the small phrase, and turned it over, and examined it properly for the first time.

*They're not all in one place.*

And beneath it, faint as a diver's bubble rising far offshore, the other thing I had seen for half a second in his eyes and dismissed: the measuring look. The pricing look. As if, while I counted out his freedom sentence by sentence, the boy had been counting something of his own — and had arrived, silently and completely and without looking back, at a total I had not put on the table.

Tomorrow at eight, I told myself. He will come at eight, or he will send word, and either way the matter closes by Friday.

I wrote the entry in my diary, blew out the lamp, and slept — not well, but adequately, which in my experience is the most a careful man should ask of a week like this one.
