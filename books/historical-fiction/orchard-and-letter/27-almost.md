## 27. Almost

The offer sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Voss had said it plainly enough, in his office that smelled of ink and camphor and the particular sweetness of a man who believes he is being generous: the berth on the *Sarawak Star*, papers signed, my name entered in the passenger list as kin of Mara Halloran — verified, subsidized, legitimate. All I had to do was hand over Callum Reeve's letters and stand before the magistrate and say the words he wanted: *the stranger coerced me, sir. He forged the documents. I was a frightened boy.*

A frightened boy. At nineteen I was perhaps still entitled to the description, though I suspected Voss meant it the way a butcher means *tender*.

I walked out of his office without answering. That was the trick I'd learned in five years of indenture — never say yes too quickly, never say no at all. Silence buys you the length of a corridor, sometimes the length of a night. I took the whole night.

* * *

I couldn't go back to the men's quarters. Too many ears, too many men who'd sell their mothers' graves for a shilling off their debt, and who could blame them. So I did what I always did when the world pressed too close: I went to the water.

Low tide had pulled back past the jetty piles, leaving the mudflats shining under a half-moon like the lid of something vast and shut. The air smelled of brine and rotting shell, the smell I'd been born to, the smell my father had drowned in. You'd think a man would learn to hate it. Instead it settled over me like a coat that fits badly but belongs to you.

I sat on an upturned dinghy some fisherman had abandoned years ago, half-sunk in the mud, and I let myself do the thing I never allowed in daylight.

I let myself want it.

Singapore. I'd never seen it, but I'd built it plank by plank in my head since Callum put that letter in my hands. A city where the sea came in every color at once. Streets with names in three languages. And somewhere in it — a house, a room, a woman at a table with ink on her fingers, because my mother's letters slanted left the way they did when she wrote fast and thought faster. My mother. Alive. Not a story my aunt told drunk, not a wound I carried folded up small behind my ribs, but a living breathing woman who had written *ask after my Osc* as if I were a boy she expected to find exactly where she'd left him.

And freedom. Not the word itself — I'd stopped believing in words like that around the time I found my own name listed between a crate of tortoiseshell and eleven bags of mother-of-pearl in the company ledger — but the thing underneath it. Papers with my name on the line marked *free*. No one to tip his hat to while measuring the distance between my shoulder and the door. No counting the days of anyone else's calendar. A morning that belonged to me before I gave any piece of it away.

Six weeks. That's all that stood between me and it.

Or rather: four words stood between me and it. *He coerced me, sir.* Four words, spoken to a magistrate with my eyes down and my voice steady, and then a ship, and then the sea, and then her.

I sat on the rotten dinghy and I wanted it so badly my teeth ached.

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like sin. It arrives looking like arithmetic. And Voss's sums were good ones. What did the town owe Callum Reeve, a stranger who'd sailed in on somebody else's trouble? What did the magistrate care about dive logs from eight years ago? Men had lied for less than a berth across the world. Men had lied for less than a decent dinner. And what I'd be buying with my lie wasn't a trinket. It was everything. It was my mother's face. It was waking up and not having to calculate, before I spoke, whether the speaking was safe.

I could almost feel the paper in my hands already. Release papers, properly signed, Voss's seal — the same seal that had witnessed my father's verdict of misadventure, but I'd have made my peace with that. You can make your peace with nearly anything if the alternative is staying.

That was the thought that finally turned me cold. Not the lie itself — I could carry a lie; I'd been carrying silence for five years and silence is just a lie with better posture. But *you can make your peace with nearly anything.* My father had taught me to read water by what it hid, not what it showed, and I looked down into myself now the way he'd have looked over a boat's side, and I saw what was living down there in the dark of me, growing fat and comfortable on Voss's offer.

A man who would sign anything, if the price was right.

Voss hadn't offered me freedom. He'd offered me membership. Join the company of men who trade other people's lives and call it commerce. Say the words, take the berth, and forever after be the sort of man who could be bought — because once you're bought once, the receipt exists, and receipts get kept. Voss would keep mine. Even from Singapore, even across an ocean, he'd hold that paper against me all his days, or worse, he'd hold nothing at all and simply know what I was, the way a man knows a dog that's learned to fetch for strangers.

No. That wasn't quite honest either. I sat with it longer, because honesty at midnight on a mudflat costs nothing but pride.

The truth was smaller and uglier: part of me didn't care. Part of me — the part that had sorted pearls by lamplight until its eyes burned, the part that had watched Mrs. Adeyemi copy registry pages by candlelight knowing it might cost her everything — that part of me was tired, bone-tired, five-years-tired, and it would have said the four words in a heartbeat if the heartbeat didn't have to pass through the rest of me first.

So there I was, two men on a sunken dinghy, both named Oscar Halloran, one reaching for the pen and one watching him reach.

* * *

The moon went behind cloud and the mudflats went black, and in that darkness my mind, having exhausted Singapore, did what minds do. It wandered home.

Not to my bunk. To the shell-sorting sheds.

I hadn't meant to think of Nell. I'd been very carefully not thinking of Nell for days, which is how I knew I would. She has a way of arriving in my thoughts uninvited and acting like she owns the place — which, I suppose, in the country of my conscience, she does.

I saw the sheds clearly, though I'd only ever seen them from outside: the long benches, the kerosene lamps guttering in the sea breeze, the girls bent over trays of shell with knives and scrapers, sorting by lustre and grain, hour after hour, year after year. Nell at one of those benches. Nell at seventeen, sharp-eyed, quick-tongued, the fastest sorter in the shed if half of what I heard was true, and I believed more than half. Nell whose laugh carried over the fences and made even the foreman smile when he thought no one was looking.

Nell at twenty-seven.

The picture assembled itself without my permission, detail by detail, the way a wreck assembles itself on the seabed — slowly, inevitably, piece finding piece.

Nell at twenty-seven, still at the bench. Her hands thickened now, nicked and scarred along the knuckles from ten thousand slips of the blade. Her aunt dead or worse, the drink finally collecting its debt. Nell married maybe — to one of the luggers' boys, a decent enough sort who smelled of pitch and spent his wages before Wednesday — or not married, because Nell was choosy and choosy women in Broome stayed choosy alone. Either way: here. Still here. Sorting shells while the tide went out and came back, went out and came back, ten years of tides, each one taking a little more of whatever it was that made her fierce.

Because that was the thing about Nell. Fierceness doesn't keep. I knew that better than anyone. I'd had fierceness once, at twelve, the day they told me about my father — I'd had it like a fire in my chest, and I'd fed it on silence until it starved down to embers, and now I was mostly ash and habit. Ten more years of the sheds would do the same to her. Not all at once. Bit by bit. A joke she didn't make anymore because the foreman frowned. An opinion she swallowed because opinions cost wages. One morning she'd look up from the tray and realize she'd become someone whose whole ambition was the end of the shift.

And she'd look up — this was the part that caught in my throat like a fishbone — and she'd never know why.

She'd never know that her brother had sat within reach of her the whole time. That he'd known for weeks their mother was alive. That a berth existed, one berth, paid for, going to Singapore, going to *her* — and that her brother had traded it away for his own skin and four convenient words.

She wouldn't know. That was the elegant cruelty of it. Voss's bargain required no confession from me. I could take the berth and sail and never tell Nell a thing, and she'd live out her decade at the bench none the wiser, and somewhere across the water our mother would ask after her Osc, and I'd say — what? That Nell was fine? That the shells were plentiful?

I thought of the letter again. *Ask after my Osc.* She'd asked after me for eight years. Eight years of writing to a son she couldn't reach, and what kind of answer would I bring her? A son who'd found his sister and priced her?

The moon came out from behind the cloud. The mudflats reappeared, silver and endless, and I noticed my hands were shaking — not with fear this time, not with wanting. With something closer to clarity, which feels, while it's happening, remarkably like grief.

Because here was the sum that mattered, the one Voss hadn't put in his ledger. His arithmetic said: *one berth, one boy, four words.* Mine said: *one berth, two children of Mara Halloran.* And children of Mara Halloran, I was beginning to understand, did not come singly. You got the pair or you got neither. Anything less wasn't freedom. It was just escape with the bill deferred.

I laughed — actually laughed, alone on a sunken boat at two in the morning, which is the sort of thing that gets a man talked about in Broome. Because I finally saw the shape of it, the whole ugly beautiful shape: Voss thought he was offering me the thing I wanted most. He was. That was the problem. He'd weighed me correctly, the way you weigh pearl shell, by lustre and grain, and he'd found the flaw and pressed on it, and I'd felt it give.

But he'd weighed the wrong man. Or rather — he'd weighed the man I'd been for five years, the dull obedient asset, the boy who kept his eyes down. He'd never met the man standing next to him on this dinghy, the one who'd spent the last week learning that he had a sister, really had one, flesh and temper and laughter, not just a face seen across churchyards.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there after that. Long enough for the tide to start turning; I heard it before I saw it, a soft creeping whisper far out on the flats, the sea coming back for its own. Long enough for the cold to work through my shirt and into my bones, though I barely registered it. Long enough to run the whole thing through twice more, because a decision like this deserves to be checked for leaks.

First pass: Take the berth. Tell no one. Sail. — I lasted about thirty seconds on that one before the picture of Nell at the bench rose up and shut it down. Fine. Rejected.

Second pass: Take the berth, but tell Nell afterward, from Singapore, send for her. — Longer, this one. Seductive, even. Everyone alive eventually. Me free, Nell rescued by remittance, mother reunited with one child and then, with luck and money, the other. Nobody had to lose.

Except it was a coward's map and I could read it for what it was. Send for her with what? Wages from what work, in what city, earned by what sort of man? A man who'd testified falsely against the one person who'd crossed an ocean to help him? Callum would hang or rot or vanish, and I'd have done it, and every letter I sent Nell afterward would be written in that ink. And besides — I knew my sister by now, three conversations deep, which is deeper than most people ever get with anyone. Nell would smell the lie across an ocean. She'd take the money and hate me with it, and hatred travels well by post.

Third pass, then. The one I'd been circling all night the way a shark circles a light.

Give her the berth. Give her the papers, the passage, the proof of kinship — and God, the paperwork alone would be a war, the parish records, the registry, Voss's men at every gate — but give it. Stay. Stay in Broome with no berth, no bargain, no release, and a harbor master who now knew for certain I'd refused him, which was a sentence of its own. Stay and find another way out, or stay and find no way out, and either way watch my sister walk up a gangway carrying our mother's letter, and stand on the jetty until the ship was a smudge and then a rumor and then gone.

It was a terrible plan. It was the worst arithmetic imaginable. Two minus one equals one grieving, and the one who remained was me.

And it was the only sum that came out clean.

I understood, sitting there, why Voss had made the offer at all. Not because he needed my testimony — a man with Brack and a magistrate in his pocket can manufacture testimony wholesale. He'd made the offer because breaking me was worth more than using me. Five years of the dull obedient asset, and he'd wanted, just once, to see the asset choose. Choose him. Choose the company. Choose the small safe lie over the large dangerous truth, so that forever after he'd know what I was and I'd know it too.

Well. He'd have his answer, and it wouldn't be the one he'd purchased.

The tide whispered closer. Somewhere out toward the luggers a bell buoy knocked its slow wooden knock, patient as a heartbeat. I stood up off the dinghy, stiff with cold, mud sucking at my boots, and I felt lighter than I had in five years — which is a strange thing to feel when you've just decided to give away your entire future, but there it was. Wanting things for yourself turns out to be heavy. I'd been carrying mine so long I'd forgotten its weight until I set it down.

One problem remained, and it was not a small one, and I could see it waiting for me the moment I let myself look at it square.

Nell.

How in God's name did you tell a woman like Nell Thornbury-Halloran — a woman who'd fought her whole life for every inch she stood on, who trusted no one's charity and least of all family's — that you intended to hand her the one thing she wanted and take for yourself the staying behind? She'd refuse. Of course she'd refuse. She'd fight me like a cat in a sack. She'd call me a fool and a martyr and worse, and she'd be right on all three counts, and underneath the fury she'd be hurt, because being given away is its own kind of wound, and I'd learned enough of wounds to know you don't heal them by hiding them.

She'd have to know tonight. Tomorrow. Soon. Before Voss came asking again, before Brack's shadow crossed her path, before the machinery I'd just refused rolled forward and began grinding without me.

The bell buoy knocked. The tide crept in, reclaiming the flats inch by inch, indifferent and certain, the way the sea always is, the way I was trying to be.

I turned toward the town, toward the lamplit lanes and the sleeping sheds and the room above the aunt's house where my sister lay dreaming whatever sharp fierce dreams she dreamed, and I started walking.

Now I only had to tell her.

And survive the telling.
