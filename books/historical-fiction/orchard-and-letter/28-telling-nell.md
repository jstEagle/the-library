## 28. Telling Nell

My aunt's house had a particular smell at half past ten on a weeknight — rum gone stale in the carpet, kerosene burning low, and the sea salt that got into everything in this town no matter how tightly you shut the windows against it. I knew the smell the way you know your own hands. What I didn't know, standing in the kitchen doorway with a cup of tea going cold in my fist, was why my brother was sitting at our table like he owned it.

He'd knocked. Actually knocked. At my aunt's door, past nine, when respectable people were already asleep and my aunt was three fingers into disrespectable.

"Oscar Halloran," I said, because saying it out loud made it real, and I'd learned early that things you don't say can be taken back. "You've got a nerve."

"I've been told." He looked up from the table, and there was something wrong with his face. Not wrong like sick or sorry. Wrong like a man carrying something heavy who's decided to set it down whether the floor holds or not.

I told myself I wasn't pleased to see him. I did a fair job of it for about four seconds, which is as long as my pride usually lasts where Oscar is concerned. Then I put the kettle back on because apparently my hands had opinions of their own.

* * *

We'd fought eleven days ago. Properly fought, the kind where words get thrown and don't get caught. He'd sat me down on the church steps after Sunday service and told me everything in one breath, like ripping off a bandage he'd been too cowardly to remove properly — the letter, Singapore, our mother alive and writing her own name across the years, and one berth on the *Sarawak Star* with six weeks left on its clock.

One berth. Two children of Mara Halloran.

I'd done the arithmetic before he finished saying it. I'm quick with numbers; it's why they keep me sorting shell instead of sweeping floors. And I'd seen the answer land on his face before he could hide it, that particular look men get when they think they're being noble and haven't yet noticed they're being insulting.

"You're giving it to me," I'd said.

"It makes sense," he'd said. "You're younger. You need—"

"Don't," I'd said. "Don't you dare finish that sentence. Don't tell me what I need like you've consulted me."

That was the fight. It ended with me walking off down Hamersley Street with my jaw set and my eyes stinging and refusing, on principle, to wipe them where anyone might see.

Eleven days of silence. And now here he was at midnight in my aunt's kitchen, waiting for the kettle like we were ordinary people who hadn't spent over a decade being strangers who happened to share blood.

* * *

"So," I said, sitting down across from him. "Either you've come to apologize, or you've come to tell me you've changed your mind and you're taking the berth yourself, and I genuinely don't know which one will make me throw this tea at you."

"The tea's mine," he said. "I made it."

"That's my point exactly."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he looked down at his hands — those long, careful sorter's hands, scarred at the knuckles from five years of shells and lamplight — and when he spoke again the dryness was gone out of him.

"Nell, I'm not changing my mind. That's what I came to say. Not to argue about it. To say it plainly, so you know it wasn't a suggestion. It was always the plan."

"And you thought eleven days would wear me down."

"I hoped it would make you listen."

"Oh, it made me listen," I said. "It made me listen to every argument you'd rehearse in that stubborn head of yours. You've probably got them numbered. First: I'm younger, so my life has more years left to waste on a mother who left us. Second: you've got the company, the job, the — what do you even have, Oscar? A cot behind the counting house and a ledger line with your name under *assets*. Third: some rubbish about a man protecting his sister because that's what men do in stories, and nobody asked the sister."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Neither of us moved to stop it.

"You want the truth?" Oscar said quietly. "All right. The truth is I stood on the jetty last night and watched the tide go out, and I tried to imagine stepping onto that ship. And do you know what I saw?"

"Singapore."

"Aunt Bessie's kitchen," he said. "This one. Your face when I told you. Nell, I've watched you since you were small. Do you know that? From the counting house window I used to watch you walk to the sheds, and I'd count your steps, and I'd think, that one's mine, that one's my family, and if anything ever went wrong for her I'd—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I never told you any of that. I just watched. Like a coward. Like an asset."

The kettle screamed. I took it off the heat mostly so I'd have something to do with my hands, because they wanted to do something stupid like reach across the table.

"You should have told me," I said. "Years ago. Any of it. Instead I find out from a stranger's letter that my mother's alive, and from my brother's face that he's decided my future without me. Do you know what my whole life has been, Oscar? Other people deciding things. Aunt Bessie deciding I'd earn my keep by age eight. The company deciding what hours I work and what they'll dock from my pay. Voss's man Brack deciding last week that he could lean against the shed wall and ask me questions about you like I owed him answers. Everyone deciding. And now the one person I might have chosen myself goes and decides for me anyway."

"I'm trying to save your life."

"You're trying to spend mine," I said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. Saving someone means asking first."

* * *

He took it well, all things considered. Well meaning he went very still and stared at the table grain like it owed him money, which is Oscar's version of taking a blow. I've seen him take worse from the shed foremen and never blink, but family lands different. Family knows where the bruises already are.

"Say your piece, then," he said finally. "If you won't take it, say why. Not the angry version. The real one."

"The real one." I wrapped both hands around my cooling cup because my voice was less steady than I wanted it to sound. "Fine. The real one is that I've spent seventeen years learning not to hope. Hope's expensive in this town. It costs more than most girls make in a year, and the interest is brutal. So I stopped buying. I got good at wanting small things. A new ribbon. An afternoon at Cable Beach where nobody yells. Rain, once, just to feel it."

I looked up at him.

"And then a letter turns up with her handwriting on it, and I find out she's alive, and something in me that I nailed shut fifteen years ago starts hammering at the boards. And I hate it. I hate that it's open again. Because if I let myself want her — really want her, want Singapore and her actual living breathing self and whatever explanation she has that better be a very good one — then I can survive losing it. Barely. But if I take it *from you*, Oscar — if I board that ship knowing my brother gave up his one chance and stayed behind in the town that owns him — then I lose it either way. Even if I reach her. Even if she holds me and cries and tells me everything. There'd be a hole in it shaped exactly like you, and I'd carry that hole onto the gangway and off again in Singapore and it would never close. That's not a gift. That's a debt with my name on it, payable forever."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the back room my aunt snored like a ship taking water.

"You'd resent me," Oscar said at last. It wasn't a question. It was him checking his sums.

"No," I said. "Worse. I'd love you. And I'd spend every day hating that loving you cost me the only thing I ever wanted. That's not partnership, Oscar. That's a slow poison. Is that what you want between us for the rest of our lives? Me owing you my freedom?"

"I want you safe."

"Safe isn't the same as free, and free isn't the same as saved. I've read enough of your precious books to know the difference." I leaned forward. "And here's the part you haven't done the sums on, brother, because you've been doing them alone like you do everything. If you stay, Voss wins twice. He keeps his asset, he buries the evidence, and the woman who fled him dies in Singapore still hiding. But if neither of us takes that berth — if we tear up the pretty little plan and build a new one — then maybe, *maybe*, we get everything. Her, and each other, and Voss in a magistrate's pocket where he belongs."

Oscar stared at me.

"What?" I said.

"You've been planning."

"For eleven days, yes, while you were sulking at the counting house. I don't sulk. I scheme. It's a family trait you clearly didn't inherit."

* * *

Here's what I'd worked out, laid out on the table between us while the lamp burned low and the tide turned somewhere out in Roebuck Bay.

First: the berth wasn't truly fixed until the manifest was signed at inspection. Which meant the name written on it could change, legally, cleanly, if we could produce the kinship proof for whoever's name we wrote — and I'd already found the parish record, the baptism entry, Mara Halloran, two children listed, dates and all. Father Doherty kept a decent hand. All I needed was access to the vestry, and I'd been sweet-talking Doherty's housekeeper for a month over the flower arrangements, so access was a solved problem. Oscar didn't need to know I'd been cultivating a housekeeper. That was the beauty of schemes: you only show your cards when they win.

Second: the berth was worth nothing if Voss buried them all before the ship sailed. The copied dive logs, the Lorelei evidence, Callum's pearls — those had to land in front of the magistrate before inspection day, not after. Timing was everything, and timing was the thing Oscar was worst at, because Oscar believed patience was the same as safety. It isn't. Sometimes patience is just drowning slowly and calling it endurance.

Third — and this was the one I said last, because it was the hardest and I wanted it to land —

"We stop treating it as your sacrifice or my rescue," I said. "Because it's neither. It's two people who happen to be the only Hallorans in Broome agreeing that Voss doesn't get to write the ending. Whatever happens — whether I board that ship alone, whether we both find another way, whether it all burns down around us — we decide it together. Both names on the plan. No decisions made over anyone's sleeping head. Can you do that? Can you actually do that, or is watching-out-for-Nell so baked into you that you'll agree tonight and start scheming solo by Thursday?"

Oscar was quiet a long moment. Then he did something I'd never seen him do in all my remembered life.

He laughed. Short, cracked, surprised out of him — but a laugh.

"Thursday," he said. "That's generous."

"It's realistic. I know your character."

He reached across the table, hesitated, and then covered my hand with his — awkward, careful, like a man handling something he was afraid of breaking. His hand was rough and warm and shaking slightly, and I found that mine was too.

"All right," he said. "Both names. Together. God help Voss."

"God's got nothing to do with it," I said. "This is Halloran business."

* * *

We talked until the lamp guttered and the sky behind the window went from black to bruise. We divided the town up between us like children dividing a cake — me the parish and Mrs. Adeyemi, him the registry and the berth papers, Callum the water and the wreck, all of us the magistrate. We argued twice, agreed on nothing the first time and everything the second, and somewhere around three in the morning I realized my face hurt from smiling and couldn't remember the last time that had happened.

When he stood to go, I walked him to the door, and in the grey light he looked older and younger at once — worn thin by the company, lit through by something new.

"Nell," he said. "For what it's worth. I'm glad you refused. I'd have hated myself by Christmas."

"Yes, you would have. I'd have helped."

He grinned, actually grinned, and stepped out into the wet dark of the street, and I stood in the doorway listening to his footsteps fade toward the harbor.

Then I closed the door, leaned my back against it, and let myself shake — the good kind, the kind that comes after — because tomorrow the real work started, and the first item on my list was the parish vestry, and the second was finding out why, as I'd passed the window an hour ago, a broad-shouldered silhouette had crossed the lane outside my aunt's fence and paused, just briefly, looking in.

Brack's silhouette. I'd know the set of those shoulders anywhere.

Well. Let him watch. Let him report that the Halloran girl sat up late talking to her brother. By the time anyone understood what we were building, it would be standing, and standing things are much harder to knock down than plans.

I banked the lamp and went to bed smiling like a thief.
