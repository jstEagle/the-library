## 29. The Plan

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens at low tide in Broome. The water pulls back and exposes everything the harbor would rather keep sunk — old rope, broken shell, the ribs of boats that never made it home. Standing there on the mudflats with three other people who had, between them, exactly one working plan, I thought: this is what we look like. Exposed. Waiting for the tide to come back and finish us.

But let me start properly, because a conspiracy deserves better bookkeeping than most marriages get.

We met on Thursday night, in Mrs. Adeyemi's back room behind the counting house — the room where she kept her tea, her ledgers-in-progress, and, as it turned out, her spine. Callum arrived first, smelling of bilge and secrecy. Nell came second, slipping through the gate like she'd been born to it, which I suppose she had, given our family's talent for arriving places uninvited. I came third, having spent twenty minutes walking a route so convoluted that even Brack — if he was watching, and he was — would have needed a map, a compass, and divine intervention.

Mrs. Adeyemi came last, carrying a lamp and an expression that suggested she'd already priced out the cost of everything we were about to say.

"Sit," she said. "All of you. And before anyone speaks — Mr. Reeve, you will put that chair down. It is not a barricade, it is furniture."

Callum set it down. He'd been angling it toward the door since he walked in, and I understood why. A man who'd been tailed through half of Broome by Voss's shadow learns to think in terms of exits. But Mrs. Adeyemi's room had one door, one window, and one occupant who tolerated no nonsense, and even a seasoned sailor knows when he's boarded a ship with a stricter captain than his own.

"Now," she said, setting the lamp in the center of the table so we all sat in its circle like moths pretending to be committee members. "You have until my kettle boils twice. After that, I am a respectable clerk again, and any of you still here become my problem."

"How long does your kettle take?" Callum asked.

"As long as this conversation requires. I'm telling you the shape of the thing, not the timing."

I looked around that table and felt something I hadn't felt in five years of indenture: the strange vertigo of being trusted. Not useful — trusted. There's a difference, and it sits in the chest like a swallowed coin. Useful means they keep you because you're worth more than the trouble of replacing you. Trusted means they've decided you're a person, and persons can disappoint, which makes trust the braver wager by far.

"The magistrate comes for inspection in four days," I said. "Voss moved it forward because he thinks the copied logs are gone. They're not gone. They're under Nell's floorboards." I nodded at her. "That's our clock. Four days to turn paper into proof."

"Paper into proof," Nell repeated, with the flat skepticism she inherited from nobody I could name and everybody I could imagine. "And how exactly do we do that? Because from where I sit, we've got a sailor, a clerk, a shell-sorter, and an indentured man whose legal status is somewhere between a spanner and a dog. Against a harbor master who owns the magistrate's wine cellar."

"He doesn't own the magistrate," Mrs. Adeyemi said mildly.

"He owns something."

"Men like Gideon Voss don't own people. They own *leverage*, and they rent people by the hour." She folded her hands. "The trick is to make the rental terms worse than the risk. That is all politics is, children. Arithmetic performed in public."

Callum leaned forward, elbows on the table, and I saw the old shipmate of my mother's surface through the weathered face — the man who'd once promised a dying woman he'd find her son, and had crossed two oceans mostly on stubbornness to do it.

"Then let's do the sums," he said. "Four jobs. Four days. We divide them tonight and nobody overlaps, because if one of us is seen talking to another, Voss draws a line between two dots and starts guessing at the rest of the picture."

* * *

He drew the plan in the dust of Mrs. Adeyemi's floor, which she permitted with the air of a woman adding it to his bill.

"First," Callum said, scratching a circle, "the pearls. What's sitting in the Lorelei's ballast right now is the whole case. Voss reported that dive as a loss — no cargo recovered, vessel unsalvageable. If those pearls come up out of that wreck and onto the magistrate's table, then every number in his ledger becomes a question. You don't need to prove he killed Tom Halloran. You need to prove he lied about what was down there, and let a jury's imagination do the drowning for him."

"You've seen them," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd watched him come off that wreck with his eyes gone strange, the way a man's eyes go when the sea hands him back something it took years ago.

"I've seen enough to know they're real and they're many." He tapped the circle. "Problem is getting them up clean. Brack walks the jetty at dusk and dawn. So I go down at slack water tomorrow, small boat, no lights, and I bring up what I can carry in one trip. One trip, Oscar. No heroics. If I'm interrupted, I scuttle the lot back into the ballast and we lose nothing but time."

"And yourself," Nell said.

"And possibly myself," Callum agreed, "but I'm cheap to replace and hard to insure, so the company won't bother."

Nobody laughed. It was that kind of joke — the kind you make so the silence doesn't get ideas.

"Second." He scratched a square beside the circle. "The logs. Adeyemi?"

Mrs. Adeyemi rose without a word and went to her writing desk. When she came back, she carried a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and she laid it on the table the way a surgeon lays out instruments.

"The falsified dive records. The Lorelei's true manifest against what Voss entered. My hand, copied fair, dated correctly, initialed where the original initials fall." She paused. "There is a complication. The originals were signed by the diving master as well — a Mr. Quillan, deceased these six years. My copy carries only Voss's initials. A careful magistrate may ask why."

"Can you forge Quillan's hand?" Callum asked.

"I can forge any hand," Mrs. Adeyemi said, with a coldness that made me glad I'd never shorted her on shell weights. "The question is whether I should. A forgery discovered is worse than an omission noticed. If the magistrate finds one false signature, he doubts every true word in the pile. No. My pages stay honest copies of dishonest documents, and the discrepancy itself becomes evidence — proof that someone scrubbed Quillan's name after the fact, which tells its own story about who had things to hide."

I remember looking at that oilcloth packet and thinking: there it is. Five years of my father's death explained in forty sheets of paper, and it weighed less than the lamp lighting it.

"There's a risk you should hear plainly," she went on, addressing me directly, which she always did when the news was worst. "If Voss discovers these pages exist — if Brack searches my desk, or if any of you are careless within earshot of the counting house — then I am finished. Prison at best. At worst, a woman alone with debts and no protectors, on a coast where accidents are cheap." Her chin lifted a fraction. "I am telling you this not so you'll pity me, but so you'll understand that I have already done the arithmetic myself, and I find the sum acceptable. Do not waste my courage by being clumsy."

"Yes, ma'am," said all four of us, in a ragged chorus that would have embarrassed a schoolroom. But I meant it more than I'd ever meant anything recited.

"Third," Callum said, scratching a triangle, "the parish record. Nell."

Nell straightened. She'd been sitting with her arms crossed, watchful as a cat at a mousehole, and I caught myself doing it again — watching her, this stranger with my mother's jaw and none of my caution, thinking *she shouldn't have to be here*. Then I remembered chapter and verse of our last argument, and shut that thought in a drawer where it belonged.

"Baptismal register," Nell said. "St. Aidan's. Me and Oscar both, entered same year, same mother's name. Mara Halloran. That's the blood proof — the berth verification needs kin documented, and the company registry lists Oscar as an asset, not a Halloran, so the parish is the only place our name exists honestly."

"You know the building?" Callum asked.

"I know the building." A grim little smile. "Aunt Peg owes the church restoration fund eleven shillings and has owed it since the Flood. Father Doyle mentions it every sermon like a man picking at a scab. I'm going to offer to clear the debt."

"With what money?" I asked, before I could stop myself. It was the wrong tone — big-brother tone, audit tone — and I saw her hackles rise like a dog deciding whether the hand reaching for it holds food or a stick.

"With money I earn sorting shells for a company that pays me less than it pays the boys, saved over three years in a tin under a floorboard, thank you very much for your concern." She dug in her pocket and set a worn leather purse on the table with a clink that silenced me better than any speech. "Eleven shillings buys me ten minutes alone in the vestry while Doyle counts it. Ten minutes is enough to see the register, learn which page, which line. On Sunday, when the church is full of everyone including Brack's cousins, I slip into the vestry during the second hymn and take what we need."

"Take?" Mrs. Adeyemi's eyebrow climbed. "Child, we are trying to appear lawful."

"A *copy*," Nell amended, rolling her eyes. "I copy the page in pencil, I put the book back exactly as it lies — and I mean exactly, Adeyemi, I've been watching how you straighten things since I got here and I've learned things about corners that would frighten you — and Doyle never knows. The debt gets paid, God gets His due, and we get our proof. Everybody wins except Aunt Peg, who loses her excuse to feel persecuted."

I wanted to say something. Something brotherly, something protective, something about Brack and about the way he'd been circling her like a shark that had learned patience. But I looked at her — at the purse, the plan, the fury banked behind her eyes like coal — and I understood that the most loving thing I could do was treat her as what she plainly was: the most dangerous person at that table.

"Sunday, second hymn," I said instead. "Which hymn?"

"Whichever one takes longest. I'll check the numbers Saturday."

Callum grinned despite himself. "Your mother," he said quietly, "would have organized the mutiny the same way."

Nell went very still. She didn't answer. But I saw her hand close around the purse, and I saw her file the words away somewhere permanent, and I thought: that's the first coin in whatever account she's keeping for Mara Halloran. Interest accruing.

"Fourth," Callum said, and his chalk finger stopped over the last shape, a line leading out of the circle of dust entirely. "The berth registration. That one's yours, Oscar. And it's the worst one."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about plans: the moment they're spoken aloud, they stop being possibilities and become appointments. Fate is a punctual creditor.

My task was the Sarawak Star's passenger manifest — or rather, the assisted-berth claim that had to be lodged with the shipping agent before inspection day, verified kin, verified funds, verified character. Three verifications, and every one of them ran through offices Voss either owned or haunted. The shipping agent, a Mr. Prynne, kept his books above the chandlery and his opinions wherever Voss told him to keep them.

"So I lodge the claim in person," I said, turning it over. "Tomorrow morning. Before the pearls come up, before anything moves. Just a young man applying for passage under assisted kin scheme, mother resident in Singapore, documentation to follow. Boring. Ordinary. The kind of request Prynne processes nine times a season."

"And Voss?" Nell asked. "The moment your name touches that manifest—"

"—he already knows my name. That's the point." I heard my own voice come out level and was faintly proud of it. "Voss summoned me last week. Offered me freedom for the letters. I refused, playing dull. He believes I'm too frightened and too stupid to act. So when my name appears on a berthing claim, it won't read as rebellion. It'll read as the dull asset finally scraping together his escape — which is exactly what he expects a Halloran to try eventually. Men like Voss forgive ambition in property. It's initiative they punish, and dull men don't show initiative."

"It's a mask," Mrs. Adeyemi observed, "worn well."

"It's the only one I own." Five years of practicing invisibility, and here at last was the dividend: I could be forgettable on demand. "Prynne takes the claim, enters it, and unless Voss checks the manifest personally, we're registered. If he does check—" I shrugged. "Then he knows, and we're no worse off than we are now, which is to say hunted."

"No worse off," Callum repeated, and rubbed his jaw. "Famous last words of every man I've ever buried."

The kettle began its second boiling. Mrs. Adeyemi glanced at it, then at the window shuttered against the town, then at each of us in turn — and I realized she was doing something I'd seen her do over cargo manifests a hundred times: balancing the load. Checking the trim. Making sure the weight sat even, or the whole vessel capsized in the first cross-sea.

"One more thing," she said, "and then you all go home by different streets. Trust." She said the word the way she said *debit* — precisely, and with an awareness of its capacity to ruin people. "For the next four days, this plan lives on whether each of you keeps faith with strangers. Mr. Reeve could take the pearls and vanish north; I could burn these pages and save myself; the girl could sell the register's location to Brack for her aunt's debts; Oscar could trade all of you for release papers, and Voss would pay gladly. Any single betrayal ends us. Do you understand? There is no redundancy in this design. We are each of us load-bearing."

Silence, then. The good kind and the terrible kind, which are often the same silence wearing different coats.

It was Nell who broke it, because of course it was. She stood, pocketed her purse, and looked around the table with the expression of someone signing articles of a ship she fully expected to sink.

"Well," she said. "I've trusted worse people for less reason. Aunt Peg drinks my wages and calls it guardianship. You lot at least have a plan and a kettle." She pulled the door open, then paused. "Oscar. Walk me as far as the corner. Different streets after that, I know, but the corner's dark and I've got a purse full of our future."

So I walked my sister to the corner of Mrs. Adeyemi's lane, through the smell of salt and lamp oil, past the counting house where my name lived in a registry under *assets*, and at the corner she stopped and did something she had never done in all the years I'd watched her from across yards and sheds and Sunday pews.

She hugged me. Briefly, fiercely, the way you grab a rail in rough weather — not tenderness so much as grip.

"Don't die," she said into my shoulder. "Any of you. Especially you. I've only just got a brother; I'm not giving him back to the sea or the company or anybody else's arithmetic."

Then she was gone down the dark street, quick and light, and I stood there a moment longer than was safe, holding the warmth of it, before I turned for home the long way round.

Four days. Four tasks. One berth. Somewhere out in the black water, the Lorelei sat on the seabed with my father's bones and a fortune in ballast, waiting — patient as everything else in this story — to see whether the living could finally add up their dead.

I didn't sleep that night. I lay awake rehearsing tomorrow's dull face, my forgettable voice, my ordinary request — and it was near dawn, listening to the first sounds of the harbor waking, that I heard boots on the lane outside my quarters. Unhurried boots. Familiar ones.

Brack stopped beneath my window and knocked once, softly, on the wall.

"Mr. Halloran," he called up, conversational as a neighbor borrowing flour. "Harbor master would like a word. About a manifest."
