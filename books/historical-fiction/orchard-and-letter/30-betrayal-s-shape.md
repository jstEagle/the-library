## 30. Betrayal's Shape

There is a particular hour in Broome when the heat gives up pretending and the whole town exhales. The light goes the colour of old brass, the sea flattens out like a sheet someone's forgotten to tuck, and every soul with any sense drifts toward shade, beer, or sleep. I have always liked that hour best, because it is the only time a man can follow another man through these streets without being followed himself. Everyone's eyes are half-shut. Everyone's memory of faces goes soft at the edges.

I had been tailing Callum Reeve for three days by then, and I will tell you plainly: he was good at it, which meant I was better, or we were both wasting our talents in a town where the biggest building is a warehouse full of shells.

But this chapter of my life — if you'll forgive a man who never learned his letters until twenty-two calling anything about himself a chapter — this part isn't about Reeve. It's about the girl.

* * *

First, so you understand what I was doing loitering outside St. Thomas' like a man waiting on a sweetheart thirty years younger than me, you should know how I came to be Voss's dog, because nobody is born one.

My name is Silas Brack. I was indentured to the Meridian Trading Company at fourteen years old, same as Oscar Halloran, though my father hadn't drowned heroically enough to earn me any sympathy. My father sold me, is what happened. Drunk on arrack and short on debt, he signed the papers with an X that the clerk turned into something respectable-looking, and off I went to the shell sheds with a number instead of a name. Number forty-one. I answered to it for six years. There are days, even now, when someone calls my name and my spine answers first with *forty-one*, and only after does Silas arrive.

I got out of the sheds because I learned to read. Not from kindness — there wasn't much of that going round — but because the tally-keeper was a lazy drunk who needed someone to copy numbers while he napped in the storeroom among the sacks. I copied numbers. Then I noticed the numbers could be made to say different things depending on whose nap depended on them. By nineteen I was doing small favours for men with large accounts. By twenty-three Voss had bought out the last of my indenture — paid it off like settling a bar tab — and told me I was free.

Free, he said, standing in that office of his with the fan turning slow as a paddle steamer, free to work for him at wages no shed hand would believe. Free to walk away too, he said, and smiled, and the smile did all the arithmetic for me. A freed man with no papers, no trade but copying, and a face known to every company man between here and Derby. That's not freedom. That's a longer leash.

So yes. When Voss says *watch the stranger*, I watch the stranger. When he says *find out who talks to the Halloran boy*, I find out. It isn't loyalty. Loyalty is for dogs who chose their master. What I have is gratitude shaped like a chain, and I've stopped trying to tell the difference.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble usually does in this town, with a woman doing something she shouldn't have to do.

The parish records live at St. Thomas', locked in a cabinet in Father Doyle's vestry, because in Broome the Church keeps better books than the Company and everybody knows it and nobody says it. Births, baptisms, marriages, deaths — the whole messy ledger of being human, kept by men who at least pretend the entries mean something.

Voss wanted to know who'd been asking after Halloran family records. He'd had Brack-shaped instructions on the matter since Reeve arrived, and they'd grown sharper since the office lockup, since Mrs. Adeyemi's midnight lamp — oh yes, I knew about her lamp; I know about most lamps in this town — and since Voss started moving the departure inspection forward like a man pulling a thread before the whole garment goes.

So I did what I do. I sat in the shade of the churchyard wall with a newspaper I couldn't finish for sweating, and I watched who came and went.

Father Doyle, predictable as tide tables. Two Chinese families from the pearling camps, come about a christening. A widow I shan't name who visits a grave every Thursday and has been doing it so long the path she walks is worn into the grass. And then, late afternoon Thursday, a girl.

She came up the side path quick, head down, hat brim low, and she moved wrong for a churchgoer. Churchgoers amble. This one walked like someone crossing a yard with a stolen chicken under her arm. She tried the vestry door, found it locked, stood a moment with her jaw set in a way I recognized from somewhere I couldn't place, and then drifted around to the front and let herself in through the main doors like any pious soul.

I gave it ten minutes and followed.

Inside, the church was cool the way stone buildings get cool, all shadow and dust motes and that smell of wax and old timber that always makes me feel I'm being quietly judged. The girl was at the side aisle, kneeling at the record cabinet — no. Not kneeling. Crouching, testing the lock with a hairpin held delicate as a surgeon's tool, and doing a poor job of looking like she was praying while she did it.

Nell Halloran. I'd seen her at the sorting sheds often enough — sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, the kind of worker who does exactly her quota and not one shell more, and who watches everything while appearing to watch nothing. Voss had pointed her out to me two nights prior over brandy: *The sister. The aunt owes money. Lean on her.* He'd said it the way he said everything, mild as milk, like he was recommending a wine.

And now here she was, picking the parish locks herself rather than wait to be leaned on. Which told me two things. One: she knew what the records held mattered. Two: somebody had told her. Which meant the Halloran business was wider than Voss believed, and my three days of tailing Reeve through fish markets and back lanes had been a tour of the wrong exhibit.

I should have stepped out right then. Announced myself. Done the job.

Instead I watched her work the hairpin, and I thought about fourteen-year-olds.

* * *

Here is what happens to you in the shell sheds, in case you were raised somewhere soft. You learn that your body belongs to a ledger. Every hour is entered against you. Every mistake is entered twice. You learn that the men above you speak in a language of *entitlements* and *forfeitures*, and you learn that the fastest way to stop hearing those words is to become useful enough to graduate into them — to be the one holding the book instead of listed inside it. That's the trap, you see. They don't keep you down with chains after the first year. They keep you down with the offer of a chair slightly higher than your stool.

I took the chair. Oscar Halloran, by all accounts, has spent five years refusing it, staying usefully invisible, and there's a part of me that has hated him for it — hated the purity, the stubbornness, the way a man can stay honest just by keeping his head down and his hands moving. Hated him the way you hate anyone who reminds you of the exit you didn't take.

His sister, crouched at a locked cabinet with a hairpin and a prayer she clearly wasn't meaning, had the same set to her shoulders. Same blood. Same refusal, wearing a smaller frame.

The hairpin slipped. The lock clicked anyway — she was better than I'd judged, or the lock was older than the church — and the cabinet swung open onto its shelves of bound registers, and Nell Halloran went still, listening, the way animals go still.

That's when I should have coughed.

* * *

Let me tell you about the last time I caught someone at something, so you understand what I was choosing against.

Three years back, a shell hand named Tomas Reyes got caught skimming — a handful of small pearls palmed each week, sent home to his mother in Manila, or so the story ran. Voss had me handle it. *Handle* meant find proof, present proof, stand in the room while the magistrate voided nothing and the Company docked six years onto Tomas's term, because theft from Meridian was punished not by law but by arithmetic, and the arithmetic always rounded upward. Tomas cried. I remember thinking, standing there with the evidence flat on the table between us, that I had once been the sort of person who might have cried too, and that this thought arrived without pain, like reading about weather in another country.

Afterward Voss bought me dinner. Real dinner, at Streeter's, with wine. He talked about the future of the town, the future of the Company, my future within both, and I sat there understanding perfectly that I was being paid not for the catching but for the becoming. Each job like that sands a bit more of you smooth. Eventually there's nothing left to grip. You slide wherever you're pushed.

Tomas finished his extended term last year. I see him sometimes hauling shell bags on the jetty. He nods to me. That nod is the worst thing in my life, and my life contains some genuinely bad things.

* * *

In the church, Nell Halloran pulled a register from the shelf — I could see the red spine, the baptismal volume, 1878 to '86 — and opened it across her knees right there on the floor, flipping pages with fingers gone suddenly clumsy.

I moved. Not forward. Sideways, along the back wall, into the deeper shadow by the confessional, where a man can stand indefinitely provided he doesn't sneeze and has made peace with God, which I mostly have, professionally speaking.

She found the page. Even from across the church I saw her whole body change — a small collapse, like a sail luffing when the wind dies. Her finger went down the line of names. She read it twice. Three times. Then she pressed the heel of her hand hard against her mouth, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped, because whatever she was, she was not a girl who could afford to cry long in a public building with the door unlocked.

Mara Halloran. Baptismal entry for a daughter, dated years after the mother's drowning. Or no baptism at all — I don't know what the register said; I was forty feet away in a shadow learning things about myself instead. But I knew what she'd found, or roughly: proof. The thing everyone in this affair was bleeding for. Proof that the dead woman wasn't dead, or that the living girl had a claim, or that the past had left a door open somewhere in the ink.

And I understood, with the clarity that arrives only when it's least convenient, that I was standing between this girl and the one subsidized berth on the Sarawak Star, and that Voss had sent me to be the door closing, and that the door had a name, and the name was mine.

* * *

A man in my position develops theories about himself, the way other men develop theories about tides. Mine run thus: that complicity is not a decision but a habit, performed daily, until the day it's interrupted — and the interruption feels less like awakening than like tripping on a stair you've climbed ten thousand times. You look down at your own foot and think, absurdly, *when did I start walking?*

The stair-trip happened like this.

She put the register back. Closed the cabinet. Stood, smoothed her skirt, and turned to leave, and the lamplight from the high windows caught her face full-on for the first time.

It wasn't that she looked like her brother. It was that she looked like *fourteen*. Not fourteen-year-old girls generally — her specifically, the age I'd been, the age at which a person learns whether the world is a ledger or a parish register, whether they are an entry or an author. She had that exact expression I remembered wearing in the sheds: the one where you keep your chin level and your eyes flat and you dare the world, silently, furiously, to write you down as nothing.

Number forty-one. I heard it in my own skull, clear as a bell struck in an empty room.

She walked up the aisle toward the doors. Ten steps from freedom. Nine. Eight.

"Miss Halloran."

God help me, that's what I said. Not *stop where you are*. Not *in the name of the Meridian Trading Company*. Miss Halloran, like we'd been introduced.

She spun, and I'll grant her this — she didn't flinch and she didn't bolt. She squared up like a bantam, chin coming level, eyes flat, daring me to write her down as nothing. "I wasn't doing anything."

"No," I said. "You were praying. Fervently. At the record cabinet. With a hairpin."

Her hand went behind her back, where the hairpin had already vanished — quick, that one. "Lots of ladies carry hairpins."

"In church, certainly." I came out of the confessional shadow, and I watched her track me, weighing distances, exits, odds. Smart. Her brother had the same calculating stillness; I'd seen it in the sorting room when Voss walked the floor, that studied dullness that is actually the sharpest survival tool a bonded person owns. "Your aunt owes the Company eleven pounds, four shillings," I said. "Since March."

Something flickered in her face — the fear arriving, being processed, being filed somewhere it couldn't reach her voice. "Is that why you're here? To lean?"

"That's generally the arrangement."

"And are you going to?"

I considered the question honestly, which is more than I've done for most questions in a decade. Outside, the brass light was failing toward evening; somewhere a cart rattled past, and the sound came into the church softened, like the town murmuring through its sleep. "Do you know what an indenture is, miss?" I asked. "Truly. Not what they tell you at signing."

"It's slavery with paperwork."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, ugly bark that bounced around all that holiness. "Slavery with paperwork," I repeated. "Yes. Well. I was one. Number forty-one, Meridian rolls, six years in the shell sheds. My father signed me over for drink debt." I don't know why I told her. I have never told anyone. It came out the way a splinter comes out — not chosen, just finally worked loose by pressure.

She blinked. Whatever she'd expected from Voss's man, a confession wasn't on the list, and I watched her recalculate me entirely — from wall to weathercock, something that turns with the wind but might, possibly, point anywhere.

"Why are you telling me that?" she asked. Sharp. Suspicious. Right to ask.

"Because you found what you needed in that register," I said, "and I am supposed to report that you found it, and to whom, and then Mr. Voss will move against whoever sent you, and the inspection will move again, and the berth—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "And none of it will matter, because Voss intends that no Halloran boards the Sarawak Star. That's not suspicion, miss. That's policy. I've heard the policy."

"You're telling me this why?" Still sharp. Good. Stay sharp, girl.

"I told you. I was forty-one."

"That's not a reason. That's a wound." She looked at me the way her brother must have looked at ledgers — auditing. "Wounds aren't reasons. What do you want?"

What did I want. I want, even now, to answer that well. Here is the nearest true thing I found: "I want to not be the door this time. Once. Just once, I'd like to be standing somewhere else when it closes."

She studied me for a long moment. Somewhere in the rafters a dove shifted and resettled, raining a little dust into the light. "If I walk out of here," she said slowly, "and you report me anyway—"

"Then I'm what I've always been, and you've lost nothing but an illusion you'd have lost eventually."

"And if you don't report me?"

"Then tomorrow I tell Voss the churchyard yielded nothing, and he believes me, because I have never lied to him." I paused. "Which means today is the first day of a new practice, and I'm unreliable at new practices. So don't dawdle."

She didn't smile — I suspect smiling is rationed for her, issued only on major occasions — but something in her jaw eased a degree. She took two steps toward the door. Stopped. Turned.

"The aunt's debts," she said. "Eleven pound four."

"What about them?"

"When this is over — whatever *this* is, and I know more than you think I do — they get called in, yes? That's how he works."

"That's how he works."

"Then consider them paid," she said, "by whoever wins. Consider that my price for believing a word you've said."

And she walked out into the brass evening, and I stood alone in a church full of recorded births and deaths, feeling lighter and more terrified than I had in fifteen years.

* * *

I reported nothing, as promised. I told Voss the trail on Reeve had gone cold near the luggers' camp, which was nearly true, and he received it with that milky nod and turned back to his window, and I stood in the office hating myself for the relief I felt at having gotten away with a kindness, as if decency were a crime I'd successfully concealed.

But here is what I know, and why my hands weren't steady on the way home.

Voss has moved the inspection. It sits now two days out, and the magistrate has been briefed, and the doctored evidence against Reeve is ready in a folder with Voss's seal, and I have seen the folder, because I carried it. Tomorrow or the day after, in a room full of townspeople, Gideon Voss will destroy a stranger and bury the Halloran name for good, and every piece of it will pass smoothly unless someone breaks five years of trained silence in front of a magistrate.

I watched the girl walk away from me tonight, and I thought: her brother won't break. He's too good at invisibility. Five years of practice. He'll stand in that room with his eyes flat and his hands still and let it happen, because that's what survival taught him, and I would know, because it taught me the same lesson in the same school.

Unless.

Unless there's a shape to betrayal that I've missed all these years — the possibility that a man trained to vanish can choose, once, catastrophically, to appear.

Two days. I give it two days. And God forgive me, part of me is already hoping to be surprised.
