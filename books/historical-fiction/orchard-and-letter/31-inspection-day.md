## 31. Inspection Day

They chose the courthouse for it, which told me everything before anyone said a word.

The Broome courthouse was not a grand building—it had been a pearler's storehouse in my father's time, and it still smelled faintly of brine and old shell, as if the sea itself had filed a grievance there and never been paid out. But it had a magistrate's bench now, and a floor that echoed, and room enough for the whole town to stand around and watch a man be taken apart in broad daylight. Voss understood architecture the way he understood people: not by loving either, but by knowing exactly what weight each could bear.

Word went round on Thursday that an inspection would be held Saturday morning—the departure inspection of the *Sarawak Star*, moved up from its proper date, conducted publicly, with His Worship Magistrate Ellison presiding over matters of cargo, manifests, and—so the notice read—*irregularities alleged against certain persons recently arrived in the district*.

Certain persons. Singular person. I stood in the crowd outside at half past eight and watched Callum Reeve walk into that building the way a man walks into weather he can't outrun: shoulders set, hat low, eyes doing arithmetic.

I should have stayed home. That's what five years of indenture had trained into my bones—stay home, stay small, stay useful, stay invisible. Mrs. Adeyemi had copied the dive logs three nights past. Nell had the parish record sewn into her bodice lining like a second heart. Callum had the pearls from the *Lorelei*'s ballast stowed somewhere I didn't know and couldn't ask about. The plan had four legs, and every one of them was supposed to hold until Monday.

Voss moved the inspection forward because Brack found pages missing, and now here we all were, two days early, standing on ground Voss had chosen and prepared the way a man salts a field before his neighbor's crop comes in.

"Move along," said a company man near the door, to no one in particular, meaning everyone.

I moved along. Into the courthouse. Into the crowd. Into the trap, wearing my face like a borrowed coat.

* * *

Magistrate Ellison was a dry stick of a man who had come north for his lungs and stayed for the quiet, and I had watched him preside over disputes about fence lines and stolen chickens with the expression of someone reading other people's letters by mistake. He did not love the Meridian Trading Company. He did not, so far as anyone knew, love anything. This was, under the circumstances, the best hope in the room.

Voss stood beside the bench in his good coat, the grey one with the silver buttons, looking less like a harbor master than like a monument to harbor masters—a thing erected rather than born. He had documents arranged on the table before him in a fan so neat it might have been laid by a surgeon. Beside them, flanked by two constables who kept their eyes on their boots, sat Callum Reeve.

Callum looked worse than he had any right to look, given that he'd spent thirty years being rained on by oceans. His knuckles were scabbed where Brack's men had held him—held him, they said, for his own protection—and there was a cut above his eyebrow that had been cleaned but not explained. He caught my eye across the room for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat is a long time when you're both pretending not to know each other.

"Mr. Voss," said Ellison, without looking up from the notice in front of him. "You've lodged a complaint."

"I have, Your Worship." Voss's voice carried the way still water carries a reflection—perfectly, and with nothing underneath. "A matter concerning fraud, forgery, and the theft of company property. I regret the necessity. The Meridian Trading Company has traded in this port for twenty-two years on the strength of its good name, and I do not bring accusations lightly."

He brought them, in fact, the way other men brought pearl shell—by the ton, sorted, graded, and priced.

"The complainant is one Callum Reeve," Ellison said. "Master of—" he squinted—"the lugger *Kestrel*, lately of Thursday Island. What is the nature of the allegation?"

"Threefold, Your Worship." Voss touched the fan of papers without disturbing it. "First: this man has represented himself to persons in this town as an agent of the family Halloran, deceased, in order to obtain money and goods under false pretenses. Second: he has presented forged correspondence—letters purportedly written by the late Mara Halloran—as proof of kinship claims upon an assisted berth aboard the steamer *Sarawak Star*. And third—" here he paused, and I swear to you he let the pause breathe, because Voss never wasted a silence—"he has conspired with parties inside this office to falsify the historical records of this company. Parties who will be dealt with in due course."

The last sentence was aimed like a harpoon. It passed through the crowd, over the heads of the pearlers and the shopkeepers and Mrs. Adeyemi, who stood near the back with her hands folded and her face composed into something beyond neutrality—something geological—and buried itself in my chest.

Parties inside this office. Plural. He knew. Or he suspected broadly enough that it made no difference, because suspicion, from a man like Voss, was a net with very fine mesh.

Ellison looked up at last. "These letters. You have them?"

"I have examples." Voss lifted three sheets of paper with two fingers, delicately, as though they might bite. "Recovered from the prisoner's lodgings at the Continental. I invite Your Worship to compare the hand."

A clerk—I didn't know whose—took the sheets to the bench. Ellison read. The courtroom did that thing courtrooms do, which is become absolutely silent while forty people pretend they aren't listening to every word.

"These are addressed to an Oscar Halloran," Ellison said slowly.

My name, spoken aloud in a public room, went through me like cold water down a well. Five years I had been a name on a ledger under *assets*. Five years I had answered to it only when shouted across a deck. Hearing it in a magistrate's mouth, attached to my mother's handwriting—or to handwriting dressed as my mother's—was a different order of exposure altogether. I felt Nell's eyes find me from somewhere near the pillar. I did not return the look. Looking was a currency; I had learned not to spend it.

"The late Mrs. Halloran," Voss continued smoothly, "was lost at sea in the year '89, Your Worship. A tragedy well known in this district. These letters are dated"—and again the pause, surgical—"four years after her death. Either the dead woman writes from the ocean floor, or someone has practiced her hand for purposes I leave to Your Worship's imagination."

A murmur ran the length of the room. Somebody behind me said, *shameful*, and I couldn't tell whether he meant Voss's accusation or the forgery, and that was the whole genius of the thing. Doubt doesn't need to pick a side. It only needs to spread.

Callum spoke for the first time. "Your Worship, those letters are genuine. I carried them myself. Mara Halloran gave them to me with her own hands in Singapore, and she asked me—" his voice cracked on the old grief and he mastered it, mostly—"she asked me to see them delivered. That's all I've done. That's all I came to do."

"And yet you also came to dive a wreck in our waters without license," Voss said mildly, "and to remove items from it. Items which, I note, have not been produced. Where are these treasures you claim to have recovered, Mr. Reeve? Where is this famous evidence?" He spread his hands, a showman inviting the audience to inspect the empty box. "You ask this court to believe a dead woman lives, a drowned diver was murdered, and a cache of pearls exists—all on the strength of documents no expert has examined and pearls nobody can produce. Forgive me, Your Worship, but in twenty-two years of shipping I have learned that men who promise evidence tomorrow are selling something today."

It was beautiful, in the way a shark is beautiful. Every word of it was built on true foundations—that Callum had dived the *Lorelei*, that the pearls existed, that the letters were real—which is the trick of the best lies: they don't fight the facts, they ride them.

And I stood there in the third row with a copy of the true dive logs folded flat inside my shirt, against my ribs, warm as a live coal, and I said nothing.

* * *

Silence has a taste. I had been tasting it for five years, so I knew its vintages well. There was the silence of the sorting shed, which tasted of salt and patience. There was the silence after my father's verdict of misadventure, which tasted of copper. And there was this one—standing in a public room while a man was dismantled for carrying my mother's words—this silence tasted of rot. It tasted like the moment a thing stops being prudence and becomes participation.

Ellison turned a page. "Mr. Reeve, do you deny entering this district under false pretenses?"

"I deny nothing except your harbor master's version of events," Callum said. "I'm sixty-one years old, Your Worship. I've been drunk in more ports than this man has audited. But I've never forged a letter in my life, and I'll swear it on any book you put in front of me."

"Swearing isn't examining," Voss observed gently. "With respect, Your Worship, sentiment is not evidence. I propose a simple test." He drew a fourth sheet from his coat—an envelope, stamped, addressed in a careful commercial hand. "This is correspondence received by this office from Singapore. Note the date. If letters can travel between here and that port, then perhaps the dead write more readily than we suppose—or perhaps, Your Worship, someone has had ample time, money, and motive to manufacture exactly the fiction most likely to move us. A dying mother. A lost son. An assisted berth." He smiled, briefly, at the room, sharing the joke of human gullibility. "Whoever arranged this knew precisely which hearts to aim at."

His eyes crossed mine as the smile faded. Not accusing. Worse. *Acknowledging.* As much as to say: *I know you're in the third row, Oscar. I know what's against your ribs. We'll come to you.*

The magistrate rubbed his jaw. "You allege conspiracy with persons inside your own office, Mr. Voss. Have you names?"

"I prefer to complete the documentary record first, Your Worship. One scandal at a time. I have served this community too long to hang its dirty linen all at once." Another pause, another harpoon loosed into the dark. "Though I expect the record will speak for itself by Monday."

Monday. After the ship sailed. After the berth closed, after the mail went south, after everything that mattered had been locked behind a departure already accomplished. He wasn't trying to win the hearing. He was trying to run out the clock.

I looked at Callum. Callum looked at the bench, jaw tight, a man watching his last coin roll toward a drain. And I thought about my father, who had once been told by men in just such coats that the storm was nobody's fault, that the salvage schedule was regrettable, that the verdict was misadventure and the matter closed. My father had died believing the company was merely careless. I had survived five years believing the company was merely powerful. Neither belief was true, and both had cost exactly what they cost, and the interest was coming due right now, in this room, in my silence.

Mrs. Adeyemi caught my eye from the back. She gave her head the smallest possible shake—not *don't*, I realized. *Not yet.* Wait for the moment. A clerk knows that documents have an order of operations; apparently so did she.

So I waited, and the waiting was the hardest labor I had done since the sheds.

* * *

Ellison adjourned to examine the disputed letters against the company's sample of Mara Halloran's hand—which sample, I noted, was supplied by Voss himself, from files Voss controlled, in a building Voss locked at night. The machinery of proof ran entirely through the accused's accuser. It was elegant. It was airtight. It was going to work.

While the magistrate bent over the papers, the crowd murmured, and in that murmur I heard the town deciding things. Old Pearly Chen saying she'd always thought the Halloran woman's drowning sat wrong. A pearler named Duffield saying strangers who turn up asking questions usually want something. A boy—Nell's friend, one of the shell-shed girls—whispering that Nell Halloran had been seen at the church records office, and getting shushed so hard she nearly vanished.

They were assembling a story out of the pieces Voss fed them. That's what crowds do; they're sorters too, and they grade whatever they're handed.

Then Callum did something foolish and magnificent. He stood up—constables pulling him back down—and raised his voice over the whole room:

"The pearls exist! I've held them! Ask what happened to the cargo of the *Lorelei*! Ask why the dive logs of that year don't tally! Ask Gideon Voss why a man drowned within sight of shore while his boat was called away!"

The room erupted. Ellison's gavel cracked like a rifle. "Sit down, sir! You'll have your chance to be heard or you'll have it in irons!"

"You've had yours," Callum shot back, wild-eyed, held by two constables and thirty years of guilt. "We all know how chances are handed out here!"

And that—God forgive him, God thank him—that was the moment. Because Ellison, dry stick, lung-man, reader of other people's letters by mistake, looked up from Voss's tidy documents and said, in a voice suddenly free of dust:

"Mr. Voss. You stated that the historical records of this office support your account. I would see those records. The original dive logs for the season of the *Lorelei*'s loss. Today. Before this hearing proceeds another step."

For the first time in the five years I had known his face, Gideon Voss hesitated.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. Perhaps less. A man of his craft should have covered it entirely, and nearly did—but nearly is a wide margin when forty people are watching. He inclined his head with perfect grace and said, "Of course, Your Worship. Though I must caution that some materials of that period were damaged by damp, and others regrettably misplaced during the reorganization of—"

"Misplaced," said Ellison. "Or produced. Bring me whichever survives."

And Voss turned to give the order to his clerk—and his clerk was Mrs. Adeyemi, who met his eyes with the smooth blankness of a woman who had spent thirty years making her face into a locked drawer—and I saw it land in him, the exact shape of his predicament: the true logs were gone, copied in midnight ink by the woman standing in front of him; the copies were against my ribs; and the only versions left in his office were the ones he had doctored with his own careful hand.

Whatever he sent for would damn him. Whatever he refused to send for would damn him faster.

His gaze swept the room once—past Adeyemi, past Nell at the pillar, past Callum straining between the constables—and stopped on me in the third row, and stayed.

"Mr. Halloran," he said, clear as a bell across the water.

The room went quiet in a way rooms rarely manage. Forty faces turned. Nell's hand flew to her bodice. Somewhere behind me somebody whispered, *which Halloran?*

"You were employed in the records office in that year," Voss continued, silk over steel, every word pitched for the magistrate as much as for me. "You handled the manifests of that season yourself. Come forward, boy. Tell His Worship what the ledgers say."

It was not a summons. It was a bet. He was wagering five years of my trained silence—my bowed head, my useful hands, my name under *assets*—against whatever I had hidden in my shirt. He thought he knew the odds. Everyone in that room thought they knew the odds.

I stood up.

The bench creaked. The coal against my ribs burned. Callum stopped struggling and stared, and Nell's face opened like a door, and Mrs. Adeyemi closed her eyes for one instant—*not yet*, she had said, but the *not* had expired, and she knew it, and something in her expression as she looked at me crossing that floor was the closest I ever saw her come to prayer.

"Your Worship," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "my name is Oscar Halloran. And I can tell you exactly what the ledgers say."

Because I had memorized them. Every line. Every falsified entry. Every ton of shell that never reached the books, every dive logged short, every hour of my father's last day recorded wrong on purpose by men who then called it misadventure.

Five years of silence, breaking like a dam—and the water, when it finally went through, went through in the direction I chose.
