## 32. Oscar Speaks

The courthouse at Broome had been built by men who believed that justice required shade, and so they'd planted two fig trees out front and left the interior to the mercy of the sea breeze, which arrived that morning carrying salt and the smell of pitch from the harbor. I stood under those figs with my back against the hotter of the two trunks, watching the town file in past me—shell sorters still in their aprons, a pearling master in a white linen coat pressed so sharply it could have stood on its own, Mrs. Adeyemi in her good grey dress with her hands folded like a woman attending a funeral she intended to conduct herself.

She did not look at me. That was part of it. We had agreed on that much: no glances, no signals, nothing for Brack or Voss or any of their quiet eyes to read. Everything we meant to do today would be done aloud.

"You'll want water," she'd said that morning, pressing a flask into my hand outside the office. "Speaking dries the throat, and you've five years of speaking saved up."

I had laughed. It came out wrong—a bark, startled out of me—but she took my wrist briefly, once, hard, the way you steady someone boarding a boat in rough water, and then she went inside to sit among the company's clerks as if she were one of them still.

The magistrate's name was Fenwick. He was new-ish, eighteen months in the post, a lean man with a drooping moustache and the particular patience of someone who had learned that half the cases brought before him would be lies told slowly. He sat beneath the portrait of the Queen with his spectacles low on his nose and called the room to order at nine o'clock precisely, because Englishmen abroad cling to precision like a rail in a storm.

The charge before him was fraud. Forgery. The accused was one Callum Reeve, master mariner, late of Singapore and points unknown, who stood at the magistrate's bench with his wrists bound loosely before him—not rope, just cord, the kind used on crates—and wore the expression of a man who has weathered worse mornings than this and intends to mention none of them.

Voss sat to the magistrate's right at a table draped in green baize, flanked by papers and by Brack, who stood behind him like a shadow that had been given a wage. Voss was immaculate. He always was. Grey suit, grey waistcoat, a chain across the front of it that caught the light when he moved, and he moved rarely, deliberately, the way a man moves when he knows he is being watched and wants the watching to confirm what he already believes about himself: that he is the steadiest thing in the room.

He had spent three days preparing this. I knew because I had watched him prepare it, in the way one watches weather come over the water.

On Tuesday he had produced, before Fenwick, a bundle of documents—the Lorelei's official manifest, certified copies of the dive logs from the year my father died, letters of testimony from two divers whose names I recognized and whose signatures I did not believe in. On Wednesday he had produced witnesses. On Thursday he had produced the story itself, polished until it shone: that Callum Reeve had fabricated evidence implicating the Meridian Trading Company in a fictitious concealment, that the "recovered pearls" Reeve claimed to have raised from the wreck were paste, that the man was a confidence trickster working some grudge older than Broome itself, and that the honorable harbor master, Gideon Voss, had been slandered by association.

Fenwick had listened to all of it with his spectacles low on his nose. Twice he had looked up at Callum and asked a question that seemed small—"Where exactly did you say you shipped out of in 'eighty-nine?"—and twice the answer had been noted down by the clerk in a hand too fast for me to read from where I stood against the wall.

That was where I stood now. Against the wall, near the door, among the ordinary crowd. A tall young man in a shell-sorter's shirt, sunburnt, quiet, forgettable. Five years of practice at being forgettable. The whole architecture of my survival had been built on this: stand still, look dull, be furniture. Furniture is never questioned. Furniture is never punished. Furniture watches everything and says nothing, and lives.

I have never hated anything the way I hated that skill in that moment.

Because Callum stood bound at the bench, and every hour the proceedings ran long was an hour closer to whatever Voss had arranged for the ending, and the ending would not be justice. Voss did not arrange endings that way. He arranged them the way he arranged manifests—entries altered, sums made to balance, the record bent until the truth looked like the forgery.

"Mr. Reeve." Fenwick set down a paper. "You claim to hold documentary evidence supporting your allegations. Where is it?"

"It was taken from me," Callum said. His voice carried easily; a man who has shouted into gales all his life does not strain in a courtroom. "Tuesday last. My lodgings were entered while I was out. Papers gone."

"And yet the harbor master's own testimony describes papers recovered *from* your lodgings," Fenwick said mildly. "Which is it? Were they stolen or seized?"

"They can't both be, sir, but I expect only one of us is keeping honest books."

A stir went through the room. Somebody behind me snorted. Brack shifted his weight, and I saw his eyes move through the crowd—counting faces, hunting one face—and I let my gaze drift toward the window as though the fig trees were fascinating.

"The pearls, then," Fenwick continued. "You allege you raised pearls from the Lorelei wreck. Produce them."

"I can produce them," Callum said, "when it's safe to. There are men in this room who'd see them disappear again, same as they disappeared thirty years' worth of other men's finds. I'll produce them when there's a witness list drawn up and signed. Not before. A pearl shown to the wrong man is a pearl buried."

"That," said Voss, rising smoothly, "is precisely the evasion one expects from a fabricator. He alleges treasure and produces rumor. He alleges forgery and cannot produce the originals—which, I note, conveniently 'disappeared.' Gentlemen, I have served this port for nineteen years. I have signed every manifest, certified every cargo. If the company had concealed finds—if men had drowned to cover a theft—I should know of it, and I tell you plainly: there is nothing. There has never been anything. This man has invented a ghost and asked us to pay it wages."

It was a good performance. That was the terrible thing—it was genuinely good. His voice had the worn warmth of a man tired of being maligned. His hands spread slightly, palms open, a man with nothing to hide. Nineteen years of signing manifests. Yes. Every word true, and every word concealing, because the manifests he had signed were themselves the crime—altered lines, underreported cargo, the Lorelei logged at a fraction of its take the season my father went down and stayed down.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking. I put them flat against the wall behind me and felt the heat of the timber, and I breathed, and I thought about lamplight and shells and the particular silence of the sorting shed after hours, and I thought about a letter written in my mother's hand six years after the sea was supposed to have swallowed her, asking after *my Osc*, and I thought about a girl with sharp eyes in the shell sheds who shared my blood and didn't know how much of it we'd both been spending to stay alive in this town.

Nell was somewhere in that crowd. We'd agreed she'd stay back, stay small, say nothing until—until. She'd argued. Of course she'd argued. "I'm not going to stand in a crowd and watch you hang yourself alone, Osc, that's not partnership, that's martyrdom with extra steps." But in the end she'd agreed, because the plan needed a witness on the outside, someone unremarked, in case the day went wrong and someone had to carry the rest of it forward. She stood now near the back by the water cooler, arms crossed, jaw set, and when our eyes met for half a second she gave me the smallest nod, the kind you give a diver before he goes under: *go on then. I'm here.*

"Mr. Fenwick," Voss was saying, resuming his seat with the sigh of the reasonable, "I ask only that we conclude this farce efficiently. The company has ships to run. Broome has work to do. I am prepared to provide certified copies of every document in question, so that this gentleman's inventions may be compared against fact and found wanting. Perhaps then we can return to the business of the port."

And Fenwick, damn him, hesitated. I saw it. He looked at Callum, bound and empty-handed; he looked at the mountain of certified paper Voss had assembled; and I watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes—the arithmetic every magistrate in a company town must do, the weighing of what is proved against what is merely claimed, with the added weight of knowing who owns the wharves and who signs the payrolls and who will remember, years hence, how a certain verdict went.

Callum caught my eye across the room. Just for a moment. He tilted his head, very slightly, like a man listening for a bell.

*Now,* that tilt said. Or perhaps it said *whenever you're ready, lad, though sooner would suit.* Callum Reeve was many things but subtle in a crisis was not chiefly among them.

My heart was going like a pump in a flooded hold. Five years. Five years of standing against walls. Five years of watching men speak for me, about me, over me—my father eulogized in a verdict of *misadventure* by a harbor master who had rushed the salvage boats out in weather that should have kept every hull ashore, my own name entered in a ledger under *assets* without anyone troubling to tell me I'd been inventoried. Five years of silence as a strategy, silence as armor, silence as the price of bread.

And underneath all of it, the whole time, the fierce buried thing—the longing I'd sorted pearls above, night after night, telling myself patience was the same as hope. It wasn't. I knew that now. Patience was just hope wearing work clothes.

Fenwick said something procedural. Voss began gathering his papers with the satisfaction of a man closing a ledger.

I stepped off the wall.

The distance from the wall to the open floor was perhaps twelve feet. I have crossed oceans shorter, in the sense that no ocean has ever been longer than those twelve feet. The room turned toward me the way water turns around a dropped stone. Brack straightened. Voss's hands stopped moving over his papers.

"Magistrate." My voice came out lower than I intended, roughened, like a hinge unused. I cleared my throat. Somewhere in the crowd Nell had gone very still. "Before you close the books on this one. There's a name missing from your proceedings, and I've come to supply it."

Fenwick peered at me over his spectacles. "Name yourself, then."

"Halloran." I heard it leave my mouth and felt the floor tilt, absurdly, as if the whole courthouse were a deck taking a swell. "Oscar Halloran. Son of Thomas Halloran, diver, lost off the Lorelei station in the storm of March 'eighty-eight. Indentured to the Meridian Trading Company since that date. Entered in your harbor master's registry—" I turned and looked at Voss directly, and it cost me more than the walking had, "—under assets."

The courtroom turned. Not literally—though it felt literal, a hundred bodies shifting like ballast—but in every way that mattered. The murmur that went up was low and rolling, and in it I heard the sound of a town remembering things it had been paid or persuaded to forget.

"Mr. Halloran," Fenwick said slowly, "indentures are a matter of public record. Your status does not require declaration in these chambers."

"No, sir. It doesn't. Which is why nobody ever declared it." I walked forward, another ten feet, close enough now that I could see the green baize table and the neat stacks of Voss's certified truths. "I stood silent five years because silence kept me fed and breathing, and I'm not ashamed of it—a man survives how he can. But I'm done buying my life at that rate. Because yesterday evening, documents reached me that your harbor master has spent three days claiming don't exist."

"Documents," Voss repeated softly. He had risen again. His composure was perfect. Only his eyes had changed—they had gone narrow and quick as a crab's, darting once toward Brack, once toward the door. "More of Mr. Reeve's vanished papers, I suppose."

"No." I set my hand on the table, on the edge of his stack of certified copies, and drew out the packet beneath them—thin, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord. Mrs. Adeyemi's copying hand, done at midnight by lamplight while the office slept, while discovery waited one floor below her desk like a debt coming due. "These are the Lorelei's original dive logs. The working copies. Not the fair versions filed for certification—the actual pages, in the clerks' hands, with the alterations visible. Weight of shell landed. Number of divers down. Times of descent. Weather entries." I untied the cord with fingers that would not quite obey me and laid the pages out before Fenwick, one by one, like a man dealing cards he has already counted. "Compare them against the harbor master's certified versions, sir. You'll find the cargo figures differ by more than half. You'll find the weather entries for the fourteenth of March read *moderate swell, diving permitted* in the certified copy—and in the original, *gale warning hoisted, all boats recalled*, in an entry dated the previous evening and countersigned."

Silence. The particular silence of a room recalculating everything.

Fenwick drew the first page toward him. Then the second. His spectacles came off entirely; he held them in one hand and read bare-eyed, which struck me, absurdly, as a man refusing even the mediation of glass.

"These are marked as clerk's originals," he said. "Certification copies are made from these. If these are genuine, then the certified records in this chamber—" he looked up, and his voice had gone flat and cold, "—are the falsified instruments."

"They are genuine, sir." And here I paused, because the next words were the ones five years had been building toward, the words I had rehearsed against ceilings and in the dark of the sorting shed until they no longer sounded like someone else's. "I know because I know the hand. The entry countersigning the recall order—that's my father's mark. Divers countersigned the weather boards each evening; it's how the crews were warned. Thomas Halloran signed that board at sundown on the thirteenth of March, 1888. He went down anyway the next morning, because the salvage orders came from this office regardless, and a diver under indenture doesn't refuse an order—he refuses, and the company holds his papers, and there are ways of making a man wish he hadn't." My voice cracked on that and I let it. What was the use of a dam if it didn't crack where everyone could see? "Two boats went out in a gale the company had already recorded. My father's line parted. The verdict filed was misadventure. The verdict that matches the actual documents is negligence, ordered from a desk, by the man sitting at it."

Every head in the room rotated toward Gideon Voss.

To give the devil his due—and I mean that sincerely, because I had spent enough time around the man to suspect there was due to be given—he did not crumble. He folded his hands on the baize and regarded me with something almost like pity, and when he spoke his voice was gentle, regretful, pitched for the room rather than for me.

"This is affecting, Mr. Halloran. Truly. A grieving son, manipulated by a stranger, presenting documents of provenance nobody can verify, copied by persons unknown. Tell me"—and here he turned to Fenwick with the weariness of a man forced to explain the obvious—"who provided these pages? Who had access to the company's working files? A clerk, I imagine. An employee of the very firm this young man accuses. Do we now accept as gospel any paper that walks in off the street, so long as it arrives wrapped in grievance?" He shook his head sadly. "Forgery is the oldest trick in the ledger, Mr. Fenwick. I have certified these records myself. My signature stands on them. Is my signature also forged?"

"Yes," I said.

The word came out of me calm and level, and it surprised even me.

"Your signature is genuine, Mr. Voss. That's the point. You certified fair copies made from pages that had already been altered—altered before certification, in the working files, over a period of years, by the office that controlled them. Your signature isn't the crime. It's the stamp on the envelope." I looked at Mrs. Adeyemi then—allowed myself the one glance we'd forbidden, brief as a blink. She sat among the clerks with her hands folded and her chin level and her eyes bright as coins, and she gave me nothing, absolutely nothing, which was everything. "But don't take my word for the alterations. Don't take hers. Take the paper's. The certified copy of the March 'eighty-eight log is written on company stock from the 'ninety lot—newer stock, sir; I've handled both lots daily for five years and I know them like skin. The original page is 'eighty-six stock. Someone re-copied the old records onto fresh paper and destroyed the working originals—or thought they had." I tapped the oilcloth wrapper. "One page survived. Filed in the wrong drawer in the year of the fire drill, mis-shelved, forgotten. Found Tuesday."

All of which was true except the finding-Tuesday part, and the finding part, and the drawer part. Mrs. Adeyemi had spent eleven years knowing exactly where that page was, keeping it the way you keep a coal banked under ash, waiting for a morning cold enough to need it. But provenance is a story, and stories are allowed a little seamanship.

Fenwick had begun laying the two sets of pages side by side, and his clerk leaned over them, and the room leaned with them, and I watched a magistrate's face do what my own face had done at a low tide's edge weeks ago when Callum Reeve first put my mother's handwriting in front of me: the slow collapse of one certainty and the terrifying construction of another.

"The weight figures," Fenwick said quietly. "The certified total for the Lorelei's final season is—" he checked, lips moving, "—one hundred and forty-two pounds of shell. The original working log records three hundred and eighty-one."

"Shell," Voss said into the hush, "was commonly under-recorded pending assay. Standard practice. Any pearling master will confirm—"

"By more than half?" Fenwick did not look up. "Across an entire season? With the divers' tally sheets countersigned at weights matching the *original* figures?"

Brack moved. It was small—a half-step, a breath, the shift of a man calculating the distance to the door. I saw Callum see it too. Callum smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

"There's more in that packet, Mr. Fenwick," Callum said pleasantly. "Manifests. Correspondence regarding the disposal of certain consignments that never reached the company books at all. And there's rather more besides, out at anchor, in the possession of persons who agreed to bring it ashore the moment it became safe to do so." He flexed his bound hands, casual as a man stretching. "Might be worth sending someone trustworthy down to the jetty. Before it becomes unsafe again."

Voss rose fully now, and for the first time in nineteen years of performing steadiness, the performance slipped—one heartbeat, no more—in which his face showed not the injured innocent nor the weary servant of the port but the actual tenant of the skull underneath: a man doing desperate sums and running out of columns. "Mr. Fenwick, this court is being managed. Managed like a market stall. These people have coordinated a pantomime—"

"Sit down, Mr. Voss." Fenwick's voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "You are not accused. Yet. You are, however, no longer conducting these proceedings." He turned to his clerk. "Send a constable to the jetty with a witness list. Two constables. Note the names of everyone present in this chamber." And then, to the room, in the dry tone of a man who had suspected since Tuesday that he was being handed a script and had decided instead to write his own: "This matter is adjourned for one hour while the documents are examined properly. Anyone leaving the building before reconvenement will be noted as doing so."

The hour that followed passed in a noise like a hive opened. Men who had nodded to Voss for nineteen years suddenly developed urgent business elsewhere. Women who had curtsied to him at church discussed the weather with unusual animation near the door he'd need to pass through. Mrs. Adeyemi rose, smoothed her grey skirt, and walked out past the harbor master without looking at him, and I swear the air bent around her like water around a keel.

I stood where I was. My legs had begun to remember they were mine. Five years of silence lay around my feet like cut rope, and I found I was trembling—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the sheer strangeness of having spoken and not been erased. I had named myself in a room full of people, and the room had not closed over the name. It had carried it.

Nell reached me through the crowd. She didn't embrace me—Thornburys, or Hallorans rather, don't emote in public, it's in the blood along with the stubbornness—but she gripped my forearm once, hard, the way Mrs. Adeyemi had gripped my wrist at dawn.

"You did it," she said. Her voice was thick. "You great useless brave idiot, you actually did it."

"Not all of it." I nodded toward the door, toward the jetty, toward the boat that had gone out before dawn carrying Callum's carefully guarded cargo ashore under constables' eyes. "There's a piece still coming up the hill. The piece that started everything."

"What piece?"

"The one my father died for," I said. "The reason Voss is standing at that table doing sums he can't make balance."

Through the window, past the fig trees, I could see the road down to the water, and on it, small in the heat shimmer, two constables walking escort beside a figure carrying a canvas sack that hung from his shoulder with the unmistakable heavy sway of a bag full of stones.

Or of pearls.

They were coming.
