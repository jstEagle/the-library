## 33. The Pearl Produced

I've carried a good many things across a good many decks in my life—crates of copra, cases of gin, one memorable consignment of live tortoises that I do not care to discuss—but never before have I walked into a courtroom carrying the ruin of a man in a canvas sack. It weighs less than you'd think. Ruin usually does.

The sack sat heavy on my palm all the same, and not from the pearls. From what they'd cost.

The magistrate's court in Broome was a long timber room that smelled of eucalyptus oil and old sweat and the particular staleness of officialdom everywhere in the world—the smell of men deciding things about other men without ever having to lift anything heavier than a pen. The ceiling fans turned with the lazy indifference of creatures that had seen every sort of justice this town produced and believed none of it. Windows stood open to the harbour, and through them came the sound of the Sarawak Star's departure preparations: hammers, bosun's whistles, the groan of cargo being swung aboard. Voss had moved the inspection forward two days, which meant the whole town was compressed into this room like cargo lashed down for weather. Shell-sorters off shift. Divers with salt still crusted in their hair. Mrs. Adeyemi at the back with her hands folded, wearing her Sunday face, which I had learned by then was her most dangerous one.

And at the front, behind his polished desk, Gideon Voss in his best grey suit, looking like a man who had dressed carefully for a funeral he assumed would be somebody else's.

The magistrate was a Mr. Callaghan, dry as a biscuit left out in the wet season, with spectacles that caught the light so you could never quite see where he was looking. He'd already spent an hour listening to Voss lay out his evidence against me—forgery, fraud, coercion of an indentured minor, the whole elegant architecture of it—and I will grant the man this much: Voss built beautiful lies. Every document dovetailed. Every testimony slotted. He'd even produced Brack, standing at parade rest, swearing with a straight face that he'd watched me bribe a parish clerk and doctor shipping papers.

It was going well for him. That was the trouble with watching a thing go well for Gideon Voss—you started counting how long it could last, the way you count the seconds between lightning and thunder.

My turn came when the magistrate looked over his spectacles and said, "Mr. Reeve. You've been named in several accusations this morning. Have you anything to say for yourself?"

"I've something to show you, sir," I said. "Which is worth more."

A ripple went around the room. Voss didn't move, but I saw his hands go flat on the desk, the way a man steadies himself on a rail when the swell changes without warning.

"Show," said Callaghan, in the tone of a man who has been shown many things, mostly unpleasant.

I set the canvas sack on the magistrate's bench and opened it.

Now, I've seen pearls my whole working life. I've strung them, weighed them, smuggled exactly two of them, and lost one down my own boot in Singapore harbor under circumstances involving a customs officer and a woman named Rosa that are nobody's business but mine. So believe me when I tell you that the room changed when those pearls came out onto the green baize. There were eleven of them, large as marbles and larger, that deep silver-pink lustre the old divers used to call "mother-of-dawn," and they caught the lamplight and threw it back warm, and every soul in that room who had ever gone down after such a thing made a small involuntary sound—a breath drawn through teeth, a half-swallowed word. You can't help it. A pearl that size does something to the animal in you.

"These were recovered yesterday," I said, "from the ballast of the Lorelei, wrecked off Cable Beach six years ago. The same vessel aboard which Thomas Halloran, pearl diver, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, drowned on the ninth of March, 1889. The company's official manifest for that voyage records eleven shells recovered and no pearls of significant value." I laid the manifest beside them, the paper soft and foxed from its years underwater. "This is the Lorelei's true manifest. Recovered with the pearls. In the captain's own hand. And it tells a different story."

Callaghan put on his other spectacles—he had two pairs, I noticed, the way some men have two faces—and read. The room got very quiet. Even the fans seemed to slow.

"The manifest records," I continued, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a silence unfilled, "a find of considerable size taken on Halloran's final dive. Nineteen pearls entered, valued by the captain at four hundred pounds. Signed, dated, witnessed by the mate. Which raises the question, sir, that I'm sure everyone here is asking themselves: if the company's books say no such find existed, where did four hundred pounds of pearls go? And why did the only man who knew where they lay end up diving in a storm?"

"Mr. Reeve." Voss's voice was silk stretched over a blade. He rose, smooth as poured cream. "The magistrate will see at once what this is. A salvage diver of no fixed address produces documents of unverifiable provenance and a sack of pearls conveniently discovered *yesterday*—after, I might add, weeks of association with parties who have grievances against this company. Any forger can age paper with seawater. Anyone can buy pearls in Singapore. This is theatre."

"Perhaps," said Callaghan mildly, and turned a page. "Though I confess I'm puzzled by something else, Mr. Voss. Your own ledgers."

Voss paused. Just a fraction. But I've spent forty years reading water, and a fraction is all I need.

"My ledgers, sir?"

"Your quarterly accounts for 1889." Callaghan lifted a ledger—Mrs. Adeyemi's midnight copying had done its work; the original had been pulled from the registry that morning by a clerk who owed her more favours than she liked to count. "Entry of the fourth of April, 1889. Here you record insurance recovery for the loss of the Lorelei, assessed against declared cargo value. And the declared value—" he tapped the line with one dry finger— "is eleven pounds, four shillings. Shells only. Yet your salvage costs for the same quarter, itemized here, include payment to a dive crew for *recovery operations at the Lorelei site* totalling sixty pounds. Now. Why would a careful businessman spend sixty pounds recovering cargo he'd insured at eleven?" He looked up over the spectacles. "Unless there was cargo the insurance valuation never mentioned."

The silence had texture now. You could have cut panels from it and hull a boat.

"That," said Voss, "would be a clerical error."

"Would it," said Callaghan. He turned another page, unhurried, a man savouring a meal. "Because there's a second one. Your private ledger—this one, marked *M.T.C. Confidential, G.V.*—records, under the heading 'Special Acquisitions,' a deposit entry dated the week after Halloran's death. No description. No source. Valued at three hundred and eighty-five pounds. Which is, curiously, almost precisely the difference between your captain's manifest figure and the sum your books admit to." He removed his spectacles entirely now, which from a magistrate is roughly equivalent to a judge drawing a pistol. "Three hundred and eighty-five pounds, Mr. Voss. Eleven pearls. Shall we count them together?"

Every head in the room counted them. I did too, though I'd done it twice on the walk over, twice the night before, and once more at dawn just to be sure the sea hadn't cheated me.

"Eleven," said someone in the back—Nell's voice, sharp as broken glass, and proud as sin.

Voss stood very still. And I'll say this for him: he was magnificent for perhaps ten seconds. The grey suit, the level chin, the faint smile of a man humouring a bureaucratic misunderstanding. I watched him run the arithmetic of his position—the witnesses, the papers, Brack at his shoulder, twenty years of bought loyalty in that room—and I watched the arithmetic fail. Because the room had stopped being his. You could feel it change hands, the way a tide turns: nothing visible moves, but everything has reversed.

"Those figures," he said at last, and his voice had lost a degree of warmth, "were entered by a subordinate. I shall of course cooperate fully with any audit—"

"There will be one," said Callaghan. "A full one. Beginning with the question of how a drowned man's find came to be in your confidential ledger while his widow was paid a death gratuity of two pounds and told the sea took everything." He glanced down at a paper before him—Oscar's statement, sworn that morning, though Oscar himself stood at the back of the room quiet as a fence post, watching Voss with an expression I found harder to look at than anger. It was patience. Five years of it, finally cashed. "There is also the matter of the dive logs. Your office certified the Lorelei's final dive as conducted in safe conditions. The harbour master's own weather station recorded a rising gale from mid-morning that day. The dive was ordered anyway. Who gave that order, Mr. Voss?"

"You cannot seriously be asking me to account for a decision made by my predecessor's administration—"

"It was your signature, sir. On the log. I'm looking at it now."

Another silence, longer than the first. Somewhere outside, the Sarawak Star's whistle blew—one long note hanging over the water, indifferent as God.

Voss's eyes went around the room. I followed them and understood what he was doing: taking attendance of his allies. Brack, staring at the floor with the face of a man who had spent the night remembering something. The two councilmen who'd dined at his table a hundred times, suddenly fascinated by their boots. Mrs. Adeyemi, meeting his eye with a calm that must have frightened him more than any accusation, because she was the one person in the room who knew exactly how much of his machinery she'd kept oiled, and exactly how easily she'd stopped.

He found no friends anywhere. I know the feeling from both sides.

"I want it understood," he said, and now the silk had frayed and something colder showed through, "that whatever irregularities exist, they occurred within the ordinary course of colonial trade, and that I acted throughout in what I believed to be the company's interest—"

"Sit down, Mr. Voss," said Callaghan, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "You'll have ample opportunity to explain yourself to the audit commission. What concerns this bench today is narrower." He drew a fresh sheet toward himself. "First: the disposition of these pearls, which appear to belong first to the estate of Thomas Halloran, deceased, and secondly to any surviving heirs. Second: the status of one Oscar Halloran, whose indenture papers I have reviewed this morning and which contain irregularities I intend to examine at length." His gaze flicked to the back of the room, to the boy, and something passed between them that I wasn't party to but recognized all the same—I'd seen it between officers before a battle they expected to survive. "Third: the matter of one Mara Halloran, reported drowned in 1889, whose name appears in this manifest's witness column."

That last one landed harder than all the pearls. I heard Nell make a sound like she'd been struck.

"Her signature is genuine," I said quietly, into the hush. "I sailed with her four years. I'd know her hand blindfolded. She signed the Lorelei's manifest as company bookkeeper the week before she was reported lost. And the report of her drowning, magistrate—" I laid the last paper down, the one I'd saved, because a man should always keep one card in his boot— "was filed by Gideon Voss, harbour master, on the authority of a passenger list from a ship that, per the shipping register, never carried a passenger of that name. I checked. In Singapore, in Fremantle, and in the Lloyd's registry. She never boarded. Somebody wanted the world to believe Mara Halloran dead, and somebody had the power to file that belief in triplicate."

Voss was on his feet. Not rising—*up*, all at once, chair scraping back, composure gone the way a mooring goes in a cyclone, all at once and catastrophically. "That woman stole from this company! She—"

"*Mr. Voss.*"

"—took documents that were company property, fled with knowledge of assets she had no right—she was *my* bookkeeper, she—"

Two constables had come through the door, and Callaghan's voice dropped into the register men reserve for dogs and floods. "You will sit down or you will be made to. Choose."

He sat. And in the sitting, something left him—the polish, the menace, the whole lacquered surface—and what remained was smaller and older and terribly ordinary: a greedy man watching his numbers come due. I have seen brave drownings and cowardly ones and I have caused a few of each, and I knew which kind this was shaping into before he even spoke again.

"The pearls," he said, and his voice had gone thin and reasonable, a man negotiating with the sea itself. "Magistrate. If the estate is owed compensation, the company will settle generously. There's no need for—spectacle. Names in newspapers. The girl's passage, the boy's release—these are administrative matters. I can authorize them today. All of it. Everyone walks away whole."

It was a good offer. I'll give him that. It was even, in its rotten heart, sincere—he genuinely believed everything had a price, including us, and the fact that he'd mispriced it wasn't malice but arithmetic. He'd spent thirty years learning that every man in Broome had a number, and he'd simply never met anyone whose number was a person.

Oscar moved. Just a step forward from the back wall, and the crowd parted around him the way water parts around a piling. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that the room had to lean in to hear it, which was, I suspected, exactly how he'd learned to use it.

"You asked my father to dive in a storm," he said. "You wrote in the log that the weather was fair. My mother signed that manifest because you held her children's papers. And when she ran, you drowned her on paper so no one would ever look for her." He set his hand flat on the bench, next to the pearls, and I saw his fingers weren't steady, and I saw that he didn't care. "You're offering money for the life of a man whose grave is the sea, and passage for a girl you tried to make an orphan twice. Keep your arithmetic, Mr. Voss. Some of us count different."

The room made a sound—not a cheer, nothing so tidy. More the sound a mob makes when it decides, collectively and all at once, which side of a rope it stands on.

Callaghan let it run its course, then rapped once. "The audit commission convenes tomorrow, eight o'clock. Until then, Mr. Voss, you are remanded to the custody of the constabulary—there is a cell behind this building and I suggest you become acquainted with it. Mr. Reeve, the pearls and all documents pass into the court's keeping pending settlement of the Halloran estate." He paused, and the dry old face did something that on another man I'd have called a smile. "And I am given to understand the Sarawak Star sails tomorrow on the evening tide. There is, I believe, a matter of a berth to be settled. See that it's settled properly."

We were filing out into the white heat of the morning when the constables walked Voss past us, wrists ironed, collar still perfect, still fighting to the last inch for the appearance of a man inconvenienced rather than undone. He stopped level with Oscar. For a moment I thought he'd say something venomous, something to carry away and gnaw.

What he said was: "She chose the sea over you. Ask yourself why."

And Oscar—who had spent five years swallowing worse than that—looked at him with something I can only call pity, and answered, "I did ask. I got the answer in her own hand. You should try reading someday, Mr. Voss. It's remarkable what people write down."

They took him out through the side door. The crowd spilled after, and the noise of it went up Chinaman Street like a fire in dry grass—by sundown there wouldn't be a soul in Broome who hadn't heard eleven pearls counted aloud in a courtroom.

I stayed on the veranda a moment, watching the harbour. The Sarawak Star rode at her moorings, black and patient, one berth aboard still empty, sailing on the evening tide.

One berth. Two children of Mara Halloran. And barely hours to decide who would carry her letter across the water.

I found Oscar at the rail, staring at the ship, and beside him Nell, and I thought—watching the two of them stand shoulder to shoulder against the bright burning sea—that whatever they decided, Mara Halloran's truest manifest had finally been read aloud: two names, entered under no company's books, belonging to no one but each other.

But the tide, as any sailor will tell you, waits for no man's feelings. And the gangway closed at six.
