## 35. Papers, Properly Signed

The courtroom smelled of dust and old varnish, and after everything—the shouting, the pearl laid out on the magistrate's table like a small moon, the sound of Gideon Voss's voice cracking for the first time in living memory—it was the smell I noticed most. Dust. The same dust that settled on the counting house ledgers, the same dust I'd breathed for five years while men wrote my name under *assets* and called it order.

Magistrate Fenwick had been at it since nine o'clock. It was past noon now, and he showed no sign of hurrying, which I understood to be a mercy. A man who hurried through a reckoning wanted it done; a man who went slowly wanted it *right*.

"Read it again," Fenwick said to the clerk, not unkindly.

The clerk read it again: the Lorelei's manifest as filed, against Mrs. Adeyemi's copied pages from Voss's own office—cargo entered, then cargo struck through, then cargo re-entered at half weight. Three lines of ink telling one story: that on the night my father drowned, the Meridian Trading Company had known exactly what came up out of that water, and had written down something smaller, and let two divers' names be read into the record as misadventure while the difference sat in a harbor master's cellar.

Fenwick looked at me across the room. He was a dry man, grey as driftwood, with spectacles that kept sliding down a nose built for disapproval.

"Halloran," he said. "You understand what you've done this morning."

I did, mostly. I'd stood up in front of forty strangers and stopped being silent. My hands were still doing something strange about it, wanting to shake and refusing at the same time, so I kept them flat on my knees like a boy at scripture lessons.

"I spoke, sir."

"You spoke." He removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back. "Do you know how many men sit where you sat and never speak? How many years a thing can go wrong in this district because everyone who knows is also afraid? I'll tell you: all of them. Every year. Until someone's afraid of something else more." He glanced toward the doors, where Callum Reeve leaned against the frame with his arms crossed and the look of a man who'd slept in his boots and didn't regret it. "You were afraid of losing your sister to the sea, I think. Or losing her to this place. Either way—you chose the better fear."

I didn't say anything. Five years of practice. But Fenwick seemed to take my silence as agreement, which was fair enough, because it was.

"Now the business." He opened a folder with the crispness of a man who enjoyed folders. "Oscar Halloran. Indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, term of seven years, commenced upon your father's death per the relief clause. Four years, eleven months served." He looked up. "Your father's drowning was ruled misadventure. This morning I have ruled otherwise. Which means the clause that bound you—" he tapped the page, "—was signed on a false verdict. False premises void contracts. That's not sentiment, Mr. Halloran. That's law."

Somewhere behind me a woman made a small sound. I didn't turn around. I knew Nell would be standing at the back with her chin up and her fists clenched, daring anyone to say otherwise, and if I turned around I'd do something undignified in front of the magistrate.

"The indenture is voided," Fenwick said. "Effective immediately. The company will bear the cost of its own falsification, as is proper. You are free, Mr. Halloran, as of the signing of these papers."

He slid them across the table. Two pages. A quill. An inkwell with a chip out of its rim that I recognized—I'd fetched that inkwell a hundred times, carried it to clerks who signed things that decided my life while barely looking at me.

And there it was, the whole joke of it, laid out plain: they needed me to sign my own freedom. Nobody had ever asked me to sign anything before. Things had been signed *for* me—my father's death, my mother's departure, my indenture, all of it inked by other hands while I stood where children stand, off to the side, being managed. Now the magistrate pushed the papers at me and waited, and the waiting was mine.

My name at the top, printed by the clerk: OSCAR HALLORAN. Not "asset number such-and-such." Not "the Halloran boy." Just the name, spelled out whole, as though I'd been carrying it around complete all along and everyone else had only been reading fragments.

I picked up the quill.

It's an odd thing, signing. Your hand wants to hurry. Mine wanted to scrawl it fast, before somebody changed their mind, before Voss produced some document from hell reversing everything—and God knows Voss had documents. He'd spent twenty years making paper tell lies. Even arrested at the gangway of the Sarawak Star with the pearls sitting on a magistrate's table, part of me expected him to produce a certificate saying none of this had happened.

But he hadn't. When they'd taken him off the gangway—he hadn't struggled, that was the worst of it, he'd simply gone still, the way a man goes still when a calculation finally comes out wrong—he'd looked at me across the crowd. Not angry. *Auditing*. As if I were a line in his ledger that refused to balance, and he was trying to find the error before they walked him away. He hadn't found it. There wasn't one. The error was his: he'd believed that owning a man's labor meant owning the man, and those are different properties entirely, and only one of them survives a signature.

I signed. Both pages. The scratch of the nib was the loudest thing in the room.

"There," Fenwick said, almost gently. He blotted the ink himself, which I suspect he'd never done for any man in this district. "You're free."

*Free.* People say the word like it's a door swinging open onto a street. Nobody tells you that sometimes the door opens onto water.

Because here was the arithmetic underneath, the one I'd been carrying since the night I gave up the berth, and which no signature could touch: the Sarawak Star sailed tomorrow morning, and she carried one assisted berth for kin of Mara Halloran, and that berth belonged to Nell. Papers confirmed it that same hour—Fenwick saw to it before lunch, with a satisfaction he tried and failed to hide, stamping her passage twice just to hear the thump. Nell would wake tomorrow on the tide and be gone by evening, over the horizon to Singapore, to a mother alive and writing letters in a hand I knew better than my own.

And I would stand on the jetty and watch.

I'd made the choice with my eyes open and I'd make it again, and I told myself that firmly, the way you talk yourself through cold water. But freedom, when it finally arrived, arrived looking strangely like what I'd already been living: nowhere paid to go, no bed assigned, no bell ringing me to work or releasing me from it. The company had fed me and housed me and owned me, and now nothing did any of those things, and both halves of that sentence felt true at once.

"Mr. Halloran." Fenwick again. I'd drifted; he noticed, because magistrates notice drifting. "There's the matter of wages owed. The company will settle accounts—four years, eleven months of credited pay, less board. It won't be much. It will be yours." He paused. "You'll want somewhere to sleep tonight that isn't their barracks."

"Yes, sir."

"And plans?"

Everyone was looking at me now. Callum by the door. Mrs. Adeyemi three rows back, her hands folded, her face giving away nothing except to me, and to me it gave the smallest tilt of the head, which from her was a speech. Somewhere near the back, Nell, burning.

Plans. I thought about the jetty. I thought about a mango seed in my pocket that I'd been carrying for reasons I couldn't yet explain even to myself. I thought about a counting house full of ledgers where my name had lived under *assets*, and how a man with steady handwriting and a memory for numbers could, given time, write his name somewhere better.

"I expect I'll stay in Broome," I said.

Fenwick raised his eyebrows, just slightly—the first expression I'd seen on him that wasn't procedural. "Most men in your position would run for the next ship."

"I've had enough of ships, sir."

A sound from the back that might have been Nell laughing or crying or both. I kept my eyes forward. Magistrates respect composure, and besides, if I looked at her I'd come apart, and I'd only just gotten the shaking under control.

Fenwick dismissed the room. People filed out past me—some nodded, some stared, one old pearler gripped my shoulder hard enough to hurt and said nothing, which was worth more than speech. Then the room emptied, and it was Nell and Callum and Mrs. Adeyemi and me, standing in the dust-smelling quiet where five minutes earlier the truth had torn a hole in the town.

Nell crossed the floor like weather coming in and stopped just short of me, arms folded, eyes wet and furious about being wet.

"Well," she said. "Free."

"Apparently."

"You don't look free. You look like a man who lost an argument with a door."

"That's roughly the shape of it."

Her jaw worked. She was going to say something enormous—I could see it loading—but instead she reached out and straightened my collar, brisk and rough, the way you fix a thing you can't fix. Then she stepped back before either of us could make it worse.

"Sail's at eight," she said. "Don't be late. If you're late I'll assume you've changed your mind and I'll throw myself overboard, and then you'll have wasted a perfectly good berth."

"Nell—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Not today. Today we're going to walk down to the sheds and I'm going to tell Aunt Vera's creditors exactly what I think of them, and you're going to carry my box, because I'm free too now, or nearly, and free women don't carry their own boxes." Her voice cracked on the last word and she disguised it as clearing her throat, badly. "Eight o'clock, Osc. Don't be late."

She left. Callum pushed off from the doorframe and ambled over, and for a while neither of us said anything, just two men looking at the magistrate's empty table where a pearl the size of a dove's egg had sat that morning and ruined a harbor master's life.

"She'll write," Callum said. "From Singapore. First mail boat, guaranteed. And I'll dive the wreck again if I have to swim the whole way back to bring it to you."

"I know."

"She's a fine girl, your sister."

"She's finer than me."

"Aye," Callum said comfortably. "Most people are. It's why I like them." He clapped my arm. "Go on. You've got a box to carry and a night to get through, and I've got a promise to keep to a woman who once saved my life and another one to keep to her son. I'm a busy man."

Then it was Mrs. Adeyemi, last, standing with her reticule clasped in both hands. She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever passed between us didn't need words, but she gave them anyway, quietly, in her precise clerk's voice.

"The registry will need rewriting," she said. "All of it. Names under assets, struck through. Correct entries made. It's a month of work, maybe six weeks." A pause, dry as her ledgers. "I'll need someone with a careful hand."

I looked at her. She looked at the window, at the light, anywhere but at me, which was her way of offering something large while pretending it was small.

"I have a careful hand," I said.

"So I've observed." She adjusted her gloves. "Come Monday, then. If you're minded. There's no berth attached. No bed. No obligation. Only work, and wages, and a desk by the window." She turned to go, then added, without turning back: "The company took your father's verdict and your mother's name and your sister's childhood, Mr. Halloran. It cannot be forgiven. But it can be *corrected*, entry by entry, and correction is its own kind of justice. I find I prefer it to grief."

She left. The courtroom emptied around me until it was just me and the dust motes turning in the light, and the two signed pages lying on the magistrate's table, and the inkwell with the chip in its rim.

Free. Nowhere paid to go. A job by a window. A sail at eight. A mango seed in my pocket that I'd been carrying around for three days like a fool, and which I now, standing alone in an empty courtroom, finally admitted was a plan.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing ordinary things, which after that morning felt like a kind of feast. Nell squared accounts with Aunt Vera's creditors—a terrifying performance involving arithmetic delivered at volume, and two of the debts simply evaporated out of embarrassment—and I carried her box, as instructed, and bought bread and cheese with money the company owed me, actual coins pressed into my palm by a trembling junior clerk who apologized four times.

Toward evening we walked out along the beach past the shell heaps, and the light did that thing it does here, going gold and then copper and then a color with no name, and Nell skipped stones and talked about Singapore as though she'd already memorized it from books she hadn't read. What their mother looked like. Whether she'd recognize them. Whether it was hot.

"She'll know you," I said. "The letter proves it. 'My Osc.' She wrote that from the other side of the world and eleven years of silence. She'll know you faster."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"She'll want to know you too. And you'll be here, sorting other people's numbers." She threw a stone flat and low; it jumped four times. "You could still come. There are ways. Callum knows ships."

"There's one berth, Nell."

"There's a whole ocean."

"Not for people like us there isn't. Not yet." I picked up my own stone, weighed it. "Besides. Someone has to stay and correct the ledger."

She looked at me sideways. "That's a lonely sentence, Oscar."

"It's a useful one."

"It's both," she said, and threw another stone, and we let it go at that, because some arguments you win by not having them yet.

At dusk we climbed to the ridge above the town, where you can see the whole anchorage, and there she was: the Sarawak Star, riding at her moorings with her lights beginning to show, black hull, white superstructure, breathing smoke like something alive. Tomorrow she'd take my sister to our mother. Tonight she just sat there, patient as a promise.

Nell stood beside me with her arms wrapped around herself against the sea breeze, and for a long time neither of us spoke. Below us Broome lit itself window by window, and somewhere among those lights was a counting house where, on Monday, I'd sit down at a desk by a window and begin striking lines through my own name.

"I'm scared," Nell said suddenly. Flatly, the way she said everything true.

"I know."

"You're not?"

"Terrified," I said. "But it's the good kind. The kind where you've decided."

She leaned her head briefly against my shoulder—two seconds, maybe three, the longest physical affection our family had managed in eleven years—and then straightened, embarrassed, restored.

"Eight o'clock," she said.

"Eight o'clock."

And we walked back down into the town to spend our last shared hours arguing about whether she should pack the blue dress or the practical one, which is to say we spent them exactly as family.
