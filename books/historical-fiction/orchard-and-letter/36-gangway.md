## 36. Gangway

The Sarawak Star sat at her moorings like a fat white bird that had never once considered flying, and I hated her on sight.

Not because she was ugly. She was beautiful, in the way of things built to leave. Three decks of fresh paint, brass rails polished by men whose whole job was polishing brass, steam breathing from her funnel in slow grey sighs — she was the most beautiful thing Broome had ever produced, which is not a high bar, and still she outstripped it. No. I hated her because she was leaving, and I was on her, and Oscar was not.

"You could come with me," I said for the fourth time since the jetty. "You could hide me in your luggage and come too."

"I'm not luggage."

"Nobody would check. Nobody checks anything in this town except pearls and other people's business."

"Nell." He said my name the way you set down something heavy. "We've been through this."

"We've been *through* it. That's different from being finished with it." I looked out at the water, which was doing its morning trick of looking gentle while hiding everything it had ever swallowed. My father had gone into that water. Half my blood had gone into that water, and come back as a verdict: misadventure. "There's room in a lifeboat locker. Callum says so. Callum has hidden in worse."

"Callum," said Oscar, "has also been shot at twice this month. He's not a role model, he's a cautionary tale with a boat."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and then I was angry at myself for laughing, because laughing meant the goodbye was winning. That's how it works, isn't it. The thing you're dreading gets closer, and your body starts betraying you into small happinesses, little pockets of ordinary, so that when it arrives it doesn't seem real until it's already behind you.

The gangway was down. A line of passengers was forming at its foot — pearlers' wives with hatboxes, a Malay family with a cage of pigeons they were taking to Singapore for reasons known only to them and the pigeons, two company clerks with leather cases and the smug faces of men carrying documents nobody had asked to see. Mrs. Adeyemi stood near the gangway itself, in her best grey dress, holding a paper manifest against her chest like a shield. She'd insisted on seeing me aboard. She'd also spent the last ten minutes finding reasons to be busy near Oscar, and I knew why. She was keeping him in her eyeline the way you keep a hand on a door in wind.

The magistrate had signed everything. That was the miracle none of us had quite learned to live inside yet — that ink could do what five years of silence couldn't. Voss arrested at his own gangway three days ago, mid-flight, his composure coming off him like paint in rain. The indenture register amended. One berth on the Sarawak Star, second class, confirmed for *Eleanor Halloran*, verified kin of Mara Halloran, resident of Singapore, who was alive, who was waiting, who had written her name on the last page of a letter I had read eleven times.

And Oscar Halloran, freed by the same pen that bound me onward, standing on the jetty with nothing but his own name and nowhere paid to go.

"It's not fair," I said. It came out younger than I meant it to. At nineteen you spend so much effort sounding older that the moments you fail are loud.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

"Then why are you letting it happen?"

"Because it isn't happening *to* anyone. That's the difference between unfair and unbearable. This one's just unfair." He turned the hat in his hands — our father's old diving hat, the one he'd kept hidden in a crate under the shell sheds for years, the one thing Brack's searchers never found. He'd brought it to give me, and now he seemed to be reconsidering, turning it over like a man checking a coin for luck. "You'll write."

"I'll write."

"And if Singapore's not — if Mother's not what the letters promised—"

"She's exactly what the letters promised. She wrote 'my Osc' on a page and kept it alive for years without knowing where to send it. That's not a woman who turns out to be a disappointment. That's a woman who's been disappointing herself on our behalf." I heard my own voice crack on the last word and pretended I hadn't. The aunt taught me that much, at least: you can feel anything as long as nobody catches you feeling it.

Oscar put the hat in my hands.

It was heavier than I expected. Copper and brass and leather, scarred at the rim, the front glass fogged forever with the memory of every dive it had ever survived. Our father's face had lived behind this glass. Our father's breath had moved through this air, filtered down from a world he surfaced into only briefly, between the long silences. I held it and understood that I was holding the only object in the world that had been present for both halves of my family — the drowned half and the fled half — and had told no tales about either.

"He'd have wanted you to have it," Oscar said.

"He'd have wanted *you* to have it. You're the one who stayed near the water."

"I stayed near the ledgers. There's a difference." His mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a small shift at one corner, a private tide. "Take it anyway. And take this."

He took the letter from inside his coat. Not the first letter, or the third, or any of the ones Callum had carried across two oceans in an oilcloth packet. The last one. The one written after Mother learned we were alive, the one with all four names in it — hers, mine, Oscar's, even the aunt's, spoken of with a weariness that made me love a woman I'd never met. The one that ended with *tell my son I counted the years wrong but I never once stopped counting.*

"You're giving me this?" I said. "This is yours. He wrote to you. 'My Osc.' You're the Osc."

"That's why it should go with you." He pressed it flat between us, both our hands on it, the paper warm from riding over his heart the whole walk down. "Because I'm the Osc, and I'm asking my sister to carry my name across the water and put it in our mother's hand. Let her touch it. Let her know it survived."

I want you to understand what that cost him. I watched it cost him. Five years of being property teaches a person to hold their face very still, and Oscar held his still, but I'd grown up learning to read people through their defenses — the aunt drank, the shed bosses lied, the buyers lowballed, and survival meant seeing the truth underneath the performance. Underneath Oscar's stillness was a man handing over the only proof he had that anyone had ever claimed him.

"You could still stow away," I said. Fifth time. Last time.

"No."

"There's a space behind the coal bunkers. Callum drew me a map. On actual paper, Oscar. A man who draws maps for stowaways is practically offering a service."

"A man who draws maps for stowaways is trying to get himself hanged for a second offense in one fortnight." He shook his head, and this time the almost-smile arrived fully, tired and true. "Nell. Listen to me. If I go in that bunker, one of two things happens. Either they find me at Thursday Island and put me off, and you arrive alone with a stolen brother and a scandal, or they don't find me, and Mother opens her door to a fugitive with no papers, no trade, no right to work in the Straits, and spends her reunion hiding me in her own house from the same company that owns half that coast. That's not a homecoming. That's just moving my cage somewhere with better weather."

"So we find another way. There's always another way. There's a ship out of Darwin, there's work on the coastal runs, there's—"

"Nell." Both hands on my shoulders now. His palms were rough from rope and shell and ledger-binding; I felt each callus like a sentence. "What did I do? In the courtroom. What did I actually do?"

I didn't want to answer. I answered anyway, because it was Oscar. "You spoke."

"After five years of not speaking. After five years of learning that silence keeps you safe. Do you know why I finally broke it?" His voice dropped, not for drama — Oscar didn't do drama; Oscar did truth at low volume, which is worse — but so that only I would hear it over the gulls and the winches and the pigeons complaining in their cage. "Because I saw you in that crowd. Third row, arms crossed, glaring at the magistrate like you could bill him for the trouble. And I thought: she's watching. If I stay quiet now, she learns that staying quiet is what Hallorans do when it matters. I'd rather lose everything than teach you that."

"Well, congratulations," I managed. "Lesson received. Loudly. In front of the entire town. Aunt Vera will talk about it until she dies, and Aunt Vera talks about things until after she dies."

"Then you already know the other lesson."

I did. I hated that I did. The other lesson was that some things you can't carry for someone else, only alongside them, and sometimes not even alongside — sometimes the current takes one of you and the honest, ugly, grown-up thing is to let the water do its arithmetic. I'd spent my whole life refusing that arithmetic. Refusing it was how I'd survived Vera's house, the shell sheds, every man who'd told me what I was worth by the pound. But there's a difference between refusing an arithmetic that's a lie and accepting one that's merely true, and I was nineteen years old and learning the difference on a jetty in front of a ship I hated.

"One year," I said. "Give me one year. I'll get work, real work, Mother has contacts — Callum says she does mending for half the wives of the Straits merchants, and those women buy pearls, Oscar, and I can *sort*, I can sort better than anyone in this town and everyone knows it — and in one year there'll be money enough for a berth. Your berth. Proper papers, proper fare, none of this bunkered stowaway nonsense. One year."

"One year," he agreed.

"Swear it. Swear it on something that matters."

He considered. Then he took my hand, the one holding the diving helmet, and pressed it between both of his, over the cold copper dome.

"On this," he said. "On Dad's hat and everything he dove for and everything the company stole from him. One year. If I'm not on a ship to Singapore within the year, you have my permission to come back here and drag me aboard yourself."

"With the pigeons?"

"With the pigeons."

That was the bargain. That was the real one, under all the false ones we'd been trading all morning — the jokes about lockers and coal bunkers, the negotiations neither of us believed. Underneath: one year, sworn on a dead man's hat, witnessed by gulls and Mrs. Adeyemi pretending to count passengers.

The bell above the gangway rang. First call. The line began to move.

* * *

I boarded the way you board a thing you've decided to resent thoroughly and forever, which is to say politely. A steward took my single trunk. A purser checked my paper — *Halloran, E., second class, kin passage confirmed* — and stamped it with the officious little thunk that I decided, on the spot, was my favorite sound in the world. Somewhere below us, engines woke in stages, like an animal deciding whether today was worth the effort.

Mrs. Adeyemi embraced me at the top of the gangway. She smelled of ink and some flower I couldn't name, and she held on longer than a clerk embraces a girl she's known for a month, because we weren't a clerk and a girl, we were two survivors of Gideon Voss's counting house, and survivors recognize each other.

"The copying pages," she said into my ear, "are under the false bottom of your trunk. Give them to your mother. Tell her Adaeze remembers 1879, and tell her the debt ran both directions."

"I don't know what that means."

"You will. She will." She pulled back, gripped my shoulders, and looked at me with eyes that had audited empires. "You come from stubborn stock on both sides, Eleanor Halloran. Spend it well."

Callum hugged me like weather — sudden, overwhelming, gone. "Your mother owes me a bottle of arrack from 1886," he said, "and if she claims she doesn't remember, she's lying, and if she remembers, tell her interest compounds." Then he stepped back, wiped his eye with the heel of his hand and blamed the spray, though there was no spray, and went to stand beside Oscar at the rail.

And then there was nothing left between me and the deck but my own feet, so I used them, and stood at the rail with the diving helmet between my boots and the letter inside my coat, over my heart, in the exact place it had ridden over Oscar's.

The lines came off. The tug took her nose. Broome began, very slowly, to move away from me — the shell sheds first, then the church spire where I'd found the parish record with my mother's name spelled *Marah*, the clerk's lazy hand unable to manage an extra syllable, then the long low shape of the town itself, the jetty, the figures on it shrinking to gestures, gestures to dots.

Oscar didn't wave. He stood with his hands flat on the rail, watching, and I understood that waving would have broken something he was holding together, so I didn't wave either. We just looked at each other across the widening water, two people conducting a farewell entirely in stillness, which, if you think about it, was the family language all along. We'd never needed words, we Halloran children. We'd needed each other to look at.

The harbor opened. The town folded itself into haze.

I waited until the point hid the jetty entirely, and then I let myself cry, briefly, efficiently, the way Vera taught me without ever meaning to — one hand on the rail, one on the helmet, salt going into the sea where it belonged. Then I dried my face, because there was work ahead. Letters to write. A mother to find at the end of a street in Singapore, holding a piece of paper with my brother's name on it, telling her *he's alive, he's free, he swore on the hat, one year.*

Below me, the engines settled into their long heartbeat. Above me, the pigeons complained. Ahead of us, six days of open water, and at the end of it, a woman I'd spent my whole life mourning and had only just started learning to be angry at, and after that, loving, which was harder.

I took out the letter and read it again, right there at the rail, letting the wind try to steal it and failing. *Tell my son I counted the years wrong but I never once stopped counting.*

"Hold on, Osc," I said to the horizon, where Broome wasn't anymore. "One year. Count on it."

And far astern, past the point, past the haze, a town went on without me — a counting house, a mango pit already in the ground behind it, and a man who had given away his freedom twice, once in ink and once in love, and called it belonging both times.

But that was his chapter. Mine ended at the rail, with salt drying on my face and my mother's handwriting warming against my heart, and the whole enormous ocean, for once, going the direction I wanted.
