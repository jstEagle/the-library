## 37. The Ship Goes Out

There is a particular cruelty in a tide. It goes out whether you want it to or not, and it takes whatever it likes with it, and it never once looks back to see how you're taking the news. I have lived beside this water my whole life and I still haven't made peace with it. We simply tolerate each other, the sea and I, the way you tolerate a landlord who owns everything including your sleep.

The Sarawak Star sat at her moorings since dawn, taking on coal and passengers in roughly equal measure, both of them black and dusty and bound for somewhere better. By half past nine she was finished with Broome. By ten she was asking the harbour's permission to leave, which is what ships do when they sound their whistle — a long, low complaint that rolled across the mudflats and into the town and through the window of the counting house where I was standing with a pen in my hand and absolutely no intention of using it.

Mrs. Adeyemi looked up from her ledger.

"If you mean to go down to the jetty," she said, "go. If you mean to pretend to work while thinking about going down to the jetty, also go. You are worse than useless at pretending."

"I wasn't—"

"Oscar." She set down her pen with the small decisive click she used for ending arguments before they began. "I watched your father stand at that same window the morning of his last dive, doing sums he had already done twice. Men in this town say goodbye to water the way other men say goodbye to women — badly, and too late, and with paperwork they don't need." She turned back to her columns. "Go and do it properly."

So I went.

* * *

The jetty was crowded and nobody in it saw me, which is a trick I learned young and have lately been trying to unlearn. Five years of being useful and silent teaches you to stand at the edge of a thing — a shed, a crowd, a life — and watch it happen to other people. I stood at the rail near the coal sheds, out of the way of the porters and the well-wishers, and I looked for one face among three hundred.

I found her halfway along the gangway, and she found me at exactly the same moment, which shouldn't surprise me anymore and somehow always does.

Nell was wearing her good dress, the blue one, which our aunt had produced from a trunk smelling of camphor and bad decisions, and which Nell had altered herself overnight because nothing fits a girl until she makes it fit. Her hair was pinned up in a style she'd copied off a fashion plate and improved upon by ignoring most of its instructions. She looked, I thought, like someone arriving rather than departing, even walking the wrong direction down a gangway.

She didn't wave. That would have been too easy for her, too much like admitting she wanted anything from anybody. Instead she stopped on the boards, put one hand on the rail, and looked at me the way she'd looked at me across the shell-sorting table all those weeks ago: measuring, deciding, daring me to be less than she'd decided I was.

Then she did something worse than waving. She smiled. Small, crooked, entirely hers — the smile of a girl who had grown up unloved by circumstance and unbowed by it, who had fought me over the berth because accepting rescue felt like dying a little, and who had finally, at the last, let me put the paper in her hand because she understood what I'd only just worked out myself: that love isn't a debt you refuse out of pride. It's a cargo. Somebody has to carry it ashore.

Her mouth moved — *write to me* — and then the gangway was drawn up behind her with a rattle and a groan, and she was aboard, and there was nothing left in the world between us except forty feet of grey-green water and an ocean beyond it.

* * *

A ship leaving is a slow event dressed up as a fast one.

The Sarawak Star didn't so much depart as negotiate. Tugs fussed at her flanks like sheepdogs around a stubborn ewe. Her lines came off one by one, each splash of rope hitting the water sounding absurdly loud, as though the harbour itself were keeping count. Steam went up from her funnel in a fat white column that the southeast wind leaned on and bent, pointing her toward Singapore like a finger.

And I stood there and did the arithmetic I've done all my life, because numbers are where cowards like me hide. Sixty-one days to Singapore if the monsoon behaved. Sixty-one days during which my sister would eat food I couldn't taste and sleep under a sky I couldn't see and grow, daily, further from anyone who remembered what our mother's laugh sounded like. Except she wouldn't. That was the whole point. At the end of those sixty-one days she would walk down a gangway into the arms of a woman named Mara Halloran, who was not dead, who had never been dead, whose handwriting I could now draw from memory the way other men draw maps home.

Sixty-one days, and then the lie ended for somebody. Just not for me.

You'd think that would taste bitter. Some of it did, I won't lie about it — there was a moment, right then, watching the tugs swing her bow toward the channel, when every selfish bone in my body woke up at once and started shouting. *You could have been on her.* The berth had been mine first. The letter had come for me. I had spent five years being owned, and here had been the single door out of the whole prison, and I had handed it to a girl who called me a coward to my face and meant it fondly.

But bones shout loudest when they're wrong. Because I kept seeing the other arithmetic underneath: Nell sorting shells in the sheds at fourteen, at eighteen, at thirty, her sharpness going dull by degrees the way ours all did, her letters home growing shorter until they were just receipts of survival. I had watched what this town does to people who stay without choosing to. Staying by force isn't staying. It's sinking slowly, politely, on dry land.

Whereas letting go — actually choosing it, opening your hand on purpose — that's different. That's not sinking. That's planting. You put something of yourself in another person and you send it where the water's warmer, and you stay behind in the cold and call it gardening.

Callum Reeve appeared at my elbow at some point during all this philosophy. He had a talent for that, materializing the way weather does, announced by a change in pressure.

"She'll do," he said. It was the highest compliment his vocabulary contained, and coming from him it was practically a sonnet.

"She'll do more than do," I said. "She'll take Singapore apart and put it back together to suit herself."

"Aye." He lit his pipe, cupping the match against a wind that didn't need cupping against, but old habits. "Your mother'll not know what's hit her. Two of them under one roof — Mara Halloran raised one stubborn woman already; she'll recognize the manufacture."

We stood shoulder to shoulder, two men watching a ship carry away everything either of us had promised a dying stranger we'd protect. Callum had known her longer than I had — had known her as a girl on decks, before she was a mother, before she was a rumor, before she was a name in a letter that broke my life open like a nut. He carried his own guilt about her, worn smooth now like a stone he'd been turning in his pocket for twenty years.

"You gave up your berth for a sister you'd spoken to four times," he said presently. Smoke went sideways. "I've sailed with men who wouldn't give up a hammock."

"It wasn't charity."

"No?"

"It was bookkeeping." I nodded at the ship, now clearing the inner moorings, her hull showing more copper as she took the channel's deeper water. "Voss spent five years telling me what I was worth. Assets, per the ledger. One line item among others. And I believed him — that's the shameful part, Callum. I believed the entry. Then I stood in front of the magistrate and read out the true figures, and I thought: right. New ledger. What am I actually worth?" I shrugged. "Turns out a man is worth exactly what he can hand to somebody else. Everything else is just inventory."

Callum was quiet a while. Ships' quiet, the kind sailors keep around big feelings.

"Mara said something once," he said finally. "Off Thursday Island, years back, drunk on bad rum and homesickness. She said, 'Whatever happens to me, make sure my boy knows he was wanted. Not needed — wanted. There's a difference, and the company will spend its whole existence trying to convince him otherwise.'" He knocked out his pipe against the rail. "I'm late delivering the message. But delivered it's been, I reckon."

The lump in my throat agreed with him, so I said nothing and watched the ship instead, which is what Halloran men do with feelings too large for conversation.

* * *

By noon she was abeam of Ganbaume Point, and the crowd on the jetty had thinned to the ones with real grief or real hope, and by one o'clock she was a shape rather than a ship, and by two she was a smudge, and by three she was a suggestion of smoke on the edge of the world, and I was still there, because Mrs. Adeyemi had told me to do it properly and I am, when it matters, an obedient man.

The tide turned around four. I felt it before I saw it — a slackening, a held breath, the water deciding which master to serve next. All day the current had run out of the bay after the ship, as if the harbour itself were trying to hold her back, and now it reversed, came flooding in past my boots, warm and brown and indifferent, bringing nothing.

That's the thing nobody tells you about letting go. The world doesn't pause. The tide comes in on schedule. The shells still need sorting. Somewhere behind me Mrs. Adeyemi was entering the Sarawak Star's departure in the manifest with a date and a time and no comment whatsoever, because ledgers record everything and grieve nothing, and there is a horrible comfort in that, and a horrible loneliness too.

I stood there while the light went long and gold across the flats, and I let myself do the thing I hadn't allowed in front of anyone: I grieved. Not dramatically. Quietly, the way water gets into a boat — not through any hole you can point to, just through the ordinary seams of things. I grieved the berth. I grieved the sixty-one days. I grieved the version of me that would have walked down a gangway and become a son again instead of remaining a line item. I grieved my father, properly this time, not as a mystery to be solved but as a man who used to lift me onto his shoulders on this very jetty and tell me the names of the ships, getting half of them wrong on purpose because he liked correcting himself better than being right.

And underneath all of it, threaded through it like pearl-shell through mud, there was peace. Actual peace. Not the peace of resignation, which I knew intimately and would not wish on a dog. A different article entirely. The peace of a debt discharged. Of a promise kept. Of a silence finally broken at the right moment instead of the safe one.

They talk about freedom like it's a place. Singapore. Fremantle. Anywhere but here. But I had given my freedom away and discovered I wasn't lighter without it — I was heavier, in the good way, the way a tree is heavy, with roots doing their invisible work. Voss had owned me because ownership requires only paper. Nobody could own what I'd done last week. You can't indenture a choice.

* * *

It was full dark when Callum found me again, sitting on the bollard at the jetty's end with my boots off and my feet in the water like a child, which I was, in every way that counted.

He sat down beside me without asking, which is the privilege of men who have shared a courtroom and a conspiracy. For a long time neither of us spoke. The phosphorescence was out — little green sparks where the water stirred, tiny cold fires answering my toes.

"I sail Thursday," he said at last. "Coaster to Batavia, then across. I'll be in Singapore inside the month — ahead of her, if the winds are kind." He paused. "I'll send word. First post, I swear it. You'll know before anyone in this town whether she got there, and whether—" He stopped, restarted. "Whether the reunion was kind."

"Kind," I repeated. The word felt strange in my mouth. Twenty years of kind and unkind, weighed against a woman who chose a ship over her children because a monster told her the alternative was funerals. "You'll tell her about the berth. About Nell insisting on writing the address herself so I couldn't pretend I didn't know where they'd gone."

"I'll tell her everything." He meant it. Callum Reeve had crossed an ocean on a promise to a dying woman; a few sentences more were nothing. "And I'll tell her about the magistrate's court, and the look on Voss's face when the dive logs came out. She'll want every word of it. She paid for that reckoning in advance, Oscar. Twenty years of interest."

"And you'll tell her—" I stopped. Started over. The tide moved against my ankles, patient, ancient, uninterested in my difficulties. "Tell her I'm not angry. Or tell her I was, and I'm not now. Whichever is truer by the time you get there. I genuinely don't know yet."

"That's honest."

"Halloran family motto. Should be on a coat of arms. A shield with nothing on it, and below it, in Latin, *We Genuinely Don't Know Yet.*"

Callum laughed — a real one, from the chest, rare enough to be worth the journey. Then he stood, and stretched, and became practical again, because that was his way of being tender: logistics.

"There'll be a letter inside six weeks," he said. "Maybe seven. Addressed to the counting house, care of Mrs. Adeyemi, because I trust her postal discretion more than I trust yours — no offense."

"None taken. I'd trust her over me too."

"Seven weeks, then. Hold onto that number. When the waiting gets loud, you count days, lad. Days are finite. Grief will tell you it's infinite, but grief lies — it's the one thing grief and Voss had in common, and we've seen the back of one of them." He put his hand on my shoulder, brief and hard, a sailor's benediction. "Seven weeks."

I watched him walk back down the jetty, pipe smoke trailing him like a comet's tail, and I sat alone in the dark with my feet in the tide and did the only sensible thing left to a man in my position.

I started counting.

* * *

Seven weeks is four hundred ninety-odd days' worth of hours, if you're the sort of person who counts hours, which I discovered overnight that I was. It's a humiliating hobby. Mrs. Adeyemi caught me at it within three days, converting shipping schedules into arrival probabilities in the margins of an actual ledger, and confiscated my pencil with the weary air of a woman defusing the same bomb repeatedly.

"The mail from Batavia connects with the coastal steamer on the second Tuesday," she said, handing me a fresh pencil, "if the coaster isn't late, which it will be. Add nine days. Now stop torturing yourself and check these entries — Mr. Fennimore's arithmetic remains a crime scene."

So I worked. That was the strange mercy of it: the company, which had eaten five years of my life as a parasite, suddenly became a scaffold. There were manifests to audit and Voss's mess to unwind, contracts renegotiated, shell-buyers re-approached with clean books, and Adeyemi taught me all of it with the unsentimental thoroughness of someone building her own successor, though neither of us said so out loud. My hands stayed busy. My mind, denied its counting, occasionally escaped anyway — to a veranda in Singapore, maybe, where a woman with my jaw and Nell's temper would be arguing with a ship's chandler and winning — and then I'd pull it back and total a column.

Callum's coaster was due to clear port on the Thursday. On the Wednesday evening I walked out past the shell heaps to the point where you can see the whole roadstead, and I stood in the wind and looked at the empty anchorage where the Sarawak Star had been, and I found — this surprised me — that looking at the empty water didn't hurt the way I'd expected. It hurt, but it was a clean hurt. The hurt of a wound that's knitting.

Because here is what I knew, standing there, that I hadn't known at the gangway: the ship hadn't taken my family away. It had delivered them. Every mile the Sarawak Star made eastward was a mile closer between my mother and my sister, two halves of one lie finally sailing toward each other across the exact ocean that had been used to keep them apart. I couldn't be in that reunion. But I had built it. Out of a forged signature, a stolen logbook, a magistrate's patience, a clerk's courage, a sailor's oath, and a berth I'd signed over with a steady hand. Somewhere ahead of that ship, in a house I'd never seen, my mother was about to open her door and find her daughter standing on it, carrying my letter and her own twenty-year-old grief, and the door would open, and —

Well. I'd know in seven weeks.

Nine, realistically. Adeyemi had been right about the coaster.

I turned back toward the town lights, toward the counting house with its lamp burning late for me, toward the life I had chosen the way you choose anything that matters — with both hands, eyes open, and no idea what happens next. Behind me the tide ran strong and full into the bay, filling every creek and channel, reaching further inland than it had all month.

Coming in, I noticed. After going out all day.

Even the sea, apparently, keeps a ledger. And sometimes, if you wait at the jetty long enough, it balances.

The lamp in the counting house window flickered — someone moving behind it, someone waiting up. Seven weeks, I thought, and turned toward the light.

The first letter, when it came, arrived in eleven weeks, and it changed everything again.
