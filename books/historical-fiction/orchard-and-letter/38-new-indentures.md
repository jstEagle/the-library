## 38. New Indentures

The thing about a company is that it does not grieve, and it does not celebrate. It recalculates.

Three days after they led Gideon Voss off the gangway of the *Sarawak Star* in handcuffs polished to a mirror shine—because even his disgrace had to look respectable—the Meridian Trading Company's office reopened at the usual hour, and Mrs. Adeyemi unlocked the door with the same iron key she'd used every morning for nineteen years, and the ledgers were carried in from the safe exactly as they always were, except for the ones that weren't.

I stood on the veranda a long while before I went in. Old habit. For five years I had entered that building the way you enter deep water—one breath held, one foot tested, everything in you braced for the cold to close over your head. Now there was no Voss behind the harbour master's desk, no Brack lounging by the door with his thumbs in his belt, no sense that the air itself was owned and I was renting my portion of it by the hour.

And yet I still stood on the veranda like a man checking whether the tide had really gone out or was only gathering itself to take him.

"Are you coming in, Halloran, or are you auditioning for a statue?" Mrs. Adeyemi said through the flyscreen. "Because if it's a statue, I'll need you to hold still longer than that."

I came in.

* * *

You would think a town like Broome would talk of nothing else for a year. A harbour master arrested in front of half the pearling fleet, pearls from a drowned lugger's ballast laid out on a magistrate's table like evidence of original sin—that's the sort of story that should keep a settlement fed till the turn of the century.

It did, for about three weeks. Then the fleet sailed again, the shell sheds filled their bins again, and the story settled into the town's bones the way stories do, becoming less an event than a fact of the landscape, like the tides or the red dust. *Old Voss*, people said, shaking their heads over beer, as though he'd been a kind of weather rather than a man. As though greed were something that happened to Broome instead of something Broome had paid for.

The company's response was more interesting, at least to me, because I was inside it. The head office in Perth sent a man named Corrigan—grey suit, grey voice, the sort of accountant who could make a funeral sound like a clerical error—and for a fortnight he sat in Voss's old office with the windows open and went through everything Voss had touched. Which was, it turned out, nearly everything.

"You knew him," Corrigan said to me once, not looking up from a page. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Broome knew what I'd done at the inspection; my name had been spoken aloud before the magistrate, which in this town was practically a christening.

"I worked under him five years," I said.

"And survived him." He turned the page. "That's rarer than you'd think."

I didn't say anything to that. But walking home that evening I turned the sentence over like a shell, looking at its underside. Survived. There was a word I hadn't let myself use. You don't call yourself a survivor while the thing you're surviving is still alive. And Voss had been alive for so much of my life—not just the man, but the whole arrangement of him, the ledger lines and locked drawers and the careful arithmetic that made a boy into an entry.

Now the arrangement was gone, or mostly gone, and I didn't yet know who I was without it.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi found me at the sorting table a week later. The new clerk's desk by the window had been hers since the restructure—Voss had kept her buried in the back room for years, close enough to use, far enough away that her competence couldn't embarrass anyone above her—and now the light fell across her ledgers all day like something owed finally being repaid.

"The apprenticeship opens Monday," she said, without preamble. She was a woman who considered preambles a form of dishonesty. "Clerk's position. Four years' term, wages, a desk."

I looked up from the shells. "A desk."

"A desk, Halloran. Wood, four legs, a drawer. Don't make me describe it further, I'll be here all afternoon."

"Whose recommendation?" I asked, though I suspected.

"Mine." She squared a stack of papers against the table edge, twice, the way she did when she was deciding how much to tell you. "Corrigan asked who in this office understood the manifests going back ten years. I said you did. He asked why. I told him a young man who memorises cargo movements he isn't supposed to notice, and says nothing about them, is either a coward or a careful man. And that the inspection proved which."

I felt my ears go hot. "You could have put your own name forward."

"My name has been forward for nineteen years. It can afford to stand still a season." She set the papers down. "Also, I am forty-six years old and have no intention of being an apprentice to anyone, least of all to a firm that only recently discovered I exist. Let them apprentice you. You're cheap, you're honest, and you're too young to know what you're worth. All three make you very attractive to accountants."

"That's the least flattering compliment I've ever received."

"It's the only kind I give." She paused, and then, quieter, in the voice she used perhaps twice a year: "Take it or don't. But understand what you'd be taking. Not freedom. Freedom was the magistrate's gift and it's already spent. This is something else. This is a place."

She left me with that, and with the shells, and with the strange sensation that someone had just handed me a choice when for five years every door in my life had opened only in the direction other people pushed.

* * *

That night I walked out past the shell sheds to the point where the jetty begins, because it seemed like the sort of decision that ought to be made near water. Everything important in Broome happens near water. We are a town that lives off the bottom of the sea and buries its dead facing it.

The tide was low and the mudflats shone under a half moon, all that silver country between land and sea where nothing decides for very long whether it belongs to one or the other. Callum Reeve had sailed on the morning tide two days after the inspection, bound first for Fremantle and then—who knew. He'd shaken my hand at the gangway of some coastal trader whose name I've already forgotten and said, "Singapore's six weeks' run. When I've seen them settled, you'll have a letter before the month's out." Then he'd looked at me with those old sailor's eyes that miss nothing, and added, "And when the letter comes, lad, you'll want to be somewhere you can read it standing still. Not halfway out a gangway with your kit bag packed."

I hadn't understood him then. Standing on the point now, I did.

Here is the arithmetic nobody puts in ledgers. I had spent five years wanting one thing: out. Away. Singapore was never really the dream—it was simply the opposite of Broome, the way drowning is the opposite of breathing, and any port in that direction would have done. My mother lived at the end of that wanting. Nell had gone to her. And every night since the ship sailed I had lain awake doing sums that wouldn't balance: *she is meeting her daughter right now, she is hearing about me right now, she is asking why her son isn't on that ship right now.*

But wanting out, I had learned, is not the same as wanting somewhere. And the strange discovery of these last weeks—the one I kept having afresh each morning like a dog rediscovering its tail—was that Broome had become somewhere. Not because the town deserved it. Half this town had watched Voss work for years and called it management. Not because I'd forgiven it; forgiveness is a long crop and I'd only just got the seed in the ground. But because of Mrs. Adeyemi's lamp burning late over the copied logs. Because of Callum coming ashore with letters in his coat like contraband hope. Because of Nell, furious and weeping and planning in the same breath, teaching me that blood you choose to stand beside is worth more than blood you merely share. Because of a magistrate who listened, and a Brack who—whatever else he was—had let my sister walk out of that parish house, and a hundred small decencies I'd never noticed while I was busy not being noticed myself.

You cannot inherit belonging. I'd tried that route. It comes to you as a father's trade debt and a mother's silence, and it owns you before you can sign anything. But you can build it, plank by plank, the way you build a jetty out over mud that would swallow you if you stopped working. And the odd thing about a jetty is that the building of it is what makes the ground yours.

I turned back toward the lights of the town. Behind the counting house there was a strip of empty ground, red dirt and spinifex and a broken dray wheel somebody had abandoned years ago. I'd been looking at it lately in a way I couldn't explain. Thinking about soil. Thinking about things that grow slow.

* * *

Monday morning I signed the apprenticeship papers at the same table where, years ago, I had first seen my own name written under *assets*. The irony wasn't lost on me. Same pen, same ink, same clerk's habit of pressing hard enough to leave an impression through three pages. But the words were different now, and the difference was the whole world: then, the document had described what the company owned. This one described what I agreed to do, for wages, on terms I had read—actually read, every line, to Mrs. Adeyemi's visible approval—and signed because I chose to.

"There," she said when I finished. "Four years. Try not to get the next harbour master arrested before you qualify."

"No promises. It's a growth industry."

Something happened to her face then that I suspect she would deny under torture: the corners of her mouth moved upward. "Report to me at seven sharp. And Halloran—" she took the pen back, capped it, "—the drawer locks. Yours. Combination's yours to change. First thing you should do as a free man is change a lock."

I changed the lock that afternoon. It sounds like a small thing. It was not a small thing. Five years I had worked among locked drawers that were never mine, holding keys to nothing, guarding secrets that were used against people like me. Now I had one drawer, one combination, one small square of the world that answered to me alone. I put the letters in it—my mother's letters, all of them, even the ones that had cut me—even though there was no one in the office anymore who'd want them. Some locks you change for practical reasons. That one I changed for the sake of the sound it made: a small clean click, the sound of a boundary being drawn around a life.

* * *

The weeks that followed were the strangest of my life precisely because they were ordinary.

I learned the front office. I learned that Mrs. Adeyemi ran the place the way a rudder runs a ship—invisibly, essentially, and with a great deal more effort than anyone topside ever credits. I learned double-entry bookkeeping, which is built on a beautiful idea: that everything taken must appear somewhere given, that no value vanishes without a trace, that the universe of numbers insists on honesty even when men don't. Voss had spent years falsifying entries against that principle. It had caught up with him. There is a sermon in double-entry bookkeeping, if you want one.

I learned, also, that being free is a skill like any other, and I was clumsy at it. Twice in my first month I caught myself lowering my eyes and flattening my voice when Corrigan passed—a reflex older than my reason, the indentured boy's armour. Both times Mrs. Adeyemi cleared her throat meaningfully, and both times I straightened like a plant finding the sun. By the third week I met Corrigan's eye and held it, and he nodded, and that nod cost me more sweat than the inspection testimony had. Fear leaves grooves in you. Freedom is the daily work of walking differently so new grooves form.

Sundays, I went to church. Not for the theology—I remain unconvinced that the Almighty keeps parish registers, though the Anglicans do a brisk trade as though He subcontracts—but because Nell had gone from that church to Singapore, and because the records there held our names side by side, baptised children of Mara Halloran, and because standing in that pew felt like standing next to her. Reverend Pike had begun to expect me. "Mr. Halloran," he'd say, pleased, as though my attendance were produce coming in on schedule. I never corrected him. A man should be allowed his small mistaken harvests.

And every day, at the sorting table or the desk, I did the other thing I had begun. In the lunch hour, when the office emptied, I went around behind the counting house to the strip of ground with the broken dray wheel, and I worked. Cleared the spinifex. Hauled water from the standpipe in kerosene tins. Turned the red dirt until it looked less like the surface of an unforgiving planet and more like something a seed might consider. The ground was poor—it would need mulch and patience and probably a small miracle—but I'd sent away to Perth for mango seedlings, six of them, and the catalogue promised they'd travel well.

Callum would have laughed himself sick. *Orchards,* he'd have said. *In Broome. Next you'll want a white picket fence and opinions about rain.* But Callum wasn't there to laugh, and that was part of the point. A man plants trees where he intends to stay. It's the most stubborn kind of promise there is, because a tree will outlast any lie you tell about leaving.

* * *

It was Mrs. Adeyemi who said the truest thing, near the end of that first month, in her office with the good window light.

"Your sister writes?" she asked. It wasn't idle. She'd been the one who taught me where the mail steamer berthed, the one who'd warned me that Voss destroyed files, the one whose midnight copying had armed the magistrate. She had earned the right to ask.

"Not yet. Callum said six weeks to Singapore, settling in, then a letter. Should come on the next steamer or the one after." I kept my voice level, which fooled neither of us.

She studied me over her spectacles. "You gave up a great deal, Halloran. Your berth. Your passage. Possibly your mother's first sight of your grown face. I watched you hand that ticket to your sister and I confess I wondered, that day, whether you were being generous or merely performing generosity for an audience of one magistrate."

"And now?"

"Now I watch you come in early and stay late, and lock your little drawer, and dig holes behind the counting house like a man burying treasure in reverse." She removed her spectacles, which she did only when about to be either kind or devastating, and with her the two were often the same. "Generosity performed is exhausting. Nobody performs for a month. Whatever this is, it's real. So I'll tell you what I know about waiting for letters, having waited for several in my life, some of which never came." She put the spectacles back on. "You wait better when your hands are full. Fill them."

I went back to my desk and filled my hands. Manifests, ink, the day's tallies. And underneath the tallies, folded small, a page I had copied out myself in fair clerk's script: the sailing schedule of the Eastern & Oriental line, steamers into Broome, dates circled. The next one was eleven days out. The one after, twenty-six.

Eleven days. Twenty-six days. Somewhere south of the equator a woman I remembered only as hands and humming and the smell of frangipani was learning her daughter's laugh, and sooner or later she would sit down with paper, and her unmistakable hand—that hand that had crossed an ocean once already to find me—would write again.

The mail bags came up the jetty on the morning tide, the same as they always had. But I watched them differently now. Every sack that swung aboard the lighter was a sack that might, eleven days hence, swing back down again carrying my name in my mother's writing.

I had chosen my ground. Now, at last, the water was bringing word of what grew there.
