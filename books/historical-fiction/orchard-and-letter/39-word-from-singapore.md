## 39. Word from Singapore

The letter came on a Tuesday, which I only remember because Mrs. Adeyemi remarked upon it. "Tuesdays bring the overseas post," she said, sliding the mail tray across the counter with the particular economy of motion she reserved for matters that mattered. "Everything else can wait a day. Tuesdays cannot."

I had been copying freight rates for the Batavia run, my pen moving in the small, obedient circles of a man who has learned that usefulness is its own kind of camouflage. Five years of indenture had taught me that. Six months of freedom had not yet taught me anything else. My hand still wrote the way it had been taught to write—small, precise, apologizing for the space it took up on the page.

The envelope was thick. That was the first thing I noticed. Broome letters tended to be thin—a bill, a summons, a scolding from a distant cousin. This one had heft, the weight of several pages folded together, and the paper was that faintly waxy stock you only got in the Straits Settlements, printed through with humidity long before it ever reached the dry heat of the northwest coast.

And the address. The address stopped my breath for a moment, because it was written in a hand I knew better than my own and had never once seen write to me.

*Oscar Halloran, care of the Meridian Trading Company, Broome, Western Australia.*

Not *Master Halloran*. Not *the boy*. Not *Oscar*, the way Nell would have written it, quick and careless as a bird hopping. But *Oscar Halloran*, spelled out whole and deliberate, each letter given its full measure, the way you write a name when you are practicing it. When you have been practicing it.

Mrs. Adeyemi watched me over her spectacles with the expression she wore when she had already calculated something and was waiting, courteously, for me to catch up. She had been doing a great deal of that lately. Since Voss's arrest she had been appointed acting harbor secretary—an interim title she pronounced as though it were permanent, because she intended it to be—and the office had settled around her like a coat that finally fit. The men who had once looked through her now looked at her, and mostly looked away again quickly, which suited her fine.

"Tuesday," she said again, gently, and turned back to her own ledger.

I did not open it at the counter. Some part of me—the part Callum called my father's stubbornness and my mother's caution, though he could never keep straight which was which—wanted to be alone for it. Or perhaps not alone. Perhaps simply somewhere that wasn't the counting house, where every surface remembered Voss, where even the smell of ink had been, until recently, the smell of ownership.

I walked out past the shell sheds. It was the season when the luggers came in heavy, and the air off the water carried that sweet, grave smell of shell and brine that I had breathed since before I could name it. The sorters were at their benches, and for a moment I saw a girl at the third bench with sharp eyes and quicker hands, and my chest did the thing it always did now, the reaching-thing, the half-turn toward a person who wasn't there anymore.

Nell had been gone four months. The Sarawak Star had carried her out on an afternoon so calm it felt like the sea itself was embarrassed by what we were asking of it. I had stood on the jetty with Callum and watched her grow smaller—not sad, exactly, at the rail, but fierce, waving with both arms like a woman signaling reinforcements, until the ship took her beyond the reach of any signal and Callum put a hand on my shoulder and said nothing, which was the most eloquent thing anyone said to me that day.

Four months of silence. Letters took weeks; replies took longer. I had written twice—clumsy pages, telling her about the magistrate voiding my papers, about Adeyemi's apprenticeship, about the mango pits I'd started in tins behind the counting house. I had told myself the silence was just distance, just the slow arithmetic of the mail. But there is a particular species of fear that lives in waiting, and I knew its name well: it is the fear that the thing you gave everything for will turn out to be less than you imagined, or worse, that *you* will turn out to be less than the giving required.

I walked out to the end of the old jetty, the one they'd let fall into disrepair after the new one went in, and sat down with my boots hanging over green water, and opened the envelope.

There were three enclosures. I'll set them down here in the order I read them, though my hands were not steady enough to obey any order at first.

The first was in Nell's hand—rushing downhill, dots over the i's like exclamation points, because to Nell every sentence was an exclamation point held in reserve.

*Dear Osc,*

*I've done it, or rather SHE'S done it, or rather we've both done it, and I don't know how to say it except all at once so forgive me.*

*She met me at the wharf. I knew her before she knew me—I'd swear it. There were forty women on that pier and I picked her out like picking a pearl out of a tray, and then she said my name, Nell, just like that, and I nearly went into the water.*

*Osc, she's small. Isn't that a stupid thing to write first? But you build someone up your whole life and then she's this small quick woman with grey coming in at her temples and hands like mine, EXACTLY like mine, and she cried on the wharf in front of the customs men and didn't care one bit.*

*She asks about you constantly. Constantly. At breakfast, at supper, in the middle of the night I hear her walking and I know she's thinking of you. I've told her everything—the trial, the logs, the pearls, all of it. She sat very still when I got to the part about Father. Very still, the way you go still. I think that's where I get it.*

*Now comes the hard part and I'm going to make you read it, because I'm a coward and if I say it aloud she'll hear me hesitate.*

*She's written you a letter. A real one, her own words, took her three days and made her cry and start over twice. I promised her I wouldn't tell you what's in it. But Osc—it starts with your name. Your whole name. She wrote it out and crossed it out and wrote it again and finally left the good copy in, and when I saw it I had to leave the room.*

*Come to Singapore. There's work here, real work, the company's a shambles without Voss and half the agents owe us their gratitude whether they know it or not. Callum says the same. Even the aunt sent money, which frightened us both more than any threat ever did.*

*But whatever you decide—read her letter first. Read it twice. And know that she asked me to tell you she isn't asking forgiveness. She said forgiveness is a debt and she won't put another debt on you. She said—"write this down exactly, Nell"—she said: "Tell him I spent ten years writing his name in my head and never once on paper, and if he reads it now, let him know it's the truest thing I ever wrote."*

*All my love and then some,*
*Nell*

P.S. Auntie sends five pounds and instructions to buy a hat. I told her you own two hats already. She said that explains everything.

* * *

I sat on the old jetty for a long while with Nell's pages in my lap and the second enclosure unopened beside me, and the tide came in beneath my boots with that patient, indifferent sound the sea makes when it has outlasted everyone's grief.

The second enclosure was thin. One page. Her handwriting—the unmistakable one, slanting forward like a woman walking into wind, the letters I had memorized off the original letter years ago, the one Callum had carried across two oceans because a dying promise outweighed his own comfort.

It began:

*My son Oscar.*

Three words. Fourteen letters. And I want to be honest here, since honesty is the only currency I have ever had much of: I could not read past them for some time. I am a man who kept accounts under a man who owned him, who sorted pearls worth more than my life with steady hands, who stood before a magistrate and broke five years of silence in front of the whole town—but four words in my mother's handwriting undid me entirely, sitting on a rotten jetty with gulls screaming at me for crumbs I did not have.

When I could go on, I went on.

*My son Oscar. I have begun this letter eleven times. Nell tells me it is twelve. She counts everything, that girl. You will have noticed.*

*I will not make excuses. Excuses are the coin of people who intend to do wrong again. I will tell you what happened and let you make of it what you must, because you have earned the right to your own arithmetic, and God knows you learned arithmetic young.*

*Your father found something that night. He told me before he went down—he always told me things on the jetty, standing close, because the wind took words otherwise. He said: if I'm right about this, Mara, we're out. We buy our way clear, we take you and the boy and we go somewhere green. He laughed. I had not heard him laugh like that since we were married.*

*Then the boat came back without him, and Gideon Voss came to my door with his soft voice and his soft eyes, and he said: your husband's find was misreported, Mrs. Halloran, there are irregularities, and a woman alone with a child should be careful about irregularities. He meant it as a threat dressed as kindness. He had been dressing threats as kindness for years; it was his best suit.*

*He wanted the location of the cache. Your father had told no one but me, and I told him nothing. So he took other leverage. He arranged papers saying I had drowned—that is easier than you would think, when you control the shipping records—and he made it plain that if I stayed, the next drowning would have a name already prepared for it. Yours.*

*I left to make sure you would live. I told myself that every day for ten years and some days I believed it. I wrote your name in my head every night. Never on paper. Paper could be seized, read, used. A name on paper, in this trade, is a man in chains—you know this better than anyone, and I am sorry, I am sorry, that you learned it as a lesson instead of a warning.*

*What I did was not brave. Bravery would have been staying and fighting. What I did was arithmetic, and I chose the sum that kept you breathing. Whether that was love or cowardice I have decided to stop deciding. Mothers are not permitted the luxury of clean choices, and I refuse to apologize for the mess of mine any longer.*

*Nell is here. She is louder than you, thank God, and she has your father's temper, which I am told skips generations and landed entirely on her. She has made me laugh until I cried, which I had forgotten bodies could do. You did that too, once. You were four. You wore a pot on your head and declared yourself captain of the kitchen and I have thought of that pot every day for ten years.*

*I do not ask you to come. I ask only that you believe this: everything I did was so that your name would survive to be written by your own hand, free, wherever you chose to write it. If you choose Broome—if you plant your life in that red dirt rather than cross the water to me—then that too is a choice I will honor, because a mother who demands is only a debt with a face.*

*But if you ever do come—my door faces the orchard. I planted fruit trees the year I arrived, because your father once said he wanted to live somewhere green, and I thought: if he cannot, I will grow the greenness for him, and maybe it will reach you somehow, across all this water.*

*You were never a line in anyone's ledger to me. Whatever the company wrote you down as, you were my son first, and you will be my son last, and in between I will spend what remains of my life making up the difference.*

*Your mother,*
*Mara Halloran*

*P.S. I am enclosing the third thing. It is not a letter. It is proof of life, of a kind. Open it last.*

* * *

The third enclosure was small and flat, wrapped in tissue, and when I unwrapped it I had to laugh—which startled the gulls and possibly alarmed a fisherman rowing past, who gave the wide berth one gives to madmen on jetties.

It was a practice sheet. A page covered, top to bottom, in repetitions of a single word, in her handwriting, growing steadier line by line: *Oscar, Oscar, Oscar, Oscar.* Dozens of them. Early ones shaky, the letters crowded together as though afraid of taking up room; later ones opening out, confident, taking their full measure on the page. And at the bottom, one final line, larger than all the rest, written slowly and without a single correction:

*Oscar Halloran, son of Thomas and Mara, brother of Nell — free.*

Underneath it, in different ink, smaller, almost shy:

*This is how it should have been written all along.*

I read it until the light went gold and then grey, and the luggers' lamps came out on the water like a fleet of low stars, and I understood something that I suppose I had been building toward since the day Callum Reeve walked onto the jetty asking dangerous questions: that recognition does not arrive all at once like a rescue. It arrives the way a tide fills a channel—imperceptibly, then all at once, and then it keeps arriving, every day, for the rest of your life.

Forgiveness, too. She hadn't asked for it, which was exactly why it came easy. Debts with faces, she'd said. I had spent five years as a debt with a face. I was not going to become a creditor in my own family. What I felt looking at that page of names was not absolution granted but something quieter: the understanding that she had done the best terrible thing available to her, that love sometimes looks like leaving, that the woman who fled across the water had been protecting the same boy the man who died had died protecting. Two parents, one plan, neither able to tell the other the whole of it. That was not betrayal. That was a siege, fought from two sides of a wall, each believing themselves alone.

I folded the pages along their original creases, carefully, the way you handle documents that matter, and I walked back along the old jetty in the dark with the tissue-wrapped practice sheet inside my shirt, against my chest, like a coal I was carrying home to start a fire.

At the counting house, Mrs. Adeyemi's lamp was still lit. She looked up when I came in, took one look at my face, and did not ask. Instead she reached into her desk drawer and produced two cups and the bottle of brandy she kept for occasions the ledgers couldn't classify.

"Well?" she said.

"They're well," I said. "They're—she wrote my name. All of it."

Adeyemi nodded slowly, as though this confirmed some calculation she'd been running for months. Then she slid a fresh sheet of paper and a pen across the counter toward me.

"Tuesday brought you something," she said. "Tuesdays also send things. The Sarawak Star mails from here Thursday next. Write tonight. Ink dries differently depending on what day it's written—every clerk knows that—and you want this page drying on a Thursday, when the mail goes out strong."

I sat down at the counter under the lamp, in the building where I had once been entered under *assets*, and I wrote the first letter of my free life addressed to my mother.

*Dear Mum. My name is Oscar Halloran...*

And halfway down the page, telling her about the mango pits in their tins behind the counting house—six of them sprouted, stubborn, leaning toward the light—about the apprenticeship, about Callum talking of fitting out his own lugger come the new season, about the orchard I had already begun to plan on paper though the ground behind the counting house was still just red dirt and broken shell—I stopped.

Because there was something else in the drawer of the old harbor master's desk, which was now Adeyemi's, which she had shown me only once, weeks ago, and which we had agreed to leave sealed until the dust settled properly. An envelope, yellowed, marked in Voss's cramped hand: *HALLORAN — HOLD PENDING FURTHER INSTRUCTION.* Addressed not to Broome at all, but onward—to Singapore.

Ten years old, that envelope. Sealed. Waiting.

Adeyemi caught my eye across the counter, and I saw that she had been thinking of it too.

"Thursday," she said quietly, "comes quickly."
