## 2. Requisition at Dawn

They came before the mist had lifted off the river, which Kestrel would remember later as the army's first act of cartographic courtesy — arriving in the one hour when the town could not see itself.

She was at the window of the survey office with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand when she heard the hooves. Not the scattered percussion of a courier, but a wall of them, many horses moving at a walk, unhurried, the gait of men who had already decided the day belonged to them. She set down the cup and went to the door, and by the time she had it open a hand's width, the first riders were entering the square below, their grey coats darkened at the shoulders by fog, their breath rising in columns like smoke from a fire that hadn't started yet.

There were perhaps forty of them. Behind the horses came wagons, and behind the wagons came infantry in loose column, boots striking cobbles with a sound like a loom working. They did not charge. They did not shout. They simply arrived everywhere at once — at the well, at the customs house, at the mouth of Tanner's Lane where it opened onto the square — until the town's center of gravity had been moved without anyone feeling the shift.

Kestrel had grown up on maps of invasions. Her master, Aldous Vane, had kept a shelf of them: arrows like migrating birds, frontiers redrawn in confident vermilion. None of those maps had shown her what an occupation actually sounded like, which was almost nothing. A sergeant calling out billeting assignments in a bored voice. The creak of a wagon brake. Somewhere, absurdly, a soldier laughing at something another soldier had said.

The notices went up within the hour. She watched a corporal paste them to the customs house door, smoothing each sheet from the center outward with the flat of his hand, the way she herself smoothed paper onto a mounting board. The print was too far away to read, but she knew what it said anyway. She had drawn requisition orders herself once, as an exercise — *the occupying authority requires for the provisioning of its forces* — and Master Vane had taken the pen from her and made her write the word *requires* forty times until she understood that the word was a blade dressed as a noun.

By mid-morning the square had filled with townspeople who stood in clusters and did not speak above a murmur, and the harvest carts that should have been rolling toward the cider presses sat unloaded along the east arcade, their fruit darkening in the sun. The orchards were being picked early this year, picked in fear, whole rows stripped by families who wanted nothing sweet left standing when the soldiers came for it. And now the soldiers had come anyway.

* * *

They reached the survey office at noon.

Kestrel heard them on the stairs — four sets of boots, taking the steps slowly, reading the building as they climbed — and she had just enough time to decide what to do with the ledger. There was no time to hide it properly; there was only time to hide it completely, which is a different thing. The atlas lay open on the great table, the coastal plate pinned at its corners, the ink of yesterday still holding a faint sheen. The ledger was beside it, fat with names, three years of Aldous Vane's quiet, illegal bookkeeping: every family displaced, every orchard seized, every acre entered in a hand so fine it looked like stitching.

She slid it beneath the atlas's lower board, into the shallow tray where the unmounted plates lived, and pulled the stack of blank survey sheets down over it, and then the soldiers were in the doorway.

The officer who entered first wore a colonel's insignia and no expression whatsoever. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten — medium height, medium build, eyes a color between brown and nothing. He stopped just inside the room and did not look at Kestrel at all. He looked at the walls.

The office walls were hung with proofs. Forty years of Aldous Vane's work: the river surveys, the county sheets, the great unfinished atlas of the borderlands that had consumed his final decade. The colonel walked the perimeter slowly, hands clasped behind him, and Kestrel understood that she was watching a man appraise a property.

"You are the apprentice," he said. It was not a question.

"Kestrel Okafor."

"Vane's people called him the old compass." The colonel paused before the coastal plate, still pinned to the table, and leaned over it without touching. "He refused every commission we offered. Did you know that? Three times. I offered him a salary that would have bought this street." He straightened. "Where is his ledger of holdings?"

Her heart performed a single hard strike against her ribs. "Ledger of holdings?"

"The record of parcels. Boundaries, owners, yields." His tone did not change; he might have been asking after the weather in a county he intended to visit. "Every office of this type keeps one. It is standard practice."

"It's in the strongbox," she said, "with the sealed archives. I don't have the key. My master kept it on his person."

"And your master is dead."

"He was killed in the shelling. In the spring."

Something moved behind the colonel's eyes — not sympathy; inventory. He turned to the sergeant behind him. "Break the box."

They took the room apart with the thoroughness of men who had done it a hundred times. Drawers came out whole and were upended onto the floor. The plan chests were opened, their contents fanned and riffled. Kestrel stood at the edge of it all with her arms folded tightly enough to ache, watching a stranger's hands move through the only inheritance she had, and she made herself catalog rather than grieve, because grief would put her on her knees and knees were where the dying ended up.

They found the strongbox under the flagstone where Aldous had always said no one would ever look, because no one ever looks at what a mapmaker hides under a map of his own floor. The sergeant brought it to the colonel, who knelt, examined the lock, and shot it open with a pistol held against the iron — one report, flat and enormous in the small room, followed by a ringing silence in which Kestrel heard her own pulse.

Inside lay the deed papers, the sealed correspondence, and the ledger.

The colonel lifted it out. He weighed it in his hand. Then he opened it to the first page and read, and read, and turned pages with a licked thumb, and the silence stretched so long that Kestrel began composing her own death in it — because she had hidden the wrong thing, or hidden it too obviously, or the atlas tray had been searched first and the tracing she'd begun was already in a sergeant's pocket —

But the colonel only closed the ledger and said: "Parcel records. Good." And handed it to the sergeant with instructions to log it among the confiscated materials, and Kestrel understood, with a cold that started in her teeth, that he had seen exactly what it was and valued it at exactly nothing — a list of farmers, a list of orchards, a list of the already-defeated. He carried the atlas plate away instead, the coastal sheet, rolled under his arm like a captured banner.

* * *

They did not burn the office. They conscripted it.

By evening, two soldiers were billeted on the ground floor, and a notice was nailed to the door reclassifying the premises as a Survey Detachment, Provisioning Directorate. Kestrel was permitted to keep her attic room — permitted being a word that now meant *not yet removed* — and at dawn of the second day she was summoned downstairs by a lieutenant with a clipboard and a manner of permanent apology.

"The colonel wants the orchard boundaries redrawn," the lieutenant said. "Supply routes. He says you're the only hand in the district worth the trouble."

"I'm not a draughtsman of roads."

"You are today."

The work was laid out on the great table under guard: the confiscated ledger, open; fresh sheets of the good rag paper; Verrec's own annotations in a small, disciplined hand. He wanted the orchard tracts rendered as logistics — each holding reduced from a family's name to an acreage, each acreage assessed for road access, water, tonnage, season. The apples of the Marchetti slope became *Provision Point Four, 900 tons projected*. The Hollis terraces, planted by a woman who had died before the war and tended since by her son, became a line item.

Kestrel sat down and picked up the pen because the alternative was visible through the window: two men from the requisition detail walking the perimeter of the Duval orchard with stakes and string, measuring out what they had already decided to take.

Humiliation, she discovered, has a temperature. It starts in the face and sinks. By the third hour of drawing her neighbors' livelihoods as supply corridors, it had sunk all the way down into her wrists, into the very mechanics of her craft, so that every line she drew felt like a cut she was administering personally. She drew the road that would carry the fruit away. She drew the depot marked on the common where the market had stood for a hundred and sixty years. She wrote *900 tons* in a hand she kept deliberately beautiful, because if the work was going to be monstrous she would at least refuse to let it be careless, and she could not have explained, even to herself, why that defiance mattered — only that Aldous Vane had taught her that the line is a moral act, that a mapmaker's honesty lives in the weight and truth of every stroke, and if they were going to use her hand they would use all of it.

Colonel Verrec came at dusk.

He stood over her shoulder long enough to make his presence a physical weight, then walked around the table, examining the day's output. He paused at the Marchetti tract.

"You've included the field boundaries," he said. "Stone walls, hedgerows. I asked for routes."

"The routes follow the walls," Kestrel said. "That's where the lanes are. If you erase the walls, the routes stop being true."

Verrec looked at her then, directly, for the first time. His eyes were the color of dishwater and entirely without warmth, but there was attention in them, and attention, she thought, was the most dangerous thing in the room.

"Vane trained you to care about true," he said.

"He trained me to care about accurate. True was my addition."

A muscle moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; the ghost of one, decommissioned. He tapped the ledger with one finger. "These names," he said. "You knew these people."

"I know them."

"They'll be processed through the relocation offices like everyone else. Paperwork. Orderly." He said it the way a man describes rain. "You resent that."

"I resent bad drafting," Kestrel said. "This map makes your supply lines look shorter than they are. The ford at Miller's Bend won't take loaded wagons past October. You'd know that if your engineers surveyed instead of guessed."

The pause that followed lasted three full seconds. Then Verrec pulled a chair from the wall, sat down across from her, and unrolled a different sheet — an army survey of the northern parishes, stamped and official, the ink barely dry.

"Then correct it," he said. "All of it. You will work under guard, you will sign nothing, and you will render every boundary in this district to my specification." He rose, buttoned his coat, and added, without looking back: "You may sleep in your attic. The guards will bring your meals. Consider yourself requisitioned, Miss Okafor — like everything else in this town that works."

* * *

They locked her in at night. She learned the rhythm of the building from the attic floor: the sentry's turn at the stairhead every quarter hour, the changeover at midnight, the long hollow hour before dawn when even the occupiers slept.

On the third night, lying awake under the eaves, she made herself think about the ledger the way Verrec thought about it — as paper, as weight, as cargo. He had logged it with the confiscated materials, intact, unburned. An orderly man does not keep disorderly documents unless they serve him. Three years of names, signed orders, dates, acreages — that was not trash to a colonel who had signed the requisitions himself. That was the record of his own crimes, preserved in his enemy's finest hand, and a careful man keeps such a thing close the way other men keep gold: not because he loves it, but because of what it can be traded for when the war ends and the accounting begins.

Everyone was bargaining for what they could carry.

Kestrel stared into the dark rafters and felt the humiliation in her wrists transmute, degree by degree, into something harder. They had taken the ledger. But they had also given her the table, the light, the hours, and the pretext — a surveyor's access, a draughtsman's hands, a reason to touch every document in the building. Verrec believed he had requisitioned a tool.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she could hear the sentry's boots begin their turn at the stairhead. She closed her eyes and began, silently and precisely, to memorize the first page of names — family, parcel, date — the way her master had taught her to memorize coastlines: feature by feature, loss by loss, until the map lived somewhere no requisition order could reach.
