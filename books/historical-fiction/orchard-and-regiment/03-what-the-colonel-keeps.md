## 3. What the Colonel Keeps

The survey office had not changed so much as it had been *reinterpreted*. That was the word Kestrel kept returning to, working her pen in the lamplight while a soldier with a bored face stood by the door and watched her hands. Her master's drafting table was still there, still bearing the shallow grooves his compass points had worn into the oak over thirty years, but now it faced the wrong wall, angled toward the door as if the room itself were expected to stand at attention. The drawers of pigments had been sorted — she could tell because the ochres were no longer where Master Aldous had always kept them, beside the blues he'd mixed himself from lapis and patience. Someone with a military sense of order had rearranged everything by hue, like ammunition.

She drew orchard boundaries. That was the work they'd given her: the army's supply routes needed to know which groves were productive, which were abandoned, which could be stripped without troubling anyone who mattered. She drew neat rows of trees and neat lines of ownership and neat little numbers for yield, and every line she laid down would become a reason for some family's winter to go hungry. Her pen moved anyway. That was the humiliation of it — that the hand obeyed even when the heart rebelled, that craft was loyal only to itself.

*You are drawing the world,* Master Aldous used to say, *so draw it honestly, or don't draw at all.*

He hadn't anticipated this particular honesty: an honest map of theft, rendered in his own inks on his own paper, by his apprentice, under guard.

The third night of the work, she noticed the ledger.

It happened the way important things happen — sideways, in the corner of the eye. A soldier came in near dusk to deliver Verrec's supper tray and set it down on the cabinet behind Kestrel, and when he lifted the tray away again the cabinet door swung open a finger's width, and inside, stacked among rolled charts and Verrec's personal correspondence, bound in its familiar grey calfskin with the brass corners gone dull, was the ledger of names.

Kestrel did not look at it directly. She had learned already — three days of occupied town teaches quickly — that looking too long at anything was a form of confession. She finished the boundary line she was drawing, blotted it, cleaned her nib, and asked leave to fetch water from the courtyard pump. When she came back, the cabinet was closed again, and the soldier was back at his post by the door, and the ledger was still in the world.

That was what mattered. *Still in the world.*

She had assumed it burned. Everyone assumed things burned in occupied towns; ash was the occupation's signature, its way of signing its name. When the soldiers had emptied the office nine days ago and carried out the atlas and the ledger both, she had watched them go down the steps and had privately mourned the ledger as one mourns the dead — completely, finally, with no expectation of return. The names of the displaced families: the Veyrans, whose terraced plums fed half the valley; old Maret Sault and her sons; the whole extended root-system of the Corbier line, eleven households pulled up like saplings before the first frost so the army could requisition their ground. Every family the occupying army had moved to take their orchards. Gone, she'd thought. Burned with the rest.

But Verrec had kept it. And once she began turning the fact over in her mind, she understood why, and understanding it chilled her more thoroughly than any cruelty might have.

A man who burns a record is a man who has decided his side will win. A man who *keeps* a record is a man hedging against the possibility that it won't. The armistice negotiators were said to be arriving within days; the whole garrison smelled of that nervous, over-polished quiet armies get when the fighting is about to stop and the accounting begins. And Colonel Verrec — precise, grey-eyed, a man who signed orders rather than shouting them — had put the evidence of his own crimes into a locked cabinet instead of a stove. Because a document naming every family displaced under his authority was not only evidence. It was also, if the winds shifted, a *ledger of debts owed to him*. Proof that he had executed policy faithfully. Proof that whatever had been done here had been done by order, in writing, with signatures — his own included — and that when the victors came looking for someone to hang, he could produce the paperwork and say: *not my decision. My diligence.*

Everyone, she thought, watching her pen lay down another honest, damning line, is bargaining for what they can carry.

Her master had carried an atlas. The families had carried what fit in handcarts. Verrec carried a ledger full of other people's ruin, believing paper could hold a door shut against the flood coming for him. And she — she carried nothing yet, nothing but memory and the shape of the atlas in her hands, the ghost-weight of it when the soldiers took it down the stairs. But she intended to carry something. She intended to carry *all of it*, page by page if she had to.

*

The night shift came to her on the sixth day, though no one told her it would. It simply arrived, the way weather arrives: Verrec appeared at the table after dark, studied her day's output in silence for a long minute, and said, "You'll continue through the night. The eastern sections are wanted by morning. There will be a lamp." He paused at the door. "No one else will be here. Do not mistake that for trust."

"I never have," Kestrel said, and heard the words leave her mouth before she could weigh them, and braced for consequences that didn't come. Verrec looked at her for a moment longer — a cartographer's look, measuring — and left.

The building settled around her. The sentry outside the door stamped his feet against the cold and then, mercifully, walked his post to the far end of the corridor, where a brazier had been lit and two other soldiers were arguing quietly about rations. Through the shuttered windows the town was a scatter of banked fires. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse at nothing.

Kestrel worked first. That was discipline, and also camouflage: for two hours she did exactly what she'd been ordered to do, laying in the eastern orchards with a fidelity that made her sick, until the sheets were done and drying and the lamp had burned low enough that the shadows reached the cabinet.

Then she crossed the room.

The lock on the cabinet was a simple warded thing — her master had taught her locks alongside lettering, reasoning that a mapmaker who couldn't pick a lock couldn't properly appraise what other people's maps were worth. It gave to her third attempt with a sound no louder than a page turning. Inside, the ledger sat exactly where she'd seen it, wedged between chart tubes, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, she found the thing she had not dared hope for: three loose plates of the atlas, unbound, overlooked or deliberately set aside. Her master's coastlines. His handwriting in the margins. She pressed her palm flat against them for a long moment, eyes closed, and allowed herself ten seconds of grief before folding them into the false bottom of her satchel.

The ledger she handled differently. This was not mourning; this was theft, and theft required method. She had prepared tracing paper during the day — ordinary sheets among her supplies, cut to the size of the ledger's pages, hidden inside a folio of blank survey stock. Now she opened the ledger to its first page and read.

Not the requisition dates. Not the acreages, the yields, the tidy columns of confiscation. The *names*, written in the clerk's careful hand at the head of each entry, with the villages spelled out the way her master had insisted village names be spelled — phonetically, as their own people said them, not as army clerks simplified them. *Veyran. Sault. Corbier.* Below each, in smaller script, the disposition: relocated, dispersed, unaccounted.

*Unaccounted.* Three times on the first page alone. She traced those words twice without meaning to, her fingers learning them the way fingers learn letters in the dark.

She laid tracing paper over the page and copied. It was slow, exacting work — every name, every date, every cross-reference — done in a fine hand small enough to pass for survey notation. An hour for the first page. The lamp was running low; she trimmed the wick and kept going. Outside, the sentry had stopped walking; she heard him settle onto a crate, heard the drowse come into the silence. On the second sheet of tracing paper she got through four more entries before her hand began to shake, not from fear exactly, but from the strange intimacy of copying grief in a clerk's fair hand — six hundred names in this book, perhaps more, and she was going to learn all of them the way a priest learns prayers.

Near dawn she stopped. She had five pages copied, folded flat and sewn — she'd done it with thread from her own hem, needle borrowed from the mending basket, stitches fine enough to disappear into the satchel's lining seam — and the ledger returned to its place in the cabinet, the lock turned, the trays realigned, every object restored to its exact position. She measured her memory against the room the way her master had taught her to measure: angles, relationships, the small betrayals of disturbed dust. Nothing betrayed her.

But as she was washing the ink from her nib, she saw it.

On the blotter beside Verrec's chair, weighted under a brass rule, lay a single sheet of the army's own survey stock, half-finished — and the hand on it was not Verrec's. It was a draftsman's hand, competent and slightly hurried, and it was drawing the boundaries of a village called Saint-Aubin-le-Brûlé, except that where the village should have been there was nothing. No houses marked. No well. Just open ground labeled *unimproved* in fresh ink, the way you'd label a field that had never held anything at all.

Kestrel stood very still. She knew Saint-Aubin-le-Brûlé. Everyone in the valley knew what had happened there last spring; the smoke had been visible from the survey office tower, and afterward no one spoke of it, which was itself a kind of map — a blank space on the tongue where a village used to be. Now the army was drawing the same blank space on paper, making official what had been made true by fire, and the sheet was waiting on Verrec's desk for authentication. For a certified surveyor's mark. For hers.

Footsteps in the corridor. The sentry straightening. The door opening, and Verrec himself in the grey light of early morning, looking from her face to the drying sheets to the blotter, and saying, without greeting:

"Surveyor. You'll want to examine the eastern boundaries before you sign off on them."

* * *

Kestrel looked at the half-drawn map of the village that no longer existed, then at the colonel's grey eyes, and understood that the next hour would decide whether she was a tool, a witness — or something neither of them had planned for.

"Yes, Colonel," she said, and picked up her loupe.
