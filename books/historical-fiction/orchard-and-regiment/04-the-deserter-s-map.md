## 4. The Deserter's Map

The cold cellar smelled of apples going soft and earth gone sweet with rot, and Kestrel had learned to love that smell in the way one loves a thing that is keeping her alive. She came down the ladder steps at dusk, as she had every evening for four nights, carrying the day's tracing folded against her ribs beneath her coat. The tracings lived here now, between the stone jars of last year's cider and the empty racks where the Vasseur family's pears had once been stored before the family itself was stored elsewhere — a village two provinces east, if the ledger was truthful, and the ledger was always truthful. Her master had built it that way. Names, dates, parcels, orchard rows counted tree by tree. *Forty-one pear trees, Vasseur holding, requisitioned for army provender, 14th of the Frost Moon.* He had written it all down while the wagons were still loading, because he understood what she was only now beginning to understand: that a thing unwritten could be denied, and a thing written might at least be mourned.

She knelt by the third jar from the wall — the jar with the chipped lip, the jar that meant nothing to anyone but her — and lifted out the oilcloth packet. Four pages now. Four of thirty-one. She smoothed the newest tracing onto her knee, checking her work in the light of the shuttered lantern: the column of names copied in her smallest hand, each letter a seed pressed into paper. Tomorrow night she would begin moving them across the river, page by page, past Sergeant Brannoc at the bridge checkpoint, who had known her master and owed him — or so she hoped — some sediment of loyalty.

A sound above her stopped her breath.

Not an animal. Animals did not shift their weight and then go deliberately, carefully still, the way a man does when he hears a latch he does not know. Kestrel closed her fingers around the lantern handle and considered the ladder. She was small; she was quick; she knew this cellar the way she knew the palm of her own hand, every root-buttress and shadowed corner. But whoever stood at the top of those steps knew she was down here, or would know within moments.

She set the tracing back under the jar, drew the lantern up, and climbed.

The orchard above was blue with early dark. Among the bare trees, half-collapsed against the trunk of the oldest apple, stood a man in a uniform without insignia, his left leg dark to the knee where blood had soaked and dried and soaked again. His right hand held a knife low against his thigh, and his face — hollowed, bearded, gray as the dusk itself — wore the expression of someone who has already decided how he will die and is only waiting for the other party to agree.

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Don't shout. I'll be gone before they come, and you'll have seen nothing."

"You can't walk," Kestrel said.

"I can fall downhill."

She looked at him properly then, and the looking took the choice out of her hands, though not in any way she could have predicted. She knew this face. Not from life — from ink. From the ledger's opening folio, where her master had recorded not only the displaced families but the orders that displaced them, transcribing each requisition document entire, signatures included, because he believed — oh, he had said it a hundred times at his drafting table, tapping the page with the bone folder — *a map without its maker is just territory, girl. Every line has a hand behind it, and the hand must be named.* And there, beneath Colonel Verrec's bold and confident flourish, sat a smaller signature, careful and slightly cramped, the hand of a junior officer diligent in his ruin:

*Ilan Sere, Lt., for the commanding officer.*

He swayed against the tree. The knife dipped.

"You're bleeding into my orchard," Kestrel said, and heard her own voice go flat and practical, the voice of a woman pricing lumber. "The cellar. There's straw and there's water, and no one comes here because the Vasseurs are gone and everyone believes the cellar's been emptied. Can you climb down five steps?"

He stared at her. In the failing light his eyes were the color of river ice. "Why?"

"Because if you die up here, the patrols will find you, and they'll search the whole property, and everything I've hidden below will hang me." She turned toward the ladder. "Because I need you alive for something. Come down or bleed out; those are your choices tonight."

* * *

He slept, or lay pretending to sleep, in the straw behind the cider jars while she worked by lamplight at the far end of the cellar, and the pretense suited them both. She traced the fifth page — the Harrow family, eleven souls, forty acres of cherry taken for a hospital that was never built — and let her mind run along the problem of him the way her pen ran along the problem of a coastline: patiently, feeling for the true shape beneath the noise.

He had signed. That was the fact at the center of him, the way a flaw sits at the center of a stone. He had signed the orders that emptied these orchards, and then — and this was the part she kept turning over, the part that did not fit any map she owned — he had deserted. Men deserted from fear, from cowardice, from exhaustion. But he had been shot in the leg doing it, which meant he had run through the picket lines rather than walk away from some garrison post, which meant urgency, which meant — perhaps — that somewhere between signing his name and fleeing his own army, something had broken in Lieutenant Ilan Sere, and he had chosen to fall toward the very country he had helped unpeople.

Or he was a spy. She allowed for that too. She was a cartographer's apprentice; she did not deal in certainties.

Near midnight he stirred and spoke without rising. "You're Okafor's girl. The mapmaker."

"Apprentice," she said. "He's dead."

"They say he wouldn't give up his office. That he stood in the doorway."

"He stood in the doorway," Kestrel said, and the words cost more than she expected, and she hated that he could hear it. She set down her pen. "They also say you signed the requisition orders. All of them. Your name is in my master's records eleven times."

In the straw, silence. Then, quietly, the sound of a man laughing at himself with no pleasure in it at all. "Eleven. He counted."

"He counted everything. That was the whole of him." She rose and crossed to the edge of the lamplight, keeping the jars between them, an old instinct she did not examine. "So tell me why a man signs eleven orders and then takes a bullet leaving the army that gave him the pen."

For a long time he looked at the underside of the cellar ceiling, at the roots threading through the stone, as if the answer were written there in some script he was learning to read. When he spoke, it was in the voice of a man reading aloud a document he wished he'd never executed.

"The first order, I read every word. The second, most of them. By the eleventh I had learned to sign where the colonel tapped." He turned his head; the ice-colored eyes found her across the dark. "There was a woman in Dolein — you'll know the name, your book will have it — Maren Dolein? She stood in her own dooryard with a ledger of her own, accounts for the cidery, three generations of figures, and she asked me, politely, whether I wanted her to sign over the trees or the years the trees had given. I told her it didn't matter, the army required the land. And she signed. And that night I understood that I had become a kind of instrument that makes men sign things, and instruments get used on anyone, including eventually the men holding them. I started copying the orders. For myself. Evidence, I thought, though I didn't know evidence for whom."

Kestrel went very still.

"You copied them," she repeated.

"Some. Before Verrec locked the archive." He pushed himself up on one elbow, wincing, and now there was something new in his face — not hope exactly, but the animal alertness of a creature smelling a possible shape to its survival. "Why are you asking?"

"Show me your copies," she said, "and we'll discuss what you're worth to me."

* * *

*Ilan Sere had been interrogated twice in his life — once by a captain testing him before commissioning, once by a farmer's wife in Dolein whose questions were harder — and he recognized the technique now, though the girl was barely more than a child. She did not press. She priced.*

*She had brought him water and a heel of black bread and a strip of linen boiled clean, and she had changed his dressing herself with hands that smelled of iron gall ink, and all the while she had asked nothing further. She simply worked at her tracing table in the corner — an actual table, hauled down here somehow, a lamp, pens, a board — turning the names of the disappeared into documents, and let him watch. It was, he understood slowly, a demonstration. This is what I am building. Consider whether you want to be inside it or outside it.*

*His copies were sewn into the lining of his coat, fourteen flimsy sheets of requisition duplicates, and when he finally drew them out and handed them across the lamplight, her face did not change. But her hands, taking them, trembled once — a single shudder, like a plucked string — and he thought:* she knows what she is holding. She has known since the moment she saw my face, and she let me bleed and sleep and talk anyway, because she is deciding, and she decides like an engineer.*

*"These corroborate the ledger," she said at last. "Signatures, dates, countersignatures. My master transcribed; you duplicated. Between them, no tribunal in the world can call it forgery." She looked up. "But documents in a cellar save no one. They have to cross the river. Three checkpoints between here and the negotiators' camp, and I cannot pass the second or the third alone, because I don't know the protocols — the countersigns, the rotation of the guards, which sergeant drinks, which searches thoroughly. You know all of it. You served at these crossings."*

*"I did," he said. "Before they transferred me to the interior. I know Brannoc at the bridge — he'll wave through survey work without a glance, he hates paperwork more than he hates smugglers. The orchard line is harder; that's a captain's post, and the captain counts barrels personally. The third—" He stopped.*

*"The third," she prompted.*

*"The third is the negotiators' gate itself. Nobody passes that by trickery. Only by invitation, arrest, or testimony."*

*She nodded slowly, filing it away, and he watched her decide — watched the decision move through her like weather. Then she set his duplicate papers on top of her tracings, squared their edges together until they were one packet, and said:*

*"Here is our arrangement. You know the checkpoints; I need them. I have food, linen, a hiding place, and a reason to keep you breathing; you need all four. We exchange nothing else. Not forgiveness, not trust, not stories. When the pages are across, we part, and neither of us owes the other anything but silence."*

*It was a cold bargain, and he admired it, and he understood perfectly what she was doing: she was refusing to make him a friend, because friends must be grieved, and she had buried enough. So he answered in kind, matching her terms like a soldier countersigning.*

*"Agreed," he said. "One amendment." He nodded at the packet, at the two hands' work lying edge to edge. "When this reaches whoever it's meant to reach — my name goes in with yours. Not for pardon. For the record. I signed the orders; I'll sign against them too. A man should be legible on both sides of the page."*

*Above them, muffled by earth and stone, boots crossed the orchard lane — a patrol, unhurried, passing. Neither moved. The lamp flame bent and straightened. And Kestrel Okafor looked at the lieutenant who had unmade her world and was now offering to witness its unmaking, and she thought of her master's rule, the first one, chalked above the drafting table where the soldiers had surely scrubbed it away by now:*

*A map is a promise made to people who cannot speak.*

*"Both names," she said. "Done." She rolled the combined packet, slid it into the oilcloth, and reached for the chipped jar. "Now tell me everything about Brannoc. We move the first pages tomorrow night, and I intend to walk across that bridge as if I own the survey — because after tomorrow, I may."*
