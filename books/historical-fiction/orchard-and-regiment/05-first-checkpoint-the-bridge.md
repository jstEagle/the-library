## 5. First Checkpoint: The Bridge

The fog came off the river before dawn, the way it always did in the cold months, rising from the water like breath from a sleeping animal. Kestrel stood at the edge of the mill road with the surveyor's satchel slung across her chest and felt every stitch of it against her ribs — six tracings sewn into the canvas lining, each one a page of names, each one a family pulled out of an orchard by a colonel's pen. The thread was Ilan's idea. He had done the sewing himself, his hands surprisingly gentle for a man who had signed orders that emptied villages, and when he finished he had held the satchel up to the lantern light and said, *Now you carry them against your heart, which is either poetry or poor planning.*

She had not laughed. She was not sure she would ever laugh again, or if laughter, once the war ended, would come back to her like a migratory bird, uncertain of its old nesting places.

The bridge was half a mile downstream, where the mill road crossed the Vell in two stone spans joined at a mid-river island. The army had held it since the second year of the occupation, first with timber barricades, later with a proper checkpoint house and a chain across the roadway that they raised for carts and lowered for everyone else. Beyond it lay the marsh road, and beyond that, folded into the hills where no soldier bothered to look, the abandoned lime kiln where she had cached the atlas's copper plates and the first of her copies. Six pages today. Forty-one names. It was not much. It was everything she had.

Ilan was already gone. He had left before moonset, moving through the orchard rows with the flat, economical walk of a man who had spent three years learning to be invisible inside his own army, down toward the old weir below the bridge. There he would do whatever he had promised to do — loosen something, break something, make the river misbehave — and the garrison's attention would slide off the bridge like water off oiled leather. She did not know exactly what he intended. She had decided, in the small hours, that not knowing was its own kind of mercy.

*You will be seen,* he had told her last night in the cold cellar, his voice low under the smell of apples rotting sweetly into cider-darkness. *That is the thing you have to accept. You cannot smuggle yourself past a man who knew your master. You can only give him a version of you that is true enough to pass.* He had turned the satchel over in his hands. *Sergeant Brannoc. Third company. He drank with your master once, I think — I remember seeing them at the Ferryman's Arms before the requisitions started. Old men of the same trade, surveyors and soldiers, arguing about gradients.* A pause. *He liked him. That may help you. It may also mean he looks at you too long.*

*And if he looks too long?*

*Then you let him. People trust what they recognize.*

Now, in the grey light, Kestrel walked the mill road and rehearsed. She was Kestrel Okafor, journeyman surveyor, conscripted to the army's boundary work, carrying corrected tracings to the engineering annex on the far bank because Colonel Verrec wanted the supply-route revisions copied before week's end. Every word of it was true except the truth underneath it, which was that the tracings in her satchel were not Verrec's boundaries at all but her own quiet counter-map, drawn by lamplight, line for line, name for name. She carried a lie made entirely of true things. She wondered if that made it easier or harder to tell.

The river grew louder. Through thinning fog the bridge appeared, black stone slicked silver, and beside it the checkpoint house with its shuttered lamp and the chain lying slack across the road. Two soldiers stood beneath the awning, one young and stamping his feet against the cold, the other — the other was Brannoc, exactly as Ilan had described him: heavy through the shoulders, grey in the stubble, a man built like a bridge pier himself, weathered and patient and impossible to move.

Kestrel's mouth went dry. She made herself keep walking. The trick, she understood suddenly, was not to slow down as she approached but to arrive — to come up the road at the pace of a woman with errands, annoyed at the cold, expecting to be waved along. Fear showed itself in hesitation. She had watched her master cross a hundred borders of one kind or another, literal and official, and he had always said the same thing: *A map is a story told with confidence. So is a face.*

"Sergeant," she said, when she was still four paces out, pitching her voice above the river. "Morning. Boundary work — I'm expected at the annex."

Brannoc turned. His eyes were pale, almost colourless, and they went to her face first and then, deliberately, to the satchel. "Okafor's girl," he said. Not a question.

"Kestrel."

"I know who you are." He said it without warmth and without cruelty, the way a man says something he has simply decided is true. "Your master drew the flood surveys for the Vell mills, winter before the war. I sat for him twice while he fixed my position." Something moved behind the pale eyes — not quite a smile. "He made me taller than I am. Said a map should flatter only the landscape, never the man, but he broke his rule for me."

"He spoke of you," Kestrel said, and it was even true; her master had kept a running catalogue of the people whose likenesses or landmarks appeared in his plates, and Brannoc was there in the margins of the flood survey, noted as *cooperative, drinks, knows the weir better than the river does*. "He'd have been glad of the memory."

Something in the sergeant's posture shifted — a degree, no more. "Open the satchel."

Her hands were steady. She had made them steady through sheer administrative will, thinking of them as instruments, as tools belonging to the craft rather than to the frightened girl inside it. She unbuckled the flap and lifted out the top layer: blank drafting paper, a scale ruler, a stub of red pencil, the army-stamped requisition chit Verrec's clerk had given her, and beneath those, visible but unremarkable, the tracing sheets themselves — and here she had gambled, because the tracings looked like what they pretended to be, boundary lines in army ink with orchard parcels numbered and annotated, and only a man who knew the ledger's existence could read the fine columns of names stitched along their margins in her smallest hand. To Brannoc they would be exactly what they seemed: boring, official, Verrec's business.

He looked. He looked longer than she liked. Downstream, faintly, there came a sound like a door being opened in the river — a deep wooden groan, then a rushing, then shouting — and both soldiers' heads turned toward the weir, where the fog was now shaking with noise. The young one swore and started for the railing. Somewhere below, voices multiplied. A whistle blew, twice, three times.

"That'll be the weir gate," Brannoc said slowly. He did not turn away from her. "Gone again, has it?"

"Third time this month," Kestrel said. "My master used to say the Vell resents being surveyed."

That earned her the smile he had withheld. It aged his whole face kindly. "Go on, then, Kestrel Okafor. Tell the annex the crossing's compromised and they'll want the east span figures rechecked." He stepped back and lifted the chain himself, holding it high so she could duck under. Then, as she passed, quieter, under the river's roar: "Whatever you're carrying — and I'm not asking — your master taught me that a good map never lies about the ground. Only about the mapmaker." He looked at her once more, pale eyes steady. "Mind which one you are."

She walked across the bridge. Her legs carried her; she was not entirely sure how. The planks of the eastern span rang under her boots, and behind her the shouting at the weir swelled and scattered, and the fog closed around her shoulders like a coat someone else had left behind.

* * *

On the far bank the world changed character. The marsh road ran straight and empty between drainage ditches, and the hills took shape above it, and the lime kiln waited in its fold of gorse like a held breath. Kestrel walked fast, then faster, then ran, satchel banging against her hip, until she reached the kiln and crouched inside its cold brick throat and unwrapped the lining and counted — six sheets, intact, dry, the ink unfed by damp. Forty-one names delivered out of the occupied town. Her hands began to shake only now, in safety, which struck her as a betrayal of some kind.

She hid the sheets in the cavity behind the third course of bricks, replaced the loose stone, and sat back on her heels to wait for Ilan, and it was there, in the silence, that the vertigo found her.

It did not feel like triumph. She had imagined triumph — had needed to imagine it, these past nights, to get herself out of bed. What arrived instead was exposure, vast and cold. Brannoc had recognized her. Brannoc had looked too long. Brannoc had said *whatever you're carrying, and I'm not asking*, which meant he knew there was something to not-ask about, which meant that somewhere in his soldier's head a small flag had been planted, and soldiers reported flags. That was their entire profession. And he had known her master, had drunk with him, had liked him — and none of that had stopped him from opening the satchel, and none of it would stop him, she understood with sickening clarity, from mentioning her name in the mess tent tonight, casually, over his cup: *the Okafor girl crossed yesterday with survey tracings, right in the middle of the weir trouble, funny timing that.* Funny timing. Verrec did not need evidence to grow suspicious. He needed only a pattern, and patterns were made of exactly such small remarks.

She had survived by being seen. That was the price Ilan had warned her of, and she had paid it without feeling the coin leave her hand.

By mid-morning the river noise had died and the fog burned away, and Ilan came up the slope from the direction of the weir, wet to the waist, breathing hard, with a splinter of pale wood caught in his hair. He looked older in daylight. Desertion had hollowed him and the cellar had softened him and the two processes had reached an uneasy truce in his face.

"The sluice gate hinge," he said, dropping to sit beside her, wringing river from his sleeve. "Rotted through years ago. All I did was encourage it. They'll lose the rest of today fishing the gate out and blaming each other." He glanced at her. "Did you pass?"

"Yes."

"You don't sound pleased."

"He knew me. He knew my master. He let me go, and he *looked*, Ilan, and men who look report." She heard her own voice climb and forced it level. "By tonight Verrec will know I crossed the bridge during a diversion I had no reason to know about. He'll do the arithmetic. He was an engineer before he was a butcher — arithmetic is the one cruelty he's fluent in."

Ilan was quiet for a moment, watching the hills. "Then we don't use the bridge again," he said finally. "There's the ford above the tannery, and there's a fisherman's path on the north bank the patrols stopped walking after the ambush last spring. Slower. Ugly. Alive." He turned to look at her directly, and there was something new in his expression, something that might have been respect wearing fear's clothing. "One crossing done. Thirty-four pages to go."

But Kestrel was barely listening. Below them, on the far bank, the mill road lay bright and empty in the morning sun — and along it, moving fast, rode a single horseman in colonel's grey, away from the town, toward the annex. Toward the east span. Toward, perhaps, nothing at all.

She watched him until the road bent and took him.

"We need to move tonight," she heard herself say. "Before he finishes his arithmetic."
