## 6. Orchards as Evidence

The summons came at the hour when the light turned to honey and the orchards below the office windows went gold in their rows, as if the trees were trying to be beautiful for someone who might still be watching.

"Colonel Verrec requests you," said the corporal, not looking at her. He had learned in four days of guarding her that she did not answer to "mapmaker" or "girl" or "apprentice," only to nothing at all, and so he had settled on silence as the most efficient form of address. "Bring your instruments."

Kestrel closed the plate she had been working on — a supply route, inked under guard, every contour of it a small betrayal — and gathered her pens, her ruling board, her magnifier with its cracked brass rim that had been Master Aldous's before it was hers. The corporal watched her pack each tool into its loop in the leather roll. She wondered if he was cataloguing them, whether somewhere in the requisition ledger there was now an entry for *one drafting set, one magnifier, condition: good*, filed beside the names of families who no longer owned anything at all.

*One hundred and forty-one households,* the ledger said, or had said, before Verrec locked it in his own quarters. *Four thousand souls displaced. Orchards requisitioned for the provisioning of the Third Army.* She had copied the first page onto tracing paper by candlelight two nights ago, her hand steady in a way that frightened her more than trembling would have. Steadiness meant she had already decided something.

She followed the corporal down the stairs, past the survey office's front room where the army's clerks now sat at her master's desks, through the square where soldiers were loading early-harvested apples into wagons stamped with the Third Army's crest, and toward the garrison house that had been the mayor's home in another life.

Verrec received her in what had been the mayor's study. He was alone, which surprised her; she had expected adjutants, witnesses, the machinery of interrogation. Instead there was only the colonel standing at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, a man built like a door — broad, flat, closed — wearing his fifty-odd years the way he wore his uniform, without a single unnecessary fold.

"Apprentice Okafor." He did not turn. "You knew Aldous Vey for how long?"

"Eleven years." Her voice came out level. Good. Let it stay level.

"He was the finest cartographer in the province." Now Verrec turned, and his face held something she had not prepared for: not menace but a kind of tired respect, which was worse, because respect could be leveraged and menace could only be endured. "I told him so once. At the survey conference in Corvane, eight years ago. He didn't remember me. Junior staff officer, standing in the back while the great men argued about river basins." A thin smile. "He signed my commission map. I've kept it all these years. Did you know that?"

"I didn't."

"No. Why would you. You were a child learning to grind pigment." He gestured to the table at the center of the room, and Kestrel saw what lay upon it, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her composure.

A map. A finished, handsome, official-looking map, drawn on good stock, bordered and titled in a fair imitation of the survey office's house style: *Parish of Saint-Ebrin and Environs, Corrected Survey, Year Nine of the War.*

There was no parish of Saint-Ebrin anymore. There had been a village of that name upriver, three hundred people, a church, terraced orchards on the north slope. Last winter a company of Verrec's Third Army had burned it after partisans cut a supply column nearby, and the burning had gone wrong, or had gone exactly as intended depending on who was asked, and there was no village now, only foundations and char and, in the spring, weeds growing up through the doorways. Kestrel had mapped its absence herself, months ago, in the atlas — a blank space where the parish should be, left blank deliberately, because Master Aldous had taught her that a blank could be an argument too. *When they erase a place,* he had told her, tapping the empty rectangle, *you draw the erasure. Make them look at the hole.*

But this map on Verrec's table did not show a hole. It showed a road running clean and unbroken through country marked as open pasture, and where the village should have been, the map simply continued — fields, fences, a ford over the river, all rendered with competent, anonymous skill. As though Saint-Ebrin had never existed. As though three hundred people had been subtracted from the world before anyone thought to write them down.

"You recognize the work?" Verrec asked.

"It's well done," Kestrel said carefully. "Whoever drew it knows our conventions."

"Our conventions." He let the word sit. "It was drawn by Captain Marest, who trained under Vey's system, as half the provincial surveyors did. And it has one flaw. Can you find it?"

Kestrel bent over the map. She understood, with the cold clarity that had been settling over her for days like frost forming on glass, that this was a test, and that the test was not about finding flaws. She examined the linework anyway, because refusing would tell him more than failing. The roads matched the army's corrected surveys. The river ran true. The orchard symbols — little crowned circles, the convention her master had invented — dotted the slopes in plausible numbers. And then she saw it, near the ford: a fence line that terminated in midair, ending against nothing, because the fence it had joined had belonged to a farmstead that the forger had erased along with everything else. A line with nowhere to go. A stitch dropped in the fabric.

"The boundary fence," she said. "North of the ford. It stops without connecting. Whoever drew it removed the holding it attached to and forgot the fence remembered."

Verrec's smile deepened by a degree. "Marest's work. Yes." He walked to the table. "Here is my problem, Apprentice Okafor. In six days, maybe seven, men in better coats than mine will sit in a tent in the garrison town and divide this province like a pie. There will be questions about requisitions. There may even be questions about Saint-Ebrin, because questions are cheap for men who never have to answer them." He laid one finger on the false map, gently, the way one touches a wound. "This map says Saint-Ebrin was never surveyed because it never existed. No parish record, no requisition order, no massacre. Only pasture. Do you understand what a document like this is worth?"

Kestrel looked at the fence line trailing off into nothing, and behind it, unbidden, she saw faces: the cooper's family from Mill Row, the widow Tarn with her two daughters, old Sef who had grafted the apple varieties everyone in the valley stole cuttings from. Names in a ledger locked in this man's quarters. Names she had begun copying by candlelight onto paper thin enough to hide in a hem.

"It's worth whatever you say it's worth," she said. "You're the one holding it."

"And I'm asking you to say it's genuine."

There it was. Laid out between them like a card turned face-up.

"You want me to authenticate a forgery," Kestrel said.

"I want you to certify a correction." Verrec's voice stayed mild, almost kind, which was how she imagined he sounded when ordering burnings. "The war is ending. What matters now is not what happened but what can be proven. If the armistice commission receives a certified survey confirming that Saint-Ebrin appears on no legitimate record, then the question closes itself. No evidence, no inquiry, no executions. Some might call that mercy. The dead don't care about inquiries, Apprentice Okafor. But living men will hang if the commission decides to make examples." He paused. "Your master is dead protecting his papers. I'd rather you weren't added to the list. Certify the map, and your name stays clean, and when this province settles, there will be a place for a surveyor of Vey's school. A good place."

Kestrel looked down at the forged map, at the confident little orchard crowns dotting slopes where ash and weeds now stood, and she felt something turn over inside her — not fear, though fear was there, familiar as her own pulse, but something colder and more awake beneath it.

Because she saw the flaw again. Not just Marest's dropped stitch — her own opportunity, folded inside it.

A certified map. Certified by *her*, the last hand of Vey's school, the apprentice whose authentication the commission would have no reason to doubt and every reason to trust. If she certified this lie, her seal would lend it the weight of everything her master had built, eleven years of training distilled into a stamp of approval. That was what Verrec was buying with his offer of a clean name: not her compliance but her inheritance, spent against the truth.

And if forgery could erase a village —

Then forgery, turned in the other direction, could raise one.

The army's own surveys lay stacked in the commandeered office downstairs. Corrected routes, requisition boundaries, supply schedules — documents bearing the Third Army's own authority, which no commission would dare dismiss as rebel propaganda. If those surveys were *amended* — if the requisition lines were redrawn to match the ledger's record, if the dispossessed holdings reappeared on army paper in army style, dated and initialed by army hands — then Kestrel Okafor, certified authenticator of the Vey school, could forge the truth itself into the occupier's own archive. The ledger's names, written back onto the map. Evidence no one could burn without burning their own testimony.

Her master's voice, in memory: *A map doesn't show the world, Kestrel. It shows what the world is allowed to be. Choose carefully whose allowance you hold.*

"Yes," she heard herself say. "I'll examine it properly tonight. If I'm to certify corrections, I do the full work — compare against the office surveys, verify the linework, annotate the discrepancies. My seal means the examination was thorough. Anything less and it's worthless to you."

Verrec studied her for a long moment. Whatever he searched for, she made sure her face offered him only the diligence of a good apprentice: careful, obedient, grieving, harmless.

"Naturally," he said. "Thoroughness is why I asked for you." He slid the forged map across the table toward her. "Take it. Report tomorrow evening." Then, as she reached the door: "Apprentice Okafor. Your master once told me — at Corvane, when I finally worked up the nerve to speak to him — that a mapmaker's loyalty is to the territory, not to the tenant. I've thought about that sentence for eight years." His eyes were unreadable. "Think about what your territory is."

She carried the false map back across the square in the failing light, past the wagons, past the soldiers, past the orchards going gold and then gray as the sun went down, and she climbed the stairs to the guarded office, and she set the forgery on her master's old table beside the lamp, and she waited until the corporal's footsteps settled into their drowsy rhythm before she pulled the army's corrected surveys toward her and opened them flat.

The requisition boundary of Mill Row ran across the first sheet in red officiant's ink, neat as a knife cut. Kestrel dipped her finest pen. Her hand did not tremble.

Outside, the first stars came out over orchards full of stolen fruit, and somewhere beyond the checkpoints Ilan Sere waited in the cold cellar among the cached tracings, and somewhere in the garrison town negotiators were packing their tents for a signature that would decide everything. Six days, Verrec had said. Maybe seven.

She began, very carefully, to redraw the world.

* * *

By the third hour she had amended four parcels and learned the rhythm of the work: copy the clerk's hand, age the ink with steam from the kettle, match the paper's wear by rubbing it lightly with the bone folder until the new lines looked lived-in. Forgery, it turned out, was mostly patience. Patience and the willingness to look directly at what everyone else had agreed not to see.

She was sealing the fourth amendment under a loose flap of the survey binding when the corporal shifted in the corridor outside, and Kestrel blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with her heart going hard, listening to the building breathe around her — the creak of the stairs, the wind in the orchards, the far-off sound of the garrison town's clocks striking midnight, one hour closer to the tent, one hour closer to the burning she could feel coming the way animals feel weather.

In the darkness, her fingers found the edge of the hidden tracing-paper copies sewn into her sleeve, four pages of names riding against her wrist like a second pulse.

Tomorrow she would certify Verrec's lie. And underneath the lie, layer by layer, night by night, she would build the truth into the army's own maps until the whole edifice of his record rested on her forgery — until the man who kept the ledger as leverage discovered, too late, that the leverage had changed hands.

She struck the match and relit the lamp, and went back to work.
