## 7. Second Checkpoint: The Orchard Line

The barrels came up from the cold cellar at moonset, when the orchard lay in that blue half-dark where even the sentries on the ridge could not say what they were seeing. Kestrel had chosen the hour the way her master had taught her to choose a scale — not by what was convenient, but by what the truth of the terrain demanded. The truth, on this night, was that the army's own doctored survey showed the Vasseur orchard as requisitioned stock, its cider already entered into garrison stores. The lie she had drawn with Verrec's own ink now had to carry the weight it was built for.

Twelve barrels. Eleven held cider, pressed early and sour from the frightened harvest. The twelfth held paper.

She and Ilan worked without speaking much. He rolled each ledger page around a wooden dowel — he had made the dowels himself from a broken broom handle, whittling in the cellar dark with a knife he kept sharper than any deserter ought to need — and slid them into waxed cylinders that had once held lamp oil. Then the cylinders went down into the barrel among the cider dregs, wedged upright between the staves so that even if a soldier pulled the bung and looked inside, he would see nothing but dark liquid and apple sediment. Only if he emptied a barrel entirely, only if he lifted out eleven soaked and swollen rolls of tracing paper, would he find anything.

"He won't," Ilan said, as though answering a question she hadn't asked. "Sergeants don't empty barrels. Sergeants weigh them."

"Then we make sure this one weighs right."

"I know." He set the dowel aside and flexed his wounded arm, testing it. The bandage beneath his coat had been changed two days ago; the flesh beneath it was healing ugly, pulling at him whenever he reached. "Kestrel." His voice dropped into the register he saved for things he did not want to repeat. "If they open the twelfth barrel — you walk away. You let them take me and you keep walking. That's the whole arrangement."

"It isn't the whole arrangement."

"It is tonight." He tapped the barrel head twice, the way a man knocks on a door he hopes no one answers. "You're the heir. I'm the debt. Debts are meant to be paid."

She wanted to argue and did not, because arguing would mean admitting how much the words had gone into her like splinters, working deeper every day. Instead she hammered the bung home and dragged sacking over the barrel's shoulders, and wrote on it in chalk, in the neat surveyor's hand Verrec himself had praised: *Requisition 114-C. Vasseur holding. For garrison stores. Authorized: Col. A. Verrec.*

Forgery, she had learned, cut both ways. She was beginning to understand just how far both ways went.

* * *

They loaded the barrels onto a farmer's flat cart before dawn — old Vasseur himself driving, hands shaking too badly to be trusted with anything but the reins, which was why Kestrel sat beside him and Ilan walked behind as a hired laborer, collar up, face angled away from the road. The cart creaked toward the second checkpoint through an orchard country gone strange with war: trees stripped of their fruit weeks early, rows of pickers' ladders leaning against nothing, the grass silvered with frost that no one would turn into cider anyone drank.

The Orchard Line checkpoint was not a wall but a gap — a lane cut through the hedgerows wide enough for carts, flanked by timber gates and a guard hut, and beyond it the long slope down to the river valley where the garrison town smoked its morning smoke. Two soldiers stood at the gate. A corporal checked papers under a lantern that still burned against the gray.

Kestrel had rehearsed the moment until it had worn smooth as a stone. She handed down the authorization sheet, the doctored survey folded beside it, and watched the corporal's eyes move across Verrec's signature, then across her own endorsement beneath it.

"Survey office confirms the transfer?" he asked.

"The colonel's office does," she said. "The survey office drew the boundaries. If your stores ledger disagrees with mine, corporal, one of us is wrong, and I'd wager it isn't the map."

It was a riskier voice than she usually allowed herself — the master's voice, the one that assumed authority because maps assumed authority — but the corporal was young and cold and wanted his shift to end. He waved the first barrels through. Then the second. Then he yawned, stamped the sheet, and turned to wave Vasseur's cart onward —

"Corporal." The second soldier had come around behind the cart, lantern raised. "This one. Turn around."

Ilan turned.

Kestrel saw it happen in the space of a breath: the lantern light crossing the deserter's face, the soldier's expression going from boredom to recognition to something harder — the particular hardness of a man who has found a piece of the war he thought was lost. She knew that face on the soldier too, suddenly. He had stood guard outside the survey office for a week last month. He had seen Ilan Sere there, in a lieutenant's coat, carrying requisition orders with Verrec's countersignature.

"Deserter," the soldier said. Not loudly. Loudly would have required explaining himself. Quietly was worse. "Lieutenant Sere. Or whatever you're calling yourself now."

The frost silence stretched. Down the slope, the first three barrels were already rolling toward the valley, irretrievable, invisible, safe. Four more waited on the cart. Five remained stacked at the gate.

Kestrel's mind ran the arithmetic she hated: nine barrels past or nearly past, three exposed, one man exposed with them. If Ilan ran, they would chase him through the orchard lines and search everything on the road while they did it. If he denied it, the soldier would call the sergeant, and the sergeant would call the garrison, and by noon every barrel within a mile would be standing on its head.

Ilan did none of these things. He straightened, and she watched him put his shoulders back the way a man squares himself before a firing squad or a photograph — deliberately, formally, arranging himself for the record.

"Lieutenant Sere, yes," he said clearly. "Returned. I was making my way in to surrender myself. This goodwoman and the farmer found me on the road and gave me work in exchange for bread. They knew nothing else." He looked at Kestrel once, briefly, and in that look was the entire remainder of the bargain, signed and sealed. "You can check the barrels if you want, corporal. Cider. All twelve of them. But the woman's papers are correct, and my name doesn't change what's in them."

The corporal hesitated, glancing between them, smelling trouble he didn't want to own. The soldier who had recognized Ilan grabbed his arm. "He's mine to report. Get the cart moving or hold it here till the sergeant comes — either way, this isn't your problem."

"Move it," the corporal said to Kestrel, already turning away, already washing his hands of the whole affair. "Papers are in order. Go."

Vasseur needed her hand on his sleeve to keep him from speaking. She took the reins herself and drove the cart through the gate at a pace that was almost calm, past the guard hut, down the slope, while behind her the voices rose — the soldier's triumph, the corporal's irritation, and once, faintly, Ilan's own voice, steady as a level line drawn across falling ground: *Take it up with the garrison provost. I'll wait.*

She did not look back. Looking back would have cost her the face she wore, and the face was all she had left to spend.

* * *

They delivered the barrels to the garrison stores by midmorning, and by noon the last page of the ledger of displaced names lay hidden in the survey office's own archive, filed under its own forged authorization, inside the machine built to erase it. Kestrel signed the receipt with a steady hand and felt nothing steady underneath it.

Guilt, she discovered, was not the sharp thing she had expected. It was granular, like the sediment in the barrels — settling slowly through everything she did, flavoring it. Every time she dipped her pen that afternoon, drawing the army's lies in clean confident strokes, she heard *I'll wait.* Every time she ate, she thought of bread given in exchange for work, and work given in exchange for betrayal dressed as sacrifice. He had spent himself to buy the pages passage, and the pages had passed, and he was sitting somewhere in a stockade cell waiting to learn whether his purchase would ever be worth what it cost.

That evening Verrec summoned her. She went with the resignation of someone who has stopped being surprised by summons.

The colonel stood at his window looking down at the garrison yard, where new tents had bloomed among the drill grounds — white tents, larger than soldiers' canvas, with pennons she did not recognize. Negotiators' colors. He did not turn when she entered.

"You've heard the rumors," he said.

"Rumors are below my pay."

"Your pay is my discretion, so mind your tone." But there was no force in it. He sounded tired in a way that frightened her more than anger would have — the tiredness of a man doing sums he cannot make come out. "There will be a signing. Soon. Days, perhaps hours. And there are men arriving with it who will want accounts of everything done in this district." Now he turned, and his eyes went to the shelf where the ledger of names sat exactly where it had sat since the confiscation, spine uncracked, dust undisturbed — the most dangerous object in the province, disguised as an ignored one. "Some men burn their accounts, Okafor. Some men carry them. I haven't decided which I am yet."

"And which would you prefer to be?"

"That depends," Verrec said quietly, "entirely on how the next few days go." He dismissed her with a flick of two fingers, and as she reached the door he added, without warmth, without menace, almost gently: "Tell your friend in the stockade that surrendering was the cleverest thing he never planned to do. Provosts process deserters slowly. Slowly may serve him. Or it may not. We shall all find out together."

Kestrel walked back through the garrison streets under a sky going the color of old vellum, and understood with terrible clarity that the deadline had arrived without announcing itself. The armistice was coming. The burning, if it came, would come the night before. And the man who had spent himself for her evidence was locked in a cell whose clock now ran on the same fuse as hers.

She had nine days' work left to finish, and perhaps two nights to do it in, and one name she owed that no atlas could repay.

She began walking toward the stockade.
