## 8. Armistice Weather

The negotiators arrived on a morning of strange light, the kind that made everything look already remembered. Kestrel stood at the survey office window and watched their column come down the valley road — not soldiers, or not only, but men in grey coats with white armbands and lacquered cases chained to their wrists, the way other men carried relics or bombs. Behind them came clerks, orderlies, a wagon of folding tables and chairs, as if peace were furniture to be assembled on arrival.

The town watched from doorways and orchard rows. Nobody cheered. The war had taught this valley that processions meant taking, whatever they carried.

"They're quartering at the garrison," Corporal Dain said behind her. He was her shadow now, Verrec's leash on her hands. "Signature's set for the day after tomorrow. Ten o'clock."

"Ten o'clock," Kestrel repeated. She had learned to hold information the way she held ink — lightly, on the surface, letting it dry where it fell.

She thought of the pages in the cold cellar beneath the Harrow orchard: nine tracings sewn into sacking, the bulk of the ledger already past two checkpoints and cached like seed potatoes in the dark, waiting for spring that might never be allowed to come. And Ilan — four days in the garrison stockade now, his name entered in a detention book she could not reach. Four days of silence. She had counted them the way you count a fever.

* * *

The rumors arrived faster than the negotiators' baggage.

By noon the market had three versions of the armistice and by evening it had nine. A cooper's wife swore her cousin had heard the terms read aloud: borders fixed, garrisons withdrawn, prisoners exchanged. An old drover said no, the signing was only a cessation — the accounting would come later, years later, commissions sitting in distant capitals weighing orchards against artillery losses until everyone who remembered the trees was dead. A boy selling chestnuts insisted he'd seen the annex pages themselves, blank ones, waiting for names that would never be written in.

Kestrel listened to all of it from behind the survey office counter, drawing supply routes for an army that might be gone within the week, her pen steady while her thoughts ran the checkpoints again and again like water looking for a crack in stone.

Because here was the arithmetic that frightened her: if the armistice contained no accounting of requisitions, then the ledger was worthless. Paper about displaced families, signed orders, confiscated orchards — none of it mattered once the ink dried on a peace that declined to mention them. The negotiators would fold their tables and go home, and Colonel Verrec would keep the ledger in some drawer or burn it in some stove, and either way the names would stop being evidence and become only memory, and memory did not survive occupation. She knew that. Her master had known that. It was why he'd written every name in iron-gall ink, the kind that bites into paper and stays.

But the other arithmetic was worse. If the armistice *did* include an accounting — if some clerk in a grey coat asked for proof of requisitions — then the ledger was the only thing in the valley that mattered, and the man who had ordered the requisitions could not allow it to exist.

Either way, the ledger's life was measured now in days. Perhaps hours.

She felt it before she confirmed it, the way cartographers feel a coastline turning before the soundings prove it. Dread sharpening into something with edges. A deadline.

* * *

She learned the truth on the second evening, from the least likely of sources: the laundress.

Maren came twice a week to the commandeered office with clean linen for the officers' quarters upstairs, and she had the gift common to women who wash other people's sheets — she saw everything and was seen by no one. She folded pillowcases in the corner of Kestrel's workroom and talked in a low stream about the garrison, and Kestrel had learned to listen without appearing to.

"Burning detail," Maren said, smoothing a pillowcase along its seam. "Tomorrow night. They've requisitioned the foundry yard — braziers, oil, a file sergeant to witness destruction of documents. Official business." Her hands never stopped moving. "There's a list, dearie. Everything goes. Correspondence, duplicate maps, old ration ledgers." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And one item entered without a number. Just 'miscellaneous records, seized.' Your master's handwriting is on the cover, I'm told. Someone described it."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed in the square.

"When?" Kestrel asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"Dusk. Before the signing, so there's nothing left to argue over when the grey coats ask their questions." Maren tucked the last towel under her arm. "They say the colonel gave the order himself. Personally."

She left. Kestrel sat with her pen hovering above a half-finished boundary line until the ink pooled and blotched, and she understood that she was watching the last hours of her master's nine years of work, and of Ilan's confession, and of every family that had ever trusted a mapmaker to remember them.

Dusk tomorrow. She had one day, and one night, and a garrison full of men between her and the fire.

* * *

Colonel Verrec could not sleep, which was unusual, because Verrec prided himself on sleeping whenever the opportunity offered, the way other men drank.

He sat in his quarters above the survey office with a single lamp and the ledger open in front of him, and he hated it, and he could not stop reading it.

It was good work. That was the obscenity of it. He had recognized the quality the first day, when he conscripted the girl — the precision of the hand, the economy, the way each entry carried not just a name but a holding, an acreage, a date, a disposition. Nine years of a dead man's discipline. You could run a campaign off this book. In another life, in another army, Verrec would have been proud to sign it.

He had signed things like it. That was the problem.

His superiors had come through the garrison that afternoon — two staff officers and a legal man with soft hands, the advance party of surrender. They had been polite the way surgeons are polite. They had explained, over brandy he paid for himself, that questions would be asked after the signature. Requisitions. Dispositions. The village of Saint-Aubin, whose name had begun appearing in dispatches from the other side. They had explained that certain documentation would be best handled locally, before any commission could form an attachment to it. They had not said *burn it*. Men like these never said anything. They arranged conditions and let lesser men draw conclusions.

Verrec had drawn his conclusion in the space of a poured drink: destroy the ledger, and he was a colonel who had followed orders in an ugly war, pensionable, possibly even promotable in whatever army survived. Preserve it, and it became leverage — but leverage only functioned if he lived long enough to spend it, and the legal man's soft hands had told him exactly how long that might be.

So: the burning. Dusk tomorrow. He had written the order himself, in his own hand, because he wanted no clerk's signature near it.

And yet he sat with the thing open under his lamp like a man visiting a grave.

He turned to the final page. Signatures — requisitioning officers, countersignatures, dates marching back through the war like footprints in snow. His own name appeared eleven times. Ilan Sere's name appeared beside several of them, small and early, a junior officer's obedient scrawl. The boy had signed before he understood what he was signing; Verrec had signed long after. He wondered, sometimes, which was the greater sin, and decided it was a question for men with time.

The girl, though. He thought about the girl more than was comfortable. Okafor. She drew his supply routes with perfect compliance and looked at him with eyes that took measurements. He had interrogated enough prisoners to know what those eyes were doing. She was mapping *him*.

He should have burned the ledger weeks ago, back at the beginning, and he told himself he hadn't because it was leverage, because a careful man keeps options. But lying in the dark now, listening to the garrison wind worry at the shutters, Verrec admitted the truth he would admit to no one: he had kept it because it was the only honest document he had touched in six years. Every map he'd commissioned lied about something. This book told the truth, and the truth was him.

He closed it. He would burn it at dusk, with witnesses, properly, and stand there while it went, and afterward he would sleep.

He was almost sure he would sleep.

* * *

Kestrel did not go home. Home was a room above a dead man's office, and rooms invited despair, and despair was a luxury the deadline had cancelled.

Instead she walked out through the dark town to the Harrow orchard, down the earthen steps into the cold cellar, and lit the candle she kept hidden in a niche behind the apple racks. The tracings hung in their sacking like cured hams. She unwrapped them and counted — nine pages, plus the atlas plates, plus the copy of the master index she'd sewn into her coat lining weeks ago. All of it safe. None of it sufficient.

Nine pages past two checkpoints had taken five days and cost her Ilan. One page past the third checkpoint — the negotiators' own gate, guarded by grey coats who answered to no sergeant she could charm — would take a miracle, and miracles were not a currency she dealt in. She dealt in ink and patience and other people's kindnesses spent against her will.

She sat on the cold earth among the smell of apples going soft, and let herself feel the dread fully for the length of one candle's worth of time. Then, as her master had taught her — *fear is weather, girl; you chart it, you don't obey it* — she began to plan.

The burning was at dusk, in the foundry yard, witnessed. Which meant the ledger would be present, unguarded by anything but ceremony, for perhaps half an hour. Which meant the theft, if theft were possible, must happen in the middle of the burning itself — the one moment when every eye would be on the flames.

And the third checkpoint — the negotiators' gate. Grey coats, foreign protocols, a chain of command she couldn't bluff with a satchel and a smile. Papers wouldn't do. Ruses wouldn't do. What passed such gates, she thought, staring at the candle flame until it printed its ghost on her vision — what passed the innermost gate of any army?

Testimony. Witnesses. The right, ancient as treaties, of any person to speak before the signing of a peace. Her master had told her once, in another life, that the oldest laws always left a door for the voice — *a map can be confiscated, but no one has ever yet confiscated a spoken word except by silencing the mouth.*

Her mouth, then. Her voice, carrying nine pages of names.

But she would need to get inside the perimeter first, and for that she needed someone who knew the garrison's protocols from the inside — someone in the stockade whose detention ended when amnesties were rumored, someone who had signed those original orders and could vouch for her claim with the weight of his own guilt.

Ilan. Who had let himself be taken so her barrels could pass. Whose pardon she would be spending, if he even had one left to spend.

The candle guttered. Above her, through the earthen ceiling, the wind moved through the bare orchard rows, and somewhere far off toward the garrison she imagined — or heard, or invented — the first stacks of wood being carried into the foundry yard.

One day. One night. Dusk tomorrow, and pens dipped at ten the following morning.

Kestrel pinched out the flame and climbed the stairs into the dark, already composing the route she would draw by heart: the stockade wall, the sentry rotations Ilan had taught her, the foundry yard, the gate, the tent. Every theme of her nine days converging, as all lines converge on a map, onto a single point —

— and the point was fire.
