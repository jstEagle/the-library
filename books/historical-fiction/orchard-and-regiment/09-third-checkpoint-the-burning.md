## 9. Third Checkpoint: The Burning

The smoke told her where to go before any sentry did.

Kestrel had imagined the burning of the ledger a hundred ways since Ilan's arrest — in Verrec's brazier, page by page, deliberate as an execution; or flung whole into a kitchen fire, casual as rubbish. She had not imagined it as a ceremony. But the garrison courtyard blazed with a bonfire the size of a harvest pyre, and around it stood officers with their hands clasped behind their backs, watching the requisition records feed the flames with the same solemn attention her master had once given a drying plate of ink.

She had come through the orchards above the town, down the terraces her atlas named row by row, wearing the surveyor's coat that had carried nine days of contraband across three checkpoints. Under the coat, against her ribs, she wore nothing now. Everything was ahead of her. The tracings sewn into the satchel had gone over the bridge and through the cider barrels, and what remained — what mattered — sat locked in Verrec's own office, in the iron strongbox where he kept his leverage. Where he had kept it for nine days, waiting to decide whether it would buy his life or burn with his crimes.

Tonight he had decided.

She counted the courtyard from the shadow of the granary wall: twelve soldiers at the fire, six officers, two sentries at the gate she'd need to leave by. And Verrec himself, standing closest to the flames, holding the ledger's spine in one hand like a man testing the weight of a slaughtered animal before butchering it.

He was feeding it slowly. That was the thing she hadn't planned for and couldn't have: he was reading each name aloud under his breath before he let the page go, and Kestrel understood, watching his lips move, that this too was a kind of testimony — his own, unwitnessed, a colonel rehearsing his indictment alone because no one else would ever hear it. The fire took the Marenne family. The fire took the Hollis cousins, four households off a single branch. The fire took names she had traced in lamp-black on paper thin enough to breathe through, and each one went up as a small bright exhalation, ash rising past the eaves toward a sky still bruised with the last light.

*The signature page is at the back.* Ilan had said it weeks ago, in the cold cellar, in the voice of a man describing his own wound: *Verrec will keep the orders intact because the signatures are the proof he wasn't acting alone. He'll burn everything except the pages that prove other men signed first. He can't burn himself out of history.*

So she didn't watch the fire. She watched the office window above the courtyard, third floor, lamplit, where a clerk moved behind the glass carrying documents in bundles. The strongbox would be open tonight. Verrec would want the final pages burned last, in order, so that the record died in its proper sequence — a tidy man even in arson.

There was a service stair behind the granary. She had mapped it herself, months ago, in another life, when mapping the garrison was simply work and the army was simply a client. Her own line, inked in her own hand, led her up into the building's throat.

* * *

The corridor smelled of tallow and wet wool. She moved the way her master had taught her to draw borders: not along the obvious lines but along the seams, the places where jurisdiction blurred and no one felt responsible. A seam between the barracks wing and the offices. A seam where a sentry's lantern-light fell short by exactly the length of a doorway.

At the office door she stopped and listened. Inside, Verrec's voice — not reading now, but speaking to someone, low and clipped.

"—the annex, then. If they insist on an accounting, give them an accounting." A pause. "No. Not the original. A summary. Totals. No names."

Her heart slammed once, hard, and went quiet, the way it did at the top of a climb when the ground drops away.

*A summary. Totals. No names.*

He was going to give them something. He had understood, hours before she had, that the negotiators would demand proof, and he meant to meet that demand with a forgery — numbers without faces, losses without families, an accounting that confessed everything and accused no one. It would pass. It might even be praised. A colonel who cooperated with the armistice annex, who surrendered documentation, whose paperwork was orderly —

And the real ledger, the real names, would be ash by midnight.

Kestrel pressed her palm flat against the door until the grain bit into her skin. She thought of her master's last lesson, the one he'd never spoken aloud but which lived in every unfinished plate: *a map is an argument about the world. Make sure you are the one making it.*

She turned the handle.

* * *

The strongbox stood open on the desk, and beside it, stacked in neat bundles tied with army cord, lay the final signatures — the requisition orders themselves, the originals, bearing the countersignatures of staff officers, quartermasters, men whose names carried weight in any capital. Verrec's own signature ran down the margins like a confession he'd been unable to stop writing.

He heard her before he saw her. Of course he did; he'd known her hands for nine days, had watched them draw the boundaries he'd ordered erased. He turned from the window with the expression of a man finding a debt come due early.

"Apprentice." He glanced at the corridor behind her. "Alone? You've grown bold."

"You're giving them totals," she said. "A summary. No names."

Something flickered in his face — surprise that she'd heard, then the cold recalculation of a man deciding whether a witness was a problem or a tool. "Numbers survive scrutiny better than sentiment. The negotiators want closure, not grief."

"The negotiators want *proof*." She crossed to the desk and put her hand flat on the top bundle, claiming it the way she'd never dared claim anything. "Names are the only thing they can't forge. You know that. It's why you kept these instead of burning them in autumn."

"They're ash by now."

"Then I'm taking your ghost story with me." She lifted the bundle. It was heavier than she expected — not the weight of paper but the weight of everything it certified. "Every signature here is a man who can be tried, or pardoned, or ruined. Including yours. You kept them as insurance. I'm cashing the policy."

For a long moment Verrec looked at her the way he must have looked at enemy positions across a river: measuring range, calculating cost. Then he did something she had not imagined in any of her hundred rehearsals.

He stepped aside.

"The right of testimony requires a vouching officer," he said quietly. "Someone of rank to stand witness that you are who you claim. You don't have one." His eyes went to the window, down toward the fire. "You have a deserter in a cell, awaiting amnesty that may not come. And even if it does — even if they release him — his word buys you a cell beside his."

"I know the protocols," she said. "I learned them from him."

"Yes," Verrec said. "That was always the flaw in my arrangement. I hired the mapmaker's heir to redraw the orchards, and I never asked what else inheritance includes." He straightened his collar, and for one unguarded instant he looked exhausted, old, a man carrying more than he could hold — everyone bargaining for what they could carry, and his load the heaviest in the garrison. "The gate sentries change at the ninth hour. The negotiators' tent stands at the far end of the parterre. Walk as though you belong there. Grief walks well when it has somewhere to go."

She stared at him. "Why?"

"Because a summary without names protects me for a year and damns me forever," he said, "and I am tired of betting on the year." He picked up his pen, dipped it, and held it out to her — an absurd, ceremonial gesture, a colonel arming a cartographer. "Take the west stair. And apprentice — when you read the names, read them *loudly*. I signed every one of them. Let me hear myself condemned properly, at least once, while I can still do something about it."

* * *

The parterre was lit like a festival. Lanterns hung in the bare trees, and beneath them the negotiators' great tent glowed from within, a lantern itself, its canvas walls throwing shadows of men pacing, seated, leaning over tables. She could hear pens. That was the detail that undid her — not drums, not argument, but the scratch of many nibs at once, the sound of a document being born, of a world being rewritten in real time by men who had never walked an orchard terrace in their lives.

Two sentries at the tent gate. Beyond them, a staff adjutant checking credentials against a list.

And leaning against the tent's guy-rope, in a borrowed uniform coat that fit him wrong, stood Ilan Sere.

Her breath caught. He was thinner, gray-faced, his wrist bandaged where the detention cells left their marks — but upright, and looking at her with an expression she had seen once before, in the cold cellar, when she'd shown him the first tracing: recognition without surprise, as though some part of him had already drawn this scene and was only now walking into it.

"They released me an hour ago," he said, before the sentries could speak. "Amnesty rumor. Half-confirmed. Which means I'm free until someone checks the list twice." He pushed off the rope and came to stand beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched hers, and addressed the sentries in the flattened accent of a staff officer. "This woman carries evidence under the right of testimony. Annex provisions, article eleven. I'm her vouching officer."

"You're a deserter," the adjutant said, stepping forward with his list. "Sere, Ilan. Your pardon hasn't—"

"My pardon," Ilan said pleasantly, "is contingent on cooperation with the annex proceedings. Vouching for testimony *is* cooperation." He turned to Kestrel, and lowered his voice so only she could hear. "If this goes wrong, they take me back inside and lose the paperwork. Say the names anyway. Promise me."

"Ilan—"

"If they ask whose authority—" He smiled, and it was the smile from the cellar, wry and terrified and true. "Tell them a dead mapmaker sent you. It's nearly accurate."

The adjutant looked from the list to Ilan to the bundle under Kestrel's arm. Somewhere behind the canvas, a bell rang the quarter-hour, and beneath it she could hear the negotiators resuming their seats, chairs scraping, the particular hush of men preparing to sign away a war.

"Well?" the adjutant said.

Kestrel shifted the bundle in her arms. Nine days of seams and satchels and sewn hems, of carrying the world in pieces small enough to hide — and here at the last gate there was nowhere left to hide it, and no need. She lifted her chin the way her master had lifted his brush: certain of the line, whatever the line cost.

"I am Kestrel Okafor," she said, "heir to the atlas of Aldous Ren, and I carry the original requisition orders for every orchard taken in this district. I claim the right of testimony. Open the tent."

The adjutant hesitated one breath longer — and then Ilan stepped forward and put his name beside hers, signing the witness roll with a hand that did not shake, surrendering his uncertain pardon to make her claim undeniable. The sentries drew the flap back.

Warm air, lamplight, the smell of ink freshly mixed.

Inside, at the head of the table, a secretary was filling three pens from a single well, and the chairman of the delegation looked up at her — at the bundle, at her ink-stained fingers, at the face of a woman who had walked out of a burning province carrying the names of everyone it had lost.

"We were about to sign," he said.

"Not yet," said Kestrel Okafor, and stepped into the light. "First you'll want to know what you're signing *for*. Begin with the Marenne family, of the upper terrace. I have all of them, and I intend to read every name."
