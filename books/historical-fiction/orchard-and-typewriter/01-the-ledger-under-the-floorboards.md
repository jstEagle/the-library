## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboards

The river town of Vellmark announced itself the way it always had: first by smell, then by sound, then by the slow reveal of its rooftops through the bare branches along the road. Ines Eriksen smelled it before she saw it — woodsmoke and wet wool, tannin from the tannery, the sweet rot of last autumn's apples still moldering in the orchard rows above the houses. Then came the sound, carried across the frozen fields like something whispered through cupped hands: the groan of the mill wheel turning under ice-crusted water, and beneath that, fainter, the church bell tolling eleven.

She had been away fourteen months. The road home had taken three days by cart and one by foot, the last stretch walked in silence because her driver — a farmer hauling turnips to the garrison market — had run out of things he was willing to say out loud. That was how Vellmark had changed, she thought, watching the town assemble itself out of the winter haze. Not in what people said, but in what they no longer said.

At the crest of Orchard Hill, the house appeared. It stood where it had always stood, squat and patient at the top of the terraced slope, its stone walls furred with lichen, its roof sagging just enough to look apologetic rather than ruined. The orchard surrounded it in long ranks of apple trees, leafless now, their branches black and intricate against a sky the color of dishwater. Someone — old Halvorsen, probably, who tended the lower rows for a share of the crop — had pruned the trees nearest the lane. The cuts were clean and white against the dark bark, like small open mouths.

Ines set down her kit bag on the gatepost's flat capstone and stood a moment without moving. Her hands were the hands of a nurse: reddened knuckles, nails cut to nothing, a scar across the left thumb where a splintered stretcher pole had opened her to the bone two winters ago. They were steady hands. She made a point of noticing this, every time, because there had been months when they were not.

*You are home,* she told herself, and the words sat strangely, the way a borrowed coat sits on unfamiliar shoulders.

She let herself in with the key hidden under the loose brick beside the door — a habit now so deep she'd have to be dying to break it — and stood in the front room while her eyes adjusted. Dust hung in the air like held breath. The furniture was shrouded in sheets she'd thrown over everything herself, the morning she'd received the telegram ordering her north to the field hospital at Kessel. Fourteen months ago. In another life, worn by another woman who had not yet learned what she knew now.

* * *

The ledger lived inside her kit bag, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the waxed thread surgeons used for sutures. She did not unwrap it until the fire was lit and the shutters closed and she had walked the perimeter of the house twice, checking the windows, listening to the orchard, hearing nothing but wind working through the bare rows with a sound like distant surf.

It was not a beautiful object. She had bought it from the stationer's in Vellmark for six coppers, a plain school exercise book with a marbled cover gone soft at the corners. Inside were forty-one pages of names, written in her own hand, in pencil, because ink could be blotted and corrected and a pencil line was honest — once made, it stayed made.

Forty-one names. Forty-one men the army had decided, for reasons of convenience or cowardice or arithmetic, did not exist.

She unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, though there was no one to see, and laid the book on the kitchen table in the light of the lamp. The first entry was nearly two years old now, the pencil faded to gray.

*Voss, Corporal Anselm. 3rd Battalion, 11th Regiment. Died 14 October, Kessel forward dressing station, 0410 hours. Wounds: shell fragment, abdomen. He asked whether his mother had been written to. I said yes. I lied.*

The official record listed Corporal Voss as missing in action. Missing. As if he had wandered off between one artillery barrage and the next, as if his body had simply declined to be where his name said it should be. She had watched the clerk at Kessel write it. *Missing, presumed.* Three words that cost nothing and erased a man entire — his death, his grief, his mother waiting at a window in some village whose name Ines would never know, holding a hope that was a lie manufactured by clerks who had never seen him bleed.

That was the night she had begun the ledger. Not out of heroism — she wanted no confusion about that, even alone in her own kitchen — but out of a fury so cold and precise it had felt less like an emotion than like a skill she was discovering she possessed. Somewhere between the fourth and fifth hour of that terrible shift, while the lamps guttered and the wounded kept coming and the clerks kept writing their tidy falsehoods, Ines Eriksen had understood that the truth was going to die of neglect unless somebody wrote it down.

So she had. Quietly. In margins and on scraps and later, systematically, in the exercise book itself. A man died in her ward; she checked the casualty lists posted each week; when his name appeared wrong — missing instead of dead, wounded instead of killed, absent altogether — she recorded what actually happened, in her hand, in pencil, with dates and units and, where she could get them, the names of the officers who signed the sanitized versions. She had filled one book and started a second. She carried them sewn into the lining of her uniform during transport, flat against her ribs, close enough that she could feel them when she breathed.

Now she counted them again, here in the lamplight: two books, eighty-three names between them, plus the loose slips tucked at the back — fragments she hadn't yet transcribed, written on bandage wrappers and the backs of ration cards. Eighty-three men restored, provisionally, to existence. It was nothing. It was everything. It was a drop of ink in a river of forgetting, and she had crossed half a country carrying it against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She wrapped the books again, carefully, folding the oilcloth over twice at each end, and went upstairs to the back bedroom.

* * *

The floorboard was the third from the wall, beneath the window, in a room nobody used because the chimney drew badly and the wallpaper had been her grandmother's choice and was hideous beyond rescue. Ines pried it up with the flat iron she kept for the purpose, working the blade along the seam until the nail gave its familiar complaint, and lifted out the tin.

Biscuit tin, blue, with a picture of a sailing ship on the lid. Inside it, wrapped in linen: her mother's letters, a photograph of her father standing stiffly before the orchard house in the summer of '88, and beneath those, a false bottom of slats that had fooled everyone since before the war, including — she believed — the two military policemen who had searched the house briefly in her first year away. They had looked under mattresses and inside the clock case. Nobody ever looked up from a floor.

She nested the ledgers among the linen, replaced the slats, settled the board back into its groove, and pressed it flush with her palm. When she stood, the floor was only a floor, scuffed pine boards running to the window, and the room was only a room, cold and ugly and safe.

She allowed herself thirty seconds of stillness. Then she went downstairs and began to unpack.

* * *

Routine, she had learned, was not merely comfort. It was camouflage. A woman who baked bread, who walked to the well at the same hour each day, who sat in her front window in the evening doing needlework — such a woman became scenery. The army that had swallowed Vellmark whole these past three years barely saw scenery. It was the women who wept openly, the men who argued in the tavern, the priest who preached inconvenient sermons who got visited by officers with questions. Ines intended to be invisible as furniture, useful as furniture, and about as remarkable.

So on her second morning home she walked down to the garrison hospital — a converted grain warehouse near the docks, all iron pillars and draughty lofts — and presented herself to the matron, a brisk Walloon woman named Sister Amery who remembered her from the training years.

"Eriksen." Amery looked up from a requisition form, and something flickered behind her eyes — relief, Ines thought, quickly mastered. "They'll send you back to the front eventually. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then why come here? There's nothing here but convalescents and paperwork."

"Because there's nothing here," Ines said mildly, "but convalescents and paperwork."

Amery studied her for a long moment. Whatever she read in Ines's face, she chose not to pursue it. "Mornings and evenings, then. We're drowning in bandages. Half the cotton in the province has been requisitioned, and what's left is fit for sacking." She pushed a basket toward the end of the table, overflowing with torn linen. "Start there."

And so Ines fell into it: the scissors' rhythm, the smell of carbolic and boiled starch, the low murmur of the wards overhead filtering down through the floorboards like weather. She rolled bandages into tight white cylinders and packed them into crates stenciled with lot numbers, and she listened — because listening was the other half of her work, the half that never appeared on any duty roster. She heard which regiments were passing through, which trains were expected, which officer complained loudest about supply. She heard a sergeant mention that the print workshop down on Binder's Lane had been put on some kind of notice, its fate discussed at headquarters. She filed the remark away without letting her hands pause in their rolling.

The convalescents liked her. Men recovering from amputations and fever liked anyone who spoke to them as though they were still entirely alive, and Ines did, unfailingly. But she noticed, and noted, how they too had learned the grammar of silence. A corporal from the hill country began a story twice about what happened to his company at the ford — and stopped himself both times, glancing toward the door, and finished instead with a joke about the food. A lieutenant with a shoulder wound talked freely and endlessly about everything except the battle that had broken him, as though the subject were a room in his house that had been bricked over.

Nobody asked questions anymore. That was the change she felt most keenly, walking the town's streets in the blue hour after supper. Vellmark had been a talkative place once — she remembered market days loud with argument, the printer Marek holding forth outside his workshop about politics and typefaces and the moral obligations of a free press, while the whole street pretended to be annoyed and nobody ever left. Now the market square was quieter, the stalls thinner, the conversations conducted in lowered voices with frequent glances at the green-and-gray uniforms drifting through. Soldiers everywhere. Soldiers buying bread, soldiers queueing at the canteen, soldiers laughing in a way that had a blade in it. And threaded through the civilian crowd, a new posture: shoulders drawn in, chins tucked, eyes finding the cobbles. The town had learned to fold itself small, like a letter that doesn't wish to be read.

Old Halvorsen tipped his hat to her at the orchard gate and said, "Good to see you home, missus," and did not ask where she had been or what she had seen, though his son had served at Kessel and Halvorsen surely lay awake wondering. Frau Ostergaard next door brought her a jar of rendered fat as a welcome-home gift and said only, "Times being what they are," which was Vellmark's way now of saying everything. Even the children had learned it. She passed a knot of boys playing at soldiers by the millrace, and their game was accurate in every detail — the checkpoints, the papers, the shouted orders — and none of the adults nearby so much as frowned.

*We are all keeping our heads down together*, she thought, climbing the orchard path in the dark. *A whole town practicing the same silence, like a choir that has forgotten it used to sing.*

* * *

On the seventh evening, sitting by her fire with her feet aching and her hands finally warm, she took out the loose slips from her memory — not the tin, never the tin unless necessary, but the contents of the tin, which she carried in her head now the way other women carried prayers — and reviewed them as one reviews accounts.

Eighty-three names. Some she had verified against the weekly lists; some rested solely on her own witness, her own hands, her own eyes. For most she had only a surname and a unit. For a few she had more: a dying request, a last word, a detail that proved a man had been a person — Corporal Voss worrying about his mother; a boy called Lindqvist who recited shipping timetables to steady himself through the pain and died mid-sentence, between the Tuesday boat and the Thursday one; a sergeant named Brecht who had held her wrist with surprising gentleness and said, "Tell someone I wasn't a number."

*I'm telling someone, Sergeant,* she thought, feeding a stick of applewood to the fire. *I'm telling everyone I can find. Slowly. Carefully. With my heart going the whole time like a drum I can't put down.*

The wind rose outside, moving through the orchard rows with that surf-sound again, and somewhere below, far off toward the river, the ice boomed — a deep, resonant crack like a door closing in the earth. The thaw was weeks away yet, but the river had begun its muttering.

She banked the fire, checked the shutter latch, and climbed the stairs to the ugly back bedroom, where she pressed her palm flat against the third floorboard from the wall — just resting it there, feeling the solidity of it, the ordinary honest pine of it — before going to her own bed.

Three days later, walking down through the market square with her bandage basket on her arm, she saw the posters going up on the customs-house wall. Fresh paste, big block letters, the garrison's eagle stamped at the head of each sheet.

REQUISITION ORDER NO. 114, they read. BY DIRECTION OF COLONEL WEISS, GARRISON COMMANDER. THE PREMISES KNOWN AS THE PRINT WORKSHOP, BINDER'S LANE, TOGETHER WITH ALL PAPERS, RECORDS, AND PRINTED MATTER CONTAINED THEREIN, ARE HEREBY REQUISITIONED FOR THE WAR EFFORT.

*All papers.*

Ines stood in the square with the crowd flowing around her, folded small, eyes down, and felt the words go through her like a bayonet. All papers. Every private letter, every account book, every scrap of printed truth in Vellmark, gathered up by men whose profession was deciding what the war had and had not done.

Her hands, on the basket handle, did not tremble. She made a point of noticing that, too.

Then she turned for home, walking at the pace of a tired nurse with nothing on her mind but bandages, while behind her the paste brush swept and the eagle gleamed and the town read the wall in silence.
