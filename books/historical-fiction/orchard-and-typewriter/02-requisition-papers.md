## 2. Requisition Papers

The notices went up on a Tuesday, in weather the color of dishwater.

Ines saw them first on the granary wall as she walked back from the hospital with a basket of mended bandages against her hip — three sheets of army paper, pasted over the butcher's faded advertisement for veal, the paste still glistening at the corners. She stopped. Around her, the market crowd flowed and eddied without pausing, women with their skirts hiked above the mud, a boy dragging a sled of turnips, two old men who had been arguing about fodder prices since autumn. Nobody stopped. Nobody even slowed.

That was the town's new skill, Ines thought. It had learned it the way a body learns to favor an injured leg. Three years of war had taught them to walk past anything — past the troop trains, past the ambulances with their canvas flaps tied down, past the lists nailed to the church door that grew longer each week and never, somehow, contained the names people were listening for. They walked past things now the way she herself had learned to walk past the ward at night: eyes forward, hands busy, heart closed like a ledger whose columns would not balance.

But she stopped. She could not help it. She was, by profession and by temperament, a reader of lists.

The paper was good stock, heavier than the town's own printing, and the type had been set with military crispness. BY ORDER OF THE GARRISON COMMANDER, it began, and beneath that, in language so bland it took her a moment to understand its full weight:

*For the efficient conduct of the war effort, the following properties and materials are hereby requisitioned: the granary premises and contents; the print workshop of A. Marek & Sons, together with all presses, type, stock, and archives; and further, ALL PAPERS OF ANY KIND held in private hands within the town limits, including correspondence, records, registers, ledgers, diaries, and documents of every description. All such materials are to be surrendered for inventory and disposition. Failure to comply will be regarded as obstruction of the war effort.*

*All papers.*

She read it twice. The words did not change. Around her the market went on — the turnip boy cursing his sled wheel, a woman haggling over salt pork with the desperate arithmetic of someone feeding six on a soldier's allotment — and Ines stood in their midst feeling the cold come up through her boot soles, through the mud, through the whole frozen earth of the river valley, as if winter itself had been requisitioned too.

*Diaries,* said the notice. *Registers. Ledgers.*

Her hand moved without her willing it, pressing flat against her coat, against the place where no paper now hid but where, until eleven days ago, a small oilcloth-wrapped book had lived against her ribs for the length of the train journey home. The habit of protection outlived its object. The ledger lay beneath the floorboards of the orchard house, wrapped in waxed linen under the loose board beside the hearth, beneath forty years of dust that she had not disturbed because disturbing it would leave a mark. It was safe. It had to be safe.

And yet the notice seemed to reach down through the mud and the floorboards and take hold of it anyway, the way a hook takes hold through a coat sleeve.

She made herself walk on. She made herself buy what she always bought — lamp oil, black bread, a twist of string — and she made herself nod to Frau Ostmann behind the counter, who nodded back with the careful blankness they had all perfected. Not asking questions was a currency now, and everyone hoarded it.

* * *

By afternoon the soldiers had reached the workshop.

Ines heard it before she saw it — the groan of the loading door, the knock of crates, a voice with the clipped authority of men who have never once been told *no* by anything smaller than artillery. She came down Kirchgasse with her medicine bag over her shoulder, telling herself she was walking toward the hospital, telling herself the workshop was merely on the way, and knew both statements for the polite fictions they were.

Marek & Sons occupied the corner where Kirchgasse met the river road, a low brick building with windows fogged by two generations of ink and steam. Ines had known the shop since childhood — had watched, from her father's cart, the apprentices sweeping letterpress shavings into the gutter, silver-grey drifts like dirty snow. Old Marek himself had printed her school primers, her confirmation card, the broadsheet announcing her brother's birth and, nine years later, the one announcing his death at Tannenberg, set in the same handsome serif face, the same careful margins. The town's joys and sorrows had all passed through Marek's hands and come out the other side in orderly lines.

Now soldiers stood in those lines themselves.

There were perhaps twenty of them, working with the unhurried efficiency of men doing a task they had done in a hundred towns already. They had backed a wagon up to the loading door and were passing crates along a chain of hands: galley trays of type, quires of paper, wooden cases stenciled with the workshop's mark being nailed shut around whatever the sergeants deemed worth keeping. Inside, visible through the open door, more soldiers moved among the shelves with bayonets, tipping books off into tubs, prying drawers from cabinets and emptying them into sacks.

And in the middle of his own shop floor, small and upright as a fencepost, stood Anton Marek.

He was seventy-one years old. Ines had last seen him at church, where he sat in the third pew with his dead wife's prayer book and sang the hymns slightly ahead of the congregation, as though impatient for God to keep up. Now he wore his good coat, the one with the velvet collar, as if he had dressed for an argument he intended to win on dignity alone.

"You cannot do this," he was saying when Ines reached the edge of the crowd that had gathered — the largest gathering she'd seen in months, drawn the way crowds are drawn to fires and other disasters, everyone careful to look like passersby. "This shop has printed this town's papers for fifty-two years. My father set the first page. I have the—"

"Your papers are included in the requisition order," the officer said. He was young, this one, a lieutenant with a pale, intelligent face and gloves too fine for the work. He spoke the way the notice read: smoothly, blandly, each word polished of any meaning beyond its function. "Everything is inventoried. Nothing is destroyed without authorization."

"Nothing is *destroyed*—" Marek laughed, a terrible sound, dry as a press jamming. "Then why the tubs? Why are your men burning my accounts in the yard?"

"They are not accounts. They are obsolete stock."

"They are the *Ledger of the Parish Almanac* and you know it, because I told you so an hour ago." Marek's voice rose, cracking on the almanac's name like ice on the river. "Fifty-two years of this town's births and deaths and marriages and grain prices, and you call it *obsolete stock*. You are carrying away every written memory these people have, Lieutenant, and calling it housekeeping."

"Lieutenant," said a sergeant, stepping close, "we're falling behind schedule."

"I am not finished," Marek announced, to the lieutenant, to the soldiers, to the crowd, to the town. His voice found a register Ines recognized — the one the old hymns were pitched in. "I have printed the truth of this town for half a century. Every harvest, every flood, every funeral. When the war is over, someone will ask where the record went, and the answer will be *they carried it away in wagons* —"

The blow, when it came, was almost gentle by the standards of the front. A corporal, tired, embarrassed by the scene, simply turned and drove his fist into the old man's mouth mid-sentence, the way one swats a fly during a sermon. Marek went down against a crate of type — Ines heard the lead scatter across the cobbles, a bright metallic rain — and lay there, one hand pressed to his face, blood coming through his fingers, his legs making a small effort to rise and failing.

The crowd made no sound at all. That was the worst of it. Forty people, and no sound. Ines felt her own silence like a stone in her throat, felt the crowd's silence pressing on all sides, and understood suddenly that this was how it worked — this was the mechanism entire — that the fist and the silence were two halves of one machine, and the machine ran on nothing else. Strike one man. Let forty watch. The arithmetic did the rest.

The medic in her moved before the rest of her could weigh it.

She was through the crowd and kneeling before anyone could decide whether to stop her — a woman in a nurse's grey, unremarkable, expected; nurses knelt where there was blood the way water runs downhill. The corporal looked at her, looked at the red cross armband, and stepped aside with something almost like relief.

"Mouth," she said briskly, to no one in particular. "Likely teeth broken. Hold still, Herr Marek."

His eye above the bloody hand found hers. There was fury in it, and pain, and beneath both something that startled her: recognition. He looked at her the way men in the wards sometimes had, in the beginning, before the morphine — the look that said *you see me, then. Someone sees me.* And Ines, kneeling in the gutter among the scattered type, holding a compress to the mouth of the man whose presses had printed every true thing this town had ever known, understood that she had just been seen also. That he had noted the nurse who came running. That notes could be dangerous.

"Inside," she said loudly, professionally, for the benefit of the lieutenant. "He shouldn't lie in the cold. I'll see him settled."

"No one enters the premises," the lieutenant said.

"Then I'll settle him in the street, and you can explain the corpse to your colonel." She kept her voice flat, bored, the voice she had used on doctors twice her rank. "Broken teeth go septic. Septic mouths kill old men. I assume your inventory doesn't include him."

A pause. The pale lieutenant studied her with mild, incurious eyes, the eyes of a man cataloguing her along with everything else — *one nurse, grey wool, no significance* — and inclined his head a centimeter. "Be quick."

They got him up between them, Ines and a boy apprentice she hadn't noticed before, sixteen or so, all elbows and ink-stained cuffs, whose face was white and set as porcelain. Tomas, someone called him — the orphan who swept for Marek, who slept in the loft. He bore the old man's left side; his hands shook, but his grip did not. As they half-carried Marek up the step and into the reek of ink and cold iron, Ines glanced back once at the doorway and caught the boy looking after her — not at her, exactly, but at her hands, still dark with the old man's blood, as though the hands were a sentence he was trying to read.

* * *

She settled Marek on a bench in the composing room, amid the smell of oil and metal, and worked with what her bag held: forceps, iodine, linen, the small brutal kit of her trade. Two teeth came out. He did not cry out. He sat with his jaw swollen to twice its size and watched, over her shoulder, through the window, as his life's work went into the wagon crate by crate.

"They'll burn it," he said at last, thickly, around the packing in his gum. "Downriver. Everything goes downriver to the depot and gets burned. That's what the burning smell was last month. Halle. They did Halle already."

"You don't know that."

"I know paper, girl." His good eye stayed on the window. "I've smelled a thousand tons of it in that stove yonder. They aren't taking it to read."

Ines bound the jaw. Her fingers were steady; they were always steady; that was the trick of her, the thing people praised. But inside, some column in her had tipped over and begun to run.

*All papers.* The notice was pasted on the granary wall, on the church door, probably on the workshop door itself ten feet from where she knelt. Correspondence. Registers. Ledgers. Diaries. Every document of every description, surrendered for inventory and *disposition*. Somewhere in a warehouse or a records room or a crate bound for the burning, there might be — there would be — an oilcloth-wrapped book with a hundred and fourteen names in it. Names of men the official lists had swallowed. Voss. Halberd. The twins from the Silesian regiment. The boy who had died calling for his mother in a dialect nobody on the ward spoke, whom no one ever claimed.

She had started the ledger because the lists lied. She had hidden it because hiding was the only safety the world still sold. And now the world was buying up all the hiding places too, by order of the garrison commander, failure to comply constituting obstruction.

"Girl." Marek's hand closed on her wrist, surprisingly strong. "You went white just now. What name did you think of?"

"No name."

"Hm." He let her go. Through the split ruin of his mouth he almost smiled. "Fifty-two years, I've watched faces in this room while they waited on proofs. People look at the window when they're afraid, and at the type when they're lying, and at their own hands when they're carrying something they can't put down." His gaze dropped, deliberately, to her hands. "Yours haven't been still since we came inside."

Outside, the wagon creaked under its load. Voices counted. Nails went in.

Ines looked down at her own hands — the steady, famous, useless hands — and saw them plainly for the first time in weeks: not a nurse's hands at all, but a scribe's. Hands that had been writing a forbidden book in a stolen country of the night, line by line, name by name, believing that a floorboard was a border and dust was a law.

The floorboard was not a border. Dust was not a law. There was a lieutenant on the river road with fine gloves and a wagon, and behind him somewhere a colonel with a stamp marked *DISPOSITION*, and between them and her ledger lay only the thickness of pine boards and the diligence of men who inventoried everything.

They inventoried everything. That was the horror and the strange comfort of it. Men who must count every quire of paper would eventually count hers.

Unless it stopped being hers.

The thought arrived whole, the way ice shifts on a river at dusk — one crack, deep and structural, changing the shape of the season. She kept her face still and finished the binding and packed her bag, and all the while the thought went on opening in her like a seam: *a thing seized cannot be missed. A thing among ten thousand others cannot be found. If it must be taken, better that it be taken into company — buried in the crowd of papers, drowned in the flood — no. No. Drowned is drowned.*

Or better than either: gone before the wagons came. Carried by feet instead of wheels. Hidden where inventories don't reach.

She didn't know how yet. She knew only that dread had turned over in her chest, the way a heavy boat turns, and was now moving forward, and had acquired a direction.

At the door she paused. Marek watched her from the bench, jaw bound, eye fierce.

"Nurse," he said — and the word had weight, a summons. "When they post the demolition order, and they will, come back. Ask for the boy. Tomas. He knows where everything in this shop is buried." The ruined mouth bent. "*Buried.* Mark the word."

Outside, the light was going copper over the river. The wagon pulled away under guard, loaded with the town's memory, and the wheels wrote long dark lines in the mud of the Kirchgasse, pointing downriver — the same direction the barges would come, when the ice broke, for everything that remained.

Ines turned up toward the orchard house, walking fast, her bag knocking against her hip, and began, silently, to make a list.
