## 3. What the Officers Omit

The requisition orders stayed on the granary door for three days, pasted there with flour paste that wept down the wood in pale tears. Ines passed them each morning on her way to the hospital annexe, and each morning she read them again, as if repetition might soften the words. It did not.

*All papers, documents, ledgers, registers, and printed matter within the district of Vellmark are hereby requisitioned by order of the Provisional Military Authority. Private citizens in possession of such materials will surrender them at the workshop yard, Wednesday next. Failure to comply will be regarded as aid to the enemy.*

All papers. She had turned the phrase over like a stone in her pocket until its edges wore smooth and terrible. All papers meant letters. Diaries. Receipts. The parish register with its christenings and marriages and deaths, going back two hundred years. The schoolchildren's exercise books. Old Marek's proof sheets and his father's before him, the whole sedimentary record of a town that had learned to write itself down — all of it to be carried off in crates and weighed and, she suspected, burned or pulped into whatever the army needed paper for this season. Bandage wrappers, perhaps. Or lists.

Lists. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She went about her work. That was the discipline she had learned at the front, the first and hardest lesson: the hands continue. Hands can be taught anything, even to move while the mind is somewhere else entirely, kneeling beside a boy on a cot in a tent that smelled of carbolic and copper, holding a hand that was already mostly elsewhere too. Her hands rolled bandages now in the annexe, forty to the hour, while her mind stood in that tent and would not leave.

* * *

It had been the autumn of 1915, and the field hospital at Kesselbach was a converted tannery, which meant the floors were stone and always faintly cold, and no amount of lime could altogether kill the smell that rose from them on damp days. Ines had been there eleven months. Long enough to stop counting the days and start counting other things: pulse rates, morphine doses, the number of cots between the stove and the door, because the men nearest the door were the ones expected to die and the ones near the stove were expected to live, and the arithmetic of that arrangement was never spoken aloud but understood by everyone.

Corporal Elias Voss came in on a Tuesday, though she did not learn until later that it had been a Tuesday; the days had lost their names by then and were distinguished only by the arrival or non-arrival of the ambulance wagons. He was twenty-three years old, a farm boy from somewhere flat and northern where they grew rye, and he had taken a piece of shell through the abdomen that the surgeons looked at once and then looked away from, in the particular way surgeons have when they have seen everything twice.

He lived four days.

In those four days he asked her, over and over, with the polite insistence of the very young, whether anyone had written to his mother. And each time she said yes, Elias, the letter's gone out, and each time it was true in the sense that she had indeed written the letter herself, dictating it to the chaplain who was too busy to come, writing it in her own careful hand: *Your son is wounded but comfortable and thinking of home.* A lie assembled from standard parts, issued by the hundred.

On the fourth day he could no longer speak, but he could still hold her hand, and he did, with a grip that surprised her, a grip with the whole stubborn machinery of the rye fields behind it. His eyes were open and fixed on the ridgepole of the tent roof, where a spider had built a web in the angle of the canvas, and she thought — she has thought it every day since — that he was watching the spider work. Building. Fastening things together. And then the grip changed, the way grips do, from holding-on to letting-go, and she felt the exact moment it happened travel up her arm like a current switching off, and she sat with him a while afterward because there was no one else to sit with him, the ward being full and the war being what it was.

His name went into the ward book in her handwriting: *Voss, E., Cpl. Died 0430, wounds.*

Three weeks later the official casualty list came through, printed on the army's own press, and she read it standing in the corridor with her mittens still on, because the corridor was the only place warm enough to read without taking them off. She ran her finger down the column. *Missing.* Not killed. Missing — believed lost — status under inquiry. As if he had wandered off between one trench and the next, as if a man with his abdomen opened by shell steel might simply have taken a wrong turn in the fog and be walking still, toward home, toward the rye, arriving any day now at his mother's gate.

Missing. The word was not an accident. She understood that later, after it happened again, and again, and after she began, quietly, without quite admitting to herself what she was doing, to keep count. The officers wrote the reports, and the reports served the officers. A man who died of wounds received in a withdrawal was a reproach to whoever had ordered the withdrawal, so he became missing. A man who died in a sector the communiqué had described as quiet died inconveniently, so he became missing. A man whose death certificate required a court of inquiry and a signature and an admission — that man was filed under M and forgotten, and the filing cabinet was a long way from his mother's kitchen table, and the fog of euphemism settled over him like snow, softening his outline until even grief could not find him.

There was a clerk at Kesselbach — a gray, methodical man whose name she never learned — who processed the lists, and she remembered watching him once through the office window, stamping pages with a small deliberate motion, stamp-stamp-stamp, like a man sewing buttons onto coats he knew would never be worn.

That night she took a school exercise book — half its pages filled with some dead girl's sums — and ruled fresh lines beneath the arithmetic, and wrote at the top of the first clean page:

*Voss, Elias, Corporal. Died Tuesday (4th day), field hospital Kesselbach, 0430. Held my hand. Mother: Frau Voss, Rye Farm, [parish unknown].*

It was not much. It was not even accurate; she never found the parish. But it was more than the army intended anyone to know, and it was hers, and it was true.

After that she could not stop. It became a kind of fever with a conscience. Every man who died in her ward went into the book — name, rank, hour, the small human details the forms had no boxes for: *sang to himself, badly, all night. Asked for plums. Was afraid of the dark and ashamed of it.* When a man was listed missing whom she had buried, she entered both versions side by side, the truth and the lie, like two photographs of the same house, one in summer and one in ash. By the time the hospital moved west she had filled the exercise book and begun a second, a proper ledger bought with three months' saved tobacco ration from a quartermaster who did not ask questions, and by the time she was invalided home — lungs, the winter cough that would not release her — the ledger held four hundred and eleven names.

Four hundred and eleven men the army had misplaced, mislaid, or deliberately mislabeled. Four hundred and eleven mothers reading *missing* and setting a place at the table anyway, because hope is cheaper than certainty and lasts longer.

She had brought the ledger home to Vellmark like contraband sewn into the lining of a coat, which is exactly what it was. She had hidden it under the floorboards of the orchard house, beneath the loose board by the cold stove, and told no one, and slept badly, and got up in the mornings and rolled bandages and said nothing, the way the whole town said nothing, the way a town learns to say nothing the way a river learns its bed.

* * *

But now the requisition orders were on the granary door, and the crates were coming out of Marek's workshop, and Ines stood in the market square on Wednesday afternoon and watched the soldiers carry history into the light.

They worked quickly, without malice, which was somehow worse than malice. Malice you could hate. This was just weather, just administration, men in gray carrying gray boxes into gray daylight. The workshop doors stood open and inside she could see the presses shrouded in sacking, and the type cases stacked against the wall like drawers in some enormous mortuary cabinet, and old Marek moving among the soldiers with his hands half raised, not touching anyone, touching nothing, a man trying to hold a building together with his palms.

"Those are my matrices," he was saying. "Those molds are sixty years old, my father cut them, they're not — they're not *paper*, Captain, they're not covered by the order—"

"Everything goes," the captain said. He was young and tired and did not enjoy this; she noted that the way she noted everything now, professionally, the triage habit. "I'm sorry."

"They printed the *Vellmark Courier*, sir. First free paper in the valley. Sixty-one years. There's a copy framed on the wall—"

"Everything goes, old man."

And then Marek made his mistake, which was to put his hand on a crate. Perhaps to steady it; perhaps to bar the door; perhaps only because his hands had handled those crates' contents for fifty years and did not understand how to let go. Whatever it was, a sergeant took his wrist and turned it, not hard, the way you turn a horse, and Marek went down onto the frozen cobbles with a sound like a chair tipping over, and lay there among the wood shavings, and did not get up.

No one in the square said anything. A woman drew her child behind her skirts. The baker looked at his hands. Ines stood very still and watched, and something in her that had been banked coals for two years — since Kesselbach, since Voss, since the ledger went under the floorboards — shifted and caught, quietly, the way fire moves through straw when no one is looking yet.

Because here was the thing she understood, standing in the square while they loaded Marek's life into army wagons: the omission was never finished. It was not an event; it was a practice. First they omitted the names of the dead. Then they omitted the presses that might print the names. Then the papers that might remember the names. Omission was not a hole in the record; omission was the record, kept carefully, swept daily, defended by sergeants and captains and young tired men who were sorry, truly sorry, but everything goes.

Her ledger was in her house. Under the floorboards. Not in the workshop, not in the crates — she had checked the wording of the order a dozen times and it said private citizens would surrender papers *at the workshop yard*, which meant searches, eventually, door to door, the thaw coming and the barges coming and the army having all spring to be thorough.

Wednesday next. Failure to comply.

She walked home the long way, along the river, where the ice lay white and solid as a road, groaning sometimes in the cold nights with a sound like a giant turning over in sleep. The ice would break soon. Everyone said so; the icebreakers downstream had begun their counting, and the barge masters had come to look at the quay and measure with their eyes. When the ice broke, the river would open like a throat, and everything the army wanted gone would go down it — the archive, the crates, the town's six decades of its own handwriting — bound for the burnings downstream, for the furnaces, for the smoke.

And perhaps, she thought, standing at the rail of the footbridge with the wind coming off the ice and finding every gap in her coat, perhaps a ledger of four hundred and eleven names, sewn into nothing, hidden beneath a loose board by a cold stove in a widow's house above the orchard.

She climbed the hill. The orchard trees stood bare and patient, silver-gray against the sky, the way they had stood through every winter of her childhood and her mother's childhood before that. Inside, the house smelled of apples and lamp oil and the cold-stove iron smell of rooms left closed. She did not light a lamp. She knelt on the kitchen floor in the blue dusk, worked her fingernails under the loose board by the cold stove, and lifted.

The ledger lay where she had left it, wrapped in oilcloth, dark and dense and heavier than any object she owned. She did not take it out. She only touched it, one finger, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm the breathing, and lowered the board, and pressed the floorboards down around it until the seam disappeared, until the kitchen floor was only a kitchen floor again and the room above four hundred and eleven men was a room full of apple preserves and dust.

Then she sat in the dark and made herself think like a quartermaster instead of a mourner, because the mourner would freeze and the quartermaster would act.

The order said surrender at the workshop yard. Which meant collection points, inventories, clerks. Clerks meant men whose whole trade was knowing paper — weight, format, provenance — men who would recognize, if her ledger ever crossed their tables, exactly what it was. That was danger. But clerks also meant men close to the paper, men who handled it, men who might be reached, or might already be sick of reaching. She turned it over. She thought of the gray clerk at Kesselbach, stamp-stamp-stamping, and wondered — for the first time, with the first thin uncomfortable spark of something that was almost strategy — whether a man who stamps lies all day ever stops feeling the stamp go through his own hand.

The ice would break within weeks. The barges would come. The army had all spring to be thorough, and she had a loose board and a town that had learned not to ask questions.

But a town that has learned not to ask questions, she thought, rising stiffly from the cold floor, knees cracking, is also a town that has learned how not to answer them.

Outside, far downriver, the ice groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a note she felt in the floorboards, in the soles of her feet, in the loose plank over four hundred and eleven names.

She listened to it the way she used to listen to the ambulances at Kesselbach, counting, timing, learning the sound of something coming.

Soon, it said. Soon.
