## 4. The Apprentice

The presses had to be fed, or the soldiers would grow impatient, and impatient soldiers had already proven what they did with their impatience.

Tomas learned this on the third morning, when Sergeant Dolek — a heavy man with a mustache like wet rope and breath that smelled of pickled herring even at nine in the morning — cuffed old Marek across the ear for moving too slowly toward the storeroom. The sound of it, palm against bone, rang through the workshop louder than any press. Marek didn't fall. He straightened slowly, one hand pressed to his cheek, and looked at Dolek the way he looked at a badly kerned line of type: with a craftsman's quiet, devastating contempt.

"You'll treat the machines gently," Marek said. His voice was hoarse from three days of arguing, of being struck, of watching crates of forty years' work carried out into the February cold. "They are older than your grandfather, soldier."

Dolek laughed. But he was gentler after that, or gentler with the presses at least, which was the only gentleness Tomas cared about anymore.

So Tomas fed the presses. That was his work now, under supervision: he kept the great iron Heidelberg oiled and true while soldiers stacked crates of paper in its shadow, crates destined for the granary yard where they would be pulped or burned, nobody quite said which. The army wanted the workshop running again within the month — Colonel Weiss's orders, passed down through officers who spoke of the press the way farmers speak of a mule, something useful and dumb — printing casualty lists, ration notices, the clean arithmetic of war. And so the presses lived, for now, while everything around them died.

Tomas was sixteen and had been an apprentice for four years, since the fever took his mother and left him standing in Marek's doorway with nothing but a bundle of clothes and his father's old composing stick. He knew the workshop better than he knew his own hands. He knew which floorboard sang when you stepped on it near the cellar stairs, knew the smell of each ink by heart — the violet bite of the good German ink they saved for fine work, the tar-and-linseed stink of the cheap stuff used for handbills. He knew the way morning light came through the north windows and lay across the stone floor in long pale panels, and how dust hung in those panels like something suspended mid-decision.

He also knew, in the way that apprentices know things without being told, exactly what the soldiers were carrying out of the building. Not just paper.

* * *

The requisition order had been posted on the workshop door in red-bordered print, official and ugly: ALL PAPERS, RECORDS, AND PRINTED MATTER WITHIN THE DISTRICT ARE HEREBY CLAIMED FOR THE WAR EFFORT. There had been more of it, clauses and sub-clauses, but everyone in town had read the same first line and understood the same thing underneath it.

For two days before the soldiers arrived, Marek had worked through the nights. Tomas had woken more than once on his pallet in the back room to hear the soft industrial breathing of the press, the clack of the treadle, and had come downstairs barefoot in the cold to find the old man setting type by lamplight — small jobs, last jobs, a funeral notice here, a christening announcement there, things people had slipped through the door after dark with coins pressed into Marek's palm and fear in their eyes. Print me this before they come. A will. An apology. A name.

On the second night, Tomas had helped him pull the type apart afterward, letter by letter, sorting each piece back into the case, because you never leave a composed page standing; that was the first law of the trade. *Distribute your type,* Marek always said. *A page is a promise made of borrowed letters.*

Now the borrowed letters were being crated along with everything else.

Tomas watched them go. That became his discipline, his private devotion: he watched. A soldier named Fennel, young and pimpled, carried out the bound volumes of the *Gazette* — eleven years of the town's weekly paper, every council minute, every price of grain, every wedding and drowning and harvest fair. Another crate received the parish registers Marek had printed as a young man, facsimiles commissioned by the priest before the war. A third took the school examination records, the merchant ledgers copied for tax purposes, the box of broadsides from the flood year — Tomas had set that flood-year headline himself, at fourteen, his first full page above the fold, and now it went into a crate marked in chalk with a number that meant nothing and would mean destruction downstream.

He began to keep count. Not on paper — he was not a fool, though he was sixteen and foolish in most other ways. In his head. Each evening, lying on his pallet listening to the garrison's boots in the street below, he would close his eyes and walk through the workshop in memory, shelf by shelf, drawer by drawer, and mark what was missing. The Gazette volumes: gone. The jobbing drawers of ornamental type: gone, crated for reasons no one had explained, perhaps because brass and lead were worth melting. The archive boxes from the records room at the back: sealed now, guarded, their contents inventoried by a thin clerk with ink-stained fingers who had arrived on the fourth day and said almost nothing to anyone.

Marek had stopped speaking much at all. He moved through the workshop like a man walking through his own funeral, touching things — the tympan, the frisket, the stone where he'd mixed ink for thirty years — with a tenderness that made Tomas look away. Once, passing the empty shelf where the flood-year broadsides had stood, Marek had paused and laid his palm flat against the wood, and Tomas had pretended very hard to be busy with the rollers.

But it was the nurse that Tomas noticed.

* * *

She came on a gray Thursday, in the middle hour of the afternoon, when the light through the north windows had gone silver and flat. She wore the gray-blue uniform of a field hospital nurse under a wool coat too big for her, and she moved through the door with the unhurried certainty of someone who has been in worse rooms than this. Tomas knew her face distantly from town — she'd come back from the front some weeks ago, the Eriksen woman, staying out past the orchards in the old Halloran house or maybe it was the other family's name, he couldn't keep the map of old families straight. What he noticed first was not her face but her hands. They were still. Nurse's hands, steady hands, hands that had done things that made a boy's stomach turn if he thought about them too long.

"Good afternoon," she said, to the room in general, and then to Dolek specifically: "Sergeant. I'm collecting for the infirmary. The garrison doctor sent me — bandage linen, if there's any to spare, and the army surgeon wants rags, cotton waste, anything absorbent."

Dolek grunted and jerked his thumb toward the scrap bins by the cutting table. "Take what's in the bins. Don't touch the crates. Everything else is counted."

"Of course," she said, mildly. "Everything is counted these days."

And then she did not go to the bins.

She went instead along the wall of crates, slowly, reading the chalk numbers, her hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring a garden. She paused at the crate holding the Gazette volumes. She paused longer at the sealed boxes from the records room, stacked nearest the back door where the loading dock ran down to the river road. Her lips moved once, slightly, as if tasting a number.

Then she turned to Tomas, who was pretending to adjust the roller tension on the Heidelberg, his hands doing the work while his mind stood entirely elsewhere.

"You're the printer's apprentice," she said. It wasn't really a question.

"Tomas." He kept his eyes on the roller. "Yes, miss."

"You've worked here long?"

"All my working life."

"And do you know," she said, in the tone of someone asking about the weather, about the price of eggs, "which of these crates goes where? I only wonder because the infirmary has been asked to supply packing cloth, and I'm told some of the crates need padding for the river journey. I wouldn't want to pad the wrong ones."

It was a careful question. It was the most careful question anyone had asked him in weeks, and it was wrapped so neatly in bandages and packing cloth that a careless boy would have answered it plainly. Tomas set the last adjustment, wiped his hands slowly on his apron, and looked at her.

He saw a nurse. He saw tiredness around her eyes of a kind that didn't come from lack of sleep — he'd seen it on men coming back from the front, a tiredness of a different organ entirely. And he saw, beneath the mildness, something alert and level, the way a heron stands in shallow water looking like a piece of the scenery until the fish comes near.

"The padded crates," he said, "are the ones going on the barges. The ones for burning don't get padding. Nobody bothers."

Her expression did not change at all, and yet somehow it changed completely. "Which ones go on the barges?"

"That's decided upstream, miss. By officers." He picked up his rag. "I only feed the presses."

"I see." She glanced once, briefly, at the sealed record boxes by the back door — a glance so quick that Dolek, dozing by the stove, caught none of it. "Well. Thank you, Tomas. If you happen to learn which numbers are barge-bound, and you feel like telling someone at the infirmary, I make a decent cup of coffee. Better than the garrison's, anyway."

"I don't drink coffee," said Tomas, who drank coffee every chance he got.

"No," she agreed quietly. "I imagine you don't."

She collected her bin of rags and went out. At the door she stopped, half-turned, and looked back at the presses — at the Heidelberg, at the little platen jobber in the corner that had printed ten thousand christening announcements — with an expression Tomas recognized because he wore it himself whenever he thought no one could see: the look of someone saying goodbye to something that isn't dead yet.

Then she was gone, and the door swung, and Dolek snorted awake and demanded to know why the roller sounded dry.

* * *

That night Tomas lay on his pallet and did not sleep.

The workshop breathed around him in the dark — the tick of cooling iron, the settle of old timber, the wind finding the gap under the eaves that he'd been meaning to caulk since autumn. Below the floor, in the cellar, Marek's treasures slept in their locked cabinet: the molds, the original matrices of the typeface old Marek's father had cut, the one thing in the building the soldiers hadn't found because they hadn't known to look. Paper burns, Marek liked to say. Letters remember. Tomas held onto that sentence the way a drowning man holds a spar.

In his head, he walked the shelves again. This time he did not count what was missing. He counted what remained.

The Heidelberg, remaining. The jobber, remaining. Twenty-two cases of body type, remaining. The stone, the galley trays, the composing sticks, the quoin keys, remaining. The cellar cabinet, remaining. And somewhere in the sealed boxes by the back door — he didn't know what, precisely, but he had seen the thin clerk carry one box in personally, days ago, seen him set it down with both hands as if it weighed more than paper — something that the nurse with the still hands wanted very much to know about.

Two watchers, he thought. Or three, counting the clerk whose eyes flicked up every time a crate lid squeaked. The workshop was suddenly full of people counting.

He thought about the nurse's question, turning it over like a piece of type examined by lamplight, all its edges sharp. Which crates go where. She hadn't asked what was in the crates. Anyone loyal to the garrison would have asked what was in them, out of suspicion or boredom. She had asked where they were going — as if the destination mattered more than the contents, as if what she feared was not loss but erasure, things carried away to be forgotten rather than simply carried away.

He knew that fear. It sat in his chest every time Fennel's boots crushed another armful of loose pamphlets into the pulping barrel.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would find a reason to be slow about loading the barge-bound crates. Tomorrow he would memorize their chalk numbers, all of them, and hold the numbers in his head next to the count of what remained. And if the nurse came again — if she came asking after packing cloth, after coffee, after anything — he might let slip, in the careless way of a sixteen-year-old who knows nothing, one number. Just one. To see what her face did.

He rolled onto his side. Through the frost-furred window, the river showed black between its banks, ice holding, patient, a road waiting for traffic. Somewhere downriver the barges were being readied. Somewhere upstream the thaw was already beginning, water working at the underside of winter like typesetting fingers working under a stuck form, loosening it letter by letter.

In the morning there would be crates. In the spring there would be barges. And in between — Tomas fell asleep on the thought, unfinished — in between there were still letters enough to say anything, if a boy was brave enough to compose the page.

The next day the nurse did not come. Nor the day after. On the third day a different uniform appeared in the doorway — a thin clerk with ink-stained fingers, carrying a ledger under his arm and an expression like a man arriving late to his own execution — and asked, in a voice pitched carefully neutral, whether he might trouble the apprentice for a list of what the workshop had held before requisition.

Tomas looked at him for a long moment, at the ink stains, at the careful eyes, at the ledger that could hold anything.

"Come in, sir," he said, and reached for a blank sheet.
