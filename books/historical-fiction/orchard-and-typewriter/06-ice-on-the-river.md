## 6. Ice on the River

The river spoke first.

Ines heard it before she understood it — a low groan beneath the floor of the world, rising through the frozen ground and up through the soles of her boots as she stood at the orchard fence with an armful of kindling. She had been listening to that sound all her life without truly hearing it: the ice shifting in its sleep, dreaming of the spring it was made to forget. Every winter of her childhood it had murmured like this, and every winter she had ignored it, the way one ignores an old dog turning over by the fire.

Now she stood very still, and the sound went through her like a needle finding a nerve.

Three weeks. Perhaps four. That was what old Halvard the ferryman had said at market on Saturday, squinting at the sky with the authority of a man who had read the river for fifty years. "Warm rain out of the south by the first week of thaw-month," he'd told the fishmonger's wife, not troubling to lower his voice. "Ice'll go out fast this year. Deep snowpack, quick melt. The barges will come up from the locks before the banks are even dry."

Barges.

She carried the kindling inside and did not light the stove at once. She stood in the cold kitchen with her breath ghosting before her and thought about water — about how the river ran south past the sawmills and the lime kilns, past the lock at Varnholt, down to the great incinerator yards outside the capital where the army burned everything it could not use and much of what it could. She had seen the smoke from those yards once, from a train window, years ago: a long gray plume standing against the sky like a question no one dared ask aloud.

Her ledger was in that workshop. She knew it as surely as she knew the scar on her own palm. When they said *all papers*, they had meant hers too — the small brown book with its careful columns, its names written in her narrow hand while men were still warm enough to have names. Somewhere among the crates in the records room it lay, filed between requisition forms and quartermaster inventories, waiting to become smoke.

And now the river was going to carry it there.

She lit the stove after all. Small routines; she had learned their worth. The kettle, the tea, the slice of dark bread with apple butter from last autumn's jars. Her hands moved through the motions while her mind moved elsewhere — to the workshop, to the guards at its doors, to the problem that would not resolve itself no matter how many times she turned it over. You cannot steal back what you do not know where to find. And you cannot find it if you are never allowed inside.

So she would be allowed inside. There was more than one way to enter a fortress, and she had spent three years learning most of them at the front hospital, where the gates only opened for the wounded, the supplies, and the women who tended both.

* * *

The garrison surgeon's office occupied the former town hall, and Ines presented herself there on Monday morning with her credentials folded in her coat pocket and her face arranged into the mild competence that officers rarely thought to question. The surgeon himself was a harried man named Dr. Lenz, younger than his rank, with ink stains on his fingers and the look of someone who had not slept a whole night since autumn.

"A nurse," he repeated, scanning her papers. "Volunteer."

"I worked the field hospital at Kesselbach for two years. I'm settled here now. The garrison has sick men and I have hands. It seemed a waste of both."

He looked up at her then, properly, and something in her tone must have reached him, because he nodded toward a chair and began asking real questions — which wards, which surgeons, whether she'd seen gas cases, typhus, the winter dysentery that was moving through the barracks. She answered each one evenly, and watched him relax degree by degree, until he was less interviewing her than confiding in her.

"We're short-handed everywhere," he admitted. "Half my orderlies were pulled for the new battery company. There's a fever ward in the granary annexe — you'll want to start there. And there's a detail at the print workshop, the officers' quarters overflow — minor complaints mostly, but Major Riedel's clerk has a bad knee and complains like a dying man." He wrote her name on a duty roster with a pencil worn to a stub. "Eriksen, is it? Fine. Report tomorrow, seven o'clock. Keep your head down and your mouth shut around the line officers and we'll get along."

"Of course," said Ines. "I've had practice."

* * *

She had forgotten, in the two years since she'd last stood inside it, how loud a print shop was even when it was dying.

She came to the workshop on Wednesday morning, medical bag in hand, and stopped a moment in the street to look at it. Under military tarps and stacked crates it looked like any building mid-evacuation, but underneath the clutter she could see its bones — the tall windows Marek had kept so lovingly clean, the hoist beam over the loading door where paper bales used to swing, the stone step worn into a shallow curve by a century of feet. A sentry stood at the door with a rifle slung loose, bored. Another leaned against the woodshed next door, smoking.

Inside, the smell hit her first: ink and oil and paper dust, the smell of every book she had ever loved, threaded now with the harsher notes of pipe tobacco and boot leather and cold iron. The great press stood silent under its canvas shroud like a body under a sheet. Crates everywhere. Soldiers moving crates. And in the corner by the type cabinets, a boy — thin, dark-haired, sixteen or so — sweeping sawdust that didn't need sweeping, his eyes moving constantly, cataloguing everything.

Tomas. She knew the name from the town's murmurs; Marek's apprentice, the orphan who'd lived above the shop since he was nine. He did not look at her directly, but she felt his attention pass over her the way a searchlight passes over a wall — brief, thorough, returning.

"Nurse!" A sergeant with a bandaged hand flagged her down cheerfully. "About time. This blister's been talking to me for two days."

She dressed his hand, then the clerk's bad knee, then a private's infected hangnail, working her way through the garrison's small miseries while her eyes did their own quiet work. The records room was off the rear corridor — she marked the door, the heavy new lock bright against the old wood, the sentry posted ten paces from it. Two crates stood outside the door awaiting inventory, stenciled *ARCHIVE — PRIORITY*. Through the propped door she glimpsed shelves, ledgers in ranks, a table where a thin man in civilian clothes sat sorting papers with the mechanical precision of a man trying not to think.

She asked no questions. Questions were how towns got people taken away. But as she packed her bag, she let her gaze rest, just once, just briefly, on the records room door — and found, when she glanced up, that the boy with the broom was watching her watch it.

Neither of them smiled. Neither needed to. In a town that had learned not to see, two pairs of seeing eyes had found each other across a room full of soldiers, and that was enough for a first meeting.

* * *

That evening she walked down to the river path, as she did most evenings, and stood where the bank dropped away to the ice. It was changing. Anyone could see it now if they knew how to look — the snow gone gray and granular on the southern exposures, meltwater threading silver along the shore shallows by afternoon and freezing again by dusk, the ice darkening at its edges like bread toasting. Out in the channel the surface still held white and solid, deceptively whole, but she had grown up on this river and knew that whiteness was a promise already being broken somewhere underneath.

A week of warm rain, Halvard had said. Then the groaning, the cracking like artillery, the whole river waking at once — and the barges coming up from the locks on the first navigable water, cranes swinging, holds open to receive the archive. Three weeks, perhaps four, between her and the smoke.

She pressed her bare hand flat against a fence post, feeling the day's warmth leaving the wood, and made herself think like a planner instead of a mourner. What did she actually know?

The ledger existed — unless it had already been destroyed, and she forbade herself that thought, because despair was a luxury the work could not afford. It lay behind a locked door under guard, in a building she could enter freely by daylight but never alone, filled with soldiers. The lock was new and strong. The guards changed on a schedule she didn't know. And whatever she attempted, she could not attempt alone — a woman walking out of a requisitioned workshop with an army ledger under her coat would be stopped before she reached the corner, questioned, searched, and everything she had built these quiet years would burn with the book itself.

She needed help. She needed someone inside the records room, someone who knew the presses, and someone — this was the part she kept flinching from — someone willing to take risks whose consequences she could not fully foresee.

The boy, Tomas. The thin clerk at the inventory table, with his guilty, careful hands.

She turned back toward the orchard house as the light failed, and behind her the river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a great animal rolling over in its sleep. The town had always said the ice spoke honestly — that it told you exactly when it meant to leave.

It was telling her now. *Hurry*, it said. *I will not hold your secrets much longer.*

At home she took down the last jar of preserved apples, set it on the table unopened, and sat looking at it for a long time — not eating, just thinking. Then she fetched paper and began, in pencil, lightly, in figures that meant nothing to anyone but herself, to draw the workshop from memory. Every door. Every window. Every crate stack. Every guard position she had counted with her nurse's unremarkable eyes.

When she finished, she looked at the little map a long moment, and then folded it into the hollow bottom of her sewing basket, beside a spool of black thread and a length of fine wire she had bought that afternoon from the hardware stall, telling the merchant she needed it to mend a window screen.

Two watchers in the workshop. One of them a boy who swept floors and missed nothing. One of them a clerk who sorted papers like a man doing penance.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would dress the boy's chapped hands. And she would find a reason to speak to the clerk.
