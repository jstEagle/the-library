## 7. An Alliance of Small Treasons

Ines came to the workshop through the alley, the way the laundry women went, carrying a basket of soiled bandages as her excuse and her armor both. The garrison had learned to look past a nurse with a basket; she was furniture, like the water pump or the chapel bell. She let them look past her.

The morning was raw, the kind of late-winter cold that got into the joints of old buildings and old people. Frost still furred the inside corners of the workshop's windows, and when she pushed open the side door — unlocked, because the soldiers could not be troubled to lock what they considered already theirs — the smell of ink and oil and paper dust rose up around her like a language she half remembered. Her mother had worked in a print shop once, in another town, another life, before the orchard house and the doctor husband and the war that had taken both husband and sons and left her nothing but names.

She stood in the great room where the presses slept under gray army blankets and let herself feel it: the ledger was here. Somewhere behind one of those doors marked with stenciled numbers, in a crate or a locked drawer, sat a small book bound in green cloth with her handwriting climbing its pages — Voss, Corporal Anselm, died of wounds at the aid station 14 March, listed missing; Halvorsen, Private, drowned crossing at night, listed deserter — page after page after page. Two years of her nights, in ink. She had written some of those entries by candlelight while men died six feet away, and now the army proposed to carry them to the river and feed them to the current, or worse, to the fire barges downstream.

"You shouldn't be in this room."

The voice came from behind the second press — young, low, careful. Tomas stepped out, wiping his hands on an apron far too big for him, Marek's apron, and looked at her the way a deer looks at a hunter who has not yet raised the rifle. He was sixteen at most, all wrists and collarbones, with ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles they looked like bruises.

"I'm delivering for the infirmary," Ines said. "They requisitioned rags too, I'm told."

"They requisitioned everything." He said it flatly, without a boy's grievance, which frightened her more than anger would have. Then his eyes did something complicated — flicked toward the records room door, back to her face, down to her basket. "You asked the sergeant last week which crates were going to the granary and which to the dock. He told me about it after. He thought you were simple."

"Did he?"

"He thinks everyone is simple." The boy hesitated, and she watched him make a decision the way one watches ice decide to crack — slowly, then all at once. "The crates going to the dock are the ones with the wax seals. Red wax. The others are just paper the officers want copied before it's burned here."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you counted them," Tomas said. "When you walked in just now, your eyes went along the wall and you counted. I count too. It's what I do instead of sleeping."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Somewhere out front a soldier laughed at something, the sound bouncing off stone. Ines set her basket down on the composing stone with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

"My name is Eriksen," she said. "I work the fever ward. And two years ago I started writing down the names the reports leave out."

The boy's face didn't change, but his hands stopped moving, and she understood that she had just shown him something more dangerous than the ledger itself: that he was not alone in the dark.

* * *

Aldous Brandt had spent eleven years learning to be invisible inside the machinery of the army's paper, and he had gotten so good at it that sometimes he surprised himself coming around a corner, reflected in glass, a gray man in a gray coat. He had arrived in the town ten days ago with orders to inventory the seized archive, and he had been inventories ever since: crate four hundred twelve, regimental correspondence, 1915–16, to be copied and destroyed; crate four hundred thirteen, casualty ledgers, quarterly, sealed. His pen moved. His mind stayed carefully elsewhere, the way a man keeps his hand away from a burn.

It did not stay elsewhere today.

He had found it on Tuesday, in crate four hundred thirty-one, mislabeled as miscellaneous hospital stationery: a ledger in green cloth, private-hand script, dense and orderly as a hymnal. He had known within a page what it was, because he knew the shape of omission the way a locksmith knows locks. Every entry contradicted a report he had either processed or, in three cases he could not stop himself from remembering, written. And one entry — the third one, dated over a year back — concerned a Lieutenant Rask, listed as killed in action, honorably, in a report Aldous Brandt had composed himself at a desk in a warm room while the truth sat cooling somewhere east of him.

He should have resealed the crate. That was procedure. Procedure had kept him alive and employed through disgrace, through the demotion that followed the affair he never named even to himself, through the long gray years of being trusted with nothing but counting. Instead he had copied out the ledger's first twenty pages into his own notebook, hidden the notebook in the lining of his coat, and reseated the crate with hands that shook only after the wax had cooled.

Now he stood in the workshop's back room among the broken chairs and the smell of glue, waiting for the nurse, because the apprentice had passed him a folded scrap of paper that read *she counts too* — and because a man who has stolen one thing from the army discovers quickly that he needs allies the way a drowning man needs a bank.

She came in at four o'clock exactly, when the guards changed and the corridor stood empty for nine minutes — he had timed it, of course he had timed it — and behind her slipped the apprentice, who shut the door and put a wedge under it with the ease of someone who had done it many times for smaller reasons.

"Frau Eriksen," Brandt said. "I've inventoried your ledger."

Her face went very still. Not blank — still, the way water is still over something deep. "Then you know what happens to it."

"It's crated. Sealed. Scheduled for the dock when the ice breaks, with the rest of the sealed material. Downriver." He paused. "There are burning barges at the works below Kessel. I signed their manifests for three years."

"And you're telling me this because?"

Because of Rask. Because of the boy whose mother's name he had entered under *engagement losses* seven springs ago, a village so small it had no line on the map, so that no one would ask why a patrol had fired on a harvest market. Because a man can swallow guilt for years and then find it sitting in a crate wearing a green cover, looking up at him in a stranger's handwriting.

"Because I falsified reports," he said. It came out quieter than he intended. "For eleven years. Small ones, then less small ones. I am the reason some of the blanks in your book exist, Frau Eriksen. I think—" He stopped, restarted. "I think I am owed no trust here. But I am useful. I know the seals, the schedules, the guard rotations, which crates are weighted to sink and which are worth money to the sergeants loading them. If the ledger goes on the barge, it burns, or it drowns, or it surfaces in some officer's private library as a curiosity. Those are the only three futures paper has in this army."

The nurse looked at him for a long time. He felt the look the way one feels weather change. Behind her, the boy watched him with an expression that held no forgiveness at all, only arithmetic.

"The guards change at four," she said finally. "Nine minutes. You knew that."

"I know everything about this building that can be known from a manifest," Brandt said. "That is either nothing or everything, depending on whether you'll have me."

* * *

Tomas had decided, somewhere between the fourth minute and the sixth, that he did not trust the clerk. He trusted the nurse — or rather, he trusted the specific hunger in her, which he recognized, having seen it on Marek's face the day the requisition order went up and Marek had stood in the street shouting about free newspapers until a sergeant knocked him down like a piece of furniture. Hunger like that doesn't lie, even when the mouth does. But the clerk had the soft hands and the apologizing eyes of a man who had done wrong for so long it had become a kind of comfort, and Tomas had been raised on Marek's stories of men who apologized their whole lives and printed nothing.

Still. He wedged the door, and he listened, and he kept his own count.

"There's a press that works," he said, when the silence had gone on long enough that he couldn't bear it. They both turned. "The old one. The 1889 flatbed. The army thinks it's broken because I broke it — properly, quietly, a cracked gib key — the day they took the building. I can mend it in an hour. I've mended it twice since, just to keep my hand in, and covered it again."

"Mend it to print what?" the clerk asked.

"The ledger," Tomas said. "All of it. As many copies as the type will stand. Marek says—" His voice caught on the name and he pushed through it. "Marek says paper burns but letters remember. There's enough standing type in the racks to set maybe two pages at a time. It'd take days. It'd take every night we have."

"Nights we don't have," the nurse said. "The thaw—"

"The thaw is weeks off," Brandt said. "The river won't carry weight before mid-March."

"You haven't heard it lately." The nurse's hands had found each other in her lap, gripping. "Two nights ago there were cracks under the north bend, big ones, the kind you feel in your boots. And the garrison knows the thaw is coming too. They'll move the archive early rather than lose it. Weiss isn't a fool."

"Colonel Weiss," Brandt said, "is worse than a fool. A fool forgets things. Weiss forgets things deliberately, and then inspects the forgetting." He rubbed his eyes beneath his spectacles. "But he is also a man of schedule, and schedules can be poisoned. If the manifest said the sealed crates were to be inspected for damp damage before loading — a routine formality, one signature, mine — they'd come out of the records room to the dry store. Closer to the press. Unguarded overnight, if the inspection ran late."

"You could arrange that?" the nurse asked.

"I could forge the request from myself, which is the one document in this building no one checks, because no one believes I matter enough to lie." A thin, bitter smile crossed his face and was gone. "That is the one gift of being invisible, Frau Eriksen. No one looks at you, so no one sees what you carry out."

Tomas looked from one of them to the other — the gray man and the quiet woman, both of them holding their secrets out now like cards they couldn't take back — and understood that something had happened in this dusty room that could not be undone. Three people knew. Three people always meant one would talk eventually; Marek had taught him that much about history. But it also meant that for the first time since the soldiers came, he was not the only one awake at night counting.

"If we do this," he said, "and any one of us is caught, the others hang faster than the press can run. So we don't know each other. In daylight we're a nurse, a clerk, an apprentice. We pass in corridors without nodding. Everything happens here, in this room, after four o'clock, in the nine minutes and whatever darkness buys us after."

"That's not trust," the nurse said.

"No," Tomas agreed. "It's better. Trust is for people with time."

Something almost like laughter moved through the three of them then — brief, startled, quickly smothered — and Tomas thought later that it was the first sound the workshop had made in weeks that wasn't grief or marching.

They bent over the composing stone together, and the nurse drew with a pencil stub the floor of the records room as she had seen it through a doorway: the shelving, the window, the crate numbered four hundred thirty-one on the second shelf, left side, red seal. The clerk corrected her twice, added the position of the sentry's stool, the lamp bracket, the hour the oil was trimmed. Tomas memorized it the way he memorized everything, letter by letter, until he could walk the room blind.

Outside, the light was failing over the river, and under the failing light the ice made a sound none of them remarked on aloud — a long, deep groan, like something enormous turning over in its sleep — but all three of them heard it, and all three of them began, privately, to hurry.

It was the nurse who spoke last, at the door, in the blue dusk, pulling her basket up onto her arm.

"One condition," she said. "Every name goes in. Not the convenient ones, not the ones that make us look generous. All of them. Even the ones that shame the living. Especially those."

The clerk nodded slowly. "Especially those," he echoed, and Tomas saw his lips move around the words like a prayer he was only now learning.

They left separately, by separate doors, into the same cold evening — and none of them noticed the figure at the end of the alley who had seen the nurse slip in and slip out again, and who stood now in the gathering dark, counting.
