## 8. The Orchard House

The apples came out of the cellar in February the way they always had — wrapped in newsprint gone soft and yellow with age, packed in straw that smelled of dust and sweetness, each one a small sun held in the dark. Ines carried the crock up the stairs in both arms, feeling the weight of it settle against her chest like something alive, and told herself that what she was doing was ordinary. That inviting two men to supper was ordinary. That the tremor in her hands was only from the cold of the cellar steps.

It was not ordinary. She knew it was not ordinary the moment she saw them standing at her gate.

Tomas arrived first, as she'd known he would, coming up the lane with his collar up and his cap pulled low, glancing back twice before he let himself through the gate. He looked younger without the ink stains on his fingers, or perhaps just more exposed — a boy in a man's coat, carrying nothing but himself and the wariness he wore like a second skin. He stopped at the door and knocked, though she had told him to come straight in, as if some part of him could not believe a door would open for him without permission asked.

Brandt came later, by the river road, walking slowly enough to look like a man out for an evening stroll and quickly enough to look like nothing at all. He stood at the gate a long moment before opening it, and Ines, watching from the kitchen window while she sliced onions, saw him turn once and survey the street behind him with the flat professional sweep of a man who had spent years checking over his shoulder. Then he came up the path, and she opened the door before he could knock, because knocking felt wrong tonight. Tonight they were not strangers meeting. Tonight they were conspirators eating supper.

"Come in," she said. "Both of you. Quickly."

They stepped into the warmth like men stepping into water — hesitating at the threshold, then all at once submerged. The orchard house took them. It was a small house, built for a family that had never quite happened, two rooms down and two up, with a kitchen that held most of the life. The stove ticked as it heated. Dried lavender hung from the beams where she had hung it three summers ago, when hanging lavender was the most dangerous thing she did. The table was set with mismatched plates — one blue willow pattern, chipped; two plain white army-surplus pieces she'd bought from a peddler years ago — and three cups, because three cups seemed less deliberate than four or five, less like a gathering, more like accident.

Tomas stood just inside the door with his cap in his hands, turning it, turning it.

"You can hang that on the peg," Ines said. "The peg has held heavier things than a cap."

He hung it carefully, adjusting it until it sat square, and Ines understood suddenly that no one had ever told this boy where to put anything. That he had been making do with hooks and floors and pockets since he was small enough to fit inside a crate of type. Something in her chest went tight and warm at once.

Brandt removed his hat and held it against his chest like a man entering a church. He looked around the room the way he looked at documents, she thought — reading it, cataloguing it, filing away exits and sightlines and the location of every object that might become evidence. She watched him notice the medical bag by the dresser. The bandages drying on the line above the stove, folded into neat gray rolls. The single photograph on the mantel, turned slightly inward so its face was hidden.

"My brother," she said, because he was trying not to ask. "He's been dead nine years. I keep the photo facing the wall when soldiers come through. They don't need to know there's anyone left to grieve."

Brandt nodded slowly and set his hat on the peg beside Tomas's cap, and the two hats hung together looking absurdly companionable, and Ines had to turn back to the stove before her face did something she didn't authorize.

* * *

Supper was potatoes fried in goose fat, black bread toasted on the stove lid, and the apples.

She brought the crock to the table and lifted the cloth from its mouth, and the smell rose out of it — cinnamon and clove and the deep caramel dark of fruit preserved in its own syrup — and she watched it hit the two of them differently. Tomas breathed in and closed his eyes, just for a moment, just long enough. Brandt went very still, the way men go still when a smell reaches back past all their defenses into some year they don't talk about.

"Preserved last autumn," Ines said, spooning them onto the plates. "From the trees out back. The orchard's older than the town charter, Marek says. Older than everything except the river." She set the crock down. "Eat. There's more."

They ate. For a while nobody spoke, and the silence was not the silence of the garrison town — that pressed, listening silence, the silence of people counting their words before spending them. This was kitchen silence, stove-tick silence, the silence of people chewing. Tomas ate the way young men eat when they've learned not to trust that food will keep appearing: steadily, completely, mopping the syrup with his bread. Brandt ate slowly and watched the boy do it, and something in his face softened by degrees, like ice going out of ground.

"My mother used to preserve them whole," Brandt said at last, to no one in particular. "With the pits in. She said the pit kept the flavor honest."

"Mine quartered them," Ines said. "She said whole ones were showing off."

A sound came out of Tomas then — brief, startled, unmistakable. A laugh. He caught it almost immediately and looked down at his plate as if it had escaped without leave, but the damage was done. The laugh was in the room now. It settled into the corners along with the lavender smell, and Ines thought: *there*. There it is. That's why I risked it.

"The floor," she said, when the plates were cleared and the cups were filled with chicory coffee that tasted like burnt earth and comfort. "You'll want to see the floor."

* * *

She led them into the front room, where the light was going blue with evening, and knelt beside the third board from the window — the one whose grain ran a half-degree off from its neighbors, the one she'd pried up a hundred nights with a knife blade worn thin from the work. She showed them first how it looked from a standing position: nothing. A board among boards, scuffed like all the others, unremarkable as a stone in a streambed.

Then she knelt, worked the knife into the seam, and lifted.

The cavity beneath was empty. Of course it was empty — the ledger had lived in the records room of the print workshop for six weeks now, locked behind a door with a soldier outside it — but the space itself still held its history. The impression of the ledger's spine pressed into the soft wood below. A ghost of paper dust. She heard both men breathe at the sight of it, two small involuntary sounds, one sharp and one slow.

"I measured the joists before I cut anything," she said, kneeling there, her hand resting on the raised board. "I wanted it to look like rot from underneath, in case anyone ever checked the cellar ceiling. And I lined it with oilcloth, because damp gets into everything in this town, even secrets." She looked up at them. "Two winters it hid there. Every name. Voss. Halvorsen. The Kessler brothers, both of them, listed as desertion when they died of fever within a day of each other. Two hundred and eleven names, written in my own hand, between the hours of midnight and dawn, with the lamp turned down to a coal."

"Why the floor?" Tomas asked quietly. He had crouched beside her without seeming to decide to, drawn down by gravity or kinship. "Why not bury it? Why not take it out of the house?"

"Because buried things stay lost," she said. "And because I needed it near. That sounds foolish—"

"No," Brandt said. His voice was rougher than usual. "It doesn't."

"It isn't," Tomas said at the same moment, and the two of them glanced at each other, surprised into agreement, and Ines felt the strange geometry of the evening shift again — three points finding each other, drawing a shape that had weight.

She lowered the board back into place. It seated with a sound like a book closing.

"That ledger is in the records room now," she said, rising, her knees cracking. "Under guard. On Colonel Weiss's inventory, item number forty-one, entered in Aldous Brandt's own handwriting." She looked at Brandt. "I've thought about that a great deal these past days. That the same hand that wrote the omissions also wrote down the record of them. There's a symmetry in it. I haven't decided yet whether it's justice or irony."

"Both," Brandt said. "Usually both." He stood with his cup forgotten in his hand, staring at the floorboard as if he could see through it, back through the two winters, back through every night she had knelt here writing while the town slept. "I inventoried it. Page by page. I had to touch every page." He paused. "You wrote in the margins."

"In case the entries themselves were ever disputed. Dates, ward numbers, witnesses where witnesses existed. A ledger can be called a forgery. Margins full of corroborating detail are harder to argue with."

"They're impossible to argue with," Brandt said quietly. "I tried. Professionally speaking. I read it the way an inquiry officer would read it, looking for the flaw, the seam, the place to drive the argument in." He shook his head once, slowly. "There isn't one. Whoever taught you to write reports should have been running the army."

"A dead nun taught me arithmetic and honesty in equal measure," Ines said. "The army never showed interest in hiring either."

Tomas laughed again — shorter this time, bitten off, but real — and this time he didn't apologize for it, and Ines counted that too, filed it away with the hat on the peg and the emptied plate, all the small evidences of a thing taking root.

* * *

Afterward they spread the map on the kitchen table, because warmth made boldness, and boldness made planning, and none of them pretended anymore that they had gathered for apples alone.

Ines supplied the paper — the backsheets of old hospital forms, blank on one side, ruled faintly with the ghosts of columns — and Brandt drew, because he had a clerk's hand, precise and unlovely and perfectly legible. He sketched the workshop from memory: the long front room with its banks of presses, the composing room behind, the records room in the old stone annex at the rear where the walls were a meter thick and the window was barred. The loading yard. The side door that stuck in cold weather. The cellar hatch beneath the storeroom, which Tomas knew opened onto a drainage culvert that ran — the boy leaned over the table, tapping with a grimy finger — under the yard wall and up into the alley behind the tannery.

"This grate," Tomas said. "It's rusted through at the hinge. I've been meaning to fix it for Marek for two years. I'm glad now I'm lazy."

"You are many things," Ines said, "but lazy has never been among them. You've been watching those guards closer than any of us."

"I count them." He said it simply, the way he might have said *I sweep the floor*. "Days there's four. Nights there's three, unless the sergeant brings his card game, and then it's two walking and four sitting down, which is worse than it sounds because the sitters get bored and bored men walk places they shouldn't." He traced the patrol route on Brandt's map, a loop around the building, another through the yard, a gap of perhaps ninety seconds at the rear corner when the walker reached the street side and the sitter was deepest into his cards. "Ninety seconds. Maybe eighty. I've counted it eleven times."

"Eleven times," Brandt repeated. He looked at the boy with an expression Ines couldn't immediately name, and then could: it was the look of a man seeing a weapon where he'd expected a child. "You understand that if we're caught, counting won't save you. They don't interrogate apprentices. They just—" He stopped himself.

"Hang them?" Tomas finished, level. "I know. My father was shot for less than counting. At least I'd die knowing the number."

The kitchen went very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, far down toward the river, the ice groaned — a long, low complaint, the sound of a giant shifting in sleep — and all three of them looked toward the window at once, hearing in it the same thing: the calendar, ticking faster than the stove.

"We're not going to die," Ines said, when the groan had faded. "We're going to be careful, and we're going to be lucky, and in the spring we're going to stand in the ashes and lie about how bored we were all winter." She said it firmly, and she mostly believed it, and she could see in both their faces that they appreciated the firmness more than the truth of it. "Now. The records room. Brandt. The lock."

"Standard army pattern, double-bitted. I have a wax impression Tomas made—" He glanced at the boy, who produced from his coat lining a small flat slab of pale wax wrapped in cloth, the key's teeth printed in it like a sentence. "—and the guards' keys live on a ring at the sergeant's desk, which means our problem isn't cutting a copy. Our problem is getting near the ring." He smoothed the map with both hands. "But locks aren't really the problem. Locks are mechanical. Men are the problem. Weiss has doubled the watch since the laundress talked. He suspects someone. He doesn't know whom, and his suspicion is general, which is the only reason we're all still free — suspicion spread thin across a whole town catches no one. But if it ever narrows..."

"Then it narrows on me first," Ines said. "The laundress saw me at the workshop. Not you two."

"Which is exactly why you'll be the least suspected," Brandt said. "They questioned you and released you. In their ledger, you're already accounted for. Cleared. It's the crudest kind of accounting, but it holds." He gave a thin, tired smile. "I know how their ledgers hold. I kept enough of them."

Outside, the light had gone fully dark, and the ice groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere a dog began to bark and was silenced by a shout. The war moved through the town at night like weather.

Tomas rolled up the map with careful hands, squaring the edges the way he squared everything. "Same time next week?" he asked, and then flushed, hearing in his own voice how much the question wanted.

"Every week," Ines said. "There will be apples. There may eventually even be butter, if the farmer's wife continues to owe me for the splint I put on her son's arm." She walked them to the door. "Hats," she said, and took them down from the peg herself, and handed each man his own, and the gesture was so small and so domestic that Brandt blinked at her like a man struck by sunlight.

At the gate they separated — Tomas down the lane, quick and low, Brandt toward the river road, slow and strolling — and Ines stood in her doorway in the cold until both shapes had dissolved into the dark, listening to the river grind its teeth.

Then she went inside, banked the stove, and knelt by the third board from the window. She didn't lift it. She only laid her palm flat against the wood, over the empty space where two hundred and eleven names had slept for two winters, and felt the faint vibration of the ice coming up through the ground like a pulse.

"Soon," she whispered to the hollow. "Hold on. We're coming for you."

Above the town, the stars wheeled indifferent and bright, and under the river the ice cracked and knit and cracked again, counting down toward the thaw in a language only patient people could hear.
