## 9. Colonel Weiss

Colonel Weiss had learned, over thirty years of soldiering, that the truth of an army could be read in its paper.

Not in its orders — orders were written to be obeyed, and obedience told you nothing. Not in its dispatches, which were composed by men who wished to be promoted. But in the ledgers, the requisition forms, the casualty returns with their careful columns and their small, deliberate silences. Paper was where an army kept its conscience, folded into quarters and filed under the wrong heading. If you wanted to know what a war cost, do not ask the generals. Ask the clerks.

He thought this as his boots rang on the workshop floorboards that grey March morning, and he thought it without irony, though irony was there for the taking: here he stood, in a town whose printing presses had given it a free newspaper before he was born, surveying the largest single collection of inconvenient paper he had ever been ordered to make disappear.

The workshop smelled of ink and cold iron and, beneath that, something sweeter — apple wood shavings, perhaps, from some long-finished repair. Morning light came through the tall windows in slanted panes, catching the dust that hung above the presses like a congregation that would not disperse. The army had been here three weeks now, and already the room had changed character. Crates stood where type cases had been. Soldiers moved along the shelving with the unhurried diligence of men who understood that nothing they did today would ever be praised. A corporal with chalked hands marked each volume before it went into a box: *D-4, D-5, D-6*, in a hand so uniform it might have been stamped.

Weiss walked the rows slowly. He liked to inspect things himself. It was a habit from the eastern frontier, where a colonel who trusted reports alone woke one morning to find the report had been describing a different war entirely.

"Progress?" he asked.

Captain Ehrenberg, his adjutant, consulted a clipboard with the reverence other men reserved for scripture. "Records room is nearly done, sir. Four hundred volumes inventoried. Another two hundred in the granary annexe. The press crew begins crating the movable type Thursday."

"And the burn list?"

Ehrenberg's pencil paused. It was a small pause, half a heartbeat, but Weiss caught it the way he caught everything, because catching things was the only talent left to him at fifty-eight besides riding and regretting.

"The burn list is being compiled, sir."

"By whom?"

"Clerk Brandt, sir. Seconded from records division. He arrived Tuesday."

Ah. Yes. Weiss had read the file on Aldous Brandt with more attention than he gave most files, because files like that one were how a colonel kept his instincts fed. A clerk of eleven years' service, competent past the point of invisibility, until the winter of 1909–10, when something had gone wrong in a district office far from any front and Brandt had requested transfer with a persistence that bordered on insubordination. No disciplinary record. No explanation. Just a man who had spent seven years volunteering for postings nobody wanted, as though distance were a penance he was administering to himself.

Weiss had known such men before. They made excellent instruments and poor comrades. An instrument with a wound in it, however, required handling.

"Have him report to me after inspection," Weiss said.

* * *

The records room occupied what had once been the workshop's bindery, a long chamber with a vaulted ceiling and windows set high enough that no passerby could see within. The army had added a lock to the door, then a second lock, then a sentry's stool, because bureaucracy, like nature, abhorred an unguarded vacuum. Inside, the shelves ran floor to ceiling, and every shelf sagged under the accumulated memory of the river district: tax rolls going back sixty years; shipping manifests; militia musters from a war so old its veterans were all dead; hospital registers; court minutes; school attendance books with children's names in faded brown ink, children now old enough to have sons at the front.

And threaded through all of it, like rot through timber, the military returns.

Weiss pulled a volume at random and opened it. *Third Garrison District, quarterly casualty return.* Names in one column. Disposition in the next: killed, wounded, missing, discharged. His eye ran down the page with the ease of long practice, reading not the entries but the spaces between them. Here, a cluster of "missing" dated the same week — nine men, ten, twelve, all missing in the same sector, which meant either a catastrophic ambush nobody had reported or a category into which someone had poured something too awkward to name. Here, a "discharged" with no discharge papers referenced. Here, a death recorded as "dysentery, field hospital," when Weiss knew — because he had signed the order transferring those particular patients — that the dysentery ward had held four men and all four had died of a court-martial's verdict.

He closed the book gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child.

People imagined that men like Weiss felt nothing about such work. The assumption amused him, distantly, the way all assumptions about soldiers amused him. He felt a great deal. He simply had arranged his feelings years ago, the way a quartermaster arranges stores: everything labeled, everything weighed, nothing perishable left where it could spoil the rest.

Order required forgetting. This was not cruelty; it was arithmetic. A nation at war could not afford to carry every grief at full weight through every street. The widow who received the letter saying her son was *missing, presumed* — she was spared the worse letter, the true one. The town that believed its boys had fallen honorably against the enemy was spared the knowledge that three of them had turned and run at the sound of shellfire, and been stopped by their own side, and buried behind the wire with their faces to the ground. Was that a lie? Weiss had wrestled with the question once, in a tent in the east, in the year of the great retreat, and had concluded that it was not. It was load-bearing. Remove it and the whole structure of morale — mothers' letters, recruits' courage, the quiet endurance of towns like this one — came down on everyone's heads.

The dead did not care what was written about them. But the living needed stories, and stories required editing.

This was what he believed. He had believed it so long and so completely that the belief had worn smooth inside him, like a stone carried in a pocket, and he could take it out and turn it over in any weather and find no crack in it. Almost none. There was a hairline one, very old, running through the year 1910, but he did not take that stone out anymore.

"Weiss." He said his own name aloud sometimes, testing it. *Vice.* That was what the word meant in the old tongue his grandmother had spoken. His family had laughed about it — a boy named Vice who would grow up straight as a plumb line. He had spent his whole life proving them right, and the proving had cost exactly what such proving always costs, which was everything except itself.

He replaced the volume and continued down the shelves, and it was here, at the third bay, between the hospital registers and the garrison returns, that he found the anomaly.

A ledger. Not army-issue — the binding was civilian, good quality, stitched rather than glued, the kind of work this workshop had produced for forty years. It sat on the military shelf where it had no business sitting, catalogued under a number that Ehrenberg's inventory assigned indiscriminately, and Weiss understood at once, with the cold drop of certainty he had learned to trust more than any intelligence officer, that this book was not part of the archive at all.

It was a visitor. Something brought here, hidden here, in the weeks since the requisition — or before it, waiting for the requisition like a seed waits for rain.

He opened it.

The handwriting was fine, disciplined, a nurse's hand or a schoolmistress's — ruled lines of names, each entry compact and complete: name, rank, regiment, date, place, manner of death. And beside each entry, in smaller script, the thing that made Weiss stand very still: the official disposition. *Listed as missing. Listed as deserter. Not listed.*

Page after page. Dozens of entries. Perhaps a hundred. Perhaps more — the ledger was thick, and some pages were dense as frost patterns.

He recognized the format immediately, because he had invented it. Or rather, because the omission reports he had signed for fifteen years followed exactly this architecture, inverted: the official column and the true one, except that in his reports the true column existed nowhere, and here it existed everywhere, in a stranger's careful hand, with dates he remembered and places he remembered and — he turned back two pages — one entry he remembered with a specificity that stopped his breath.

*Corporal Voss. Died of wounds, field hospital, third hour. Held my hand at the end. Official disposition: MISSING IN ACTION.*

Held my hand at the end.

Weiss stood in the vaulted silence with the ledger open in his hands, and for a moment — a moment he would later deny having had — he was not a colonel conducting an inspection. He was a man reading another man's mail, except the other man was dead and the mail was addressed to history.

Someone had done his work in reverse. Someone had taken the edited story and restored the cut passages, entry by entry, with the patience of an embroiderer. And not from malice — he could see that; there was no rage in the hand, only care — but from the conviction that the names mattered. That a soldier who died holding a nurse's hand deserved a different line in the record than *missing*.

He understood the conviction. God help him, he understood it better than the woman — he was sure it was a woman, the hand said so — could ever understand his own. They were two editors of the same text, she cutting nothing and him cutting everything, and between them lay the whole insoluble argument of the age.

But understanding was not permission.

He closed the ledger and held it a moment longer than necessary. Then he set it back on the shelf, precisely where it had been, spine flush with the neighboring volumes.

"Ehrenberg," he called.

His adjutant appeared in the doorway, clipboard first, face second.

"This shelf section — the military returns — I want them crated first. Ahead of schedule. Before anything else leaves this building." He kept his voice level, conversational, the voice of a man discussing firewood. "They go onto the barges under guard. Double manifest. And the crates are stencilled *BLANK FORMS*."

"Blank forms, sir?"

"No one steals blank forms, Captain. No one even looks at them. That is the entire value of the designation." He let his gaze travel the length of the records room, the high blind windows, the sagging shelves, the thousand sleeping names. "The rest can wait for the thaw. This cannot."

"Yes, sir." Ehrenberg wrote it down. Then, because he was a decent man and therefore occasionally careless: "Shall I have the clerk re-inventory the section? Brandt's been through it twice — he'd flag any irregularities—"

"No."

The word came out harder than Weiss intended, and he watched it land, watched Ehrenberg blink and straighten. Weiss softened nothing — softening was for chaplains — but he amended: "I'll review the inventory myself. Some sections want a senior eye."

Because here was the thing he did not say, the thing he filed away in the drawer where he kept the year 1910: whoever owned that ledger had put it *here*. Into the one place in this town guaranteed to be sealed, guarded, and scheduled for destruction — the safest hiding place in a burning house. That was not panic. That was calculation. Someone in this town was clever enough to hide a truth inside the machine built to erase it, and clever people who cared that much about paper did not stop at one ledger.

Somewhere beyond these high windows, a person was counting the days until the barges the way he was counting them. Two watchers, one clock.

He almost admired it. Almost.

"Anything else, sir?" Ehrenberg asked.

Weiss looked at the shelf where the ledger sat, anonymous among its enemies, patient as a planted mine.

"Double the sentry on this door at night," he said. "And find out which civilians have reason to enter the workshop grounds. Nurses, contractors, tradesmen. Anyone with a routine." He picked up his gloves from the crate lid where he'd left them. "Someone in this town loves these documents more than we do. I want to know who — before the ice breaks."

* * *

That evening, in the garrison canteen, Weiss played cards with three officers and lost eleven hands out of fifteen, deliberately, because a commander who won at cards collected resentments the way wet wool collects burrs, and he preferred his men relaxed around him. Between deals he watched the door. Clerks came and went with message satchels. Orderlies carried in the smell of snow. At the far table, a group of noncoms argued cheerfully about horses.

And near the stove, half in shadow, sat a lean man in civilian clothes with the posture of a filing cabinet — Clerk Brandt, drinking tea instead of schnapps, watching the room with eyes that counted everything and enjoyed nothing.

Weiss studied him across the smoky air. The file said broken. The posture said functional. But there was a third thing in the man's face, visible only when the lamplight crossed it at a certain angle: the expression of a man carrying something in a pocket that he cannot stop touching. Guilt, or contraband. In Weiss's long experience the two weighed the same and behaved identically, and only time told you which one you were holding.

Brandt felt the attention and looked up. Their eyes met for a moment — a moment in which Weiss smiled, pleasantly, and raised his teacup an inch, a salute between professionals — and saw the clerk go pale beneath his lamplight tan, pale and controlled, the way men go when they realize the inspection has begun and they have not finished tidying.

Interesting.

Weiss returned to his losing hand. He discarded a king he could easily have kept, and thought about ice, and barges, and the arithmetic of days. The engineers said the thaw would come in three weeks, perhaps four. Three weeks was enough — barely — if the incriminating returns went aboard first, if the sentries stayed sober, if no one in this stubborn, quiet town decided that a ledger was worth more than a life.

He intended to see that no one did. He took no pleasure in the prospect of thwarting them. Pleasure was not part of the work. But he would do it thoroughly, because thoroughness was the only prayer he had ever offered, and because he genuinely, sincerely, with thirty years of smoothed stone in his chest, believed that forgetting was mercy — that the town would thank him someday, in the dumb grateful peace of a town that never learns what its sons truly died of.

Across the canteen, Clerk Brandt rose, drained his tea, and left without looking at anyone — which is itself a way of looking at everyone.

Weiss marked it. He marked everything.

Three weeks, he thought, gathering the cards for the next deal. Give them three weeks, and see what love of paper is worth against the spring.

Outside the canteen windows, the river lay white and silent under the moon, holding its cargo of secrets the way it held everything: patiently, and not forever.
