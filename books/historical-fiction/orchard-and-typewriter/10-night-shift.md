## 10. Night Shift

The garrison kept its own hours, and Tomas learned them the way he had once learned the rhythms of the press: by listening.

By day the workshop belonged to soldiers — boots on the floorboards where his master's slippers used to whisper, crates thumped and roped, orders barked in a language of abbreviations he was not meant to understand. But at night the building exhaled. The soldiers withdrew to their barracks two streets over, leaving only a sentry at the loading dock and, after midnight, sometimes no one but the dark itself, settling into the corners like soot.

It was Marek who first suggested it, or something like it. *They'll want someone to keep the presses oiled,* the old man had said, staring at nothing, his hands folded in his lap as if they'd been confiscated too. *A press left dry is a press ruined. Tell them you know the machines better than any soldier does.* He had said it flatly, without looking at Tomas, and only later — much later, turning the words over in the dark of the loft above the workshop — did Tomas hear what else was folded inside them: *keep the presses alive. Keep them ready.*

So he volunteered.

Sergeant Halik, who managed the workshop detail with the weary contempt of a man assigned to guard paper, had laughed when the boy offered. "Night shifts? Alone? You afraid the presses get lonely?"

"They bind," Tomas said. "Rollers warp. Ink skins in the tins. My master says—" He stopped himself. "He said a neglected machine costs more than an attentive one."

Halik shrugged in the way that shrugs off responsibility onto someone else's shoulders. "Fine. If anything's broken in the morning, it comes out of your hide." And that was all. A signature scrawled on a duty roster, and Tomas belonged to the nights.

* * *

The first night shift, he did nothing but oil the machines and listen to his own heart.

The workshop after dark was a different country. Moonlight came through the high windows in pale trapezoids, lying across the floor like dropped sheets of paper nobody had dared to print on. The great press — the old one, the one Marek called *the widow* because it had outlived everyone who built her — stood in the corner with its iron arms folded, patient as a church. The smaller jobbing press hunched beside it. The type cabinets lined the wall, hundreds of shallow drawers, each one a small wooden mouth full of metal teeth.

Tomas walked among them with his oil can and rag, touching each machine the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. He could not have said why it felt like trespass and homecoming at once. These were still his machines. The army owned the building now, the paper, the crates, the very air, apparently — requisitioned everything down to Marek's apron — but they did not own this: the knowledge of which bearing sang when it ran dry, which screw backed out if you looked at it wrong, how to coax the flywheel through its dead center without stalling. That knowledge lived in his hands, and hands were not yet requisitioned.

At eleven the sentry on the dock made his round past the windows, boots ringing on the cobbles, and paused — Tomas froze, rag in hand — then moved on. At half past twelve the same boots again, slower now, bored. At two there was a gap, long enough that Tomas counted four hundred heartbeats before he heard them return. He wrote the times in his head. He had always been good at counting; it was what apprentices did — sheets per hour, ems per line. Now he counted guards instead of quads, rotations instead of impressions.

By the fourth night he had the pattern whole. The sentry walked the perimeter at fixed intervals until midnight, then the intervals stretched — a stop at the cookhouse for coffee, a long pause against the warm brick of the bakery chimney next door, once, memorably, twenty minutes of silence that Tomas spent certain the man had fallen asleep standing up. Between one and three the building was, for stretches of six, eight, ten minutes, unwatched from the inside. Not safe. Never safe. But *possible*, which was a word Tomas was learning to treasure.

On the fifth night he brought wax.

* * *

Marek had shown him the key cabinet on the second day of the requisition, back when the old man still spoke in complete sentences. It stood in the passage between the pressroom and the records room — a new door, a new lock, brass-faced, army issue, bolted onto wood that had never needed a lock in forty years. Behind it lived everything the officers wanted forgotten: the town's newspaper archive, the granary ledgers, birth registers, marriage rolls, the casualty lists with their careful blanks, and — Tomas had seen it carried in on a clerk's cart, wrapped in sacking like contraband, though neither of them had known what it was at the time — a small green ledger that had belonged, somehow, to the nurse Eriksen.

He didn't know why she'd want it back. He knew only that when she'd come asking her quiet questions about which crates went where, her eyes had gone to that records-room door and stayed there a half-second longer than curiosity required. Two watchers recognizing each other across a room full of soldiers. Neither had spoken. Speaking was a luxury the workshop could no longer afford.

But the lock. The lock was a problem with a shape, and shapes had solutions.

He stole the wax from the chandler's shop — a stub of sealing wax, red as a wound, paid for honestly with coin he'd been saving for boots, because stealing from Frau Lindqvist would have felt like one theft too many in a town already being picked clean. On the sixth night shift, during the long gap between rounds, he knelt at the records-room door with the wax cupped in his palm like a moth he was afraid to crush.

His hands shook. He noticed this with a strange detachment, as though observing another boy — some farm kid, some stranger — kneeling in the dark doing a thing that could get him shot. Deserters got shot; he'd seen the notices nailed to the granary door. What did saboteurs get? Worse. He breathed through his nose, slow, the way Marek had taught him to breathe before pulling the bar on the big press: *the machine feels your hurry, boy. Give it calm or give it blood.*

The wax softened against the brass. He pressed it into the keyhole's shadow, held it, held it, counting — thirty, thirty-one — then eased it out and looked at what it held.

A ghost. An impression of the key's bite: notches and wards rendered in miniature, precise as type set by a master hand. He turned it in the moonlight. It wasn't a key. But it was the memory of one, and memory, he was beginning to understand, was the only thing in this town the army hadn't figured out how to confiscate.

He slid it into the lining of his boot, where it lay against his ankle, warm and secret, all night long.

* * *

The sergeant found him on the ninth night.

Tomas heard him coming before he saw him — a voice raised in song two streets away, wavering, cheerful, getting closer, and beneath it boots that did not belong to any rotation Tomas had memorized. Off-schedule. Off-rhythm. His whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the March damp.

He was at the records-room end of the passage. Of course he was. He'd been checking the lock again, confirming the impression hadn't smeared, confirming the door sat flush in its frame — legitimate reasons, he told himself, all legitimate — when the side door banged open and Sergeant Drasch stumbled in with a lantern and half a bottle.

Drasch. The worst possible answer to a question nobody should ask. A thick-necked man with a temper like a struck match, currently lit.

"Who's there?" The lantern swung. Light raked across the type cabinets, the widow's black shoulder, the passage wall. "Who's in here?"

Tomas stepped out into the open, deliberately, because a shape glimpsed in shadows was a spy and a boy standing under lamplight was a boy fixing a machine. He had the roller wrench in his hand. He hadn't planned that either, but his hand had closed around it off the bench as he moved, and now it hung at his side, heavy, honest, an alibi made of iron.

"Tomas, sir. The apprentice."

"The what?" Drasch weaved forward, squinting. The bottle dangled from his fist, catching light. "Apprentice to what? This is army property now, boy. Army property." Each word arrived separately, like parcels thrown from a moving cart.

"Yes, sir. I'm on night maintenance. The rollers—"

"The rollers." Drasch laughed, a wet sound. He lurched closer, and the lantern light fell full on Tomas, and Tomas understood with total clarity that his life in the next ten seconds would depend entirely on whether a drunk man believed a story. "Nobody fixes rollers at two in the morning. Nobody. What were you doing by that door?"

Every lie Tomas had ever considered — I lost my rag, I heard a noise, I couldn't sleep — arranged themselves in a panicked parade behind his eyes and were dismissed one by one, too thin, too thin, all of them too thin. What remained was the truth wearing work clothes.

"A jammed roller, sir. On the jobber." He pointed back toward the small press, and his voice — this astonished him — came out steady, even bored. "It binds near dawn when the temperature drops. The ink goes stiff in the ducts. If I don't free it before morning, the detail loses half a day waiting on me." He let a note of grievance creep in. "I'd rather do it at night than be shouted at for it at seven."

Drasch stared at him. The lantern flame shivered between them. Somewhere outside, the real sentry's boots passed on his round, oblivious, and Tomas thought absurdly: *you're late, you're always late, where are you when a man needs a witness.*

The sergeant took another step. Close enough now that Tomas could smell the spirits on him, sour-sweet, and see the suspicion working through the drink like a fish under ice — visible, circling, unable to break through.

"You were at the records door," Drasch said.

"I pass it every time I go to the ink cellar, sir. It's the only way through." This was true. Tomas blessed Marek silently, blessed the architecture of the old workshop, blessed every stubborn fact that happened to be on his side. "You can check the roller if you like. It's still warm."

That was the gamble. He'd actually done the work first — freed the jammed roller at one o'clock as part of his rounds, because Marek's training lived in his spine and shortcuts were sacrilege. The ducts would still carry heat. The ink would be soft.

Drasch swayed. Looked at the door. Looked at the boy. The fish under the ice turned, turned again, and sank.

"Huh," he said at last, with the enormous gravity of the drunk arriving at a conclusion. "Well. Fix your roller." He raised the bottle in a vague salute and began, horribly, to sing again, some barracks song about a girl from the coast, and wove off toward the front room, where he proceeded to fall asleep across two crates of sealed documents with the lantern guttering beside his ear.

Tomas stood in the passage a long time, listening to the snoring, feeling his heartbeat arrive in his ears like distant artillery. Then, very carefully, he put the wrench back on the bench, wiped the handle — his palms had sweated through everything — and finished his rounds with steady hands that were not his own.

In the loft afterward, he took the wax impression from his boot and held it up to the moon. Red as ever. Unsmudged. He thought about Drasch's lantern sweeping the wall, about the fish under the ice, about how close the bottom of the river was to everything now.

Then he thought about Nurse Eriksen's eyes on the records-room door, and the green ledger under sacking, and the blank spaces in the official lists that everyone pretended not to see. He was sixteen years old. He had just learned that terror and exhilaration were the same sensation wearing different uniforms, and that he could tell them apart only afterward.

He slept with the wax under his pillow, and dreamed of letters marching.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, smelling of thaw. When the day detail arrived, Halik found the jobbing press running sweet, the rollers clean, the ink tins skimmed and covered, and grunted the closest thing to approval his rank allowed. "Machine behaves better for you than for us," he said. "Maybe we should requisition you."

Tomas almost smiled. Almost.

Because while Halik talked, Tomas saw, through the high window, a sight that stopped his breath: at the dock, where the barges would tie up when the ice broke, soldiers were driving new pilings, extending the moorings — making them longer, stronger, ready for more weight than anyone had mentioned. And beside them, unhurried, unmistakable, Colonel Weiss himself stood watching the river, gloved hands clasped behind his back, reading the ice the way Marek read proofs: patiently, expertly, hunting for flaws.

The thaw was coming. Everyone knew that. But the colonel's face, as he studied the cracking white sheet of the river, wore the expression of a man deciding not to wait for it.
