## 11. The Name in the Margins

The records room smelled of dust and lamp oil and, beneath both, something older — the sour breath of paper that had been shut away from light for years. Aldous Brandt had learned to breathe it in slowly, through his mouth, so that his coughing would not draw the sergeant's attention from across the hall. Three days he had been at this work. Three days of lifting volumes down from shelves that groaned under the weight of a town's memory, weighing them, cataloguing them, writing on his inventory sheets the numbers and dates that would decide which of them went into crates for the barges.

He was good at this work. That was the obscenity of it. His hands moved with the fluency of two decades' service, sorting and stacking with a clerk's economy, and no one watching would have guessed that every box he sealed felt to him like a coffin being nailed shut.

* * *

It was the fourth crate of the morning that betrayed him. A regimental file, bound in gray board gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked from handling. The label had faded to a ghost: *7th Jäger — Field Reports — 1910.* Seven years ago. Brandt knew the year the way a man knows the year of his father's death — not by effort, but because everything afterward was measured against it.

He should have passed it along with the others. He told himself he would pass it along with the others. But his hands set it apart anyway, laid it flat on the trestle table as if it were something fragile, and when the sergeant drifted past on his rounds Brandt angled his body over it, casual as a man consulting a reference.

The room's lamp threw his shadow long across the page when he opened it. He read standing up, because sitting would have meant staying, and some animal part of him wanted to be able to walk away.

*Report of engagement losses, action near Kaltenbach, 14 October 1910.*

He had written those words himself. Not these exact words — the ink had been different then, the desk different, a younger man's hand moving across the form with the smooth confidence of someone who believed that what he wrote became true simply because he wrote it. *Engagement losses.* Fourteen men. He remembered the number; he had entered fourteen where the draft report said forty-one, and he had done it without trembling, without even pausing over his tea, because Colonel Weiss's adjutant had stood at his shoulder explaining that the higher figure reflected "irregularities" that would trouble the ministry, and that a clerk who could not distinguish between what happened and what was recorded had no future in the service.

Twenty-seven names, erased between one line and the next. He had never learned them. That had been part of the arrangement — the corrected figures came to him already stripped of detail, a column of arithmetic instead of a list of men. Someone else did the forgetting before it ever reached his desk. He had only pressed the seal. He had told himself for seven years that pressing a seal was not the same as telling a lie.

Here, in this gray folder, in this room full of doomed paper, was the original draft. The adjutant's preliminary return, filed carelessly or perhaps deliberately, tucked behind the fair copy like a body buried under a floorboard. And in the margin, in a hand Brandt did not recognize — small, slanted, urgent — someone had written the names. All twenty-seven of them. Written vertically, crammed into the narrow white space beside the falsified figures, as if the writer had understood there would never be room anywhere else.

He read the first name twice. *Kovac.* Then the next. *Lindqvist.* His lips moved without sound. The margin ran out of space halfway down and the hand had continued onto the bottom edge of the page, sideways, upside down, letters marching off the paper's edge like soldiers refusing an order to halt.

Brandt stood in the lamplight for a long time. Somewhere beyond the shuttered window a sentry's boots rang on frozen cobblestones. The river spoke in its sleep under the ice, that deep groaning the whole town had begun to hear at night.

Then he did the thing he had not known he had come here to do. He took out his knife, slit along the binding's inner seam, and drew the page free. The rest of the folder he closed and stacked with the others, unremarkable, gray, anonymous. The page itself he folded once, twice, until it was small enough to disappear inside his glove, against his palm, where his own pulse beat against twenty-seven names.

His hands were steady. That surprised him most. For seven years they had not been.

* * *

He walked to the orchard house after dark, taking the long way past the granary so that any watcher would see a bored clerk stretching his legs, a man with nowhere particular to go. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the frozen ruts. Ines Eriksen's windows showed lamplight the color of weak tea. There was a barrel of apples by her door, packed in straw, and the smell of them reached him even through the cold — sweet, cidery, patient — and it struck him suddenly that he could not remember the last time he had smelled anything but ink and gun oil and the cold iron breath of a garrison.

She opened the door before he knocked. Perhaps she had seen him coming down the lane; perhaps she had simply been waiting, the way she seemed always to be waiting, with the vigilance of a woman who had learned that the difference between safety and catastrophe was often a matter of ninety seconds.

"You found something." It was not a question.

"I need to sit down," he said, which was true, though not for the reason she would assume. His legs had carried him all the way from the workshop on pure momentum, and now that the door was shut behind him and the warmth closed over his face, the momentum failed all at once, the way ice fails.

* * *

She gave him tea he barely tasted and the chair nearest the stove, and Tomas — the boy had been there, mending a broken typecase across the table, and rose to leave — she stopped with a hand on his sleeve. "Stay," she said. Not unkindly. "If we are going to trust each other, let us begin practicing."

So the boy stayed, folding himself back onto his stool, watching Brandt with the frank, measuring gaze of someone too young to have learned to disguise curiosity.

Brandt set the folded page on the table between them and did not unfold it at first. He looked at his own hands, splayed on either side of it, the hands of a man who had spent twenty years making paper do things paper should not have to do.

"Before I show you this," he said, "you should know what I am."

Ines said nothing. She had a way of not speaking that made silence feel like a held door.

"I was a records clerk for nineteen years," Brandt said. "Field returns, casualty ledgers, commendation rolls. I was considered meticulous. Commended twice." He heard his own voice and marveled at how level it was, how much like reading aloud. "Meticulous means you make the lie look exactly like the truth. Carelessness gets discovered. Precision never does."

Tomas had gone very still.

"Seven years ago I processed a report out of Kaltenbach. An action against villagers — not soldiers, villagers, though the distinction was mostly academic since half the village had been feeding partisans. Forty-one dead." He unfolded the page now, slowly, smoothing it flat against the wood with both palms, the way one smooths a sheet before printing. "The report that reached my desk said fourteen. Engagement losses. Clean arithmetic. And I signed it. I sealed it. I filed the correction and destroyed nothing, because there was nothing left to destroy — the names had already been taken out before I ever saw them. I didn't know their names. I told myself that mattered. That I was only a seal, not a pen."

Ines had drawn the lamp closer. Her eyes went over the page — the official figures in their neat column, and then the margin, that desperate vertical script climbing the white space, twenty-seven names written where no names were permitted to live.

"Someone wrote them back," she said softly.

"Yes. God knows who. Some other clerk, some officer's secretary, someone whose hand shook less than mine. They committed them to the only place left — the edge of the lie itself." He touched the margin without touching it, hovering a finger above the ink. "I found it today in the fourth crate of the morning, in a folder destined for the barges, and I stole it. I want you to understand that word. Stole. From the army I served for nineteen years. And it is the first honest act of my career."

The stove ticked. Outside, far off, the river made its long complaint against the ice.

"Why tell us?" Tomas asked. His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through it. "Why now?"

Brandt looked at the boy — sixteen, ink-stained to the wrist, carrying more grief than his shoulders had been built for — and found he could not give the easy answer, because there wasn't one.

"Because I have been carrying this for seven years the way you carry a stone in your boot," he said. "Small enough to ignore. Heavy enough to change how you walk. And because three weeks ago a nurse walked into a records room and looked at me as if she knew what I was, and I have not slept properly since." He turned to Ines. "You asked me, that first day, whether I ever checked the figures against the hospital returns. You knew. You knew what I'd been, and you still let me work beside you. Why?"

It was her turn to be silent a moment. When she answered, her voice was quiet and worn smooth as river stone.

"Because a man who steals a page of names out of a doomed archive is not the same man who seals them away," she said. "And because I have learned that shame is not the end of a person. It can be the beginning. Mine was."

She rose and went to the corner where the loose floorboard lay, and lifted it, and showed them the empty cavity beneath — dark, smelling of apples and earth, the ghost-shape of a ledger pressed into the dust.

"This is where I kept every name they tried to erase," she said. "Voss. Halonen. The boy from the mill with the burned hands. I held Corporal Voss's hand while he died and watched them write *missing.* After enough of that, you stop being able to not write it down." She replaced the board and stood over it, one hand flat on the floor as if feeling for a pulse through the house itself. "Your page belongs with them, Herr Brandt. Twenty-seven names that were almost forgotten twice."

Brandt felt something loosen in his chest — not relief, nothing so kind, but a shifting, like ice deciding to move. He thought of the report in his glove all afternoon, warm against his skin, and understood that he had not stolen it for the archive, nor for history, nor even for the dead of Kaltenbach. He had stolen it because he could no longer stand to be the man who had sealed it.

"There will be more," he said. "If one draft survived, others did. Sloppy adjutants, lazy clerks, officers who kept private copies against their rivals. This archive is seven years of everyone's insurance against everyone else. Somewhere in those crates are every name this war has eaten and called a rounding error." He looked from Ines to Tomas and back. "We cannot save the papers. But the names — the names we could take out of here one page at a time, the way I took this one."

"The press," Tomas breathed. He was looking at the page the way Marek looked at a fresh ream of paper. "The old press in the back shop. If we had the names set in type—"

"Tomas." Ines's voice was gentle but it stopped him like a hand on the chest. "Not tonight. Tonight we only know it's possible." She gathered the page up with a care that Brandt recognized — the care of a woman who had handled the belongings of the dying — and slid it into her apron pocket, against her heart, the same place he had carried it.

But as she did, her fingers stilled. She drew the page out again and held it closer to the lamp, frowning, and turned it so the light fell slantwise across the lower corner.

"Herr Brandt," she said slowly. "Look at the filing stamp."

He leaned in. The purple stamp was faded but legible: *Processed, Records Section, 19 October 1910.* Beneath it, in smaller characters, the clerk's initials — his own initials, cut into the rubber seven years ago by his own order, in his own hand.

And beside the initials, half-erased by the stamp pad's uneven pressure, was a second mark. Small, deliberate, pressed into the wet ink by someone else's finger before it dried. A tiny cross. Or perhaps — Brandt's blood went cold as he bent nearer — the first letter of a name.

Ines met his eyes across the lamp.

"It's a signal," she whispered. "Whoever wrote these names in the margin wanted whoever processed this page to find them. They marked it. Which means somewhere in this archive—" her hand tightened on the paper, and outside, downriver, the ice boomed like distant artillery, "—there may be more pages. Marked ones. Waiting to be found."

Tomas looked from one of them to the other, the lamplight jumping in his eyes.

"How many?" he asked.

No one answered. The river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere beneath the floorboards the orchard house seemed to hold its breath along with them.
