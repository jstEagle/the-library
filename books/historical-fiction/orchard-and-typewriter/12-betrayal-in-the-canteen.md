## 12. Betrayal in the Canteen

The laundress's name was Petra Voss — no relation to the corporal, though the coincidence had struck Ines like a bell the first time she'd heard it spoken in the hospital corridor. She was a broad-shouldered woman of fifty with forearms like a stevedore's and a face weathered into permanent squint, as though she'd spent her life looking into a sun that never quite arrived. She boiled the garrison's linens in great copper coppers behind the workshop annex, and her hands were always pink from the water, and she sang hymns off-key while she wrung out sheets stiff with other men's blood.

Ines liked her. That was the first mistake.

She'd liked her for small reasons: Petra never asked questions about the stains she washed; she hummed to dying men without being asked; she once pressed a heel of black bread into Ines's pocket on a day when the hospital had run out of everything but morphine and silence. In a town that had learned not to see, Petra still saw things — she just chose, mostly, not to speak of them.

Mostly.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with nothing. A Tuesday of gray sleet. Ines had come to the workshop at midday under her medic's pretext — checking the splint inventory stored in the loading room — and had lingered three minutes too long by the door of the records room while Brandt stood inside it with his clipboard and his careful face. Three minutes. She had counted them afterward a hundred times, the way one counts the seconds between lightning and thunder, trying to determine how far away the strike would land.

Petra was at her copper that day, steam rising around her like a curtain. Steam is honest work; it hides nothing and conceals everything, depending on which side of it you stand. Ines had nodded to her. Petra had nodded back. And then Ines had turned toward the records room, and for those three minutes her back was to the steam, and the woman behind the steam watched her stand at a locked door with an expression no nurse wears when counting splints.

Ines did not know this yet. But suspicion travels through a garrison like damp through plaster, and by Friday she could feel it in the walls.

* * *

Friday began with bread. The baker had butter again — real butter, hoarded and sold only to officers' tables, which meant someone in the supply chain had been bribed, which meant the town's economy of small corruptions was functioning normally, which meant, for once, that the world made sense. Ines bought two rolls with her medic's chit and ate one walking home along the river road, watching the ice.

The ice had changed its voice overnight. For weeks it had groaned and ticked in the cold, the sound of contraction, of tightening. Now there was a new note beneath it — a long, wet sigh, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. The cracks along the shore had widened enough that she could see water moving in them, black and quick, and where a week ago the surface had been white as paper it now showed patches of gray rot, slushy and dark, like frost melting off a windowpane.

Two more weeks. Perhaps less. Weiss would have his barges.

At the orchard house she fed the stove, took down the jar of preserved apples she was saving against some future worse than the present — there was always a future worse than the present; the war had taught everyone that arithmetic — and sat down at the kitchen table to mend bandages, because mending was what her hands knew how to do when her mind needed to be elsewhere. Her mind needed very much to be elsewhere. It wanted to be inside the workshop, counting crates, checking Tomas's wax impression against the actual lock, reviewing the plan until it wore smooth as river stone. But plans reviewed too often develop cracks of their own, seams where doubt gets in, so instead she rolled bandages and let the rhythm steady her: tear, fold, pin. Tear, fold, pin. The same motion she'd made over ten thousand wounds. If she closed her eyes she could hear the field hospital again, the moaning low chorus of it, and Corporal Voss saying *don't write me down as missing, Sister, I'm right here* —

A knock at the door.

Not Tomas's knock — his was quick and light, three raps with the edge of a knuckle, a boy's knock. Not Brandt's either; Brandt knocked like a man apologizing for existing, a soft hesitant tap-tap. This was a military knock: measured, authoritative, delivered with the flat of the hand.

Ines set down the bandage. She looked at the floorboard beneath the dresser — empty now, its secret long since moved — and felt the old reflex of concealment rise and find nothing to hold onto. There was nothing in the house to seize. That should have been comforting. It was not. An empty house tells a searcher there is nothing here, and nothing, in wartime, is rarely believed.

She opened the door on two military policemen in slate-gray greatcoats, snow gathering on their shoulders, and behind them, half-hidden by the taller one, a third figure whose pink hands she recognized before she saw anything else.

"Sister Eriksen." The taller MP held a folded paper he did not offer. "You'll come with us to the garrison office. Some questions regarding your movements near the print works."

"May I bring my needlework?" she asked, because absurdity was the only weapon left that couldn't be confiscated.

Neither of them smiled. Behind them, Petra Voss looked at the ground, and her mouth moved — whether in apology or in prayer, Ines could not tell, and decided, with a coldness that surprised her, that it hardly mattered.

* * *

The garrison office had been the town's insurance bureau before the war. The brass plaque was still screwed above the door — *HALLORAN & SONS, ASSURANCES AGAINST FIRE AND LOSS* — and Ines thought, as they marched her beneath it, that there was a whole novel in that alone. Assurances against loss. As if loss could be insured against. As if any ledger anywhere had ever been complete.

The questioning room held a desk, two chairs, a lamp, and a stove that clicked as it cooled. They left her alone for forty minutes first, which she understood perfectly well was itself a technique. She used the time to study the room. A map of the river sector hung behind the desk, weighted at the corners with spent shell casings, and she memorized what she could of it without appearing to look — the barge landing marked in red pencil, the double line indicating the ice road, the small annotation beside the workshop that read, in a hand she suspected belonged to Colonel Weiss, *demolition pending thaw.*

Demolition pending thaw. So it was written. So it was scheduled. The town's hundred-year-old press, Marek's father's press, the press that had set the first free newspaper in the valley — pending.

The door opened and a captain came in, young through the eyes and old through the mouth, carrying a folder thin enough to be encouraging or damning depending on how one read thinness. He sat, arranged the folder, and did not open it.

"Sister Eriksen. You've been volunteering at the garrison infirmary since January."

"I have."

"Your papers are in order."

"They are."

"You also visit the print workshop." He said it mildly, conversationally, a man discussing weather. "Frequently. More frequently than splint inventory requires, our quartermaster thinks."

"The army requisitioned the workshop," Ines said. "The army stores medical supplies in the workshop. When the army moves my supplies, I check on them. Would you prefer I didn't?"

He almost smiled. Almost. "I'd prefer," he said, "to understand why, on Tuesday last, you stood outside the records room for several minutes. The room contains no medical supplies, Sister. It contains paper. Dead paper. Lists and reports and ledgers nobody has opened in years. What interest does a nurse have in dead paper?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the cellar of the world, the river groaned against its shrinking margins.

"Paper interests everyone lately," Ines said. "Your requisition orders say all papers are claimed for the war effort. Mine included. Perhaps I came to see what mine looks like in a crate."

It was a risk and she knew it — offering truth as camouflage, the way a bird shows its feathers to prove it isn't hiding anything. The captain studied her for a long moment, and she met his gaze with the particular steadiness she'd learned over three years of telling families the worst news of their lives: level, compassionate, utterly unrevealing.

"Someone reported seeing you there," he said finally. "More than once. Taking an interest."

"People report many things. It's winter. There's little else to do."

"There's rationing," he said, and the word dropped between them like a coin into a well, and she heard it hit bottom, and she heard whose pocket it had come from, and her heart went quiet and hard as the ice on the river. "Extra flour. Extra tallow. Small considerations for useful information." He tapped the unopened folder. "Unfortunately for whoever reported you, information requires substance, and substance we lack. No search of your residence found anything irregular. Your passes check. Your hours check." He stood. "You're released, Sister. But I'll say it plainly, because plainness is a kindness: the print works is now a restricted zone. Nurses mend soldiers. Paper belongs to the army. Stay on your side of that line, and we'll have no further conversations."

"And if I cross the line?"

"Then the next folder won't be thin."

* * *

They walked her back through the falling snow, and the town received her the way towns receive scandal: with shutters half-opened, with greetings a fraction too warm, with the butcher giving her an extra sausage bone for the soup pot and refusing payment with a look that said, plainly, *we know something happened, and whatever it was, we're on somebody's side.* Whose, even he probably didn't know yet. Loyalty in occupied towns is like groundwater — invisible, everywhere, and rising toward whichever crack opens first.

Petra was not at her copper when Ines passed the annex. The coppers steamed unattended, and through the fogged annex window Ines could see the laundress sitting on an upturned crate, hands motionless in her lap, staring at nothing. She had taken the extra flour. She had traded three minutes of a nurse's back for a week of bread. And she was discovering, in the privacy of the steam, what every informer discovers too late: that the ration gained weighs less than the thing lost, and that the hymns will not come anymore.

Ines felt no triumph. That frightened her most of all. She felt the way one feels at a funeral for someone one loved and resented in equal measure — hollowed, uncertain what exactly has been buried. She had liked Petra. Petra had sung to dying men. And now Petra's name would go, unrecorded, unwritten, into the category of people the war had bent until they broke sideways, and Ines could not even enter her into a ledger, because there was no column for betrayal-by-hunger, and because writing her name might hang her.

Everyone's name had become a weapon. That was the whole terrible discovery of her life, distilled: a name written can kill or save, and the difference is only who holds the pen.

* * *

Tomas came to the orchard house after dark, coming the back way through the orchard with his collar up, leaving the kind of footprints that fresh snowfall would erase by morning. He didn't knock with his boy's knock. He scratched softly at the kitchen shutter, the signal they'd agreed on, and Ines let him in to the smell of stewed apples and woodsmoke, and the boy stood dripping just inside the door and said, in a voice carefully emptied:

"They doubled the watch. Two-hour rotations now, four sentries on days, six at night. They put a new chain on the records room — I saw the corporal carry it in this morning, wrapped in oilcloth like it was ammunition. And they've stopped letting anyone but Brandt and the officers inside the workshop at all. I'm supposed to tend the presses under escort. A sergeant stands behind me and watches me oil rollers and asks questions about printing that don't make sense, like he's testing whether I'll talk about something else." He pulled off his cap and turned it in his hands. "And Sister — they asked me about you. Whether the nurse talks to me. What about. Whether she's ever asked to see the presses run."

"What did you say?"

"That you asked once if the press could print bandage labels, because the infirmary was short. Which is even true." A ghost of a grin crossed his face and died. "Sergeant Hessler wrote it down anyway. Everything goes in a book now. He has a little book and he writes in it and I think—" Tomas stopped. Started again, lower. "I think the little book is going to the Colonel."

Ines poured him tea she couldn't afford and watched his hands around the cup. Sixteen years old, and he'd learned in two months what took armies decades to teach: that surveillance doesn't need to catch anyone to work. It needs only to make everyone perform innocence until innocence becomes indistinguishable from guilt, until the innocent exhaust themselves proving what no one believes.

"They'll move the barge date up," Tomas said. "That's what doubling the watch means, isn't it? It means they're scared too."

"It means," Ines said, "that we have less time than we planned for, and that every hour we spend being careful is an hour they spend getting ready." She sat across from him, the lamp between them, and told herself to say the prudent thing — to tell the boy to stay away, to keep his head down, to let the grown conspirators absorb the risk. The words assembled themselves and she declined to send them. Prudence had gotten the corporal listed as missing. Prudence was a luxury the dead could not advise.

"Tomas. Can you fire the press alone? Truly alone? No escort, no sergeant, in the dark?"

He looked up, and she saw in his face the exact moment a boy decides to become something else. "Marek says I could fire it asleep. He says my hands are better than his were at twenty." He hesitated. "There's something else. Marek gave me the molds. The original typeface molds, from the cellar — the ones his father cut. He says paper burns but letters remember. I've got them somewhere no sergeant will look."

"Where?"

Tomas smiled properly this time, and for a moment he was sixteen again. "Under the floorboards," he said. "Of the workshop cellar. Where nobody would ever hide anything important, because everybody knows that trick now — don't they, Sister?"

The lamp hissed. Outside, far off, the river spoke in its new voice, long and wet and patient, and Ines thought: *the thaw is ahead of schedule, and so must we be.*

* * *

Brandt arrived past midnight, gray-faced, having walked the long way twice to be sure he wasn't followed. He brought the news she already dreaded, delivered in the clipped tone of a clerk reading an official notice: the watch doubled, the records room re-chained, and — he set a slip of paper on the table, carbon-copy thin — a memo initialled in Weiss's own hand. *Loading operations advanced. Barges to be ready prior to break-up.*

"How far prior?" Ines asked.

Brandt's jaw worked. "The date is blank. Left blank deliberately. Which means it's whatever Weiss decides, whenever he decides it, possibly tomorrow, possibly tonight." He sank into the chair Tomas vacated for him and pressed both palms against his eyes. "I've served clerks and colonels my whole career and I'm telling you what a blank date means. It means the decision has already been made and the paper is waiting to agree. We are not planning against a deadline anymore, Ines. We are planning against a whim."

The stove settled. Snow ticked against the shutter where Tomas had scratched an hour ago. On the table between them lay the thin carbon slip, and Ines looked at it and understood that the laundress's flour had purchased all of this — the chains, the sentries, the blank date — and that the price of one woman's hunger was now being paid by everyone who had ever trusted another person in this frozen town.

Trust had become a liability. Fine, then. They would proceed on something sturdier than trust. On need. On the dead, who required nothing and therefore could not betray.

"We move everything forward," she said. "Every piece. Tomas learns the press by heart this week, not next. You get those crates to the loading dock however you must — invent a fire hazard, invent God himself, invent anything an army believes faster than the truth. And I start setting type in my head tonight, every name, in order, so that when the press is ours for one hour, my hands already know the work." She gathered the carbon slip and held it over the stove until it curled and flamed and was gone. "They've doubled their watch. So we'll halve our mistakes."

Brandt watched the ash of the memo settle among the coals. "And if we're caught?"

Ines thought of Corporal Voss, listed as missing. Of the blank stones behind the hospital. Of a thousand names crated for the barges like cargo, bound downstream for burning.

"Then we'll be caught," she said, "with ink on our hands. Which is more than the army can say."

Outside, the river groaned — longer now, louder, a sound like a door being forced — and somewhere in the dark the ice began, quietly, to fail.
