## 13. Marek's Hands

The workshop had gone quiet in the way a body goes quiet — not at rest, but held. Tomas felt it every morning now, crossing the yard under the gray eye of the guard posted at the door: the presses sat under their canvas shrouds like patients waiting for a surgeon who would never come, and the air that had once smelled of ink and hot metal and the sweet dust of cut paper smelled instead of cold ash and tarpaulin and the soldiers' boot grease.

Three weeks since the requisition. Three weeks since Marek had been struck down in his own doorway for raising his voice at a lieutenant, and Tomas had learned to keep his eyes lowered and his hands busy and his mouth shut around the garrison men.

But this morning there were no soldiers in the composing room. The sergeant who usually supervised the inventory had been called away with most of the detail to help haul crates to the granary, and the one left behind was young and bored and had already asked twice if he could warm himself at the stove by the loading dock. Tomas said yes to both questions, because saying yes was how you made yourself invisible, and then he climbed the back stair to the loft where Marek lived now, above the shop he was no longer permitted to enter except under escort.

Marek was sitting by the window with his hands open on his knees.

Tomas stopped on the last step. He had seen those hands every day for six years — setting type, pulling proofs, guiding Tomas's own fingers along the grooves of a letterform until the boy could read them blindfolded, the way other fathers taught sons to read faces. They were broad hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained so deeply with ink that no lye or pumice had ever fully cleaned them; the stain had gone into the skin itself, into the whorls of the fingerprints, as though the letters had decided, somewhere over fifty years, to claim him entirely. Now they lay open and empty, and they trembled, very slightly, the way a plumb line trembles when a wagon passes too near.

"You're supposed to be counting crates," Marek said, without turning his head.

"Kovar is counting them."

"Hm." The old man's gaze stayed on the window, on the river beyond the rooftops, white and hard as hammered tin. "And what does Kovar count them with?"

"His thumbs."

A sound came out of Marek that might once have been a laugh. It frightened Tomas more than anger would have. In six years he had never heard the old man make a sound like that — thin, and dry, and finished.

"Come here, boy. Sit where I can see you. My eyes aren't what they were."

Tomas dragged the second stool from under the eaves and sat. Up close he could see what three weeks had done. Marek had always been lean, but now the tendons stood out on his neck like guy-ropes, and there was a yellowish cast beneath the skin, the color of old varnish. The blow from the lieutenant's baton had raised a swelling above his ear that had never quite gone down. But it wasn't the wound that had broken him, Tomas understood suddenly. It was everything the wound stood for.

"They took the ledgers today," Marek said. "The job books. Fifty-one years of them. Every commission since I was apprenticed to my father. Wedding notices. Funeral cards. The grain prices. The flood year." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The soldier wrote *miscellaneous* on the manifest. Miscellaneous."

"I know. I watched them carry them out."

"You watched." Marek turned his head at last, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "You watched, and you counted with your thumbs, and you said nothing. Good. That's what I taught you. Keep your head down and the press turning. Well." He looked back at the window. "I taught you wrong."

* * *

For a long while neither of them spoke. Below them, faintly, came the sound of the young soldier whistling at the stove, some marching tune played badly through his teeth, and the river groaned once, far off, the ice shifting in its sleep.

"My father built this shop," Marek said finally. "Before me. Before the railway even came. You know that?"

"Some."

"You don't. Nobody knows anymore, because nobody printed it, because printing is how things get known, and we let them take the knowing." He rubbed one thumb slowly across the fingers of the other hand, an unconscious motion, a compositor feeling for a letterform in the dark. "There was a time when nothing happened in this town unless it passed through this room. Births. Deaths. The price of rye. The council's lies. My father set the first free newspaper in this valley on these presses — not free as in cost, boy. Free as in *no one told him what to put in it*. He lost advertisers for it. He was sued twice. Once a magistrate sent two men to smash the case of type, and my father stood in front of it with a poker and told them they'd have to go through him, and they believed him, because they could see he meant to die there if it came to that." Marek's mouth twisted. "The newspaper lasted eleven years. Then the war before this war came, and then the taxes, and then the censorship laws, and bit by bit my father narrowed himself down to wedding cards and funeral cards, which are safe, which offend no one, which say only what everyone already agrees to say. And when he died he told me the trade was dying anyway and I'd be wise to follow him into it. Narrowing. He thought narrowing was survival."

"And you?"

"And I did the same." The word came out of him like a stone dropping into a well. "Forty years, Tomas. Forty years of safe print. I told myself the town needed its funeral cards, someone honest to spell the names right, and that was true, and it was also a lie I slept behind. Because all those years I kept the molds."

"Molds?" Tomas said. "What molds?"

Marek looked at him then, really looked, and something in his face shifted — the fury settling, or perhaps sinking, into something older and heavier and more deliberate.

"Get the lamp," he said. "And bring the key off the nail behind my coat. We're going down to the cellar."

* * *

The cellar stairs were steep and smelled of cold earth and coal and, beneath that, the ghost of linseed oil. Marek went first, slower than he used to be, one hand on the rail, the lamp held out ahead of him so that his shadow lunged enormous against the walls. At the bottom he did not turn toward the coal bin or the store room where the paper stock lay under canvas, requisitioned now too, every ream inventoried and sealed. He walked past all of it to the far wall, where the foundation stones of the original building showed through the plaster, black and sweating with cold.

"There's a false course," he said. "Third row up, fourth stone from the corner. My father put it there during the flood year, to hide the ledger of debts when the bailiffs came. Bailiffs are long gone. Debts are long paid. The stone has been waiting forty years for someone to need it again."

He worked his fingers into the mortar joint above it and pulled. The stone came out easier than it should have, on a hidden pivot, silent as a well-oiled drawer — because of course it was oiled, Tomas realized; of course it had been maintained all these years, checked, kept ready, kept alive the way you keep a lamp trimmed for a bridegroom who may come at any hour. Behind it lay a cavity a hand's depth deep, lined with oiled cloth.

Marek lifted out a flat wooden box, iron-cornered, heavy as a Bible.

"What is it?" Tomas whispered, though the cellar was theirs alone and no one had whistled at the stove in ten minutes.

"Open it."

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age, lay rows of molds. Not type — Tomas knew type, had sorted it since he was nine — but the matrices, the mother-molds from which type itself was cast. Brass and copper, each one carrying a letter sunk in reverse, sharp-edged, immaculate. And not ordinary letters. These were taller and narrower than the house style, with serifs like small wings and a vigor in the cutting that modern foundry work had flattened out. Tomas lifted one to the lamplight and saw his own reflection swim inside the counters of an *O*.

"The first face," Marek said. "Cut for the newspaper. My father bought the punches from a wandering engraver who owed him money — a Hungarian, half mad, brilliant. There are ninety-one sorts. A full alphabet, figures, punctuation. Enough to set anything." His voice dropped. "When the censors came the second time, my father hid these rather than surrender them, and after he died I kept them hidden, and I told myself it was sentiment. Sentiment." He laughed his dry terrible laugh again. "It wasn't sentiment. It was ammunition, and I didn't have the courage to fire it."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because they're going to burn the shop, boy. You've seen the surveyors. You've heard the bargemen talk at the dock. When the thaw comes, the army ships what it wants downriver and blows the rest into rubble, and everything this room ever touched turns to smoke." Marek closed Tomas's fingers around the edge of the box. His hands were shaking now openly, but his grip was still strong, a compositor's grip, trained across fifty years to hold a line of type true. "Paper burns. I've burned proof sheets myself, whole editions, orders from the censor. Paper burns fast and glad. But letters—" He tapped the box, once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "Letters remember. Cut them deep enough, in brass, in copper, and they outlive every hand that held them, every law that banned them, every fire that ate the pages they printed. My father knew it. That's why he buried them. That's why I'm burying them again — in you."

"I can't—"

"You can. You will." For a moment the old man's voice cracked wide open, and beneath the crack Tomas heard something he had never heard in six years of apprenticeship: fear. Not for himself. "They struck me down in my own doorway and I let them, Tomas. I lay on the cobbles and I thought, good, at least they'll leave the boy alone. That's what I am now. A man who lies on cobbles. Don't you dare become a man who lies on cobbles. Take the molds. Hide them where I'd never think to look, somewhere I couldn't betray even under questioning, because I would question badly, do you hear me? I'm old and I'm proud and I'd give them up to save you. So don't tell me where. Promise me you won't tell me where."

Above them the floorboards creaked. Both of them froze. Boots crossed the composing room overhead — the young soldier, restless, or the sergeant returned early — paused, moved on. The whistle resumed, off-key, oblivious.

Tomas looked down at the box in his hands. Ninety-one letters. An entire alphabet, hidden through two generations of fear, waiting. He thought of the nurse who asked careful questions about crates, and of the clerk with the haunted face who lingered too long over certain volumes, and of his own silent count, thumbing crates in the dark — and for the first time the count felt less like mourning and more like arithmetic, the beginning of an answer.

"Where will you say it went?" he asked.

"Nowhere," Marek said. "Old men misplace things. Let them think the requisition already swallowed it. Let them think whatever they like about Marek the printer — they already think he's broken." He smiled, and it was almost the old smile, wry and crooked. "Broken men make excellent hiding places."

Tomas buttoned the box into his coat, against his chest, where it rode heavy and warm as a second heart. At the foot of the cellar stairs he stopped.

"The newspaper," he said. "Your father's. What was it called?"

Marek's eyes went bright and fierce in the lamplight.

"*The Honest Word*," he said. "Eleven years, and every issue signed. Names in full, always. He said a name without its name is a rumor, but a name given whole is a fact, and facts are the only weapons that never rust."

Tomas carried that up the stairs with him, along with the box, into the gray morning light where the soldier whistled and the presses waited under their shrouds and the river groaned again, louder now, the ice remembering spring.

That night, wrapped in oilcloth and buried beneath the loose bricks behind the orchard house's woodshed — a place he chose alone, telling no one, keeping faith with the promise — Tomas lay awake doing sums. If the press could be fired, and the records opened, and a compositor stood ready with ninety-one letters that remembered...

Then tomorrow he would need to find the nurse, and risk everything on asking her one question aloud.
