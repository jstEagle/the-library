## 14. The Plan on Apple Paper

*Ines*

The apples came out of the cellar the color of late candlelight — preserved in syrup since the last good autumn, before anyone had heard of requisition papers, before the word *war effort* had been spoken over Marek's workshop like a sentence passed. Ines ladled them into three chipped bowls and set them on the orchard house table, where the lamp threw a ring of amber across the wood and left the corners of the room in shadow.

Tomas sat with his hands wrapped around his bowl as though it were something alive he'd been asked to carry. Brandt stood by the window, not eating, watching the dark line of the river through the bare branches.

"Sit," Ines said. "You can't plan a theft standing up like a man waiting for a firing squad."

"I have stood waiting for firing squads," Brandt said mildly. "It's not so different from planning one." But he sat.

She had spread the paper across the table an hour before they arrived — sheets she had made herself two winters ago in the kitchen of this house, boiling down apple peelings and cores until the water went thick and sweet-smelling, pressing the pulp flat between cloths, drying the sheets on the linen line above the stove. Apple paper. It browned at the edges like autumn, took ink reluctantly, and held it forever. She had made it for letters home that never got sent. Now she smoothed the top sheet flat and set her pen beside it, and waited for the others to understand what she was showing them.

Tomas touched the edge of the sheet the way he touched type — with his fingertips first, reading it. "You made this?"

"In this kitchen. When there was no paper worth trusting."

"And now?"

"Now we make a list," Ines said, "of everything we need, and everything we don't yet have."

Outside, the wind moved through the orchard with a sound like water finding cracks in ice. The trees were black and patient, their buds still furled tight, holding spring inside themselves the way she was holding several hundred names.

She dipped her pen and wrote the first item: *Press.*

* * *

*Tomas*

He knew the press better than he knew his own hands, which was fortunate, because his own hands had started shaking whenever he thought about Thursday night.

"The old Heidelberg," he said, when Ines looked up at him. "Not the platen jobs the army uses for their forms. Those are theirs; they'll be locked or guarded or both. But the Heidelberg hasn't been crated because Colonel Weiss's men think it's dead. Marek let them believe it. He told them the bed is cracked and the rollers are perished and it would cost more to move than it's worth." Tomas allowed himself the smallest smile. "The bed isn't cracked. I cracked nothing. And the rollers — I've been reconditioning them at night, slow, with glycerin and tallow. They're soft again. They'll take ink."

"You've been repairing it under their noses," Brandt said.

"I've been *cleaning* it under their noses," Tomas corrected. "Cleaning is what apprentices do. Nobody watches an apprentice clean. That's the whole point of apprentices." He turned his bowl a half-circle on the table. "But listen. The press is the easy part. The hard part is the lock on the composing room, and the fact that Sergeant Dravic sleeps four hours after his shift ends and drinks for three of them, but the other hour he walks the yard. I know when. Quarter past two, quarter past three. He always checks the loading dock last, and he lingers there because the wind comes off the river and he can smoke without the lieutenant smelling it on him. That gives us—" he counted on his fingers, and hated how young his fingers looked doing it— "maybe twenty minutes, twice, if we need it."

"And if he doesn't walk when you expect?" Ines asked.

Tomas had thought about this every night for a week, lying in his cot above the workshop listening to the ice groan on the river like something enormous turning over in its sleep. "Then we stop. Everything stops. Ink down, lamp hooded, me back up the ladder like I'm checking the dampers. We lose the night and try the next one." He looked at her. "I'd rather lose five nights than hang once."

"That's not cowardice," Brandt said quietly. "That's arithmetic."

It was strange, Tomas thought, to be sitting in a warm kitchen with a nurse and a clerk, planning a crime over stewed apples. Stranger still that it didn't feel like a crime. It felt like composition — like setting a page, each element in its place, waiting for the moment the whole form locked together and could be lifted as one thing.

He reached into his coat and laid the wax impression on the table: the records room key, pressed into a gray disk of sealing wax no bigger than a coin.

Brandt went very still.

"Where did you get this?"

"The door," Tomas said. "I waxed the strike plate and let the key mark it when the sergeant locked up Tuesday. It's shallow. You'd get a better impression off the actual key if you can borrow it."

Brandt picked up the disk and held it to the lamp, tilting it, and Tomas watched the man's face change — watched seven years of careful blankness crack along some fault line he hadn't known was there.

"This is my key," Brandt said. "My inventory key. I sign the records room open twice a day." He set the wax down with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "You never needed to steal anything. You needed only to ask me."

"We couldn't ask you," Tomas said. "We didn't know you yet."

* * *

*Brandt*

He had spent the walk from the garrison rehearsing excuses for why he might turn back — the mud, the cold, the impropriety of a clerk visiting a nurse uninvited. All of them had been lies, and he had known they were lies, and he had walked on anyway, and now here he was at an orchard-house table being handed a conspiracy on a plate like the last course of a meal he'd been starving for.

"The lock itself is a standard garrison pattern," he said, because his voice worked better when he gave it facts to hold onto. "Warded. Three levers. I could make a working key from your wax in an evening with files from the workshop — assuming Tomas can get me files."

"I can get you files," Tomas said. "And brass rod. There's a broken padlock in the scrap bin nobody will miss."

"Then the lock falls." Brandt turned his bowl slowly. "Which leaves the crates."

That was the part he had not said aloud to anyone, the part that had been sitting in his chest since the night he found the falsified report folded against his ribs like a second, rotten heart. He took the page from inside his coat now — Voss's company, the ravine, *engagement losses: 34*, where the true figure was 34 deserters shot in a drainage ditch by their own officers — and laid it in the lamplight between the three of them.

"I signed this," he said. "Seven years ago. My hand. My stamp. The order came down and I processed it the way I processed everything, because processing was my whole self, and thinking was not in the job description." He heard how flat his voice had gone and made himself lift it. "There are more. Not many — the omissions are mostly sloppiness, laziness, officers who couldn't be bothered with names. But there are eleven reports like this one in the archive. Deliberate. Signed. And every one of those pages has to come out of the crates before the barges load, because if the barges carry them downstream intact, then someday someone honest will find them and read them, and they will say: see, it was recorded, it was handled, the army kept its books. And the ledger—" he nodded at Ines— "your ledger becomes just one woman's grievance against the official record."

"So we take the false pages too," Ines said.

"We take the false pages and the true names and we print them *together*. Page by page. What the report says, and what actually happened, side by side. Let the town hold both in its hands and feel the weight of the difference." His voice cracked on the last of it and he let it. "That's the only atonement I know how to make. I can't give the dead back their lives. I can give them back their numbers."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a branch shed its load of snow with a soft whump, and Ines's apple trees stood up straighter under the sudden lightness.

Tomas was the one who broke it, in the practical voice of an apprentice listing materials. "How many pages?"

"Eleven false reports. Your ledger — how many names?"

"Four hundred and six," Ines said. "As of Tuesday."

"Four hundred and six names, set in type, printed on—" he touched the apple paper— "this. How many sheets can you make in four days?"

"With the stove going day and night? Perhaps sixty good ones. A hundred if I accept the thin ones that tear."

"Print small, then," Tomas said. "Ten-point. Two columns. On the Heidelberg I can pull maybe four hundred impressions in a night if everything holds — and nothing ever holds, so say three hundred. We won't print four hundred copies of everything. We print enough that the town becomes the archive. One copy nailed to the church door. One in the granary. One tucked into the hymnals, one in every market basket, and fifty slipped into coat pockets at the laundry, and the rest—" he shrugged— "the rest goes wherever the wind thinks they should go. Paper travels, Frau Eriksen. Paper is the only soldier that deserts successfully."

Brandt laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse — and found that he meant it, and that laughing did not undo him.

* * *

*Ines*

They drew the map on the apple paper because it was what they had, and because there was a rightness to it she could not explain and did not try to. Tomas sketched the workshop from memory — presses, composing room, records room, loading dock, the yard, the guard's route drawn as a dotted loop like a stitch around a wound. Brandt marked the crates with small crosses: the eleven false reports in crate nine, her ledger in crate twelve, nearest the door, because Brandt himself had shelved it there weeks ago without knowing why, and only understood now that some part of him had already chosen a side.

Ines wrote the sequence in her narrow hand beneath the map:

*Night before demolition.*
*Tomas opens the Heidelberg. Ink ready, type cases staged.*
*Brandt unlocks the records room. Removes ledger and eleven reports. Replaces weight in the crates so the count stays true.*
*Ines sets the names. All of them. Voss first.*
*They print until the ink runs out or the sun comes up.*
*They distribute before the ice breaks.*
*No one signs anything. No one speaks of it after. Ever.*

"Read it back to me," she said, because a plan unspoken is only a wish.

They read it back, all three voices overlapping and correcting, and she listened for the holes the way she had once listened to wounded men breathe — for the rattle, the catch, the thing that meant trouble coming. She found one.

"If any one of us is taken," she said, "the other two must be able to finish alone. So each of us learns the whole plan, not our piece of it. Brandt, you'll set type — I'll teach you the case tomorrow, it's simpler than it looks. Tomas, you'll learn where every name sits in my ledger, in case I can't read them out. And I—" she paused— "I will learn the guard's route, in case you're lying drunk in a ditch, Tomas, which at sixteen is a genuine possibility."

"I resent that," Tomas said, with dignity. "I've been drunk exactly never."

"Then you have something to look forward to and a reason to stay alive. Good."

Brandt was looking at her across the table with an expression she recognized from hospital wards — the look of a man watching someone else be braver than he feels. "And if it fails? If Weiss catches us?"

Ines looked at the map, at the little crosses marking the crates, at the dotted stitch of the guard's route, and thought of Corporal Voss's hand going heavy in hers while a clerk somewhere wrote *missing* in a neat column, and thought of four hundred and five more names after that, and of the river ice groaning all night like a conscience.

"Then it fails," she said, "and the names go down with us, and the town forgets, and the river carries blank ledgers, and the world stays tidy. That is what failure means. I have considered it thoroughly." She dipped her pen. "Now — the order of names. We print Voss first, and after Voss, the men of the ravine, and after them, alphabetical. Not rank. Never rank. Alphabetical, so that a boy who deserted shares a column with a captain who stayed."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Marek says type doesn't care whose name it is. He says that's the whole religion of the trade."

"Marek," said Brandt, "sounds like a man I would like to have met before I helped break his life."

"He doesn't know about any of this," Tomas said quickly.

"No," Ines said. "And he must not, until it's done. After — after, he may know everything. But if we fail, he must be able to say he knew nothing, and be believed." She capped the inkwell. Outside, far off, the river made a sound she had begun to hear even in her sleep — a long internal shifting, like a great animal testing the walls of its pen. "One more thing. The date. The thaw is early this year. Weiss's barges could come any morning now, and the demolition order follows the barges. So we do not wait for the eve of demolition if the eve does not wait for us. If the crates move early, we move earlier. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Brandt.

"Agreed," said Tomas.

They put their hands on the apple-paper map — hers thin and ink-stained, Tomas's stained with grease, Brandt's trembling very slightly until it pressed down and stopped — and for a moment the kitchen held them all, warm and ridiculous and doomed and certain, three people and a lamp and a plan written on fruit peelings, about to steal the truth back from an army.

Ines blew out the lamp so their shadows could leave separately.

At the door, Tomas paused. "Frau Eriksen. Four hundred and six names. Will there be time to set them all?"

"There will be time," Ines said, "because there has to be."

But long after they had gone, standing at the window watching the river ice shiver silver under the moon, she admitted to the dark what she would not admit at the table: that the thaw sounded closer tonight than it had sounded last night. That the barges might already be loading at some downstream depot. That hope, at this size, was indistinguishable from recklessness, and she had chosen it anyway.

Two streets away, in a lit window of the garrison headquarters, a figure she could not see bent over a telegram form and began, in a careful clerk's hand, to change the schedule of everything.
