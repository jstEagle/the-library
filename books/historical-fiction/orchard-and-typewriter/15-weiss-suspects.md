## 15. Weiss Suspects

The report lay on Colonel Weiss's desk like something dead that had been carried in from the cold.

It was a single sheet of garrison paper, the cheap grey stock they used for laundry manifests and bread requisitions, and on it a military policeman named Dorn had transcribed, in his careful, graceless hand, the observations of one Frau Hilda Kessler, laundress, contracted to the garrison since November. Weiss read it twice. He read it the way he read everything now — slowly, because speed was how mistakes were made, and he had not made a mistake in thirty-one years of service and did not intend to begin in this muddy river town where nothing happened except paperwork and thaw.

*The nurse Eriksen,* the laundress claimed, *has been seen three times near the records room of the print workshop when her duties do not take her there. She speaks to the clerk Brandt. She speaks to the boy. On the fourteenth she stood by the loading dock for eleven minutes doing nothing at all.*

Doing nothing at all. Weiss set the page down and looked out the window at the river.

He had been a soldier long enough to know that no one did nothing for eleven minutes. A woman might stand idle at a window; a man might pause over his pipe. But no one stood at a loading dock in February wind for eleven minutes without wanting something from it. Wanting was measurable. Eleven minutes of wanting was practically a confession.

The river lay under its ice the way a sleeping animal lies under snow — you could see the shape of it, the pale ridge down the middle where the current ran strongest, and if you watched long enough you could almost believe it was still. But it was not still. Nothing under ice was ever still. Every night now, as he sat over his correspondence, he heard it groan — a long, low complaint that traveled up through the pilings of the warehouse and into the floorboards and into the soles of his boots, as though the whole valley were clearing its throat before speaking.

The thaw was coming early this year. The old men in the market said so, spitting into their scarves. The ice would break within the fortnight, perhaps sooner, and when it broke the barges would come up from the depot at Grunwald, and everything crated in the workshop would go downriver — every ledger, every casualty list, every omission report that some clerk with a soft conscience had signed and sealed and tried to forget.

Weiss was not ashamed of what was in those crates. That was the thing his superiors had never understood about him, and the thing the town had never understood either, though they looked at him with such careful blank faces that he sometimes wondered whether they understood him better than anyone. Order required forgetting. This was not cruelty; it was hygiene. A body kept alive by sealing a wound, not by displaying it. A nation kept alive the same way. The men who had died badly — the deserters against the wall behind the drill hall, the companies written off as missing when they were merely destroyed, the names that would have made widows ask questions the army could not afford to answer — those men were wounds. And a wound that is displayed is a wound that festers.

Let them be missing. Missing was a kind of mercy, if you squinted at it right. A woman could live forty years beside a missing man, hoping. She could not live forty minutes beside a photograph of what the wall behind the drill hall had done to him.

So the archive would burn downstream, and the town would keep its blank stones, and in twenty years no one would remember there had ever been anything to forget. That was not villainy. That was surgery.

And yet.

He turned the laundress's report over, though there was nothing on the back but Dorn's initials and the date. And yet someone in this town cared too much about paper. It had begun as a itch between his shoulder blades back in January, when the requisition went through and he had stood in the workshop among the crates and felt — not seen, *felt* — attention settle on him like weather. Marek's eyes, yes; the old printer hated him openly, which was harmless, hatred being the most honest emotion in any occupied town. But there had been another gaze, quieter, angled, patient. A watcher watching the watchers.

Now the laundress had given it a shape: the nurse. The one who had volunteered for garrison duty with such unremarkable efficiency that she'd been approved inside a day. The one who mended bandages in the converted schoolhouse and never asked for anything and spoke, according to Frau Kessler, exactly enough and no more.

Weiss had reviewed her file himself. Ines Eriksen, field hospital service, two citations, transferred back from the front after the autumn push. Her papers were clean. They were so clean they read like a room that has just been straightened for company. He had noticed that in December and thought nothing of it; everyone's papers were straightened for company these days. But noticing was a habit he did not allow himself to drop.

*She stands by the loading dock doing nothing at all.*

He rang the bell for his adjutant.

* * *

"Move the barge schedule," Weiss said. "Up two days."

Lieutenant Falk, who was twenty-six and had a good jaw and a mind like a well-kept stable — everything in its stall, nothing startled easily — blinked once. "Sir?"

"The ice breaks early. The old men say so, the river says so, and I don't propose to have our transport sitting at a broken dock waiting on a thaw that already happened. The barge comes up from Grunwald in six days, not eight. Crating finishes in five."

"Yes sir." Falk wrote it down. Then, because he was better than his jaw suggested, he paused. "The carpenter said the last crates wouldn't be sealed before—"

"Then tell the carpenter to seal faster. Tell him I'll stand in his shop while he does it if that helps him find his hands." Weiss rose and took his coat from its hook. Outside the window, a patrol crossed the square at the slow, deliberate pace he had ordered — visible, unhurried, a show of permanence. Permanence was mostly pacing, he had learned. March like you'll be here forever and mostly people believe you.

"And the guard rotations?" Falk asked. "If we're moving the date—"

"Doubled. Day and night. No soldier stands the same post twice running, and nobody stands post alone, including the ones who complain loudest about company." He buttoned the coat. "And Lieutenant — the records room key inventory comes to me personally each evening. Not to the sergeant. To me."

Falk noted it. He was too well-trained to ask why, which Weiss appreciated, and too young to guess, which was also useful.

At the door Weiss stopped. One more thing, and it was the thing that mattered, and he wanted to deliver it standing still.

"There is a nurse," he said. "Eriksen. She volunteers at the workshop. From today she is to be treated with perfect courtesy and perfect attention. If she so much as looks at a crate, I want the hour, the minute, and which way the wind was blowing. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, sir." A hesitation. Falk had earned it. "Is she suspected of something specific?"

Weiss considered the question the way he considered all questions: honestly, and then not at all. "She is suspected of caring," he said. "Which in my experience precedes everything else."

* * *

That evening he did what he always did when an itch needed scratching: he arranged to lose money at cards.

Not deliberately — he played to win, always; losing on purpose was a lie told with your hands, and lies with hands were the first kind people caught. But he chose the table, and the hour, and the company, and he made sure the wine was good enough that tongues loosened around it, and in that sense the evening was rigged before the first card fell.

The officers' mess occupied the former grain exchange, a tall cold room with a tiled stove and windows fogged with the breath of too many men. Four of them played tonight: Captain Reiter, red-faced and jovial; Lieutenant Ostrau, who cheated mildly and believed no one knew; Sergeant-Major Vossen, invited for his memory, which was encyclopedic and entirely devoted to other people's debts; and Aldous Brandt, the clerk, who sat at Weiss's left with his cards held close and his face held closer.

Brandt fascinated Weiss the way a hairline crack fascinates a mason. Here was a man the army had discarded — falsified accounts, seven years ago, a scandal hushed rather than prosecuted, dumped into civilian exile in a river town — and then, when the army needed hands for its dirty paper, here he came crawling back, inventorying the very documents whose smell he must have hoped never to encounter again. Men like that were either broken or bent. Broken men were safe. Bent men could be levered. The trick was finding the angle, and angles revealed themselves over cards better than over any interrogation, because cards gave a man something to do with his cowardice.

"Ace," announced Reiter, slapping it down with unnecessary force and gathering a pot he had no business winning. "Colonel, you fold like a man with a clear conscience. It's unnerving."

"A clear conscience folds quickly because it has nothing to defend," Weiss said, and dealt.

He let the game run. He drank less than the others and laughed at the right intervals. And he listened — to Ostrau complaining about the doubled guards, which meant the order had rippled through the ranks by afternoon and the town's ears had surely caught it too; to Vossen describing, with relish, the laundress Kessler's new ration privileges and the envy she was sowing among her colleagues; to Brandt saying almost nothing, which was itself a kind of speech.

"Brandt." Weiss laid a king down and pushed chips toward the center. "How goes the great cataloguing? Have you reached the bottom of the workshop's sins yet?"

A small pause. Not long — a half-second, maybe less, the time it takes a man to decide which version of himself to be. "There are no sins, sir. Only ledgers. Ledgers are innocent; they only record."

"Innocent." Weiss smiled and showed his teeth doing it. "You've inventoried archives in three districts, haven't I read? Before your — interlude." He let the word sit gently on the table, neither cruel nor kind. "You know better than most that no ledger is innocent. A ledger is a verdict wearing arithmetic."

"I'd say it's the opposite, Colonel. Arithmetic wearing a verdict." Brandt played his card — a low one, throwing the trick away, keeping his strength hidden. "Numbers can be re-counted. Verdicts only re-sentenced."

Reiter roared at nothing in particular; Ostrau refilled the glasses. The stove ticked. Somewhere below the floor, faint as a heartbeat, the river groaned against its ice, and Weiss saw Brandt's eyes flick downward at the sound, just once, the way a man's eyes flick toward a sound he has been listening for.

Interesting.

"You were at the loading dock Tuesday," Weiss said conversationally, arranging his next discard. "With the nurse. The medic — Eriksen. Checking the crate seals, were you?"

"Weak seals rot the paper," Brandt said. "Damp gets in off the dock. I've lost documents to damp before." He said it flatly, professionally, and then — and this was the crack, the hairline fissure Weiss had come hunting for — he added, unprompted, unnecessarily: "Some things shouldn't rot."

"No," Weiss agreed pleasantly. "Some things shouldn't."

He studied the man across the rim of his glass. Forty-ish, ink-stained, a face built for forgetting — the kind of face you passed on a street and could not describe an hour later. Hands steady. Voice level. But beneath the levelness something was running, some current under ice of its own, and it had shown itself for exactly the length of one sentence: *some things shouldn't rot.* A man content with his work does not volunteer philosophy. A guilty man does not volunteer opinions. Only a man at war with himself volunteers both.

Weiss played the hand out and lost it — genuinely lost; Ostrau's cheating finally aligned with the deck — and while the pot was gathered and the jokes were made, he asked, lightly, the question he had actually come to ask:

"When the barges leave, Brandt, what will you do with yourself? The workshop comes down the week after. There'll be little use for a clerk in a town with no paper in it."

The room went on around them — Reiter dealing, Vossen grumbling, the stove ticking — but Weiss watched only the small muscles around Brandt's eyes, the ones no training reaches.

"I hadn't thought," Brandt said. "Past Friday, I mean to say. Clerks learn not to think past the next stamp."

"Bad habit." Weiss collected his coat from the chair back. "In my experience, the men who don't think past the next stamp are precisely the ones who should. Good night, gentlemen."

* * *

Outside, the cold hit like a slap of clean linen. The square was empty except for the sentry at the corner, stamping his feet, his lantern making a small trembling island in the dark. Above the rooftops the sky had cleared, and the stars were hard and close, winter stars, indifferent.

Weiss stood a moment on the mess steps and listened to the river.

It was louder tonight. He was sure of it — louder than last week, louder than yesterday, a grinding undertone beneath the groan, the sound of ice working against itself, of a surface deciding to become a current. Two days. Maybe three. The old men were right, damn them; the thaw was coming ahead of schedule, and schedules were the one thing he refused to surrender to weather.

Two days, then. The barge up from Grunwald in six instead of eight. Everything crated and sealed and floating south before the ice finished its argument — and the town waking one morning to a river running free and a workshop full of nothing but empty shelves and the smell of ink, and no proof anywhere in the world that any of those names had ever mattered.

It would work. It would work because it had to, because order depended on it, because he had spent thirty-one years building a machine that worked and this was simply one more gear turning in it.

And yet he found his eyes going, unwillingly, to the far side of the square — to the dark bulk of the orchard house at the town's edge, where one window burned low and yellow, late, later than a nurse had any reason to be awake. He watched it for a long moment. A lamp in a window was nothing. People burned lamps for a hundred harmless reasons.

But he had learned, in thirty-one years, to count the harmless reasons against the eleven minutes at the loading dock, and the doubled guards, and the sentence about things that shouldn't rot, and the way a whole town could look at a man with such perfect, polished blankness that it stopped being blankness and became a wall — a town hiding something behind its own face, all of them together, the baker and the laundress's neighbors and the schoolteacher and the boy who swept the workshop steps, everyone holding one same silence the way soldiers held a line.

One lamp. One window. One town holding its breath.

Weiss turned up his collar and walked back through the cold, and behind him the river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere out in the dark he heard — or imagined he heard — the first thin crack of spring splitting across the ice like a shot.
