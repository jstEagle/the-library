## 16. The Thaw Begins

The river spoke first.

Ines heard it in the hour before dawn, when the garrison lamps had burned down to orange cores and even the sentries on the bridge stood hunched and half-asleep against the cold. A sound like a great door groaning on rusted hinges — long, low, rising from beneath the ice as though the river were turning over in its sleep. She lay still in her narrow bed at the orchard house and listened, one hand pressed flat against the cold plaster of the wall, as if she could feel it through the timbers: the ice flexing, testing its own strength, beginning at last to fail.

Three weeks. Perhaps four. Perhaps less.

She dressed in the dark, wrapped a shawl over her uniform blouse, and went downstairs without lighting a lamp. The floorboards knew her weight now; they creaked their small greetings under her feet, and she thought of how Tomas's eyes had widened when she showed them the loose board by the hearth, the hollow where the ledger had slept through two winters. Empty now. The ledger was locked behind army steel across town, waiting for the barges, and every morning the ice thinned a little more, and every morning the arithmetic of what remained grew shorter and crueler.

She stood at the kitchen window with her hands around an unlit candle stub — no sense wasting light — and watched the first grey come up over the orchard. The old trees stood bare and patient in the half-dark, their branches black as ink strokes on wet paper. Marek would have said they were resting. He had told her once that an orchard is a kind of archive too, that every ring in every trunk remembers a year, that you can cut a tree open and read back decades the way Brandt read back through a column of figures. She hadn't understood him then. She understood him now.

Out past the orchard, the river ran silver-grey between its banks, and along its spine she could see the cracks from here — pale seams where frost had healed and re-healed and finally given up healing, spiderwebbing out from the pilings of the ferry landing toward midstream. The soldiers had begun to gather at the railings in the evenings to watch the ice work itself apart. They laughed about it. They made wagers on the day the channel would open. Spring meant letters from home moving faster, meant fresh bread instead of hardtack, meant the war remembered them again after the long winter of waiting.

Spring meant barges.

Ines counted hours the way other people counted coins. Sixty-two days since the requisition papers were nailed to the workshop door. Fifty-one since she had stood in the records room and seen her own handwriting looking back at her from the evidence shelf, bound in its plain brown cover, catalogued in another hand as *miscellaneous private documents*. Forty since the laundress's betrayal and the doubled guard. And now — she counted again, carefully, the way she counted pulse rates — perhaps twenty days until the thaw opened the channel, and Weiss moved his schedule up or didn't, and everything they had planned came due.

Twenty days. Less than the ice could hold.

* * *

The hospital took her back gladly. They always did; there were never enough hands, and Nurse Eriksen asked nothing and volunteered for everything, and if Major Renner found it curious that a woman with her record should prefer the garrison infirmary to a transfer somewhere quieter, he had the good manners of the overworked never to say so. She mended bandages in the long ward off the kitchen, tearing linen into strips with a practiced jerk of the wrists, boiling instruments until her knuckles cracked from the lye. It was honest work. It kept her hands busy and her face unremarkable, and unremarkable was armor now.

But it also gave her reason to walk the back path.

Behind the hospital, past the laundry lines where the garrison sheets froze stiff as shrouds in February and dripped all day now in the warmer air, there was a plot of ground the townspeople had used for generations and the army had simply absorbed, the way armies absorb things. Soldiers' graves, mostly — men who had died in the converted wards rather than at the front, far enough from any official battle to be nobody's statistics. And among them, in rows the army's surveyors had never bothered to map, older stones: townsfolk, river pilots, children taken by the fever year. And then the ones Ines came for.

The blank stones.

They stood in a cluster near the alder hedge, six of them, seven, eight — she had never settled on a number because counting them felt like surrendering to the officers' arithmetic, and she refused to do the army's sums for free. Plain fieldstones, uncut, unhallowed by any mason's chisel. The dead beneath them had been brought downriver on stretchers in the first winter of the war, when the front hospital overflowed and the wounded came by barge-load, stacked like cordwood, and died faster than anyone could keep records. There had been no time for names. Or rather — and this was the truth she carried like a stone behind her own ribs — there had been time, but not will. A clerk had been assigned. The clerk had fallen behind. The clerk had been reassigned. And the dead had gone into the ground unnamed while the paperwork went forward clean and empty and officially complete.

Ines had been there. Ines had held the hands.

She came today because the thaw had softened the ground enough that crocuses were forcing up through the frost-heaved soil along the hedge, purple fists punching through the last crust of snow, and she could not bear to let that small courage go unwitnessed. She carried nothing but herself. That was the point. She knelt in the wet grass before the first stone, heedless of the damp soaking through her skirt, and she began to read aloud.

"Corporal Anselm Voss," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to break. "Fourth Infantry, second company. Died the fourteenth of October, three years ago, of wounds to the abdomen. He liked chess and complained constantly about the coffee. His mother's name was Marta and he wrote to her every week and every letter ended the same way — *don't worry, Mutti, I am eating well.*"

The stone said nothing. The stone had never said anything. But saying it aloud put it into the air, into the morning, into the ears of the alder hedge and the waking crows and whatever patient God presided over river towns, and that was not nothing. Words spoken over graves are not prayers exactly and not testimony exactly; they are a third thing, older than either. A keeping.

She moved to the next stone. "Private Ludek Harn. Died the nineteenth of October. He was nineteen." Her throat tightened on the number. Nineteen. Younger than Tomas. She had bathed his face with cool water while he called for a brother who was himself already dead two trenches away, and neither of them had known it, and Ines had learned of it later and written both names in the same evening, side by side, in the ledger that now sat in an army crate waiting to be burned. "He wanted to be a clockmaker," she continued, when she could. "He talked about it the whole last night. Escapements. He explained escapements to me for six hours and I understood none of it and I let him talk because it kept him here."

The third stone. The fourth. She gave each one its name, its company, its date, its single human detail — a sweetheart named Greta, a fear of horses, a habit of singing low when the shelling started, a joke about wanting to be buried facing the river so he could watch the boats go by. She had all of it. She had carried it for years in the vault behind her sternum where she kept the things that would have broken her if she ever set them down, and now she set them down, out loud, into the softening air, and discovered that they did not break her. They built her. Each name laid on a blank stone was a course of brick, and she was building something, though she could not have said what — a wall against forgetting, perhaps. A foundation. A press bed.

By the eighth stone her knees were soaked through and her voice had gone rough and the sun had cleared the eastern hills and was laying gold along the river, where the ice groaned again, louder, a sound like furniture being dragged across the floor of the world. Two soldiers walking the hospital path stopped and looked at her — a nurse kneeling in the grass talking to rocks, clearly mad, clearly harmless — and one touched his cap awkwardly and hurried the other along. Let them think what they liked. Madness was excellent cover. Half the town had concluded some time ago that the quiet Eriksen woman was not quite right in the head, wandering the riverbank, asking careful questions, and the conclusion protected her better than any uniform.

She stayed until the sun was fully up. Then she rose, brushed the wet from her skirt, and did what she had done at every grave: she stood a moment longer than comfort required, and said, quietly, to all of them together, "I have your names. All of them. And they are going into print."

* * *

The walk back along the river road gave her the town, and the town gave her the season.

It was impossible, some mornings, to reconcile the place with what was coming. The market square had shaken off winter the way a dog shakes off rain; awnings were going up, the fishmonger had resumed his ancient feud with the cheesewoman at full volume, and children were running sticks along the iron railings to raise that long ringing note that means the world has warmed. On the steps of the church, old women sat in the first real sunshine with their faces tipped back like planted bulbs. And everywhere the soldiers — laughing, loud, buying flowers from the flower-seller's first buckets of the year to send downriver to girls whose names they'd only know through the mail censors' blue pencil.

They were celebrating the thaw. Of course they were. To them the breaking ice meant spring, meant rotation orders and fresh rations and the campaign season that at least promised movement after the frozen stalemate. Ines passed a knot of them outside the canteen — young, pink-cheeked, one of them balancing a bottle on his palm like a waiter — and heard them cheering the river. *"Hear that? She's opening! Another week and the barges run!"* And laughter, bright and unburdened, and one boy threw his cap in the air for no reason at all except that it was warm and he was alive and the war had not yet finished teaching him otherwise.

Ines smiled at them as she passed. It cost her something to make the smile sit correctly on her face. Another week and the barges run — yes. Barges loaded with crates, crates packed by Aldous Brandt's careful inventory, and inside those crates the paper memory of this valley: the granary ledgers, the workshop archives, Marek's fifty years of printing history, and a plain brown ledger full of names that the army intended to carry downriver and burn in some incinerator yard beyond the last bend, where smoke goes up and no one asks what fed it.

Weiss believed order required forgetting. Ines had heard the Colonel say as much, in his dry, reasonable voice, at the inspection — *a nation is a story it tells itself, Nurse, and some chapters are better left as footnotes, and some not written at all* — and she had hated him a little less for the honesty than she hated the comfortable men who did the same thing without the philosophy. But understanding his logic did not soften it. Every regime in history had burned its inconvenient pages and called it tidiness, and the ash looked identical whether the fire was lit by monsters or administrators.

She turned the corner by the cooperage and saw the workshop.

Even from here she could see the change: the new guard post beside the loading dock, timber and sandbags thrown up in a hurry after the laundress's betrayal, a sentry pacing the length of it with his rifle slung muzzle-down, breath steaming. Two guards now at the dock, one at the alley door, one making rounds. She counted them without appearing to count them, the way she'd once counted pulses, the way she counted everything now. Four visible. Which meant at least two more invisible, because Weiss was not a fool, whatever else he was.

And beyond the guard post, through the tall dusty windows of the workshop, she caught it: lamplight, deep in the building, at an hour when no authorized work was being done. A flicker, moving. Someone was in there tonight, in the dark, among the presses.

Tomas. It had to be Tomas, on one of the night shifts he had begged his way into, alone with the machines that raised him, learning the guards' rhythms the way she had once learned the rhythm of a dying man's breathing — close attention as a form of love. Her chest ached with something that was fear and pride in equal measure, braided tight as rope. A sixteen-year-old memorizing the machinery of his own destruction so he could turn it, at the last, into testimony. Marek's blood ran true in the boy, whatever the boy's actual blood was.

She did not look twice. She had learned that lesson well. She walked on past the workshop at the pace of a tired nurse heading home, and did not turn her head, and gave the sentry at the dock a nod as bland as bread, and kept walking until the corner took her out of his sightline. Only then did she let her heart pound. Only then did she stand in the shadow of the cooperage wall and press her hand flat against the cold brick, feeling her own pulse hammer against her palm, sixty-two days of it, all of it, the whole long vigil gathering itself toward a single point.

Twenty days, she had counted at dawn. Standing in the cooperage shadow, she revised the figure downward, because she had learned to read rivers the way Brandt read columns and Marek read proofs. The ice was failing faster than it looked. The groaning had come nightly now, and each night a little angrier, and yesterday she had seen open water — actual black water, breathing — in the channel below the ferry pilings. The barges could move within a fortnight. Sooner, if Weiss grew impatient. And Weiss had grown impatient before.

* * *

That evening she climbed the hill to the orchard house with her basket of bandage linen, and found the others already there.

Tomas arrived first, flushed from running the back ways, smelling of ink and lamp oil, full of whispered report — the guard rotations, the sergeant who drank, the wax impression of the records room key hidden in the heel of his boot like a relic. Then Brandt, punctual as a filing system, carrying bread wrapped in a cloth and the particular hunched silence he wore when he had spent the day touching paper he was ashamed of. Marek did not come anymore; the workshop broke something in him daily, and Ines suspected the old man was saving what was left of himself for the night it would matter. But his presence was there anyway, in Tomas's recitations, in the way the boy quoted him: *paper burns, but letters remember.*

They ate preserved apples from Ines's cellar jars — the last of the autumn, soft and amber and tasting faintly of clove — and they spoke in the low shorthand they had developed, a language of glances and half-finished sentences, because walls had opinions and the laundress had taught them that even kindness can be sold for extra rations. Tomas reported. Brandt confirmed the crate numbers. Ines laid out, on the scrubbed table, in pencil marks she would burn afterward, the sequence of the night they were planning: press fired, records opened, type set, pages run, distributed, done — before the ice finished breaking, before the barges, before Weiss's order reached the incinerator yard.

"The water's opening below the ferry landing," she said, when the plan was laid. "I saw open channel today. We don't have weeks. I don't think we have ten days."

Tomas went still, the way he did when a press jammed and he was listening for the fault. "Then we go early."

"We go when we can," Brandt said quietly. "Not when we want. The records room—"

He stopped. Ines watched something cross his face — not doubt, exactly; something worse, something arriving. He was staring at the table, at his own hands, and his mouth worked once as though tasting a word he didn't want to swallow.

"Aldous?" she said.

"I was at the dock today." His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man reading figures he hates. "Crates are being staged for early loading. Weiss has doubled the carpenters. And—" He lifted his eyes to hers, and Ines saw in them the thing she had seen in soldiers' eyes on nights before an assault: the exact moment a plan meets the ground and finds it different from the map. "There's a new lock on the records room door, Ines. Brass, army pattern. I saw the locksmith's cart leaving at noon."

The fire ticked in the grate. Out in the dark, faint but unmistakable, the river groaned — long, low, impatient, like a creditor at the door.

Tomas looked at Ines. Brandt looked at Ines. And Ines, with the taste of preserved apples still sweet on her tongue and the sound of breaking ice filling the silence, heard herself say the only true thing left:

"Then we find another way in — and we do it sooner than we ever dared to plan."
