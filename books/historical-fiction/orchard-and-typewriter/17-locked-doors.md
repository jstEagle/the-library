## 17. Locked Doors

The key had always fit the way a word fits a sentence — snugly, inevitably, with a small click of rightness at the end. Aldous Brandt stood in the corridor outside the records room on the eve of demolition, and the key would not even enter the lock.

He tried again, telling himself his hands were simply cold. The brass was new. That was the first thing he noticed, before the refusal of the key: the lock itself was new, bright as a coin, its face unscratched by years of Marek's thumb or the garrison's careless boots. Someone had come with a screwdriver and a fresh mechanism sometime in the last day, and no one had told him.

His heart did not pound so much as sink — a slow settling, like ballast shifting in a hull. He looked down the corridor. Oil lamps burned at intervals along the wall, and the shadows between them lay long and still. A sentry stood at the far end near the loading dock doors, stamping his feet against the damp, his breath ghosting in the lamplight. He did not look over.

Brandt put the old key back into his pocket with exaggerated care, the way one returns a borrowed book to a shelf, as though the gesture might undo everything else. Then he walked past the door without looking at it, the way he had taught himself to walk past all the things in this war that could not be helped: the wounded men stacked two to a cot, the lists with their neat lacunae where names should have been, the faces of officers who spoke of *efficiency* the way other men spoke of God.

*All papers are claimed for the war effort.* He had signed such requisitions himself once, in another life, in another uniform. He knew exactly what happened to papers claimed for the war effort. They went downstream with the barges, and downstream there was a furnace, and after the furnace there was nothing — not ash, not memory, not even the dignity of having been destroyed honestly. Just an absence, tidied, like a name struck from a ledger by a clerk who never learned what the man's laugh sounded like.

Tomorrow night the demolition charges would be set. Tomorrow morning the barges would take the crates. And somewhere behind that new bright lock lay Ines Eriksen's ledger, with every name she had gathered like a woman gleaning a field after the reapers had passed — Voss, and the others, the hundreds, the ones the reports had folded away into the clean blank language of *missing*.

He needed Tomas. But Tomas was not due until the night shift, and it was not yet dusk.

* * *

Brandt spent the afternoon doing what he did best and worst: being invisible among documents.

The inventory tables in the workshop's front room were buried under ledgers, dispatch copies, hospital registers, quartermaster rolls — seven years of a town's paper life, condemned. He worked through them with a clerk's mechanical diligence, entering crate numbers in a column, and all the while his mind turned the problem of the lock like a stone in a pocket.

Who had changed it? Weiss, most likely. The Colonel had been circling closer for days — Brandt felt it the way one feels weather changing behind a hill, in the bones before the sky admits anything. There had been the card game on Tuesday, Weiss's pleasant voice dealing questions across the table like cards themselves: *You were at Halbenstadt, weren't you, Brandt? Before your— situation. You'd know the filing conventions there.* And Brandt had held, barely, smiling his clerk's smile and losing three hands in a row because his mind was elsewhere. Now a new lock. Now the crates scheduled to move up ahead of the thaw. The noose had not tightened; it had simply been redrawn, smaller, neater, the knot moved to where he couldn't see it.

He copied figures. His pen scratched. Around him the workshop smelled of ink and cold iron and lamp oil, and beneath those smells, faint but unmistakable now, the river — thawing, restless, exhaling through the floorboards of the world.

A soldier came to collect the completed manifests at four o'clock, and Brandt handed them over with both hands and a bow of the head that was precisely correct and precisely empty. When the man had gone, he allowed himself, for one moment, to press his palms flat against the table and close his eyes.

In the darkness behind his eyelids he saw Halbenstadt. He saw the report with its columns waiting to be filled, and Captain Ehlers standing over him saying *engagement losses, Brandt — write engagement losses* — and the number forty-one becoming eleven, the way numbers become smaller when they pass through certain hands. He had stolen that page from the crates two weeks ago. It lived now inside his coat lining, against his chest, a paper wound that had somehow become the only honest thing he owned. He had confessed it to Ines at the orchard house, over preserved apples, and she had listened without flinching and then said, quietly, *Then you know how it's done. Which means you know how it's undone.*

He opened his eyes. On the table before him lay a fire-safety inspection form. It had been sitting in the stack for days — garrison bureaucracy breeding its usual paperwork, forms about forms. *Inspection of storage premises for hazard of combustion.* Required before any material was loaded for transport, per regulations he knew better than anyone alive, because he had helped write the circular that established them, three years ago, in a warm office in the capital, believing then that regulations were a kind of virtue.

Regulations were a kind of virtue. Regulations had teeth. Regulations said that no paper stores — and this archive was nothing if not paper stores — might be staged for loading in proximity to open flame, oil lamps, or heating stoves, and that the loading dock must therefore be cleared and inspected, and that staging must occur in daylight hours under supervision of the inventory officer.

The inventory officer was Brandt.

He sat very still while the idea finished assembling itself inside him, part by part, like type being set in a composing stick. Then he began to laugh — silently, horribly, his shoulders shaking in the empty room — because the army had trained him so thoroughly in its own rules that it had never thought to wonder whether the rules might one day be used against it. They had locked the door. Fine. Let them keep the door. He didn't need the door anymore. He needed the crates out of the building, onto the dock, and left alone in the dark for one hour, and regulations — beautiful, indifferent, obedient regulations — would hand them to him.

One hour. It would have to be enough.

* * *

Finding Tomas took until full dark.

The boy was in the cellar, as he often was now, among Marek's molds and the smell of earth and machine oil, and when Brandt came down the stairs with a lamp, Tomas straightened from the workbench like a wire pulled taut. Sixteen years old and already carrying the workshop's whole grief in his shoulders; he had Marek's hands already, ink-stained and steady, though he didn't know it yet.

"The lock's changed," Brandt said, without preamble, keeping his voice below the hearing of the floorboards. "New mechanism. Installed within the last day. We're sealed out."

Tomas went pale in the lamplight. For a moment he looked his age entirely — a boy standing in a cellar holding a rag, the enormity of what they had undertaken suddenly visible on his face like frost. Then something in him hardened, some grain of Marek in him refusing to dissolve. "Did Weiss do it?"

"Weiss, or someone acting for him. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that tomorrow we can't get into that room, and tomorrow night there won't be a room." Brandt set the lamp on the workbench. Its light made a small warm island in the cold cellar; beyond it the shadows held the shape of presses, of crates, of everything they were about to risk. "So I've found another way. Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once."

He laid it out: the inspection order, the regulations, the staging on the loading dock. "At fourteen hundred I file the hazard report citing paragraph nine of Circular Forty-One. By regulation, the crates designated for first loading must be moved to the dock and the dock cleared of all flame sources before evening loading begins. The dock will be supervised during the move. Afterward—" he paused, tasting the audacity of it, the sheer naked nerve required, "—afterward there is the changeover of the guard at seventeen hundred, and the evening mess, and a gap. One hour, perhaps less, in which the dock is technically awaiting escort that has been delayed on paper."

"On paper," Tomas repeated. A ghost of a smile touched his mouth, quick and terrified and proud. "Paper's been good to you today."

"Paper is the only thing the army believes in," Brandt said. "That's how we beat it. We speak its language fluently." He met the boy's eyes. "It will be Ines who does the rest. She can't get into the records room — no one can, not now. So the ledger comes to her. If the crates come out to the dock, and the dock stands alone for one hour, she can find her own book among them. She knows the binding. She knows the weight of it in her hands better than I ever could."

"And the printing?"

"Tomorrow night. Everything depends on tomorrow night anyway — the plan hasn't changed, only narrowed. We print from whatever pages we can save. If we get the ledger, we print all of it. If we don't—" he made himself say it, "—if we don't, we print what we remember. Voss. The others. Whatever names we can hold in our heads between us."

Tomas was quiet a long moment. Down here the river's voice came faintly through the foundation stones, a low groaning grind, the ice talking in its sleep. Both of them listened to it without meaning to. Everyone in town had begun listening to it without meaning to.

"One hour," Tomas said finally. "And if the escort isn't delayed on paper? If someone actually shows up?"

"Then I stand on the dock," Brandt said, "and I apologize, and I cite paragraph twelve, and I delay them further with forms." He almost smiled. "I have spent my whole life being the man nobody waits to see. Tomorrow it becomes a weapon."

He climbed back up toward the lamplit rooms, and at the top of the stairs he stopped and looked back. Tomas had already turned to the workbench, and his hands — Marek's hands, young and sure — were sorting type by feel in the dark, letter by letter, as if rehearsing.

* * *

The report took him twenty minutes to compose, and he composed it the way he had once composed falsifications: beautifully.

*Circular 41, para. 9: premises storing combustible materials to be inspected and cleared prior to staging. Recommend immediate transfer of priority crates to eastern loading dock, dock to be purged of open flame per para. 11. Respectfully submitted, A. Brandt, Inventory Clerk.*

He read it twice, signed it, blotted it, and carried it through the cold streets to garrison headquarters with his heart beating in his ears like a second pulse walking beside him. Snowmelt ran in the gutters. Somewhere a church bell counted six. At the duty desk a bored lieutenant glanced at the form, glanced at Brandt's impeccable clerk's posture, and stamped it without reading a line — because that was the final irony, the final mercy: no one in this army read anything anymore except Brandt, and he had stopped believing in what he read years ago.

*"Approved. Move them in the morning."*

Morning. Not afternoon. The schedule shifted under him like ice underfoot, and he bowed and withdrew and walked back out into the blue dusk recalculating everything — guard changes, mess hours, the gap, the hour. The dock, then, would hold the crates from midmorning. Longer exposed. More eyes. But also more time for Ines to reach them, if the escort truly delayed.

He found her at the hospital, folding bandages with the terrible calm she wore like armor, and told her all of it in a low voice while her hands never stopped moving. She listened the way she did everything — completely, without wasted motion — and when he finished she was silent for the space of three folds.

"They changed the lock because they're afraid of paper," she said at last. "Good. Fear means they understand what we understand." She set down the bandage and looked at him, and her eyes in the ward's dim light were steady as a pilot's. "One hour on that dock, Aldous. Bring me my book, or bring me its ashes — either way, tomorrow night the names go into type."

Outside, the river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a great door swinging on rusted hinges, and every soul in the garrison heard it and pretended not to.
