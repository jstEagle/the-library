## 18. The Night of Names

**Brandt**

The fire hazard report took Aldous Brandt eleven minutes to write and seven years off his life.

He composed it in his careful clerk's hand — the hand that had signed away so many names, now pressed into service returning them. *Accumulated paper stock in the records room constitutes an extreme fire risk to the garrison's printing capacity,* he wrote. *Recommend crates be staged at the loading dock for expedited removal prior to demolition.* He signed it with the rank he no longer believed in, stamped it with the inventory office seal he had been carrying in his coat pocket for three weeks like a stone, and walked it through the slush to the adjutant's billet at half past nine.

The adjutant was young and bored and wanted only for Brandt to stop talking so he could return to his cards. "Dock by midnight," the boy said, waving the paper as one waves a fly. "And if anything is missing from those crates, Brandt, they'll hang you with your own inventory lists."

"Yes, sir," Brandt said, and felt something in his chest that he realized, with astonishment, was laughter.

He crossed the yard between buildings under a sky scoured clean by wind, stars sharp as nailheads, the river below making its low grinding sound — ice shifting, complaining, beginning to dream of breaking. Two days early, Weiss had moved the barges up. Two days. The thaw would not wait for their plan and neither, now, would the army. Everything they had rehearsed in the orchard house over cold apples and whispered arithmetic was useless; everything they had not rehearsed was all they had.

He found Tomas crouched behind the woodpile by the loading dock, breath steaming, eyes enormous.

"Well?" the boy whispered.

"He bought it. Midnight, the crates come out. One hour, maybe less."

Tomas nodded once, the way Marek nodded when a job was set — no fear permitted past the nod. Then he pressed his palm flat against the frozen ground, as if checking whether the earth itself approved, and slipped away toward the cellar door where the old press slept under canvas like a beast in winter.

Brandt stood alone in the dark and let his hands shake, just for a moment, just this once. Tomorrow they could hang him. Tonight his hands were allowed to shake.

* * *

**Ines**

Ines Eriksen had copied names in field hospitals by the light of guttering candles and once, memorably, by the light of a burning supply wagon, and she had thought herself beyond trembling. But her fingers shook now as she laid out the type cases on the composing stone in the workshop's back room: the brass sorts gleaming dull gold in the lamplight, each letter small and cold as a seed.

Voss. She set the V first. *Korporal Anton Voss.* The letters fit together with a click she felt in her teeth. Behind the V came the O, round and open as a mouth, and she remembered his mouth, the last word it shaped — *mother*, or maybe *water*, she had never known which, and she had written down both possibilities in the ledger's margin because even uncertainty deserved recording when a man was being erased.

The ledger lay open beside her, recovered from the records room by Brandt's own hand two hours ago, smuggled out beneath his inventory apron while a sentry checked his papers. Three hundred and forty-one names. Some she knew whole — face, voice, wound, last word. Others were fragments gathered from other nurses, other clerks, men who talked too much in the night ward: a surname overheard, a regiment guessed, a village. For those she had left blanks, and blanks, she had decided, were also a kind of truth. A blank admitted what the army's tidy dashes refused to admit: that someone was missing here, and someone had done the missing.

She heard the crates begin to move outside — boots, grunts, the scrape of pine on frozen mud. Brandt directing traffic in his dry clerk's bark. Every crate that went to the dock was a crate the guards would count and guard, drawing their eyes away from the back room, away from the press, away from the woman setting dead men's names in living metal.

Her lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the river groaned like a thing turning over in sleep.

"Hurry," she whispered to no one, to everyone, to the spring.

* * *

**Tomas**

Firing the old press was like waking a grandfather who had sworn never to rise again.

Tomas worked by feel and memory, no lamp, the canvas dragged off, his hands moving over iron he had oiled and tended since he was nine years old. The great platen press was older than Marek, older than the free newspaper, maybe older than the town's church bells; it had been retired to the cellar years ago because its bearings sang and its frame had settled crooked, and Marek had covered it rather than sell it, saying nothing, which was how Marek said most things.

Now Tomas greased the bearings with tallow he'd stolen from the mess kitchen, tested the screw, wedged the frame true with shims cut from a broken apple crate. His palms sweated inside frozen-stiff gloves. Somewhere above him, boots crossed the floor of the shop proper — guards walking their rounds over the very room where he knelt like a thief in his own inheritance.

He counted their steps. Fourteen across, pause, fourteen back. He had counted them for three weeks of night shifts; he knew Sergeant Dluhy's uneven gait, the drag of his left heel, better than he knew his own heartbeat. Fourteen across, pause, fourteen back, then the long turn at the window, two minutes, sometimes three if Dluhy stopped to smoke.

Two minutes was enough to raise the platen. Not enough to print. But the press would be ready, and readiness, tonight, was everything.

In the cellar's black corner sat the molds Marek had given him, wrapped in oilcloth — the original typeface, cast before the war, before the censorship, when the town's newspaper printed whatever anyone dared to say. *Paper burns,* Marek had told him, pressing the bundle into his arms with both ruined hands. *But letters remember.*

Tomas unwrapped them now, just to see them, just once, in the dark where seeing was impossible and therefore safe. Brass edges cold as river water. Letters remember, he told himself. Be worthy of them.

Above, the heel-drag paused at the window. Held. Moved on.

* * *

**Brandt**

At midnight the crates came out into the moonless cold, forty-two of them, and Aldous Brandt stood among them with his clipboard like a shepherd of condemned sheep.

The sergeant supervising the move was the drunk one — Halas, cheeks red as stove paint, breath a fog of schnapps. He counted badly, lost track twice, and started an argument with a corporal about whether crate nineteen had been counted already. Brandt watched the numbers dissolve and did not correct them. Let them argue. Let the count be wrong. Wrongness was a gift tonight; he intended to spend every coin of it.

"Inventory says forty-four," Halas declared belligerently, jabbing at Brandt's clipboard.

"The inventory is mine to reconcile," Brandt said mildly. "You are responsible for physical custody. Forty-two present."

Halas squinted at him, swaying slightly, some slow machinery turning behind his eyes. Brandt held the gaze the way he had learned to hold it across seven years of desks — empty, obedient, a man made entirely of procedure. It was the look that had made him invisible enough to falsify reports and invisible enough, perhaps, to unmake them.

"Forty-two," Halas conceded grandly, as though granting a pardon. "Post two men. Rest of you, back inside, it's freezing the marrow."

Two men. Not six. Two men, who within twenty minutes had built themselves a sheltered nook between crates thirty and thirty-one, produced a bottle from somewhere, and begun to talk about women in the low reverent tones of the truly frozen.

Brandt walked back toward the workshop slowly, clipboard under his arm, heart hammering a rhythm he refused to name as hope. In the shadows beside the cellar door, a shape detached itself: Tomas, sleeves rolled, face smudged black.

"One hour," Brandt breathed.

"Then we'd best not waste it arguing." And the boy vanished below.

* * *

**Ines**

By lamplight the names came faster now, as though released.

Ines worked the composing stick with the economy the hospital had taught her — triage of the hands, no motion wasted. *Ludvik Havelka, private, 3rd Rifles, listed missing, died of wounds 14 October.* Click, click, click of brass. *Emil Ruzek, listed deserter, executed 2 November, age unknown, mother's name uncertain — Maria?* The question mark she could not set in type; she left the line honest and incomplete. *Jan Sova.* *Petr Malina.* *The brothers Kolar, both listed as fever deaths, both shot the same morning by the same wall.*

Three hundred and forty-one. Her wrist ached. Her eyes burned in the lamp's small circle. But there was a strange mercy in the work she had not anticipated: each name set in type became solid, became weight, became a thing that existed outside her skull. For a year the ledger had lived only in her — a secret carried like a stone in the chest, heavy, solitary. Now the stone was being broken into ten thousand pieces of brass, and the pieces were arranging themselves into sentences, and the sentences were going to live in other people.

Footsteps overhead — many, then few, then none. Somewhere out front the loading dock glowed faint orange with the guards' lantern. She heard Tomas below her feet, the soft iron music of the press waking, the first experimental groan of the screw. She heard Brandt's voice in the yard, performing boredom for the sentries.

And then, cutting through it all, the river spoke — a long, splintering crack that ran the length of the valley like a rifle shot fired under the world.

Ines closed her eyes. *Not yet,* she begged the ice. *One more night. Hold, you old gray beast. Hold until morning, and I will give you every name I have.*

The river groaned, considered, and held.

* * *

**Tomas**

The first proof came off the press crooked, ghosted, beautiful.

Tomas pulled it and held it to the lamp and read his own handiwork upside down and right side up: *A TRUE LIST OF THE DEAD OF THIS DISTRICT, WHOSE NAMES THE ARMY HAS TAKEN FROM YOU.* Below it, in columns, the names began — Voss at the head, because Ines had insisted Voss go first, and none of them had argued with the nurse who had held the man's hand.

The impression was imperfect. The old press sang its bearing-song on every pull, and the type sat a hair proud in places, and the ink — ink stretched with lamp oil because there had been no time — printed brown-black instead of black. Marek would have wept at the quality. Tomas decided Marek could weep later. Imperfect names beat perfect silence.

They worked like a machine themselves now, the three of them: Ines setting type at the stone, sliding finished lines into the chase; Tomas locking the form, pulling proofs, re-inking; Brandt folding the dried sheets — no, there was no time for drying, the sheets went into the satchels damp — folding, counting, stacking. None of them spoke except in numbers and directions. There was no need. Something had happened to the three of them in the orchard house over apples, something none of them would ever say aloud: they had become, without ceremony or agreement, the sort of people who did this.

Forty sheets. Eighty. A hundred and twenty, and the stack still looked absurdly thin against three hundred and forty-one names and a whole town full of doors.

Then — boots.

Tomas's hand froze on the lever. Through the coal chute above, faint but unmistakable: not the fourteen-and-fourteen patrol, not Halas's drag. Quick steps, several, coming around the building from the dock side. A lantern beam swept the high windows of the shop proper and laid a yellow bar across the ceiling joists.

"Down," Ines breathed. "All lights."

The lamp went out. The dark fell complete and total, smelling of oil and ink and cold iron. Tomas stood with his hand on the platen in absolute blackness, listening to his own blood. Beside him he heard Ines breathing — slow, deliberate, the breathing of a woman who had learned to be quiet in wards full of dying men. On his other side, Brandt's breath came fast and shallow, and Tomas reached out and gripped the clerk's sleeve, just to hold him to the world.

The lantern beam crawled across the ceiling. A voice outside, muffled: "Said he heard something. Machinery-like."

"Old building settles," another voice answered. "Whole town's settling. Ice is talking to it."

The beam lingered. Ten seconds. Twenty. Tomas's palm sweated against the frozen steel of the lever, and in the darkness he mouthed, silently, the alphabet — *a, b, c* — the way Marek had taught him to steady his nerves as a boy, letters holding him up the way they held up everything else.

The beam slid away. Boots receded toward the warmth of the brazier at the dock.

Nobody moved for a full minute after. Then Ines struck the match, and the little flame stood up brave in the dark, and she said, "Again."

* * *

**Brandt**

The second near-discovery came at four in the morning, and it nearly broke them, because it was Weiss himself.

Brandt saw the colonel's silhouette through the coal chute — the unmistakable erect carriage, the staff cap, two aides flanking him. The inspection party had come out, God knew why, to walk the perimeter in the black end of the night, and now they were standing not fifteen meters from the cellar door discussing the thaw schedule in clipped officer voices.

"—barges loaded by noon tomorrow, gentlemen. Whatever remains in the building goes with the demolition. Nothing stays. Nothing is *left lying about* for sentimental townspeople." A pause, and then Weiss's voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "I am told Nurse Eriksen has been volunteering at the garrison infirmary. Diligent woman. Make a note of her hours. I like to know where diligent people spend their nights."

Below ground, in the dark, Brandt heard Ines go utterly still. Even her breathing stopped. Tomas's hand found Brandt's arm again and squeezed hard enough to bruise.

Then the officers' voices moved on, gravel crunching, fading toward the granary, and the three conspirators stood in their lamplit burrow looking at one another with the wild-eyed communion of people who have survived a thing they cannot yet believe.

"He knows," Brandt said hoarsely. "Or he guesses. Either way—"

"Either way we finish," Ines said.

It was not a shout. It was barely above a whisper. But it had the flat finality of a surgeon's decision, and both men obeyed it as such.

* * *

**Ines**

The last name went into the stick as the sky behind the coal chute turned the color of ash and milk.

Ines had saved it, though she had planned no such saving — her hands had simply known. *Anna Lindqvist, laundress, listed as influenza, drowned in the millrace 19 March 1916, witnessed by no one official.* The laundress. The girl from the mill whose body the army had logged as disease because logging the truth required paperwork nobody wanted to file. Ines set her letters with a tenderness she did not examine, locked the final line into the chase, and stepped back from the stone.

"Tomas."

The boy fired the press one more time. The great screw descended, singing its cracked old song, and the platen kissed the form, and the two-hundredth sheet took the impression of every name they had.

They pulled sheets until the satchels bulged — two hundred copies, folded wet, stacked warm. The pages steamed faintly in the cellar cold, ink still curing, oil-smell rising off them like bread. Warm paper. Ines pressed her palm flat against the top sheet and felt the heat of the work still in it, the heat of the lamp, the heat of three people's hands, and she thought: *this is what a living list feels like.* The army's lists arrived cold. These would arrive warm.

Outside, the east went from milk to rose. The guards at the dock had dozed around their brazier; Halas snored audibly across the distance. The river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a giant stretching after a century of sleep, and somewhere upstream a crack propagated with a sound like tearing linen, running, running.

Dawn. They had made dawn.

Ines looked at the two of them — the disgraced clerk with ink to his elbows, the boy with iron dust in his hair — and could not speak, and did not need to. Brandt began dividing the stacks into satchels with clerkly precision, muttering distribution counts: market first, the church second, the barracks door third, the hospital fourth. Tomas crated the type with the reverence of an altar boy, every sort back in its case, letters remember, letters remember.

And Ines stood a moment alone before the press, in the smell of ink and oil and spring coming, and let herself feel it, fully, for exactly five seconds: joy. Fierce, terrified, borrowed-time joy.

Then the satchels went over their shoulders, and the cellar door opened onto the freezing gold of the last morning, and the three of them stepped out into a town that did not yet know what it was about to read at breakfast — while behind them, down at the river, the ice gave a long white shudder and began, at last, to break.
