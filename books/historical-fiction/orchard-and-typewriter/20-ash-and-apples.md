## 20. Ash and Apples

The smoke had not finished leaving when Tomas walked back into what remained of the workshop.

It rose in gray ribbons from the blackened skeleton of the roof, curling up into a sky so blue and clean it seemed to mock the ruin beneath it. Noon had come and gone. The dynamite's echo still lived somewhere in his chest, a second heartbeat that would not quiet, and every few minutes he felt it again — that deep concussion, that shudder through the ground — though there was nothing left standing tall enough to fall.

He stood at the edge of the property, where the cobbles gave way to the packed dirt of the yard, and made himself look. It was a discipline he was learning: to look at things instead of away from them. Marek had taught him that with type, once — *you must see each letter whole before you can set it right* — and now Tomas applied it to the wreckage as if it were a page of misprint he alone was responsible for correcting.

The great press was gone. Not broken — *gone*, unmade, its iron bones folded into shapes iron should never hold. The composing stones were shattered slabs. The paper store had burned with a ferocity that smelled, even now, hours later, of glue and rags and something almost sweet, like the memory of every pamphlet and broadsheet and newspaper the workshop had ever produced, all of it going up together in one final edition no one would read.

And yet.

Tomas stepped over the threshold — or where the threshold had been — and his boots crunched on ash and char and, here and there, fragments of type. He knelt. Picked one out of the grit. An *e*. Of course an *e*; there were more *e*s in any font than any other letter; Marek had drilled that into him the first week. *The humble e, boy. The workhorse. Every sentence leans on it.*

He closed his fist around the little piece of metal, still faintly warm from the fire or perhaps only from his own hand, and understood that he could not leave it here. That when the soldiers came tomorrow with their carts and their shovels to clear the lot for whatever sanitized thing they would build — a depot, a stable, a monument to forgetting — there must be nothing left for them to find. Nothing that remembered.

Which meant the molds.

They were in the cellar. Or the cellar was where the cellar had been; the floor above it had collapsed into a tangle of beams, and the stairwell was a throat of rubble. But Marek had built that cellar with his own father, and old men who build cellars build them to survive the century, and when Tomas picked his way along the outside wall to the low delivery hatch — half hidden behind a fallen lintel — he found it warped but intact. He put his shoulder to it. It groaned open like an old man asked to rise.

Inside, the dark smelled of earth and oilcloth and, impossibly, of apples — because Marek had stored a crate of the orchard house's preserved apples down here last autumn, back when the requisition papers were only a rumor, and the glass jars had survived everything above them. Tomas lifted one into the thin light falling through the hatch. The apples inside had darkened to amber, suspended in syrup, perfect and patient, as if the year below ground had simply declined to touch them.

*Paper burns,* Marek had said, pressing the wrapped bundle into Tomas's arms two nights ago in this same darkness. *But letters remember.*

Tomas took up the bundle — three oilcloth-wrapped packages, heavy, dense with the cold particular weight of cast metal — and climbed out into the daylight carrying them against his chest like something newborn.

* * *

Marek was waiting by the orchard gate.

He had not been told to come; there had been no time to tell anyone anything. But the old printer had stood in the crowd at noon when the charges went off, had watched forty years of his life fold inward like a hand closing, and then had turned and walked — slowly, leaning on his stick — in the only direction his feet knew how to go. Toward the trees.

He looked smaller than Tomas had ever seen him. The requisition had bent him; the strike from the officer's glove had marked him; but the demolition had taken something else entirely, and it showed in the way he held his head, as if the neck had grown tired of the weight.

"I brought them," Tomas said.

He unwrapped the bundles on the flat stone beside the gate. The typeface molds lay gleaming in the spring light — brass matrices, some of them older than Marek, older than the town's charter maybe, the original faces from which every letter of the town's first free newspaper had been struck. Marek's father had carried them across two borders sewn into a coat lining. They were the true archive. Everything else had only ever been copies.

Marek looked at them for a long time. His hands, those enormous scarred hands that could feel a misaligned letter blindfolded, hovered above the brass without touching it, the way a man hovers over a sleeping child.

"They wanted them burned with the rest," he said quietly. "Weiss's order said all matrices and molds to be surrendered with the equipment. He knew. The clever bastard knew exactly what he was destroying."

"He didn't get them."

"No." Marek lowered himself onto the stone, joints cracking. "No, he didn't."

And then the old man wept.

Not the dry, angry weeping of the requisition day, when he'd been struck down in front of his own door. This was something without defense in it at all — his shoulders shaking, his face buried in his scarred hands, great ragged sobs torn out of him as if forty years of composure had been a dam and the demolition had finally cracked it. He wept for the press his grandfather had installed. For the newspaper that had named the town's dead honestly through the last war and the famine both. For his wife, long dead, whose name was scratched into the frame of the old case drawer. For the apprentices before Tomas who had grown and married and gone. For the *e*s, maybe, all ten thousand of them.

Tomas did not know what to do, so he did the only thing there was: he sat down on the cold grass beside him and let him. He put one hand on the old man's back, between the shoulder blades, and kept it there, and watched the orchard trees budding around them, white blossom opening on the branches as though the world had not noticed anything had happened at all.

When it was over — when the sobs slowed and deepened into breath, into the ordinary labor of lungs — Marek wiped his face with his sleeve and looked at Tomas with eyes red-rimmed and, somehow, clearer than they had been in weeks.

"Now," he said. "Bury them properly. Not here at the gate. Under the oldest tree, where my father planted the first stock. And mark the spot in your head and nowhere else. No map. No written word." He tapped Tomas's temple with one cracked finger. "In there. That's the only safe place left."

* * *

Digging under the oldest apple tree was slow work. The roots gripped the soil like knuckles, and twice Tomas struck root thick as his arm and had to negotiate around it, apologizing under his breath as if the tree could hear. Perhaps it could. The blossoms drifted down onto his shoulders as he dug, settling in his hair, landing in the small mound of dark earth, and he did not brush them off. Let the letters be buried in blossom. Let the tree keep them.

The hole was barely a spade-depth deep — shallow enough to reach, deep enough to hide — and he lined it with the empty apple crate before laying the bundles in, because Marek said metal slept better in wood, and because it seemed right that the molds should rest in the same box that had carried fruit from these very trees. He wrapped them in the oilcloth again, and then, on impulse, pulled off his own jacket and laid that over the top as well. Let it wear something of mine, he thought. Let it know whose hands will come back for it.

Before he filled the hole, he took out the single piece of type he had carried from the ashes. The *e*. The workhorse. He set it on top of the bundles, in the middle, where his hand would find it first.

Then he covered them over and pressed the earth down flat with the spade's back and scattered blossom across the turned soil until it looked like every other patch of ground beneath every other tree, which is to say: like nothing. Like everywhere. Like the best hiding place in the world.

Marek watched the whole operation from the stone, saying nothing, and when Tomas came back to him he nodded once, the smallest nod, and said, "Good. Now wash your hands. You've company coming, and dirt under your nails is a confession."

* * *

They came at dusk.

Tomas saw them from the kitchen window of Marek's rooms above the ruined shop — Ines first, crossing the square with her medic's bag over her shoulder as if she were making rounds, unhurried, ordinary, invisible in the way she had perfected. Behind her, at a careful distance that fooled no one, walked Aldous Brandt, in civilian clothes that fit him like an apology, hat brim low.

By the time Tomas opened the door they were both on the stairs, and none of them spoke until the door was shut and bolted and the lamp lit and Ines had checked — quickly, without comment, the gesture of a woman who had learned the habit in hospitals where the wrong visitor meant the wrong kind of death — that the window curtains met fully.

"Well," she said. "We're alive."

"We are," Brandt said. He removed his hat and held it in both hands, turning it, a man with nothing left to do with his fingers. "I have spent the day drafting my statement. For the inquiry. They arrive Thursday — I had confirmation this afternoon."

"You don't have to—" Tomas began.

"Yes, I do." Brandt set the hat down on the table with great precision, as if it might otherwise escape. "Listen to me, both of you. What happened here — the pages, the doors, the night — none of that can be traced to a sixteen-year-old apprentice or a garrison nurse. It *can* be traced to a disgraced clerk with a falsification already in his file and nothing left to lose. I signed omissions for seven years. I will sign one more — the largest omission of all, which is the two of you." He looked from Tomas to Ines and back. "This is not sacrifice. This is arithmetic."

Ines studied him for a moment. Then she reached into her medic's bag and set three jars of preserved apples on the table, amber and glowing in the lamplight, rescued from the cellar hatch that morning.

"Arithmetic," she said, "after supper."

They ate at Marek's narrow table, and it was the strangest meal Tomas had ever known: four people passing bread and apples and a hard sausage Brandt had produced from nowhere, speaking in low voices about the weather and the ice going out on the river and whether the buds would hold against frost — while underneath every word ran the other conversation, the one none of them dared say aloud. That yesterday at dawn they had printed eight hundred names on a press the army believed destroyed. That the names were on doors and in hymnals and in market baskets all over the town, and in a thousand fists that would not open. That Colonel Weiss had stood in the square raging at a crime committed by everyone and no one, and had ordered the workshop dynamited at noon in pure spite, and that spite had accomplished precisely nothing except to give the town its martyrdom and the conspirators their freedom.

Nobody said *the ledger*. Nobody said *the pages*. Nobody said *we*. To speak it would be to fix it, to make it evidence; unspoken, it stayed fluid, deniable, safe — a river running under ice that no longer existed anywhere but between the four of them.

But near the end, when the jars were empty and the lamp burned low, Marek raised his cup — tea, weak and oversteeped, the last luxury in the house — and said, "To the free press of this town. Founded by my father. Suppressed by empires. Dynamited by a colonel." He drank. "Currently operating underground."

Tomas laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in days — and saw Ines smiling behind her cup, and even Brandt's mouth twitched, that careful clerk's mouth learning, late in life, the shape of joy.

* * *

The goodbyes happened on the stairs, in the dark, because goodbyes spoken in lamplight are too easy to remember.

Ines went first. She shook Marek's hand with formal respect, the old printer bowing over it like a courtier, and then she took Tomas's hand in both of hers and held it a moment longer than the handshake required.

"The honor roll," she murmured. "Next winter, when it's safe. We'll need someone who knows letters."

"I'll be here," Tomas said.

"I know," said Ines Eriksen, who had written nine hundred names and never her own. "That's why I'm asking."

Brandt lingered last. At the bottom of the stairs he paused, and turned half back, and said to the darkness above them, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the banister:

"Thursday. If anyone asks, I acted alone, for reasons of private conscience, documented in my statement, which is quite thorough and quite mad." A pause. "Thank you both. For letting an old coward die useful."

And he was gone into the evening, a hat brim and a straight back, walking toward his arithmetic.

Marek closed the door. For a while neither of them moved. Then the old man put his arm around Tomas's shoulders — the first time he had done such a thing since Tomas was small enough to fit under it easily — and steered him to the window that overlooked the orchard.

Below them, in the last light, the apple trees stood in full bloom, white against the darkening ground, and somewhere among them, under the oldest tree, under a scattering of blossom, a crate of brass letters slept in the dark earth, waiting.

"Come spring next year," Marek said, "we dig."

Tomas looked out at the trees, at the small white flowers opening in their hundreds like pages, like names, like a town refusing to forget, and thought of the *e* resting on top of the bundle in the dark — the workhorse, the humble letter every sentence leans on — and understood that nothing, really, had ended here.

Something had been planted.

Far off across the roofs, he could hear the river running loud and free where the ice had broken, carrying the barges downstream toward the sea — carrying, as far as any record would ever show, nothing but blank ledgers and a colonel's fury.

And on Thursday, he knew, soldiers would come asking questions, and Aldous Brandt would answer them alone.

Tomas pressed his palm flat against the cold glass, over the orchard, over the buried letters, and made the only promise left to make.

*I'll be here.*
