## 21. The Inquiry

They came on a morning of thin rain, three officers and a stenographer, riding up from the landing in a requisitioned carriage with mud to the axles. Brandt saw them from the window of the canteen where he had taken to drinking his coffee — one cup, slowly, in the sight of as many people as possible, because a man who hid was a man who was guilty, and he could not afford guilt that showed.

He set down the cup. His hand did not shake. Seven years ago it would have; seven years ago, at the desk in the records annex at Vellmark, when the order came down to recategorize the dead of Halstow Field, his hand had shaken so badly he had spilled ink across the requisition form and been made to copy the whole page again. He remembered the blot spreading like a wound. He remembered copying the lie a second time in a steadier hand, and how the steadiness itself had frightened him more than the trembling ever had.

The rain came down fine as sifted flour. Somewhere beyond the river the ice groaned — not breaking yet, only shifting, a great animal turning over in its sleep — and the sound carried through the wet air like a warning no one had asked for.

The officers posted a notice on the canteen door: *By order of the Inspectorate General, an inquiry will convene regarding irregularities in the disposition of records at the garrison print workshop. All personnel connected with said workshop will present themselves for interview.*

*All personnel.* Brandt read the words twice. Then he folded his hands behind his back, the old clerk's posture, spine straight, heels together, and went out into the rain to be counted.

* * *

They gave him a room in the commandeered schoolhouse: a deal table, two chairs, a lamp with a cracked chimney, the stenographer with her fingers already poised. The senior officer was a Major Ostrava, grey at the temples, with the patient face of a man who had spent thirty years listening to lies and had stopped being angry about them long ago.

"Name and rank."

"Aldous Brandt. Clerk, second class." He paused. "Formerly."

"Formerly?"

"I was cashiered in the spring of 'eleven, sir. Irregularities in my own filing." Let them find that themselves or not at all; better they heard it from his mouth first, shaped into confession before it could be shaped into accusation. "I was reinstated in a civilian capacity to inventory the seized archive."

Ostrava's eyes came up from the folder. They were pale eyes, the colour of weather. "You were cashiered for irregularities in filing."

"Yes, sir."

"And then the army saw fit to trust you with its most sensitive documents."

"The army saw fit to use what was cheap, sir." It was almost insolence. He let it stand. A man who insulted himself first left an interrogator nothing to pry open.

The stenographer's pencil scratched. Rain ticked against the shuttered window. From the yard outside came the ordinary sounds of the garrison — boots, a mule complaining, someone laughing at nothing — the world going on with its business while the room filled slowly, like water rising, with everything Brandt had carried for seven years.

Ostrava turned a page. "Your inventory logs show an anomaly. On the night of the eleventh, the loading dock was opened between the hours of two and four. Crates were moved out and back. You signed the hazard-transfer order yourself."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Fire risk, sir. The lamp oil stored above the type cellar. I reported it, I was told to resolve it, I resolved it by moving the crates to the dock for ventilation and inspection." Every word true. Every word a wall built in front of the door. "Nothing was removed from garrison custody. The crates went back inside before morning inspection."

"Yet during those same hours," Ostrava said mildly, "the workshop's oldest press was found warm. Ink in the reservoir. Freshly cast slugs in the sort case — type that matches no matrix registered to this facility."

Silence. The rain ticked. Somewhere in the walls of the schoolhouse a pipe knocked twice and went still.

Brandt looked at his own hands, flat on the table, and thought about how much a pair of hands can hold without spilling. Ines's voice saying *every one of them* — every name, Corporal Voss, the boys from the ferry crossing, the whole erased congregation of the missing. Tomas's hands black to the wrist, feeding the press. The pages running out warm and damp as new bread. A thousand copies gone into a thousand hands before dawn, nailed to doors, tucked into hymnals, pressed into market baskets, so thoroughly distributed that by the time Weiss understood what had happened there was no confiscating it — you cannot arrest a town for reading.

"You have nothing to say, Clerk Brandt?"

"I'm thinking, sir."

"Think quickly."

He thought instead of Halstow Field. That was the thing about the end of things — they arranged themselves backwards, the whole seven years standing in a queue behind the moment, waiting their turn. The annex at Vellmark, hot that summer, flies at the wire screens. The order stamped *RECLASSIFY PER INSTRUCTIONS*, which meant: these men did not die in a massacre of disarmed prisoners at a watering station, they died honourably in an engagement, and the difference is a matter of paperwork. Forty-one names. He had copied each one onto the new form, changing the column headings, moving *executed* to *killed in action*, and the pen had moved smoothly, smoothly, and when it was done the colonel had clapped him on the shoulder and said *good work, Brandt, clean work*, and he had gone out into the evening feeling nothing at all, which was itself the worst thing, the nothing, the terrible administrative calm.

It had lasted six years, the nothing. Until a ledger surfaced out of a floorboard in a river town and a nurse's careful handwriting showed him a name he knew — *Voss, E., corporal, listed missing* — written in her small fierce script with the true date beside it, and the nothing cracked like river ice.

"Sir," he said, "I'd like to make a statement. Not answers to your questions. A statement. I'd like it taken down formally, with your witness present."

Ostrava sat back a fraction. Interrogators dislike statements; a statement is a gift wrapped in barbed wire. But procedure was procedure, and men like Ostrava lived inside procedure the way monks live inside a rule.

"Proceed."

* * *

So he told them. Not all of it — never all of it, never the orchard house, never the supper of preserved apples, never the wax impression of the key warming in Tomas's palm, never Ines's name spoken aloud in any official room — but enough, carefully chosen, load-bearing enough to hold the roof up over everyone else.

He told them he had falsified the Halstow report. He named the colonel who ordered it, dead now two years, beyond reach, which was a cowardice and he knew it, but the dead cannot hang and the living conspirators could. He told them that when he arrived to inventory the seized archive he recognized the format of the omission reports, having authored such formats himself, and that his presence in this town was not misfortune but repayment coming due. He told them he alone had engineered the transfer of crates to the loading dock; that he alone had possessed knowledge of the lock schedule; that any printing done that night was done by persons unknown to him, whom he would not describe even under sanction, because he had watched an army spend seven years describing forty-one dead men as something other than what they were, and he declined, finally, at the end of his life, to describe anyone falsely ever again.

The stenographer's pencil kept scratching. It sounded, Brandt thought, like rain on a tin roof, like the small persistent weather of record-keeping, and he found he loved the sound. All his life he had been on the wrong side of that scratching. Now it ran beneath his words like a river under ice, taking them down faithfully, and whatever they did with them afterward, at least the words would exist somewhere whole.

Ostrava let him run to the end. Then: "You understand what you're claiming. Falsification of a casualty report is a capital-adjacent offence in wartime. The Halstow matter alone—"

"Is seven years old, sir, and the officer who ordered it is buried with honours in the capital. I'm the only living signature on that page. And I stole the page." He reached into his breast pocket — Ostrava's hand twitched toward his sidearm, absurdly, and Brandt almost smiled — and laid the folded sheet on the table. The original. *Engagement losses, 41. Executed, 0.* His own young hand all over it, smooth and steady and damned. "I've kept it since the eleventh. I imagine you'd want it for your files."

Ostrava looked at the page for a long time. Something moved behind the pale eyes — not sympathy, nothing so generous, but recognition perhaps, the look of a man finding in another man's ruin a shape he half-remembered from his own.

"You're aware," Ostrava said quietly, "that a full confession to the Halstow matter makes the rest of this nearly irrelevant. A man who confesses to the greater crime renders the lesser inquiries redundant. It's — tidy."

"Yes, sir."

"It's also convenient. For you, and for whoever else was in that workshop."

"It's true, sir. Convenience and truth occasionally share a bed. My experience is they rarely do, and one learns to distrust it, but here—" he touched the stolen page "—they happen to be lying together."

Something almost like humour crossed the major's mouth and was gone.

* * *

They held him eleven days.

Not in the guardhouse proper — that held deserters and drunks — but in a storeroom at the schoolhouse with a cot, a bucket, a barred vent, and, because Ostrava was a procedural man and procedural men are capable of small decencies, a shelf of books left over from some confiscated library. Brandt read them all, even the treatise on drainage engineering, twice.

The sentence came down on the twelfth day, read aloud in the schoolyard in the rain: *Aldous Brandt, former clerk second class, convicted of falsification of official records and theft of military documents. Stripped of all rank and standing. Sentenced to six months' detention, to be served at the district facility, followed by permanent removal from army rolls.*

Six months. He had braced for years. He had privately braced for a wall and a firing squad, and had decided, in the nights among the drainage treatises, that if it came to that he would go upright, and that this too would be a kind of payment, though a poor currency for the dead. Six months was almost funny. Six months was mercy administered by bureaucracy, which is the only place mercy survives anymore.

Weiss was not present at the reading. He had wanted Brandt hanged — Brandt learned this later, from the talk of guards, from the way the garrison's anger needed somewhere to go after the lists appeared on every door in town and the barges pulled away downstream carrying nothing but blank ledgers and the colonel's humiliation. Weiss had demanded a general court-martial, charges of sedition, the whole apparatus. Ostrava had given him Brandt instead: one man, self-condemned, whose confession closed the file. Sedition requires conspirators, and Brandt's paper trail contained exactly one conspirator, and he was standing in the rain with his head bowed while a lieutenant read out his ruin.

*Invisible*, Ines had said once, over apples, in the lamplight of the orchard house. *That's the trick of it. We've spent years making the dead invisible. Now we learn the trick ourselves.* She hadn't meant it as a lesson. She'd meant it as comfort.

The rain fell. The ice on the river groaned its long note. Brandt stood in the schoolyard with his rank stripped and his file closed and felt — he searched for the word and it took him a moment, because he had not felt it in seven years and had begun to suspect it was a thing other men invented —

light.

Not happy. Not safe. Six months of detention waited, and the world was still a machine for grinding names, and somewhere downstream the barges were burning paper. But the weight — the specific weight, the Halstow weight, the forty-one names he had carried in the pit of him like swallowed stones — that was gone. Paid down. Not forgiven; forgiveness wasn't on offer and he didn't want it, wanting forgiveness would have been another theft. Just paid. A debt settled at the price of everything he had left, which turned out to be almost nothing, which turned out to be enough.

As they led him to the wagon, past the canteen, past the shuttered shell of the print workshop with its boarded windows and the raw scar of its demolition, he saw, pinned to the canteen door among the notices, a single printed page, curling in the damp. The true list. Someone had nailed it there the morning the ice broke and no one had dared take it down; it hung there still, weeks later, soft-edged with rain, the names blurring gently into one another like faces seen through water.

He read the ones nearest the top. *Voss, Emil, corporal.* Held a dying man's hand and wrote down the truth of it, Ines had told them, that night in the orchard house, apples and lamplight and the empty hiding place under the boards. *She held his hand. Somebody has to say he existed.*

A guard cleared his throat. Brandt looked once more at the blurred names, then climbed into the wagon.

* * *

The district facility was a converted granary upriver, stone-floored, cold, honest in its ugliness. The warders had heard the story — of course they had; the story had travelled faster than the prisoner, riding the same current of paper — and they treated him with a strange, embarrassed courtesy, the courtesy owed to a man who has done a notorious thing for reasons no one dares applaud aloud. One of them, a young fellow with a farm boy's hands, slipped an apple into Brandt's ration bowl the first week, and said nothing, and looked away, and kept doing it, week after week, all through the winter of his sentence, until the little pile of cores in the corner of his cell was a private orchard, a small sweet conspiracy of one.

He wrote letters. To no address — there was no address he could safely write to, and none needed; the letters went into a tin box under his cot, addressed to *The Orchard*, and were less correspondence than practice, rehearsals of a conversation he might someday be free to have. *Dear friends. Today the warden asked me why I confessed. I told him the truth, which surprised us both...*

In the spring they walked him out the gate with his pay arrears in an envelope and his name on no roll anywhere in the army of his country. Free, in the technical sense: a man of no rank, no post, no prospects, middle-aged and unskilled except in the one skill that had ruined him.

He stood on the road outside the granary a long time, letting the sun land on his face. Then he turned south, toward the river town — walking, because he had money for the train and chose to walk, choosing to feel every mile of the distance he was closing, the way a man counts out coins he owes.

It took him nine days. The town, when he reached it, was wearing spring: blossom on the ruined orchard's surviving trees, the river running high and brown and loud, carts on the landing road, children shouting at the ferry. And on the rise above the town, smoke rising thin from the chimney of the orchard house, and a figure in the dooryard — broad-backed, sleeves rolled, kneeling at a fresh-dug bed with seed potatoes in a basket — who straightened as he came up the path and shaded her eyes.

Behind her, in the doorway, a young man with ink-stained fingers was fitting something into the frame of a rebuilt porch, and an old man sat in the sun with his ruined hands open in his lap, watching the road.

Brandt stopped at the gate. He had rehearsed a hundred openings in the tin box of his letters, and every one of them deserted him now, and what came out instead was simply:

"The apples. I owe you an apple. I've been eating yours all winter — a farm boy at the granary kept slipping them into my bowl, and I know — I know whose orchard they reminded me of."

Ines Eriksen looked at him across the gate for a long moment. Then she stepped back and held it open.

"Well," she said. "Then you'd better come in and plant some."
