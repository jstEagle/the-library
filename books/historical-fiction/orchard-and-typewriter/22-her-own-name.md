## 22. Her Own Name

The orchard bloomed a year to the day after the workshop fell.

Ines noticed it first from the kitchen window, the way she noticed most things now — slowly, with her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory coffee that had gone lukewarm without her drinking it. The trees had decided something in the night. All along the row they had opened at once, white and reckless, as if the year of grief had been nothing but a long winter's sleep and they had woken unbothered, unforgiving of no one, simply ready. Petals were already coming loose in the wind off the river and drifting across the yard like the paper snow of some gentler war.

She stood there a long time. The rebuilt corner of the house — new boards still pale where the old wood had gone grey, new glass in the window that caught the morning differently than the old glass ever had — framed the orchard the way a locket frames a portrait. She had chosen this window deliberately when the carpenters asked her where she wanted it. She had not told them why. Some reasons you keep the way you keep seeds: dark, dry, waiting for their season.

The town had changed too, though it wore its changes more quietly than the orchard wore its blossoms. The garrison had withdrawn in the autumn, its colonels reassigned, its barges gone downriver carrying nothing anyone remembered wanting. The granary had been given back. The market stalls had returned, and with them the small commerce of ordinary grievance — women arguing over the price of salt herring, boys running messages for pennies, the butcher's dog asleep in the doorway where everyone had learned to step over him. A town learning again how to be bored. Ines found she loved even the boredom. Boredom was what peace sounded like when it was telling the truth.

But the honor roll was what had brought her back to the ledger.

* * *

It sat on the table now where the supper dishes usually went: a new book, bound in grey linen, sewn by the widow Halvorsen who had once made shrouds and now, she said, would rather make cradles of paper. Its pages were heavy and cream-colored, milled from rags by the little hand press Tomas had coaxed back to life in the shed behind Marek's ruined workshop — the shed that had held the molds all those years, the cellar beneath it untouched when everything above it came down in dust and thunder.

Ines dipped her pen. Outside, the petals kept falling.

She began the way she had begun the first ledger, three years and a lifetime ago, beneath the floorboards of this very room, writing names the army had refused to say aloud. But this was not that book. That book had been an accusation. This one was a welcome home.

*Eriksen, Ines* — no. Not yet. Not yet.

She started instead with the ones who had come back. That had been the widow Halvorsen's idea, proposed one Sunday after church with the directness of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and had run out of patience for euphemism. *You wrote down who died,* she said, gripping Ines's arm hard enough to bruise. *Now write down who lived. Write down that we came home. Otherwise it's all graves and no harvest.*

So the first page read:

*Halvorsen, Erik — returned, wounded at Kolvary Ridge, walks with a cane, mends nets.*
*Brandt, Aldous — returned.*

She paused over that one. Her pen hovered, and she let it hover, because some entries deserved to be written slowly.

Aldous Brandt had come back to the town in the spring, walking up from the prison depot at Vessel with his uniform coat folded under his arm like a thing he was returning to a shop. He had served eight months of his sentence — stripped of rank, stripped of everything except, as he put it, the excellent company of his own conscience, which he now had to live with at close quarters for the first time in years. When he stepped off the road into the orchard yard, hat in hand, thinner than she remembered and grey at the temples, he had not asked forgiveness. He had asked if there was work.

There was. There always is, in an orchard, in spring. He pruned the apple trees badly the first year and well the second, and he sat at her table on Sundays, and he never once called himself forgiven, which Ines suspected was its own kind of penance and its own kind of peace. Last month he had taken over the schoolroom records — the town had need of someone who understood ledgers, someone careful, someone who knew exactly what a name was worth — and the children had already begun calling him Herr Brandt with the affectionate insolence children reserve for men they trust.

*Returned,* she wrote. *Teaches the young ones their letters.*

Underneath, in smaller script: *He signed the omissions once. He spends every day signing the restorations now.* Then she crossed that out — the honor roll was not the place for judgment, even merciful judgment — but she did not blot the ink entirely, so that the words remained faintly legible, ghosted into the paper, the way true things remain faintly legible under everything written over them.

That, she thought, was rather the point of the whole book.

* * *

Tomas arrived at midmorning, as he did most days now, letting himself through the gate with the particular creak-and-click he had taught the latch — a private knock, a signature. He was seventeen and taller, shot up through the shoulders that winter, his hands stained permanently with ink in the creases the way a farmer's are stained with earth. He carried a wooden case under one arm and a loaf of Marek's bread under the other, because Marek insisted, and because bread was how that old man had decided to speak his love since the day he wept.

"How is she today?" Tomas asked, nodding at the ledger, setting the bread down, kissing the widow Halvorsen's binding with two fingers the way printers kiss good paper.

"She's waiting for you," Ines said. "I've saved the last line."

His face did something complicated then — the boy's face and the man's face both showing through at once, the way dawn shows through a window with the shutter half closed. He knew what she meant. They had talked about it in February, over the press in the shed, while the type cases breathed their cold metal smell and the river groaned its ice apart outside, a year almost to the season since the last thaw, since the night of names. She had told him then, quietly, setting a line of type upside down so only he could read it: *When the book is done, I want my own name in it. At the end. Set properly, in your hands, not mine.* And Tomas had looked at her with an expression she could not entirely read — part awe, part grief, part the fierce protectiveness of someone who has watched a person risk everything and does not want them spending anything more — and had said only, "Then I'll set it clean."

Now he opened the case. Inside, nested in wool, lay the type: the original molds' descendants, cast fresh in the cellar under the ruins, letters with the slight irregularity of hand-cut faces, each character a little imperfect, a little human. *Paper burns,* Marek had told him, pressing the molds into his hands among the ashes. *But letters remember.* Tomas had proved it. Every notice, every broadsheet, every hymnal insert the town printed now came off that press wearing the same face the first free newspaper had worn forty years ago. The town read it without knowing why it felt familiar. Letters remember, and readers remember too, somewhere below thought.

"Read me where you are," Tomas said, and pulled the stool to the table beside her, the way apprentices have always sat beside their masters and masters beside their apprentices, the distinction dissolved here in this kitchen where neither of them was quite either anymore.

Ines read. She had been reading the pages aloud all week, in fact — a habit begun accidentally when the widow Halvorsen came by and stayed, then Brandt, then old Marek himself, propped in the corner chair with a blanket over his knees though it was April, listening with his eyes shut while she recited the town back to itself. It had become, without anyone deciding it, a kind of liturgy. Every evening the honor roll read aloud in the orchard house, and every evening the names settled into the listeners like seed into turned ground.

*Voss, Corporal Anders* — the first name she had ever written, the first omission she had ever refused. She read the entry she had composed for him here, different from the ledger's stark record: *Died 14 October, held my hand, asked about his mother's garden. His mother lives on Mill Lane. She grows beans.* Somewhere in the town, on Mill Lane, a woman grew beans above a son's grave that finally bore his name, cut by Tomas's chisel into the stone that had stood blank for two years until the town raised money for a carver. Ines had visited it that morning. The stone was warm in the sun. Stones should be warm, she had thought. The dead should get to feel the weather somehow.

Page after page. The deserters whose executions the reports had called "disciplinary transfers." The drowned, the frozen, the fevered, the boys listed as missing so that no widow's pension need be paid. Each name now paired with its truth: not just *died* but *was*, a line about a laugh or a limp, a sweetheart in the next valley, a habit of whistling off-key, the way Private Lund had shared his bread with stray dogs until the dogs of the garrison followed him in formation. Memory as an act of defiance — she had learned that at a field hospital cot, holding a dying corporal's hand — but memory as an act of *tenderness*, that she had learned here, in this room, reading aloud to people who nodded and wept and sometimes laughed, saying yes, yes, that was him, that was exactly him.

Outside, the petals came down. Inside, the names went into the book.

* * *

By late afternoon the roll was full to the last ruled line, save one. The light had turned gold and low through the new window, laying long amber panes across the table, and the four of them — for Marek had come up from the shed leaning on Tomas's arm, and the widow Halvorsen had appeared with a jar of the first rhubarb, drawn, she claimed, by pure coincidence — had fallen into the comfortable silence of people who have nothing left to hide from one another.

Tomas cleaned the type one last time. Ines watched his fingers move through the little metal forest, selecting, and felt her heart do something strange and steady, like a clock being wound.

"There," he said softly, and turned the composing stick toward her.

Set in type, letter by letter, in the face that remembered:

*ERIKSEN, INES — RETURNED.*

Nothing else. No list of deeds. The others had lines about canes and beans and lessons; hers was only those two words. But she looked at it and understood that no other entry needed saying. *Returned* — to whom? To what? To a town that had learned not to ask questions and had unlearned it; to a floorboard pried up and left loose on purpose now, a family relic; to a press fired in the dark by a boy and a clerk and a nurse while guards' boots rang on the dock stones; to a thousand hands that had carried her pages into hymnals and market baskets and the pockets of men who wept reading them on the docks as the blank-ledgered barges pulled away.

She took the pen. Her hand, she noted with distant amusement, was steadier than it had been for any name before this one — steadier than for Voss, steadier than for the hundreds, steadier than for the blank gravestones she had filled aloud behind the hospital while the ice groaned its surrender.

At the end of the roll, beneath the last entry, she wrote in her own small, slanted hand:

*Eriksen, Ines — kept the names.*

Then, because the truth deserved its whole width: *and was kept by them.*

Marek made a sound in his throat that might have been a cough and might not. The widow Halvorsen blew her nose with tremendous dignity. Brandt stood at the window with his back to them, and his shoulders shook once, briefly, and stilled, and no one mentioned it, and everyone knew.

They printed it that evening in the shed, the five of them crowded around the old press while the lamp burned low and the river ran open and loud beyond the fence, free of its ice at last, going about its long business of carrying things away and bringing things back. Tomas worked the lever. The impression bit deep and true. One copy only — the first of the new edition, to lie beside the original ledger in the rebuilt corner of the orchard house, where the floorboards had been left loose over the empty hiding place like a healed scar left uncovered, proof of what had been risked and survived.

Ines held the warm sheet up to the lamplight. Wet ink, warm paper, her own name rising out of the page in letters older than the war, older than any of them, patient as seeds.

"Well," said Marek, into the ringing quiet after the press. "Now it's history."

"No," said Ines, folding the page with both hands, carefully, the way you fold a letter you mean to keep forever. "Now it's ours."

Outside, the last light left the blossoms, and the petals went on falling anyway, unhurried, covering the ground like a slow snow that meant, for once, that nothing was buried — only planted.
