## 1. The Weight of Oil

The lamp wanted trimming at half past four, and Juniper Ashgrove trimmed it at half past four, because a light that kept its own hours was a light that would one day fail a man in the dark.

He worked by feel as much as sight, his hands moving through the ritual they had learned across eleven years: the brass key turned three times to lift the burner carriage, the wick drawn up and squared with the scissors mounted on a chain beside the lamp, the char scraped away until the flame could breathe clean. The oil reservoir he topped from the can he'd carried up that morning — forty pounds of it, no more, because forty pounds was what a man could carry up ninety-one steps without setting it down, and Juniper had long ago stopped pretending he was stronger than arithmetic. Everything on Merrow's Reach came down to weight in the end. What a man could carry. What a man must set down.

The flame steadied, tall and pale gold behind the lens, and the great crystal drum threw its beam out over the water in the slow wheel he knew better than his own heartbeat. Three seconds dark. Nine seconds bright. Out past the headland the sea lay like poured pewter under an autumn sky the color of old dishwater, and somewhere beyond the far edge of that gray, the mainland went about its business without him.

He checked his watch against the clockwork that drove the lens rotation, found them within two seconds of each other, and let himself be satisfied with that. It was the fifth of October, 1893. He wrote it in the logbook in his careful hand — *Lamp trimmed 4:30. Weather fair, wind NW freshening. Sea moderate.* — and blotted the page, and stood for a moment in the lantern room with nothing left to do, which was always the worst part of any day.

Eleven years, and the island still did not know what to make of him, and he had stopped expecting otherwise. He had come as a stranger with a dead wife's trunk and a government posting, and the village below had been polite the way people are polite to weather — acknowledging him, accommodating him, never once inviting him indoors. He kept their light. He signed for their oil. In eleven years he had been asked to supper twice, both times by the harbormaster, both times ending early when the conversation ran dry as a summer cistern.

He descended the stairs now, ninety-one of them, counting without meaning to. Counting was how he prayed, if it was praying. Numbers held still when everything else moved.

* * *

The store room smelled of paraffin and salt and the ghost of hemp rope, and Juniper spent an hour there after supper taking inventory, because inventory was the other way he prayed.

Oil: nine drums in the outer store, four hundred gallons give or take the dregs, enough to last through December if the winter ran ordinary. He wrote the figure down. Wicks: fourteen new, six in service. Glass: clean, no cracks, though he'd replace the eastern pane before the equinox gales if the supply boat ever remembered him. Matches: eleven boxes. Bread: getting low. Tea: lower. He stood in the doorway of the store room and looked at the shelf where the tea should have been plentiful and did the arithmetic again, as though the numbers might have softened overnight. They had not.

The supply cutter came once a month in fair weather, less often as autumn deepened, and last month she'd come short — half his requisition, with a printed apology from some office in Portsmouth explaining that naval demands upon the coastal service had increased. Naval demands. There were soldiers on Merrow's Reach now, a whole company of them at the old fort above the harbor, sent in the spring to mind the channel against whatever threats the Admiralty imagined lived in these waters. Soldiers ate. Soldiers drank tea. And the island's harvest had been poor this year, the apples small and scabby from a wet summer, the barley thin, and Juniper had watched from his gallery as carts rolled up the cliff road toward the garrison granary all September, loaded high, escorted.

He wrote *requisition tea & flour URGENT* in his order book, pressed hard enough to score the next page, then smoothed the paper down and told himself that pressing harder changed nothing. It never had.

The wind was rising when he stepped outside. He could hear it coming up the channel before he felt it, a long low note like a finger run along the rim of a wet glass, and the first leaves off the rowan by the door went skittering past his boots toward the sea. Winter was coming early this year. He could taste iron in the air.

Before bed he climbed back up, not because the lamp needed him but because climbing was what his legs expected, and stood in the lantern room watching the dark come down over the water. Somewhere out there, past the shoals he guarded, were towns with streets and voices and women selling fish from barrows, and none of it concerned him. His world was ninety-one steps and a rotating lens and a logbook that never lied, because a logbook was the only thing in a keeper's life that couldn't leave him.

That thought arrived unbidden and sat down in his chest like a stone, and Juniper Ashgrove, who was precise about everything except his own grief, took the stairs down faster than usual and did not count them.

* * *

The trunk sat under the window in the front room, and every October he opened it, and every October he measured.

It had been her mother's trunk originally, cedar-lined, brass corners gone soft with handling, and Edith Ashgrove had packed it herself the week before they married, when they'd learned the posting to Merrow's Reach meant leaving everything behind but what two people and a porter could carry. She'd been laughing while she folded the dresses in. He remembered that. She'd said something like, *Well, if I can't carry it, I don't need it,* and thrown her good gray shawl onto the discard pile, and then picked it back up, and then thrown it again, laughing harder, and finally wrapped it around her neck right there in the middle of the packing and worn it all evening like a queen.

Juniper knelt by the trunk now and lifted out the dresses one at a time, holding each to the lamplight, checking for damp or moth the way he'd done every year since. The lavender paper between the folds had faded to the color of weak tea. Her blue walking dress, the one with the mended hem — she'd caught it on a gorse bush their second month on the island and laughed about that too. Her Sunday black, brushed and boxed and never worn on Merrow's Reach because there had been nowhere worth wearing it to, which she'd pretended not to mind. A lace collar. Two petticoats. A hat with a pheasant feather that had seemed fashionable in another life, in another country almost, in the years before.

He measured them the way he measured everything: by weight, by usefulness, by whether a thing could be carried. It was a habit she'd teased him for. *You weigh your words before you spend them, Juniper Ashgrove. I've seen you do it. Like coins.* She'd meant it kindly. Most of what she'd said had been kind; grief had taught him that memory keeps only the kindest fractions and lets the rest wash out with the tide, which was perhaps mercy, and perhaps theft, and he had never decided which.

The question he asked the trunk every October was the same one, and he asked it aloud this year, to the empty room, because the silence had gotten long lately and his voice sounded strange and human in it:

"What am I keeping you for?"

No answer. The wind worried at the shutters. Down the coast a wave broke on the outer rocks with a sound like a door closing.

Because here was the truth he circled every autumn and never landed on: there was no ship coming that required him to choose. No transfer, no evacuation, no fire, no flood. Nobody was going to ask Juniper Ashgrove to carry anything off this island but himself, and himself weighed less every year, he suspected. The measuring was pure ceremony. He kept the trunk packed as if for departure the way he kept the wicks trimmed — against a need that might never come, because preparation was the only form hope had left to him.

But this October, kneeling in the lamplight with her blue dress in his hands, the ceremony cracked a little. The wool smelled of cedar and almost, almost of her, and he pressed his face into it before he could decide not to, and stayed there a long time, a big weathered man kneeling on cold floorboards with a dead woman's dress against his face, while the light upstairs turned and turned and turned, faithful as a heartbeat, asking nothing of anyone.

When he finally rose, his eyes were dry. They usually were. Grief, after five years, stops being a storm and becomes a climate — something you live inside, something that shapes what grows.

He refolded the dress along its original creases. He laid the lavender paper back between the folds. He closed the lid and shot the brass latch, and set the trunk's weight in the ledger of things carried, and went to check the lamp a third time, though the clockwork had not yet wound down and the flame stood perfectly true.

* * *

In the morning the wind had swung north and sharpened, and Juniper walked the cliff path to the signal mast to hoist his supply flag — the square red pennant meaning *stores required* — and found, halfway along the path, that he was not alone.

A boy was sitting on the turf above the path, maybe fourteen, with a sack of windfall apples beside him and a slingshot in his pocket visible as a Y of wood, and he scrambled up when Juniper appeared, wary as a gull.

"Morning," Juniper said.

"Morning, sir." The boy touched his cap brim. Hallows' boy — Tam, that was it, the orchard family from the village, the ones whose trees marched down the south slope in rows older than anyone living. Juniper had bought their cider at the harvest market every year, paying too much, and every year Maren Hallow had quietly overfilled the jug in return, and that silent exchange of excesses was the closest thing to friendship either household practiced.

"Heard the cutter's coming today," the boy said, nodding at the horizon, where a smudge of smoke showed against the gray. "Saw her glass off the point an hour ago."

"Did you." Juniper looked. The cutter was indeed making for the anchorage, weeks ahead of her schedule, which meant either generosity or trouble, and the Admiralty dealt mostly in trouble.

"They say the bridge is gone." Tam shifted his weight, apple-sack over his shoulder. "The tidal bridge, over to the mainland road. Storm damage, they're saying. My da heard it from the ferryman." He hesitated, then added, with the careful neutrality children learn too young: "They're saying nobody crosses till spring."

Juniper looked at the cutter again. At the island behind him — the village huddled under its smoke, the garrison flag crawling up the fort pole, the orchards stripped bare for winter, the whole small world of Merrow's Reach balanced on its rock with the sea going gray and heavy all around it.

Sealed, then. Sealed for a season, with a garrison full of soldiers and a harvest already carted uphill into other men's cellars.

"Well," Juniper said, "then we'll want that tea more than ever," and the boy grinned uncertainly, not knowing what else to do with the remark, and went on down the path toward the village with his windfalls knocking against his back.

Juniper hoisted the red pennant and stood a while watching the cutter come, her wake unspooling white behind her, carrying sealed orders and official ink toward his small precise world. The wind pushed at his coat. Behind him the light tower rose against the sky, and the lens turned within it, unseen in daylight, keeping its patient nine seconds bright.

Three seconds dark, he thought. Nine seconds bright. A rhythm built for warning.

He just didn't know yet what it was warning him of.
