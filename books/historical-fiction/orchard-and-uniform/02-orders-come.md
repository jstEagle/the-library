## 2. Orders Come

The cutter came in on the morning tide, black-hulled and low in the water, with a bow wave like a drawn knife. Juniper saw it first from the gallery rail while he was wiping the dew off the brass, and for a moment he simply stood there with his cloth in his hand and watched it grow.

Boats were not unusual on Merrow's Reach. The supply sloop came monthly when the weather allowed, and fishing boats came and went as they pleased from the harbor at Stonequay. But a naval cutter was another animal entirely. It did not come to trade. It did not come to fish. It came because someone on the mainland had decided something about the island, and had sent a ship to say so.

He counted the men at her rails — eight, maybe ten, dark against the grey water — and then made himself stop counting. Counting was a habit of his, one Maren Hallow teased him about gently whenever he stayed for supper. You count everything, Mr. Ashgrove. Spoonfuls, steps, pennies. One day you'll count your own breaths and wear yourself out.

I already do, he'd said, and she'd laughed, and Tam hadn't understood the joke, which was fine. There were things on this island that fourteen-year-olds ought not to understand yet, though the island seemed determined to teach them anyway.

Juniper went down the spiral stair two turns at a time, hung his oilcloth coat over his shoulders, and took the cliff path toward the landing stage. The wind was out of the southwest, carrying the smell of salt and coming rain, and the gulls wheeled overhead with their usual commentary. Below, at Stonequay, he could see figures gathering — the Hallows among them, Maren's red hair unmistakable even at that distance, Tam a smaller shadow beside her. Word traveled fast on an island. A strange ship was worth climbing down for.

The cutter anchored off the stage in thirty feet of green water and lowered a gig. Six men came ashore: four sailors in ratings' uniforms who stood at parade rest as though the shingle itself might inspect them, a lieutenant with a ledger under his arm, and behind them both a broad man in a garrison captain's coat, gold braid dull with spray, boots polished to a wet shine that the shingle immediately began to ruin.

Juniper knew the face, or half-knew it. Captain Ellery Voss commanded the garrison company stationed at Fort Bramble on the island's eastern headland — forty men, mostly, whose duties consisted largely of drilling on the parade ground and drinking at the Salt Cellar. Juniper had seen him twice before, once at the founding-day ceremony three years past and once across the market square, and both times Voss had struck him as a man built like a door: wide, closed, and intended to keep something either in or out.

"Keeper." The lieutenant found him first, consulting a folded paper as though Juniper's name might be written somewhere other than on his face. "Ashgrove, is it? Of the light?"

"That's me."

"Captain Voss has orders for the island's civil administration. There'll be a reading at the garrison hall at noon. Your attendance is required."

Required. The word sat oddly in Juniper's ear, like a coin of the wrong country. On Merrow's Reach, attendance was requested, begged, bribed with cider — never required.

"I'll be there," he said.

Voss himself had been surveying the landing stage, the harbor, the line of villagers now strung along the quay, with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory. Now his eyes came around and settled on Juniper, and Juniper felt the look the way he sometimes felt weather before it arrived — a pressure behind the ears, a heaviness in the air that had not yet become anything.

"You're the keeper," Voss said. Not a question. His voice was level and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone within earshot already understood who held the authority. "Ashgrove. I've read your logs. Or the summaries the Admiralty forwards. Very thorough."

"They're meant to be."

"Mm." Voss looked past him, up at the lighthouse where it stood white against the scudding cloud, its gallery a small crown of iron. "A lonely posting. Most men don't last. You've lasted." He said it without warmth and without coldness; he said it the way he might have noted the depth of water under his keel. "You'll find the next season tests that. The storms are coming early this year, by all accounts. We've had reports from the mainland stations."

"The barometer's been falling since Tuesday," Juniper said. "And the gulls went quiet yesterday. That's earlier than most years, aye."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth — not quite approval, but the recognition of useful information. "Good. Keep your observations coming to the fort. Detailed ones. Wind, sea state, visibility, anything unusual. My men will call weekly for your records." He turned away, then paused, as though an afterthought had occurred to him, though Juniper would learn later that nothing Ellery Voss ever said was an afterthought. "You keep the light burning no matter what else happens on this island. Whatever you see, whatever you hear — the light is your duty. Everything else is mine. Do we understand each other?"

It was phrased as a question. It was not a question.

"The light burns," Juniper said. "It's burned every night since my father lit it, and his before him. It'll burn while I'm keeper, whatever else happens."

Voss nodded once, satisfied or appearing to be, and walked on up the quay toward the garrison road, his lieutenant trailing with the ledger, the sailors falling in behind. The villagers parted for them in silence, the way water parts for a hull — without protest, without welcome, closing again behind.

Maren drifted to Juniper's elbow. She smelled of woodsmoke and apples; she always smelled of woodsmoke and apples, even in the months when there were no apples. "Well?" she said quietly, watching the uniforms recede up the hill. "What's the word?"

"There's a reading at noon. Orders for the whole island." He kept his eyes on the cutter, where a launch was being swung out on davits. Something in the way the sailors worked — quick, practiced, careful — snagged at him. "Storm damage, likely. The bridge piers have been groaning since the spring tides. They'll close it for the season, I'd wager."

"Close it." Maren's voice didn't rise, but it flattened, went thin as wire. "Close the tidal bridge? Juniper, that bridge is our post, our grain, our—"

"I know what it is."

They looked at each other. Around them the quay murmured, the fishermen shifting from foot to foot, old Sorrel Petch spitting thoughtfully into the water and watching the cutter's reflection break apart. Nobody said anything loudly. Islands learn early that loud words travel farther than you want them to.

* * *

The garrison hall at Fort Bramble had been a granary once, back before the Crown decided the island needed soldiers more than it needed storage, and it still smelled faintly of old wheat and mouse. By noon it held nearly every soul on Merrow's Reach who could walk the distance — farmers, fisherfolk, the miller, the schoolmistress, Juniper standing near the back with his cap in his hands because a man who lived alone forgets what to do with his hands in crowds.

Tam Hallow had wedged himself beside Juniper's knee, ostensibly because the hall was crowded and the view was bad from the middle, actually because fourteen-year-olds gravitate toward the tallest thing in the room. Juniper let him. He liked the boy's weight against his leg, the restless heat of him, though he'd have bitten his tongue off before saying so.

Lieutenant Corrigan — for that was the lieutenant's name, learned now through the grapevine that moved faster than any post — mounted the platform, unrolled a document sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and read.

The storm damage to the tidal causeway bridge was declared severe. Engineering assessment concluded the structure unsafe for cart traffic until extensive repairs could be effected, repairs requiring materials and skilled labor unavailable before spring. The bridge would therefore be closed to all traffic effective immediately. No carts. No pack animals. No crossing at any tide.

Furthermore, owing to the isolation thus imposed and the forecast severity of the approaching storm season, command of the island's civil affairs would pass temporarily to the senior military officer present. Captain Ellery Voss would administer rationing, requisition, communications, and justice until such time as normal service resumed.

There was more — dates, signatures, countersignatures, phrases like *in the interest of public order* and *temporary measures proportionate to circumstances* — but Juniper heard the shape of it rather than the letters. The island was to be shut like a drawer. And Voss was to hold the handle.

A murmur ran through the hall, low and many-voiced, the sound of a hundred private arithmetic problems beginning at once. Juniper caught fragments. *No post till spring, then.* *My sister's due in March, how'll we—* *Repairs, he says. What repairs need the whole harvest—*

Then Voss stepped forward, and the murmur died the way a candle dies when a door closes on it.

He did not shout. He spoke at the volume of a man explaining something to a slow child, and somehow that was worse than shouting, because shouting at least admitted anger.

"Islanders," he said. "I know how this sounds. I know what you're thinking, because I'd think it too, in your place. So let me be plain with you, plain as I can make it."

He let the silence stretch. He was good at silence. Juniper recognized the technique distantly, professionally, the way a locksmith recognizes a lock — Voss was letting them fill the pause with their own fears so he wouldn't have to supply any.

"The bridge will close. That is not my choice and I wish it were otherwise, but stone does not argue and neither shall I. What is my choice — what I *can* promise you — is this. No one on Merrow's Reach goes hungry while I hold command. The garrison stores will supplement yours if the winter runs long. The harvest will be brought in, accounted for, and distributed fairly — fairly, mind — according to need. I ask only your cooperation, your patience, and your trust. Give me those three things, and in spring you'll cross your bridge again and wonder what all the fuss was about."

He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, even white teeth, the smile of a man photographed for a regimental portrait. It reached his eyes about halfway and stopped, like a tide that comes up the sand and thinks better of it.

"Questions?"

For a long moment there were none. Then the miller, a broad cautious man named Hedley, raised his hand as if in school. "Captain. The requisition. When you say the harvest's to be accounted for — accounted to whom, and stored where?"

"To me, and in the garrison granaries." Smoothly. Instantly. As though the answer had been waiting behind his teeth all morning. "Under guard, dry, and inventoried weekly. Any householder may request to see the ledger."

*Any householder may request,* Juniper thought. Not *may see.* Request. A request could be declined. A request left no mark on paper.

Hedley opened his mouth again, considered something — perhaps the forty muskets stacked in the armory fifty yards away — and closed it.

Beside Juniper's knee, Tam whispered, barely audible under the shuffling as the hall began to empty: "Mr. Ashgrove. Why's he want our grain *inside* the fort?"

Juniper looked down. The boy's face was screwed up in honest puzzlement, and beneath the puzzlement, faint as a fish under ice, something quicker moved.

"Why does any man want the food inside his own walls?" Juniper said.

Tam frowned. "So nobody can take it."

"Aye," Juniper said. "That's one reason."

* * *

The requisition notices went up that evening, nailed to the church door and the market post, ink still sharp-smelling. Every farmstead was to deliver its surplus grain, root stores, and preserved stock to the garrison granaries within six days, retaining only rations calculated per household per the attached schedule. The schedule was precise. It was, Juniper noticed, precise in the manner of all Voss's documents — every figure defensible, every allowance just barely sufficient, the whole thing constructed like a wall with no obvious gap in it and yet somehow no door either.

He read it twice by lamplight in the village, standing in the drizzle outside the Salt Cellar while inside the fishermen argued in the low voices of men who have not yet decided whether to be angry or afraid. Then he walked the cliff path home in the dark, and the lighthouse met him with its beam sweeping overhead, steady as a heartbeat, and he climbed the stairs and sat at his desk with the logbook open before him.

This was the hour he loved best and trusted least — the hour when the day had to be written down and became, in the writing, permanent. He dipped his pen.

*Oct. 3rd, 1893. Wind SW freshening, sea moderate, swell building from the south. Barometer 29.1 and falling. Naval cutter arrived 0800, departed 1100. Sealed orders read at garrison hall, noon. Tidal bridge closed for the season. Civil authority assumed by Capt. E. Voss. Requisition of harvest stores ordered, delivery within six days.*

He stopped. Pen hovering. The ink drying on the nib.

There was nothing false in what he'd written. Every line of it was true, and the truth of it was exactly the trouble — because written plainly, in his plain hand, in his plain book, the truth sounded like what it was: an army taking charge of an island's food at the start of winter, and calling it safety.

His father had taught him the first rule of the logbook when Juniper was seven years old, his hand too small for the pen: *Write what happened. Not what you fear happened. Not what you hope happened. What happened. The lamp doesn't care about your feelings, and neither does the man who reads this after you're dead.*

But there was a second rule, unspoken, learned later, the way the deepest rules always are: *What you write may be the only thing that survives.*

He looked at the page for a long time. Then he added a final line, small, factual, deniable to anyone but himself:

*Requisition schedule allows 14 oz. grain per adult per week. Garrison complement: 42 men. Granary capacity, est.: 900 bushels. Island population: 214 souls.*

Numbers. Just numbers. Arithmetic that anyone could check and no one could call treason. But he wrote them slowly, and he wrote them knowing — with the particular clarity that comes to men who live alone with instruments — that he was not merely recording the season.

He was starting to measure it. And measurement, done honestly, done completely, was the one thing Ellery Voss's handsome documents had no answer for.

Outside, the wind backed into the southeast and began, very softly, to sing. Down the coast, invisible in the dark, the great bridge timbers groaned against the rising tide, and somewhere below in the village a dog started barking at nothing, or at something, and did not stop for an hour.

Six days until the grain was due. Two hundred and fourteen mouths. Forty-two rifles. One bridge, closing like a book.

Juniper trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork, and climbed to the gallery to watch the dark water, because that was his duty, and duty was the one cargo he had never yet learned to put down.
