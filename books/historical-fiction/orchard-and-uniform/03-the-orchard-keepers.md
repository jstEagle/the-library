## 3. The Orchard Keepers

The path down from the lighthouse had not been made for a man carrying two lanterns of oil he could not spare and one jar of pickled herring he could.

Juniper Ashgrove counted his steps the way he counted everything — because counting was the only prayer he trusted. Two hundred and forty steps to the stile at the top of the orchard rows, if the wind was from the southwest and pushed against him; two hundred and ten if it came kindly off the sea. Today it came kindly, which felt like an accusation. The wind had no right to be gentle on a week when the garrison's requisition carts had rolled through the village with their beds stacked high and their drivers' faces carefully empty.

He paused at the stile. Below him the Hallow orchard ran down the slope in long ranks, bare-branched now, the apple trees standing like patient congregation waiting for a sermon they'd heard every winter of their lives. Beyond them the village of Merrow's Reach proper — forty roofs, a chapel, a smokehouse, and the low stone hall where the council met — huddled against the hill as though bracing for weather that hadn't arrived yet but would.

His wife had loved this view. That was the trouble with descending. Every direction held her.

*Elsbeth, you would have gone straight down. You'd have knocked on the first door with bread smell coming out of it.*

He had not knocked on doors in fourteen months. He had kept the light, signed for his oil, walked down for supplies when the boat came, and walked back up, and the islanders had watched him do it with the particular patience people reserve for a man grieving correctly — visibly, silently, without imposing himself on anyone's supper. Grief gave him a kind of license here. He could be alone forever and no one would call it rudeness.

But Captain Voss's requisition orders had changed the arithmetic.

Juniper had spent thirty years measuring what could be carried: oil by the gallon, wicks by the fathom, grief by the pound. Now he found himself trying to measure something with no unit at all — how much grain had been taken, whether the village could survive until spring, and what exactly a lighthouse keeper who saw everything and reported to no one owed the forty roofs below him.

He could calculate oil consumption to the dram. He could not calculate hunger. So he went down to ask.

The Hallows' cottage sat at the orchard's foot, stone below and timber above, its roof weighted with netting against the gales. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, disciplined line. A dog of no discernible breed lay across the doorstep like a dropped coat, lifted its head, decided Juniper was neither threat nor interest, and put its chin back down.

Before he could knock, the door opened.

"You've been standing there four minutes," said the boy. "Da's been gone since spring and Mam says men stand outside doors when they've forgotten how knocking works."

Juniper blinked. The boy was perhaps thirteen or fourteen, all elbows and collarbones, with a smear of grafting wax on one knuckle and his mother's dark eyes set in a face that was otherwise entirely his own — quick, unimpressed, faintly amused at the world's expense.

"I hadn't forgotten," Juniper said. "I was deciding."

"Deciding takes four minutes?"

"It can."

The boy considered this, found it acceptable, and stepped aside. "Maren! It's the light-keeper. He's come to decide something."

"Maren," Juniper repeated, before he could stop himself. He knew her name the way he knew everyone's name on the Reach — from registers, ration lists, the census he helped count each decade. Maren Hallow, widow, orchard leaseholder, cider press owner, the woman who had once argued the chapel committee into rebuilding the seawall instead of the vestry and won. But knowing a name from paper and hearing it spoken as a household fact were different things. *Maren*, said casually by her son, meant a person. On paper she had only ever been a line.

"In here, then, if he's decided." Her voice came from the kitchen, warm and unhurried, with the slight rasp of someone who talked to trees more than to people.

The kitchen was small and fiercely warm. A fire burned in the range; onions did something fragrant in a black pan; loaves cooled under cloth on the windowsill; and a woman stood at the table with a paring knife and a bowl of winter apples, turning the fruit in her hands with a speed and certainty that made Juniper think, absurdly, of the lens mechanism — motion so practiced it looked like stillness.

Maren Hallow was perhaps forty, built like the apple trees themselves — stocky, weathered, pruned hard by life into a shape that wasted nothing. She looked up, took him in — the keeper's uniform coat he still wore out of habit though he hadn't been Navy in years, the oil cans, the jar of herring — and her eyes did a thing he would come to know well: they measured him, quickly and completely, and filed the measurement somewhere behind her face.

"You're the one who's been watching us starve from up on your rock," she said. Not unkindly. The way one notes the tide.

"I—" The herring jar suddenly weighed forty pounds. "I brought oil. For your lamps. And fish. I don't— I'm told the requisitions took your winter stores."

"They took them." She went back to the apples. Peel fell in one long unbroken ribbon, which struck him as showing off. "Every sack, every barrel, 'for fair redistribution,' says Captain Voss, and we'll see it again when the season turns, says the sergeant, and my grandmother used to say *if a man tells you twice, he means it once*. Sit down. Tam, wash the third bowl."

"I'm not staying," Juniper said. "I only wanted to ask about quantities. What was taken, what remains. If I could write it down—"

"You can write it down after supper."

"It isn't—"

"After. Supper." The knife paused. She looked at him over the apple, and there was nothing soft in her expression at all, which paradoxically made it impossible to refuse. "You walked two hundred steps downhill carrying oil you need yourself, Keeper Ashgrove. Don't insult both of us by pretending you came for arithmetic. Sit."

He sat.

It occurred to him, sitting on a bench worn smooth by generations of Hallows, that he had not been given an order since Elsbeth died. Orders had a texture he'd forgotten — the strange relief of them, the way they removed the burden of choosing. He sat because she told him to sit, and some locked room in his chest opened half an inch, and he hated it, and he breathed anyway.

* * *

Supper was onion soup thick enough to stand a spoon in, bread still warm, salted pork stretched with potatoes, and a jug of last year's cider that tasted like the orchard's whole opinion of itself. Tam ate like a stevedore and talked like a court record, narrating between mouthfuls: the garrison carts, the sergeant who wouldn't meet anyone's eye, the new padlocks on the storehouse that were "bigger than the old ones, which is either honest or the opposite."

"Tam," Maren said, without heat.

"What? He asked about quantities. That's a quantity. Locks are quantities."

"How many sacks left the storehouse?" Juniper asked quietly. "In your counting."

Tam's face sharpened. He was good at being taken seriously, Juniper noted — hungry for it, the way boys are when there's no father in the house to take them seriously first. "Eleven carts over six days. Big carts, garrison ones, double-horse. Say sixty sacks a cart if they were full, and Tam Hallow does not say *if* lightly." He held up a finger. "Because they weren't full going in, see. They were full coming out. I counted the axles sitting deep."

"Axles sitting deep," Juniper repeated. It was the language of his own trade translated — draft, load, displacement. The boy measured weight by the bend of iron. So did he.

"And where did they go?" Maren asked her son, and there was something underneath the question, smooth as river stone, that Juniper almost missed. Almost.

"Garrison granary. That's where they *say*." Tam shrugged. "Granary's locked. Granary's always locked. But Marta Tregowan's brother drives forage for the horses, and he says the granary floor's got room enough to dance in."

"That's gossip."

"That's *data*," said Tam, with such dignity that Juniper nearly smiled, and caught himself smiling, and stopped, because smiling felt like taking something he hadn't paid for.

Maren refilled his cup without asking. "And what does the light-keeper want with our quantities? You report to the Admiralty, not to us."

"I report to no one just now," Juniper said. "The bridge is closed. The cutter won't run till spring. My logs go nowhere until the boats do." He turned the cup in his hands. Cider, warmth, the dog now asleep against his boot — sensations arriving from a country he'd emigrated from. "But if the harvest is sitting in one place while forty households eat through what's left of autumn, then someone should have the numbers written true. Before the official numbers get written wrong."

Silence. Tam looked at his mother. Maren looked at Juniper with that measuring look again, longer this time, and whatever she filed away behind her face seemed to settle somewhere permanent.

"You think they'll lie," she said. Not a question.

"I think," Juniper said slowly, "that I've kept a logbook for thirty years, and I know what happens to records when the man who writes the report is also the man who caused the event. The fog rolls in. The page stays blank. And a year later nobody can prove the fog wasn't real." He met her eyes. "A light doesn't choose what it shows. Someone has to write down what passed through it."

Another silence, but a different one — the sound of a door unlocking rather than closing.

"Well," Maren said at last, briskly, as though he'd agreed to mend a fence. "Then you'll want to see the ledger. After supper. And you'll want to see it properly, which means you'll be here past dark, which means you'll sleep in the loft, because I'll not have you climbing those cliffs at night like a man with something to prove." She stood and began stacking bowls, ending the discussion by the simple method of drowning it in dishes. "Tam, show him where the ladder is before he decides about it for four minutes."

"There's no deciding needed, Mam, the ladder's a ladder—"

"*Tam.*"

Juniper opened his mouth to protest — he had oil to trim, a lamp to light within the hour, duties stacked like cordwood — and found that the protest wouldn't form. The lamp would burn whether he trimmed it at dusk or midnight; the mechanism forgave an hour. And the loft smelled of apples and cedar, and the bedroll up there had been laid out already, he noticed. Laid out. As though she'd expected him.

As though she'd been expecting him for weeks, and had simply been waiting for him to finish deciding.

* * *

The ledger sat between them at the scrubbed table while Tam washed dishes and pretended not to listen, which meant he listened to every word. It was a farm ledger, columns ruled in a careful hand: what the Hallows had surrendered to the requisition, dated, signed by a Sergeant Brandt whose signature started confident and grew cramped as October wore on.

"Two hundred and forty bushels," Maren said, tapping the total. "That's the orchard's tithe and the village share both. We were promised receipt vouchers against spring return. These—" she fanned three slips of paper like losing cards "—are what came instead. Unsigned. No seal. No counterweight recorded."

"No weights recorded at all," Juniper said. His voice had gone flat and precise, the voice he used for weather entering the log. "No tare, no gross. A requisition without weights is a requisition that never happened. Legally speaking, those carts carried air."

"Legally." Maren almost laughed. "And who's the law on Merrow's Reach this winter, Keeper? The man who signs the captain's orders."

The fire ticked. Outside, the wind had turned, coming around to the northeast now, and Juniper felt it in his teeth the way he felt all weather changes — a pressure, a warning. First storm of the season would come inside a fortnight on a northeaster like this. Maybe sooner.

"The boy counted eleven carts," he said. "Sixty bushels to the cart, loaded heavy. Six hundred and sixty bushels, give or take. Your village surrendered two hundred and forty. Where did the other four hundred come from?"

"Other farms. Kestrel's Field, the Priory plots, the fishing families' potato ground that got requisitioned as 'arable.'" She closed the ledger. "Over a thousand bushels off this island, by the boy's arithmetic. Into a granary with room enough to dance in." Her hand rested flat on the cover, and Juniper saw that it was not quite steady. "And a hundred mouths looking at a winter with no bread in it."

"You've told no one."

"I've told you."

"Why me?" The question came out rougher than he intended. "Why not the council? Why not the chaplain, the—"

"Because the council will shout and the shouting will reach Voss by morning, and then Tam goes hungry first, because that's how men like Voss punish — smallest and closest." She looked at him steadily. "And because you're the one man on this island who owes Voss nothing, sees everything, and writes it down in a book no one reads. I've watched your lamp every night for fourteen months, Keeper. Steady through the worst blows. Never missed a turn." A pause, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been gentleness wearing work clothes. "Steady is what this island needs. Shouting, we've plenty of."

Juniper looked down at the ledger, at Brandt's deteriorating signature, and understood that he had just been recruited — quietly, without ceremony, the way the Hallows apparently did everything — into something that had no name yet but had a shape, and the shape was a hidden column of figures marching toward a truth somebody powerful didn't want written.

He should feel afraid. He noted, distantly, that he mostly felt awake.

"I'll need copies," he said. "Dates, amounts, witnesses. Everything in your ledger, everything Tam counted, everything Marta Tregowan's brother saw. In my hand, not yours. If it's found, let it be found on me."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the nod of a woman closing a deal she'd opened weeks ago in her head.

"Tomorrow night, then. Come after your lamp's lit and trimmed. And Keeper—" she gathered the ledger to her chest, and over its worn cover her voice dropped to something almost too quiet for the boy at the basin to hear "—you eat supper here first. Every night. That part's not strategy. That part's just supper."

Up in the loft later, wrapped in wool that smelled of cedar and apples, Juniper lay listening to the wind gather itself in the northeast and thought about weight. Axles sitting deep. A jar of herring. A bedroll laid out in advance. Fourteen months he had measured what could be carried, and had concluded, with the precision he applied to everything, that the answer was: very little. Oil. Duty. Grief.

Now a woman who argued with chapel committees had handed him a ledger and a standing invitation, and both weighed nothing in the hands and everything everywhere else.

Below the loft floor he heard Maren bank the fire, heard Tam's footsteps overhead-crossing to his bed, heard her pause at the foot of the ladder.

"Keeper Ashgrove." Her voice, low. "The lamp tonight. Light it true."

"Always," he said into the dark.

And far below, at the bottom of the cove where the water went black beyond the harbor stones, something moved against the swell that was not a fishing boat, not on any schedule Juniper had ever logged — a shape sliding toward the Reach without a single light showing, quiet as ink poured into ink.

He would see it plainly soon enough. From the gallery. Through the glass.

But that was tomorrow's entry, and tonight — for the first time in fourteen months — he slept in a house instead of a tower, and the wind sang against the eaves like something practicing a welcome.
