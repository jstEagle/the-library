## 4. Grain That Isn't There

The ration ledger lived in a cedar box under the floorboard behind the flour bin, and Maren Hallow had kept it for eleven years — since before her husband died, since before Tam could reach the shelf where the scales hung. It was not an official book. No one from the garrison had ever asked to see it, and that was precisely its value. In her own hand, in her own ink, in numbers only she and two other orchard wives could read at speed, it recorded what came into Merrow's Reach village and what went out again: sacks of wheat and barley from the mainland cutter, salted fish offloaded at the quay, apples pressed and barreled in autumn, wool baled in spring. A private arithmetic. The island's true account.

She opened the box on the last evening of October, after supper, while Tam did his sums by the fire and the wind worried at the shutters like a dog begging to be let in. She had been putting this off for three days, telling herself there was no hurry, that the season's tallies could wait until the first hard frost. But she was a woman who weeded a row because the weeds were there, and the discrepancy was there now, growing like bindweed through every page since September, and she could not weed anything else until she had looked at it straight.

*September the twelfth.* Cutter *Kestrel*, out of Penharrow. Wheat, forty hundredweight. Barley, twenty. She remembered the day well — bright and cold, the whole village turning out on the quay because a supply landing meant gossip as much as bread. She remembered Sergeant Brandt checking the manifest against his clipboard with his little brass pencil, and she remembered thinking he checked it twice, which was either diligence or nerves.

*September the twelfth,* her own hand recorded. *Received at storehouse, wheat 40 cwt., barley 20 cwt.*

Then below it, in the column she kept for distribution — what the garrison actually released to the village stores for grinding and rationing out — she had written: *Wheat 22 cwt., barley 9.*

Eighteen hundredweight of wheat missing. Eleven of barley. Enough to keep forty families in bread through November.

She had assumed, that night in September, that the rest would follow on the next landing. Grain often came in two lots; holds were temperamental things, and Captain Voss's quartermaster was forever complaining about damp and rats. So she waited for the second lot. And when the *Kestrel* returned on the fourth of October, the manifest said wheat thirty hundredweight, barley fifteen — and the storehouse issued the village eleven and five.

Maren turned the page with her thumb, though there was nothing beneath it but blank paper and the ghost of the next entry pressing through.

Tam looked up from his slate. "You've got your ledger face."

"I haven't got a ledger face."

"You do. You get a line right here—" he touched the space between his own eyebrows, "—like you're adding up someone's sins."

"Then I'm adding up my own," she said, and closed the box. "Finish your sums."

He finished them. He was quick with figures, quicker than his father had been, and she watched him carry the one and double-check it without being told, and she felt the familiar ache-and-gratitude of loving a child who was becoming a person. Fourteen years old and already taller than her, all elbows and earnestness, with his father's dark hair and her own stubborn chin. He blew out his breath over the slate to erase it, the way children did, as if arithmetic were a thing that could be breathed away.

Outside, the wind rose a note. Somewhere down toward the bridge, the sea was working at the pilings with the patience of a prisoner digging a tunnel.

* * *

She walked to the village store the next morning with a basket she didn't need, because a woman carrying an empty basket was a woman come to talk, and a woman come to talk could ask questions that a woman with a ledger could not.

Old Sorrel Tregaskis ran the village store the way a spider runs a web — patiently, from the center, missing nothing. She was seventy if she was a day, widowed twice, and she weighed out sugar and flour with hands that trembled like leaves but never once gave short measure. When Maren set her basket on the counter, Sorrel looked at it, then at her, then reached beneath the counter and drew out her own notebook — a cheap thing with a marbled cover, swollen with damp.

"Before you say a word," Sorrel said, "look at this."

The pages held columns in a crabbed hand. Weights received. Weights distributed. Dates going back to the middle of September. Maren read them twice and felt the cold move up her arms.

"It matches mine," she said quietly.

"Aye." Sorrel snapped the elastic band around the notebook. "I've been keeping count since the old famine year, when I swore no man on this island would go hungry while I stood behind this counter and called myself its keeper. Forty-one years I've done it. Never once has the tally come up short by more than a sack or two, and that was spillage and mice." She leaned across the counter, and her breath smelled of peppermints and strong tea. "Since Voss took authority, we're short better than two ton of grain, Maren. Two ton. That's not mice."

"Could it be spoilage? The storehouse is old. Damp gets in."

"Damp doesn't eat eighteen hundredweight of wheat and leave the sacks standing neat as pews. I've seen the storehouse. Brandt let me look round the outside of it a week past — proud of his new paint, he was — and I'll tell you what I saw. I saw fresh boards on the floor where none were needed, laid down over the old ones, and I saw the stacks piled higher near the door than near the wall, the way a man piles things when he wants the back of the room hidden." Sorrel's voice dropped further. "And I saw wheel ruts, fresh ones, leading from the storehouse door down to the cove path. Cart tracks, Maren. Going to a cove where no cart has had business since the pilchard boats stopped coming."

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the back of the shop a jar lid popped as the weather changed.

"The cove," Maren said. "That's garrison ground now. Voss posted it."

"He posted it, right enough. Put a sentry on it nights." Sorrel straightened up and began weighing out the sugar Maren hadn't asked for, one pound, two, with her trembling exact hands. "Now here's the question sits on my chest at night like a cat. Do we take it to him? Walk up to the garrison house, polite as churchgoers, and say, 'Captain, our ledgers don't match your manifests, can you explain?' Because if he's honest, we've embarrassed a proud man. And if he isn't—"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The word *if he isn't* filled the whole shop, and both women stood inside it.

"We'd be accusing the man who controls every grain sack and every rifle on this island," Maren said slowly, "with nothing but arithmetic."

"Arithmetic's a dangerous weapon," said Sorrel. "It cuts whoever holds it wrong."

* * *

She thought about it all that afternoon among the apple rows, stripping the last fruit before the frost took it. Her hands worked on their own; her mind was elsewhere, running the sums again and again, looking for the mistake she must surely have made. Eighteen hundredweight. Eleven. On the October landing, nineteen and ten. She had checked her weights against the store scale herself, more than once, hauling sacks onto the great balance beam while Brandt watched with his clipboard. She had not imagined it. Numbers did not lie, but men did, and the numbers said the lying had begun the very week Voss's orders came ashore.

She thought about the harvest feast they hadn't had. Every year since anyone could remember, the village had held its harvest supper in the tithe barn — long trestle tables, the garrison invited down, Captain Voss making a speech the first autumn he was stationed here about the *sacred partnership between soldier and soil*. This year the supper had been cancelled. Not cancelled by decree — nothing so crude — but deferred, and then deferred again, on the grounds that stores were low and winter must be provisioned for first. The village had accepted it with the islander's habit of swallowing inconvenience whole. They were used to being managed. They were used to believing that the people above them knew best, or at least pretended to.

But hunger made believers out of nobody. Already the Widow Chegwin was boiling nettle tops for soup, and the Pengelly children had stopped asking for bread with their dripping because they'd learned the answer. Small signs. Early signs. Signs she recognized from stories her grandmother told of the famine year, when the mainland had eaten well and Merrow's Reach had buried fourteen of its own between Candlemas and Lady Day.

And here was the thing she could not stop turning over, like a stone in her pocket: the garrison tables were not short. She had eyes; she had ears; she had a son who delivered eggs up the hill twice a week and came back full of what he'd seen. The soldiers ate meat twice a day still. The officers' mess had butter in October, real butter, and sugar in bowls that were emptied and refilled without ceremony. If the island's grain was short, then somebody's table was long, and it wasn't the one where children sat.

She stripped an apple from the highest branch, tested it with her thumb, and put it in her apron pocket instead of the pail. Some fruit you saved for eating later, alone, where no one asked you to share.

* * *

That evening Tam came home from the garrison with his egg money and a strange stillness on him. She noticed it at once, the way she noticed weather. He set the coins on the kitchen table, counted them twice, and then stood there with his cap in his hands.

"Well?" she said. "Out with it."

"There were crates," he said. "At the boathouse slip, down past the officers' quarters. Big ones, stenciled. I couldn't read the stencil proper, but they were heavy — four men to lift one, and they carried them like they were eggs, careful-like. And there was a boat come in. Not a navy boat. A private sloop, dark hull, no lights showing, and it weren't on the schedule board because I checked the schedule board." He swallowed. "A sergeant caught me looking. He grabbed my arm hard enough to leave marks and told me to mind my eggs and my own business, and he said—" Tam's voice went careful, reciting, the way he recited scripture he didn't fully understand, "—he said the captain doesn't care for curious boys, and curious boys' families find their rations get lighter. Then he let me go and told me to run along home."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked on the hob. Maren found that her hands had gone cold around the dish towel she was holding, and that her mind had gone perfectly, terribly clear.

Crates at night. An unlit boat. A sergeant threatening a child. The cart ruts to the cove. The missing tonnage. Each piece alone might be explained away — she was a fair woman, she tried each explanation on like a coat, and each one fit badly and hung wrong at the shoulders. Together they made a shape she recognized, and the recognizing frightened her more than any single piece, because she understood now that she was not looking at carelessness or confusion or damp in an old storehouse.

She was looking at theft. Organized, protected, uniformed theft, run from the top of the island by the man whose signature authorized every sack.

"Did you tell anyone else?" she asked. "On the way home?"

"No." Tam hesitated. "Should I have?"

"No." She said it too quickly and softened it. "No, love. You did right. You come home and you told your mother, and that's exactly the order of things." She crossed the kitchen and put her arms around him, and he let her, though he was nearly too big for it now, folding down a little the way he did. Over his shoulder she looked at the window, black except for the room's reflection, and beyond it the dark mass of the headland where the lighthouse stood, and beyond that the open sea.

Voss. The name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had dined at his table her first autumn on the council; he had praised her cider; he had shaken her dead husband's hand and called him a pillar of the island. Men like that wore authority the way the lighthouse wore its paint — thick coats, renewed yearly, covering whatever was underneath.

Say something aloud, and it would be her word against a captain's. Say it in company, and the company would scatter like gulls before they repeated it, because fear traveled faster than truth on an island. Say it to the wrong ear and Tam's name would find its way onto some list, some quiet adjustment of rations, some accident waiting for a boy on a cliff path. She had heard how such things were done in the famine year. Starvation needed no soldiers. It only needed a ledger kept against you.

So she said nothing aloud. What she did was bank the fire, kiss her son's hair, and go upstairs to the cedar box's twin — a small locked drawer in her late husband's desk — where she wrote out, in her tightest hand, on loose sheets rather than in any book that could be found and read: everything Tam had described, dated, and signed with her initials. Then another sheet: the ledger discrepancies, landing by landing. She folded them into oilcloth, tied them with waxed thread, and put them beneath the false bottom of the flour bin, where Sorrel Tregaskis kept hers, because the two of them had agreed on that much without ever saying so directly — two women exchanging a glance across a counter and understanding it whole.

If the truth was going to survive the winter, it would need to be carried somewhere safe. Hidden, duplicated, patient. She was good at patient. She had buried a husband and raised a boy on patient.

Down in the village, lamps were going out one by one. Up on the headland, the lighthouse swept its steady white arm across the water, around and around, faithful as breathing. Maren stood a moment at the window watching it, and thought: *someone should tell that keeper. He sees everything that moves on this water. If there are boats coming in dark, he'll know.*

She resolved to walk up the cliff path tomorrow, with eggs, the way neighbors did, and see what Juniper Ashgrove's logbook might hold.

She did not yet know that the same night, far below in the black cove, a sloop with no lights was already sliding toward the rocks.
