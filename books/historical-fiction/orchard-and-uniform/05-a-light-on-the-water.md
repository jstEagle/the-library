## 5. A Light on the Water

The wind came around to the north-northeast a little after nine, and Juniper Ashgrove felt the change in the glass before he read it. He had learned long ago not to trust his instruments over his body — the barometer could lie, the anemometer could stall, but the ache in his old shoulder joint never had. It ached now, dull and patient, like a dog waiting at a door.

"Northeast," he said to the empty room, and wrote it down.

He said things aloud these days when there was no one to hear them. Edith had teased him about it in the first winter after she died — *you talk to that lamp more than you ever talked to me* — and he had answered, truthfully, that the lamp at least answered back, in its way, with its steady breathing of flame and oil. She had laughed until she coughed. That was three years gone now, and he still measured the days by what he carried up and down the tower stairs: oil in copper cans, wicks in their tin box, the brass polishing cloth folded to a precise square. Grief he had learned to carry like the oil — in measured quantities, at fixed hours, so it would not spill.

At ten o'clock he climbed the ninety-one steps to the lantern room, counted them as always without meaning to, and set about the evening's trimming. The great lens turned overhead with its soft hydraulic sigh, gathering the lamplight into a single white blade and sweeping it out across the dark water, once every twelve seconds. Twelve seconds. A man could set his heart by it. Some nights, Juniper thought he did.

He checked the wick height. He wiped a fingerprint from the lower prism — *his* print, no one else's, for no one else came up here — and stood a moment watching the beam go out and come round again, out and come round, touching the black water like a hand checking on a sleeping child.

That was when he saw the sloop.

She was standing in toward Merrow's Reach from the southeast, running dark. No riding light at her masthead. No sidelights. No lantern glow at her rail where any honest vessel would show one coming into strange water after full dark. Just a shape, low and quick, eating up the sea with a bone in her teeth, and the only reason Juniper could see her at all was that his own beam swept across her hull every twelve seconds and gave him a flash of her: dark hull, patched foresail, the silhouette of four men at least along her deck.

He watched her through two full turns of the lens. Out, and come round. There she was. Out, and come round. Still there, closer now, standing in toward the island on a line no pilot would choose by daylight — unless the man steering knew the water intimately, which meant he was local, which meant this was no lost trader blundering in from the open sea.

Juniper went to the log desk in the corner of the lantern room, opened the book, and dipped his pen.

His hand hesitated above the paper.

It should not have. In nineteen years as a keeper — first at Cape Ferren under old man Quill, then here, alone since Edith died — he had written down exactly what he saw, every time, without variation. Fog at 0400, visibility fifty yards. Steamer *Corvid* passing eastbound, showing proper lights. Flare observed bearing southwest, distress or celebration, unknown. The log was not a diary. The log was a contract between the keeper and every soul who ever put to sea trusting that somewhere on that dark coast a light burned and someone watched. You wrote the truth because lives hung on the record. That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it.

But his pen stayed above the paper, and he found himself thinking, absurdly, of Captain Ellery Voss.

Voss had come ashore six weeks ago with the storm damage orders and taken over the island the way frost takes over a window — quietly, completely, from the edges inward. He requisitioned the harvest stores "for fair distribution." He posted sentries on the tidal bridge before the storms closed it anyway. He smiled a great deal. And last week, when Maren Hallow had asked — gently, reasonably, at the supper table, over Maren's own bread — why the ration ledger showed forty bushels of barley issued to the village that no village hand had seen, Voss had smiled wider and said the books were garrison business now, and passed her the butter as if nothing had been said at all.

Juniper had logged that supper too, in his own private fashion, though not in the official book: the way Tam Hallow, fourteen years old and all elbows, had stopped chewing; the way Maren had kept her eyes on her plate and her spine straight as a mast.

Now here was a sloop running dark into a cove on the island's unprotected side, at night, off every schedule Juniper kept — and Juniper kept them all, had kept the arrival book faithfully even through the bridge closure, even though the only traffic now was garrison cutters that reported themselves by flag hours in advance.

Official traffic announced itself. That was the whole grammar of the sea.

This sloop was saying nothing, and it was saying it loudly.

*Report it,* said the keeper in him, the nineteen-year habit, the contract. *Log it, and report it to the garrison at first light. That is your duty. That has always been your duty.*

*And to whom will you report it?* said another voice, quieter, older, the voice of a man who had buried a wife and learned what institutions do with the people who trust them. *To Voss? To the sergeant at the gate who looks through you as if you were weather? You'll write it in the official log, and the official log goes to the garrison, and the garrison is Voss, and whatever that boat carries — grain, brandy, guns — it carries it for him.*

Juniper set the pen down.

Out, and come round, went the beam. The sloop was nearly at the cove mouth now. Through the glass — he took up his telescope with hands that were steadier than he deserved — he made out more detail as the beam caught her again and again: the patched foresail confirmed, a cargo hatch open amidships, and men moving with the unhurried economy of men doing familiar work. Not smugglers in a panic. Professionals. Or soldiers out of uniform.

The cove took her. From the gallery, eighty feet above the sea, Juniper watched her sail come down neat as a folded napkin, watched her swing to whatever mooring waited for her there — and something *did* wait for her there, because she stopped as surely as a horse at a hitching post, in a cove that had no moorings on any chart Juniper owned. Men and crates went ashore into the black teeth of the rocks, where lanterns blinked twice — twice, deliberately, a signal — and went dark.

Twice. He would remember that. Two flashes, pause, two flashes. Someone ashore answering someone afloat.

He stood at the rail a long while, the wind pulling at his coat, the lens sighing its circle above him. The cold worked into his joints and he barely felt it. Below, invisible past the headland, the village slept, and in the village Maren Hallow was sleeping, or not sleeping, and Tam, and the Ollerenshaws, and the Widow Petch, and every family whose barley had vanished off a ledger page into a night like this one.

*Grain,* he thought. *I'd wager my pension it's grain going out.*

Grain bought cheap from hungry men, or taken outright, rowed out dark to a sloop, sold to mainland brokers at mainland prices while the island ate nettle soup and boiled hides. And in spring, when the bridge opened and the historians and inspectors came, they would find ledgers — Voss's ledgers — showing everything issued fairly, everything accounted for. Paper against paper. The word of a captain against the memory of farmers.

Unless there was another paper. Unless somewhere, in a keeper's hand, in a keeper's ink, dated and timed and initialed, there was a line that said:

*Sloop, unidentified, dark, entered Cove of St. Brannoc at 2240. No lights shown. Four men or more. Cargo unlabeled. Answered from shore by two signal flashes.*

Juniper returned to the desk. He uncapped the ink. This time the pen did not hesitate.

He wrote it all. Time, bearing, description, the signal flashes, the tide, the wind. Every fact, plain and unornamented, in the official log, because the official log was the contract and he would keep the contract or die at this desk keeping it. Then he closed the book and sat looking at its cover — canvas-bound, stamped *TRINITY HOUSE — MERROW'S REACH STATION*, addressed each quarter to the district office, which forwarded to the Admiralty, which forwarded, these days, to whoever wore the most stars nearest to hand.

Which was Voss.

So the honest page would travel straight into the pocket of the man it accused. He understood that now with perfect clarity, the way you understand a current by watching what it does to floating weed. The log was a contract, yes. But a contract signed over to a counterfeiter becomes a forgery's certificate of authenticity.

Juniper rose, trimmed the wick a final half-turn, and went down the ninety-one steps carrying nothing but the thought, which was heavier than any can of oil.

In the kitchen he did not light the lamp. He sat in the dark at the table where Edith used to mend his coats, and he looked at his hands — a keeper's hands, precise hands, hands that had never once falsified a record — and he thought about measuring. All his life he had measured what could be carried. A man can carry so much oil up ninety-one steps. A man can carry so much grief before it carries him. A man can carry duty like a yoke, and bear it, and be proud of the bearing.

But some things, once you have seen them, you cannot put down. The sloop was like that. Those two signal flashes were like that. Somewhere below in the dark, crates were coming ashore that would become bread in some broker's shop and hunger in some child's belly, and Juniper Ashgrove had watched it happen and written the truth down and could do precisely nothing with it, and the nothing was the heaviest thing he had ever been asked to carry.

Except.

He got up. Found a candle. Lit it, and the small flame threw his shadow huge against the rafters, and for a moment the room looked like a church and he looked like a man at an altar deciding something.

From the drawer beneath the stairs — the drawer where Edith's letters lived, and his spare spectacles, and the account book from his first year at the Cape — he took a second ledger. Old stock, blank pages, bought years ago in a lot and never needed. He laid it on the table beside the candle and opened it to the first page.

He did not write yet. Writing it would make it real, and making it real would make it a thing he must protect, and protecting it would put him — a widower of sixty-one with bad shoulders and one barrel of lamp oil less than comfort — into opposition with a garrison of armed men and a captain who smiled.

But he knew, sitting there in the candlelight with the blank book open before him like an unfired gun, that he was going to write it. Tonight. Every sighting, every schedule broken, every flash of signal, copied in duplicate against the day the official log proved worthless or vanished. Because the light did not shine only for ships. It shone for anyone in the dark, and lately everyone on Merrow's Reach was in the dark, and Juniper was the man who kept the light.

*Two flashes,* he thought, dipping the pen at last. *I'll watch for them again tomorrow. And the next night. And the next.*

A record needs more than one line. A proof needs weight, and weight takes time, and time was the one cargo the island had plenty of, sealed behind its closed bridge, all the way until spring.

He began to write, small and exact, in a hand that would not shame a bank clerk. Outside, the beam swept its twelve-second circuit over a sea that hid its work in darkness, and Juniper Ashgrove, alone in the kitchen of the lighthouse, committed his first act of smuggling.

What he smuggled was the truth itself — and he was only beginning to learn how much it weighed, or how far it would need to be carried, or who on this island would help him carry it before the season turned.
