## 6. Tam's Errand

Mother gave me the petition folded in thirds and sealed with a dab of wax she'd melted over the stove, because proper wax was something we'd stopped being able to afford sometime around the second week of October.

"Hold it flat," she said. "Don't let it bend. And Tam — you speak to the sergeant at the desk, no one above him. You say your piece, you hand it over, and you come home. No wandering."

"No wandering," I said, because that was easier than telling her I'd already planned to wander.

She tucked my collar down and looked at me the way she'd been looking at me all winter — like she was measuring me against some older version of myself and finding me not quite grown enough for what she needed, but too grown to keep sheltering. Fourteen was a strange country to live in. Too old to be sent nowhere, too young to be told everything.

"Mind the ice on the cliff path," she said.

"I will."

"And if anyone asks why *you've* come instead of me, tell them the truth. Tell them every pair of hands is needed in the rows, and mine are the strongest we have left."

That part was true, at least. The apple trees stood bare as fishbones now, but winter pruning waited on no one, and since Da died Mother had taught me the shears herself. My palms had gone hard as boot leather. When I made a fist, the skin didn't split anymore. I considered this proof of something, though I couldn't have said what.

The garrison sat at the island's east shoulder, a squat gray fortification the Crown had thrown up thirty years ago when someone important decided Merrow's Reach might matter someday. It didn't, mostly. But the storms of autumn had cracked the tidal bridge and drowned the ferry schedule, and suddenly the island mattered very much — mattered enough that Captain Voss had been given authority over it, mattered enough that our harvest had been "requisitioned for equitable distribution," which was a phrase from the requisition notice that everyone in the village had read forty times and understood exactly none of.

The grain was supposed to come back to us. That was the whole arrangement. We grew it; they stored it; they doled it back through the ration ledger so nobody hoarded and nobody starved. Except the ledger said grain issued, week after week, and the village tables stayed empty as churches on a Tuesday. Mother kept her accounts in a little book with a green cover, and she'd gone quiet over it in November in a way that frightened me more than shouting would have. She never shouted about money. She went quiet, and did sums, and the sums came out wrong in the same direction every time.

So: the petition. Signed by eleven households, asking — politely, oh so politely, in Mother's careful copperplate — for an accounting of the December issue, which according to the ledger had been delivered in full to the village storehouse, and according to the storehouse had never arrived.

I carried it up the cliff path with the wind pushing at my back like a hand between my shoulders, as if the island itself wanted me there quickly and done with it.

* * *

The gate was ice-rimed iron, and the sentry on duty was a boy not much older than me wearing a uniform two sizes too big, which somehow made it worse. He looked cold. He looked like he'd rather be anywhere. He looked, most of all, like he wasn't allowed to talk to me, so he didn't, just jerked his chin toward the guardhouse door.

Inside smelled of lamp oil and boiled mutton and wet wool — a smell I would carry in my nose for weeks after, though I didn't know it yet. There was a desk by the door, and behind the desk sat Sergeant Brandt.

I knew him slightly, or thought I did. He'd come round in the autumn with the requisition detail, all politeness and stamped papers, and he'd once lifted Tam Hallow — me — clear off the ground by the armpits when I'd got underfoot of a cart wheel, saying *careful, lad, you're worth more than the cargo*. It had seemed kind then. Kindness looks different when you find out later what the cargo was.

"Sergeant." I held out the petition flat, both hands, the way Mother had shown me, like an offering. "From the households of the lower village. Concerning the December grain issue."

Brandt took it. He didn't open it. He held it in both hands and looked past me at the wall, and I watched something move across his face — quick, like a bird crossing a window — and then it was gone and he was just a tired man at a desk again.

"The December issue," he repeated.

"Yes, sir. It says here—" I recited it, because I'd memorized it, because Mother had drilled me at the table until I could say it in my sleep: "*issued in full, four hundred weight mixed grain, receipt signed by the village factor.* Only there's no signature, sir, and nothing came to the storehouse. Not one sack."

Silence. Somewhere deeper in the garrison a door closed, and boots went along a corridor, unhurried.

"You'd best wait," Brandt said finally. "Captain Voss will want to see this himself."

That was wrong. I knew it was wrong even then — Mother had said *the sergeant at the desk, no one above him*, and petitions went in ledgers, they didn't go up the chain to captains, not for a missing four hundred weight. But I was fourteen, and he was a sergeant, and the room was warm, and I said, "Yes, sir," and sat down on the bench by the wall to wait.

He disappeared through the inner door with the petition, and I sat, and I counted things, because counting kept the nerves quiet. Counted the coat pegs (nine). Counted the cracks in the plaster (a long one shaped like the coast of the Reach itself, which I found funny and told no one). Counted the minutes by my own pulse, because I'd forgotten my watch, until counting time stopped working and I started listening instead.

Because through the inner door, half-muffled, I could hear voices. And one of them was Brandt's, and it had changed. It was low and fast and frightened, and I caught only pieces — *the Hallows' boy*, and *she'll ask questions next*, and something that might have been *he's fourteen, sir, he's nobody* — and then another voice cut across his, smooth and unhurried and pleasant the way a blade laid flat on a table is pleasant, and that voice said something I couldn't make out except the shape of it, which was *no*, and *not yet*, and then, clearly, terribly:

"Let him look. Curiosity's cheaper than bread, and we're short on neither for people who mind their business."

Then the inner door opened and Brandt came back without the petition, and his face was composed the way ice over water is composed, and he said, "Captain will attend to it. You can go home now, lad."

But I didn't go home. Not straightaway.

* * *

Here is what I told myself, walking out the gate with the wind in my teeth: I was taking the long way because the cliff path was iced and the shore road was safer. This was even true. It was also true that the shore road passed the cove.

The cove below the garrison's seaward wall was where the supply sloop tied up — officially, on the official schedule, once a fortnight, with the escort boat and the manifest and the whole machinery of honesty. But the tide had been strange all season, and I'd lived on this island all fourteen years of my life, and I knew what boats sounded like at night, and twice in December I'd heard oars and a hull kissing shingle at hours when no scheduled thing had any business arriving. I hadn't told Mother. Some part of me had understood, even then, that telling her would make it real, and real things require decisions, and decisions require being older than fourteen.

Today, though, in daylight, in the gray slant of January noon, I simply walked the shore road, and when I reached the lip of the cove I stopped, because there was a boat there.

Not the sloop. A private craft — a ketch, black-hulled, rakish, flying no colors at all, which on Merrow's Reach meant one thing: a mainland broker who didn't want to be counted. She lay against the quay stones with lines fore and aft, and men were moving along her deck in a rhythm I recognized from harvest days, the rhythm of loading, steady and practiced, crate to rail, rail to hold.

And the crates were our crates.

I knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Garrison stenciling, yes — *H.M.S. STORES / MERROW'S REACH* in faded black — but underneath, burned into the wood at the corner where the stencil didn't reach, the orchard brand. Every crate in the requisition had been built that autumn from our own orchard offcuts, because lumber was short and Maren Hallow — my mother — had sold the garrison forty boards and helped nail the first ones herself. Her hammer marks were on those crates. I could have picked our work out of a thousand.

Grain went into sacks. I knew that too; I'd filled enough of them. Grain did not go into crates, unless someone wanted it counted in units instead of weight, priced per crate, sold per crate — unless someone wanted it to be merchandise.

A crate swung past me on a shoulder, close enough to touch, and the man carrying it grunted with the weight, and I heard — I swear I heard, through the slats, as it turned — the dry whisper of shifting grain. Oats, maybe. Barley. The sound of four hundred weight of December, whispering its way onto a black ketch while children in the lower village licked porridge bowls that had held nothing but hot water and a prayer since Advent.

I stepped back off the road. Behind a stack of lobster pots, half-hidden, heart going like a gull in a net. I told myself I was only waiting for the road to clear. I told myself a lot of things in those three minutes, and every one of them was a lie dressed as caution.

They loaded six crates. Maybe eight. Then a voice on the quay — and my stomach dropped through the shingle, because it was Brandt's voice — called the men off, and there were footsteps coming up the road toward the lobster pots, and I pressed myself smaller behind the pots and watched the boots stop not ten feet away.

"Lad."

He'd seen me. Of course he'd seen me; he'd probably seen me the moment I rounded the headland; soldiers see everything, that's most of what being a soldier is. I stood up slowly, brushing peat dust off my knees, and put on the face I wore for Mother when she asked whether I'd fed the hens.

"Afternoon, Sergeant. Just walking home."

"Shore road's washed out past the point." He said it evenly. His eyes weren't even at all. Up close I could see he hadn't slept; there were bruise-colored crescents under his eyes, and his breath, when he spoke, carried last night's rum under today's nothing. "You'll want the cliff path."

"Aye, sir. Only I stopped to watch the boat. We don't get many ketches."

Something flickered behind his eyes — that bird again, crossing the window. "No," he said. "You don't."

"It's a fine boat. Whose is she?"

And here is the thing I will remember when I'm old, if I live to be old: he looked over his shoulder first. A sergeant of the Crown, on Crown soil, guarding Crown grain, looked over his shoulder before answering a child's question, and the answer he gave was not the answer of an honest man.

"She's nobody's. Now get home, Tam Hallow, and don't come down here again."

"My name's—"

"I know your name." He stepped closer, and the kindness went out of his face like a lamp turned down to a spark, and what was underneath was worse than anger — it was fear wearing anger's coat, fear doing a bad job of the costume. "I know your mother signs petitions with wax she can't afford. I know she counts better than the factor does. And I know boys who stand behind lobster pots watching things that are none of their business grow up into men who ask questions, and men who ask questions on this island this winter end up on lists. Do you understand me? Lists. Your mother's name is already on paper somewhere, boy. Don't add yours."

The wind came off the water and pushed between us, and I heard the ketch's rigging clank, and somewhere inside me a small clear voice observed that I should be terrified, and I was, I was shaking so hard my teeth clicked — but underneath the terror was something else, something new and hard and bright as frost, and it said: *so it IS stolen. It IS. He just told you.*

"Understood, Sergeant," I said. "Sorry, Sergeant. Cliff path, Sergeant."

He stared at me a long moment, hunting for mockery and not finding it, because there wasn't any. Then he stepped aside, and I walked past him up the road, and I did not run until the headland was between us and the cove.

* * *

I ran anyway, after. Ran the cliff path with the petition's absence feeling stranger than its presence ever had, ran past the storehouse, ran into the yard, and stopped dead at the kitchen door with my lungs on fire, because Mother was standing in the doorway already, pruning knife still in her hand, looking at my face.

She read me the way she read weather. One look, and the knife went into her apron pocket.

"Tam. What happened?"

"Nothing," I said. "They took it. Said the Captain would attend to it. That's all."

She looked at me for three full seconds. On this island, three seconds is a long time to lie to a woman who has spent her whole life reading the sky, and I lasted maybe two of them.

"That's all," she repeated.

"That's all."

She let it go. That was the frightening part — how easily she let it go, how quickly she turned back to the stove, as if she'd already known whatever it was I was hiding, had perhaps known since November, since the green ledger, since the first week the numbers went wrong. As if my face had only confirmed a sum she'd finished long ago.

I went to my room and sat on the bed with my knees drawn up, and I did my own sums, the way she'd taught me. Six crates, call it eight, call it a hundredweight each — no, more, they'd been heavy, call it a hundredweight and a half. Call it ten pounds the hundredweight on the mainland, winter prices, and the ketch would run twice more before the bridge reopened if she'd run twice already, and the arithmetic climbed and climbed until it wasn't arithmetic anymore but a picture: a black boat, going and coming, going and coming, all season, while the village ate hot water.

They knew I'd seen. That was the other sum. Brandt had seen me plain, and Brandt had reported to somebody — I'd heard it through the door, *the Hallows' boy* — and the somebody had said *let him look*, and I turned that word over and over in my mind like a stone with a fossil in it. Let him look. Not because I was harmless. Because I was a warning. A message delivered free of charge to the lower village, in the person of a fourteen-year-old: *we know you know, and knowing costs nothing yet, but the price list is posted.*

Well. They'd mispriced it.

I waited until the house was dark and Mother asleep, then I took the candle stub and the pencil stub and the back of an old seed order, and I wrote down everything. Time, tide, the ketch, the crates, the brand marks, the count — six, maybe eight — the sound of the grain, Brandt's words, all of it, in my best hand, the hand Mother had drilled, dated and signed *T. Hallow, age 14,* because I had a sudden fierce conviction that names mattered, that a record with no name attached was a rumor and a record with a name was a weapon.

I hid it under the loose floorboard where I kept my marbles, which felt absurd and correct at once.

Then I lay in the dark and listened to the wind worry the shutters, and thought about Keeper Ashgrove up in his white tower. Everyone said the lighthouse keeper logged everything — every boat, every hour, every oddity, ink-stained fool that he was, keeping his books like scripture. If boats came into the cove at night, unlit, unscheduled —

If a man like that wrote down what he saw, in a book that couldn't burn easy, in a tower the garrison had no reason yet to search —

Then there was more than one record on this island. And records, Mother always said, were the one crop that couldn't be requisitioned — provided somebody was brave enough to keep planting them.

Tomorrow, I decided, I'd climb the cliff to the lighthouse. I'd bring the keeper one of Mother's apples, the last wrinkled Russets from the cellar, because you couldn't visit a man empty-handed. And I'd ask him, casual as anything, what the sea had looked like lately from up there at night.

I fell asleep planning the walk, and did not dream at all.
