## 7. What Can Be Carried

The lamp needed nothing from him that evening, and that was the trouble with quiet nights: they left a man alone with his hands, and Juniper Ashgrove's hands had never once in forty-nine years been idle without consequence.

He trimmed the wick anyway. There was always trimming to be done, if a keeper was honest — the carbon building at the edges, the flame leaning a half-degree off true. He adjusted the mantle, watched the light swell and steady, and stood for a moment in the lantern room with the wind worrying at the glass like a dog at a door. Below him the sea worked at the rocks in the dark, patient as a debt collector. Out past the point, where the cove folded itself into shadow, the water lay flat and innocent.

Three nights since he'd seen the sloop.

Three nights since he'd written *unlit vessel, no running lights, entered cove 11:40 p.m., departed before dawn* in the official log in his plainest hand, and then sat looking at those words until the ink dried, understanding exactly what he had done. He had told the truth to a book. The book lived on a shelf in the garrison's reading room now — every keeper's log went to the garrison at the end of each week, copied by a clerk, filed under Captain Ellery Voss's authority. Which meant the truth was sitting in the enemy's house, neat as a confession, and Voss would read it whenever he pleased.

Or never read it at all, because who bothered with a lighthouse log? Weather, wind, lamp hours, oil consumed. A clerk's eyes would slide over it. That was the likeliest thing: indifference, not discovery. And yet every time he thought of that page, Juniper felt the particular cold of a man who has set something down in the wrong place and cannot pick it back up.

He climbed down the ninety-one steps — he knew the count the way he knew his own pulse — and let himself into the keepers' quarters, where the stove ticked and the room smelled of coal oil and the lavender Maren Hallow had pressed into his hands three weeks ago, saying he smelled like a chandler's shop and it was a disgrace to her kitchen. He had kept the sprig on the sill above the sink. It had gone dry and brown but still gave up its scent when the sun touched it in the afternoons, which was most afternoons lately, because the storms were saving themselves.

Supper was barley and salt fish, the garrison ration, issued Tuesday last and already thinning. He ate standing up, the way he'd eaten for eleven years, and washed the bowl, and dried it, and set it in the rack. Then there was nothing between him and the evening except what he had been avoiding since the sloop.

Juniper Ashgrove did not avoid things. Avoidance was a form of untidiness, and untidiness, in his experience, killed people. An untidy wick smoked. An untidy log misled a captain onto rocks. An untidy grief —

Well. He crossed the small room to the chest beneath the window, the one he had packed in spring and not opened since, and knelt before it, and lifted the lid.

Maren's things. Not Maren Hallow — his Maren, Edith, dead these six years of the fever that had come off a mainland ship and taken a tenth of the island with it. Her good shawl, grey wool, moth-safe under camphor. Her Bible with the cracked spine. The blue dress she'd been buried in a cheaper copy of, because the blue one had been her Sunday best and Juniper could not bear to see it go into the ground; so he'd buried the copy and kept the original, and had never once been able to explain to himself whether that was sentiment or theft.

He took out the shawl and weighed it in his hands. This was an old exercise, older than her death. When they'd talked of leaving the Reach — early years, before Tam was born, before the Hallows' orchard had rooted them all — Edith would ask him what they'd take, and Juniper, being Juniper, would make a list. Not a fanciful list. A measured one. The trunk held this much by volume; the ferry allowed this much by weight; therefore, these items, no more. She used to laugh at him, that low laugh through her nose, and say, *You'd carry arithmetic to the end of the world, Jun Ashgrove.* And he'd say someone had to know what could be carried, or everything would be dropped.

She'd died in March, in the blue dress's twin, and he had packed her things the way he packed everything: carefully, finally, against a departure that never came.

He set the shawl back. Closed the lid. Sat on the floor with his back against the chest, the way he used to sit when the grief came on him like weather, letting it pass over rather than through.

But tonight the grief brought company. Tonight, kneeling at the chest, he had thought — clearly, in words, in his own voice — *if I leave, this is what I take.* And the thought had arrived with its arithmetic attached, automatic as breathing: trunk volume, boat weight, the list assembling itself behind his eyes. Shawl. Bible. The photograph of them on the gallery stairs, her hand in his collar. Four items. Eleven pounds, perhaps.

And then a second thought, arriving slower, heavier, the way deep water arrives after the wave has passed:

*If I stay, what will I have carried instead?*

* * *

He lit the second lamp on the desk — the small one, the working one — and pulled the official logbook toward him, and then reached underneath it for the thing he had bought in September from the mainland chandler along with four drums of oil: a ledger, blank, bound in oiled canvas, the kind merchants used for accounts they did not show their creditors.

He had told himself, buying it, that it was for spare records. Lamp hours. Oil consumption. A keeper could always use a spare book. That lie had lasted six weeks, which was longer than most lies deserved.

Now he opened it to the first page and sat looking at the empty ruled lines, and understood that he was about to do something that could not be done halfway. A hidden ledger was a loaded weapon lying around a house full of children. Either it recorded the truth completely enough to matter, or it recorded enough to hang him.

He dipped his pen.

He began with the sloop, because that was the beginning. *Night of October 14th. Vessel unmarked, darkened, entered Merrow Cove approx. 23:40. No lights shown, contrary to all practice. Crew estimated eight to ten. Cargo handled under tarpaulin, crates consistent in size with government grain issue, sixty-pound standard.* He wrote it the way he wrote everything — exact, unsentimental, each sentence load-bearing. He dated it. He signed it. His signature looked strange to him on this page, as though the man who owned that name had split into two men, one writing for the Crown and one writing for whoever would come after, and only one of them was going to survive the season.

Then he turned back through the official log — his own fair copy of it, kept here before the weekly trip to the garrison — and began the colder work: finding everything else.

It was astonishing, once he looked, how much a careful man noticed without noticing he had noticed. He had logged the harvest requisition in September: *garrison takes 40% of island stores per order of Capt. Voss, receipt promised, none yet received.* He had logged the second requisition in October, and the third. He had logged the days the supply cutter failed to arrive, and the days it arrived light — drawing less water than a fully laden boat should, riding high, and Juniper knew boats the way priests knew scripture. He had logged, without comment, the garrison's new activity at the north quay, the private boat that came and went on no schedule any signal station acknowledged.

None of it meant anything alone. Together, read straight through, it was a story, and the story was simple: grain was coming onto this island in the Crown's name and leaving it in somebody else's.

His pen stopped. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below the floor, the sea said its one long word against the rock.

Here was the problem, laid out as plainly as a chart: the official log went to the garrison every Friday. If Voss ever read it closely — if anyone ever asked the clerk why a keeper had noted an unlighted vessel and then heard nothing further — the pages would vanish, or the whole log would, and with it any record that Juniper Ashgrove had ever seen anything at all. And come spring, when the bridge reopened and the historians came to write the season's account, they would write it from the garrison's files. From Voss's reports. The reports would say the storms closed the bridge, the storms spoiled the harvest, the storms took the grain, acts of God all, and the island's hunger would be filed under weather.

Paper was the only witness that couldn't be starved, threatened, or posted to the mainland. But paper in the wrong drawer was worse than no paper at all.

So: the second ledger. Everything the official log held that mattered, copied in his own hand, kept where no clerk would file it and no soldier would think to look. Not a diary — he distrusted diaries, all feeling and no figures — but a parallel account. The truth, kept double-entry, the way merchants kept money: one book for showing, one book for knowing.

He worked until his hand cramped and his tea went cold, copying entries forward, and as he worked he found himself adding the things he had *not* written in the official log, because a keeper's log was a public document and some observations were too dangerous for public paper:

*Maren Hallow, orchardist, states the village allotment for October short by her count eleven sacks against the ledger's promise. She counted twice. She does not miss counts.*

*Tam Hallow, age fourteen, sent to garrison with petition re: rations. Returned shaken. Would not say why. Sergeant present at garrison gate was Brandt, R.*

*Garrison table observed (from path above) serving fresh meat and white flour, same week village children seen cutting seaweed at low tide.*

Each line cost him something to write. Not fear, exactly — though fear was there, sitting in the room like an unpaid boarder — but the cost of admitting what he was doing. Writing it down made it real, and made him responsible to it. A man who writes *the commander is stealing the island's food* is a man who has decided, whether he says so aloud or not, that the commander must be stopped. The pen had made the decision before the mind caught up. Juniper looked at the sentence and did not strike it.

He thought about Edith, and was surprised how easily she came now, sitting here among figures. She would have understood this ledger better than the trunk. She had spent her life keeping accounts nobody asked her to keep — who owed the school fund, whose roof wanted mending, which widow on the lane had eaten and which hadn't. *Somebody has to know what can be carried,* she used to say, throwing his own words back at him across the supper table, *and somebody has to decide what's worth the weight.* He had thought she meant possessions. He had been slow, as he was often slow about the large soft things, and fast about the hard sharp ones.

What could be carried. He looked at the trunk against the wall, holding eleven pounds of a dead woman, and at the ledger under his hand, holding — what? Not weight. Weight was nothing. This book weighed less than her shawl and could sink a captain.

That was the arithmetic he had never learned until now: that the heaviest things were the light ones. Paper. Names. Dates. The difference between a sack of oats delivered and a sack of oats recorded. The whole truth of a starving island might weigh two pounds and hide inside a lens housing, and the whole official lie might fill a filing cabinet in a warm garrison office, and the fight between them would be fought with neither.

He blew out the small lamp's neighbor, trimmed the working flame lower, and kept writing.

Near midnight he heard footsteps on the rock path and put his hand flat over the ledger, heart going like a boy's — but it was only the wind changing, pushing sound up from the village, a door banging somewhere down in the rows. He waited until the night settled again, then went on.

By two in the morning the second ledger held thirty-one entries reaching back to mid-September. He read it through once, front to back, checking it the way he checked the lamp: for completeness, for accuracy, for anything that would fail under strain. It would hold. It was not proof — proof needed manifests, witnesses, signatures other than his own — but it was pattern, and pattern, laid beside whatever evidence might yet surface, would be a rope a man could be hauled up by. Or hanged by. Both ropes looked similar from this end.

Where to keep it. He considered the floor stones, and rejected them — soldiers searching a house start at the floor. He considered the oil store, and rejected it — the first place any quartermaster would inventory. He considered the sea itself, and sat with that one a long while, because the sea was honest: it kept everything given to it, asked no questions, and surrendered nothing. But the sea also drowned the wrong things as readily as the right ones, and a record that existed nowhere existed not at all.

Then his eye went up. Through the ceiling, through the watch room, up the tower's throat to the lantern room, where the great lens turned its slow circles over the water — the Fresnel apparatus, brass and crystal, sacred machinery that even a colonel hesitated to put a hand on. No clerk inventoried the lens. No sergeant searched the light. The light was Juniper's country, sovereign territory, and its one law was that it must burn.

*Later,* he told himself. *Not tonight. Tonight it sleeps in the oil store's false bottom, and tomorrow I build it a proper home.*

He wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, tied it with the knot Edith had taught him — the one that tightened under strain instead of slipping — and carried it down the stone stair to the store, and hid it beneath the spare mantles where his hands had memorized the darkness so thoroughly he could find any object blind. Then he stood a moment in the cold with his palm flat on the crate lid, the way he had once, in another life, rested his hand on a coffin lid, committing something precious to a keeping place.

Resolve, he discovered, felt very little like courage. It felt like bookkeeping. It felt like deciding, entry by entry, that some accounts would be kept honestly no matter who was defrauded by their honesty — and knowing, with a keeper's certainty, that he was the one who would pay the arrears.

He climbed back up the ninety-one steps. In the lantern room the beam swung its patient circle: dark, flash, dark, flash, out over a sea that hid its traffic and its thieves and its drowned alike. He stood at the glass and looked down at the black fold of the cove, and made himself a promise in the plain style he made all his promises, the kind with numbers attached:

Every boat that landed dark, he would log. Every sack that left, he would estimate. Every day the village went short while the garrison ate, he would write it down in a hand no clerk could misread. He would carry it all — every date, every figure, every name — because carrying was the one thing Juniper Ashgrove knew how to do without failing, and because come spring, when the historians arrived with their clean paper and their official ink, someone on this island would have the account ready.

Let them audit the season. Let them add the columns. He would be the column that didn't lie.

Below him, far down in the rows, a single window still showed light — the Hallows' kitchen, he judged, Maren up late over her own accounts, her own ledgers, guarding her own arithmetic of who had and who had not. Two books, then, burning oil on the same night, neither knowing of the other. Juniper watched that small light a while, the way a man watches a fire he means to sit beside come morning.

Then the wind backed northeast and began to build, and he turned to his instruments, because the barometer had started falling the way stones fall — faster and faster — and whatever was coming off the sea would want seeing to.

He did not yet know that the storm gathering beyond the horizon would bring the garrison's cutters to harbor and its sergeants to his door. He knew only that the pressure was dropping, that the light must hold, and that somewhere below in the dark the truth lay wrapped in oilcloth, tightening its knot against the strain.

He trimmed the wick, and stood his watch, and waited for morning to tell him what the night had planned.
