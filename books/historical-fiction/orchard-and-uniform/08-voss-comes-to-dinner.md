## 8. Voss Comes to Dinner

The invitation came by soldier, which was how everything came now. Juniper met him at the foot of the tower stairs, the man's boots ringing on the iron treads like a clock that had learned to walk.

"Captain Voss requests the pleasure of Keeper Ashgrove's company at supper," the soldier recited, holding out a card as stiff and white as a shroud corner. "Saturday evening. The Hallow farm."

Juniper took it. The paper was heavy stock, mainland-made, the kind that cost more than a week of island bread. *Captain E. Voss, Commanding.* No *please* in it anywhere, though the word *pleasure* stood there doing its work.

"Supper at the Hallows'," Juniper said.

"The captain thought a small gathering would be — congenial." The soldier's eyes went past Juniper's shoulder, up the spiral of stairs toward the lamp room, then back. "He asks particularly that you attend. He values your records, Keeper. Your precision."

*Your records.* The words sat in Juniper's chest like a swallowed stone. He had logged the unlit sloop in the cove eleven days ago, honestly, because honesty was the only thing he had left to keep clean. He had not yet reported it further. Every day he did not report it, the log entry became a debt, and debts of that kind gathered interest.

"I'll come," he said.

"Six o'clock." The soldier turned, paused on the bottom step. "The captain dislikes waiting. And he dislikes being watched while he eats. He'll mention it if either happens."

Then he was gone down the cliff path, and Juniper stood in the doorway of his lighthouse holding an invitation that was not one, looking out at the gray water where the bridge should have been visible but wasn't — closed since October, its far span folded up like a bird with a broken wing.

He went upstairs and trimmed the wick, though the wick needed no trimming. It was what his hands did when his mind refused to sit still. Maren had asked him to supper once already this season, plainly and without ceremony, and he had gone gladly and washed dishes and let Tam beat him at cards. That had been supper. This would be something else — a stage built in a farmhouse kitchen, and every person in it assigned a part they hadn't agreed to play.

He wondered which part Voss intended for him. Witness, probably. Witnesses were most useful when they knew they were seen.

* * *

Maren Hallow turned the goose twice in the oven and told herself the heat in her face was from the fire.

"He'll take the good room," Tam said from the table, where he was setting places under her direction. "Sergeant Brandt said officers always get the good room."

"Then we'll put the good chairs in the kitchen," Maren said, "because the kitchen is where we eat." She heard her own voice and softened it. Tam had been strange since his errand to the garrison — quiet in a way fourteen-year-olds weren't meant to be, watching doors. She'd stopped asking what had happened there. Not because she didn't want to know. Because she knew enough already, from the ledger, from the grain issued on paper and absent from the storehouse, to know that asking aloud could cost them. Her silence was a wall she'd built around her son, and she lived inside it too.

Three place settings for the garrison: Voss, his adjutant Lieutenant Pell, Sergeant Brandt — Brandt, who had threatened her boy, invited to break bread in the boy's own house. And three for themselves, plus Juniper, whom she'd asked separately, properly, two nights before the card arrived, so that he would know he was coming for *them* and only incidentally for the captain.

She had spent two days deciding what to serve. Too much, and it looked like plenty existed where none did — an insult to hungry neighbors, an invitation for Voss to conclude the village lied about hunger too. Too little, and it looked like defiance, or worse, like a message. In the end she'd cooked what autumn actually gave: the goose Tam had raised, potatoes from their own row, apples in honey from the orchard, brown bread. Honest food. She would set the truth on the table and let Voss do with it what he liked.

"Mother." Tam's voice, low. He'd stopped laying cutlery. "What if he asks me about the boat again?"

The question hung between them like smoke. There had been no boat, Tam insisted, when she'd pressed him after the garrison errand — but he'd said it the way people say things they've been told to say, and she had let it lie because pressing harder might crack whatever the sergeant's threats had sealed shut.

"Then you tell him what you told the sergeant," Maren said. "Nothing. You know nothing about boats. You're a farmer's son who delivered a petition about flour."

"And if he doesn't believe me?"

She came around the table and straightened the fork he'd set crooked, buying a moment. "Captains believe what's convenient," she said. "That's what makes them captains. Now finish the places. And Tam — tonight, whatever happens at this table, your job is to be dull. Can you be dull?"

Something almost like a smile crossed his face. "I'm good at arithmetic."

"Then talk arithmetic all night if you must."

* * *

Juniper walked down from the light at half past five, dressed in his second-best coat, the one with the mended cuff, carrying nothing. On the path above the orchard he passed neighbors' windows with their shutters drawn, and understood that the whole village knew where he was going, and that some of them — the ones whose children had eaten thin soup since November — resented the smell of roast goose that would drift from the Hallows' chimney whether anyone wanted it to or not. The dinner was not Maren's choice. Everyone knew that too. Hospitality at gunpoint was still hospitality; it just tasted of iron.

Voss arrived at six exactly, which was to say he arrived when he wished and called it punctuality. Juniper saw him come up the lane on a borrowed horse, dismounting with the easy economy of a man who had never once had to hurry. He was younger than Juniper expected — forty perhaps, broad through the shoulder, with a face that seemed designed to be trusted: open brow, steady gray eyes, a mouth made for reasonable sentences. His uniform was immaculate. That was the first thing about Voss, Juniper thought. Everything about him was maintained. A man who kept his buttons bright through a starving winter was a man who understood appearances as ordnance.

"Keeper Ashgrove." Voss extended his hand. His grip was warm and dry and precisely calibrated — firm enough to mean respect, gentle enough to mean he didn't need to prove anything. "I've wanted to make your acquaintance properly. Your light is the finest piece of engineering on this island, and I include my own artillery."

"You flatter the lamp, Captain. The lamp doesn't care."

"No," Voss said, laughing, delighted. "It doesn't, does it? That's what I admire about keepers. You deal in facts. Wind, tide, oil, hours. No sentiment." He said *no sentiment* warmly, as though it were a virtue he personally lacked and mourned.

Lieutenant Pell followed him in — a narrow young man who wrote things in a small book — and Sergeant Brandt last, ducking under the lintel with his cap in his hands. Brandt's eyes found Tam across the kitchen and flinched away from him. Juniper marked that. A man ashamed is a man with a seam in him.

"Mistress Hallow." Voss bowed over Maren's hand. "I am told your orchard kept this island fed when the mainland forgot us. I intend to say as much in my reports."

"It was God and the trees, Captain," Maren said. "We mostly stayed out of their way."

"Modesty. Another island virtue." He looked around the kitchen with genuine-seeming appreciation — the copper pots, the dried herbs hanging from beams, the long table scrubbed pale. "You know, in twenty years of service I have dined in admirals' cabins and governor's palaces, and I cannot recall a single meal. But a farmhouse kitchen —" he spread his hands — "*that* is England, isn't it? Or near enough."

*Or near enough.* Juniper filed the phrase away. Voss spoke of the island the way a collector speaks of a purchase — fondly, possessively, already planning the shelf it would sit on.

They sat. Voss placed himself at the head without anyone offering, the way water finds low ground. Juniper ended up across from him, with Tam at his elbow and Brandt down at the far corner eating carefully, like a man defusing something.

The goose went around. For a few minutes there was only the honest noise of eating, and Juniper felt the old treacherous comfort of a full table — the thing he had not had since Elowen died, the clink and murmur of people bound together by a shared dish. He caught Maren watching him watch it, and looked down at his plate.

"Keeper." Voss wiped his mouth. "Tell me about the night of the fourteenth."

The kitchen did not go silent — Maren saw to that, passing the potatoes with a steady hand — but Juniper felt it go quiet inside his own skull, the way the tower goes quiet when the fog signal cuts off mid-blast.

"The fourteenth," he repeated.

"Of November. My adjutant shows a supply movement in the cove that night, and I find your logbook — which I am told is exemplary — makes no mention of any vessel. Curious. A keeper who logs every fishing skiff missed logging a sloop." Voss smiled. "Unless there was no sloop. Unless the adjutant's figures are wrong. Which would you say, Keeper? You deal in facts."

There it was. Not a question — a door held open, and beyond it either a lie that made Juniper complicit or a truth that made him a target. Voss had even given him the shape of the escape: blame the adjutant. Deny the sighting. *No sentiment.*

Juniper cut a slice of potato and ate it before answering, because a slow answer reads as fear and a fast one as rehearsed.

"On the fourteenth of November," he said, "there was sea fog off the south shore from nine until past midnight. Visibility under thirty yards. I logged the fog. If a vessel moved in the cove that night, Captain, it moved below my horizon and outside my duty. My light marks the shoals. It does not mark men's choices."

Silence. Pell's small pen scratched. Then Voss laughed — a real laugh, or such a fine copy that no instrument Juniper owned could tell the difference — and struck the table gently with his palm.

"There it is! *Outside my duty.* Do you hear that, Pell? Write it down. That is a professional speaking." He pointed his fork at Juniper, friendly as a schoolmaster. "I have officers, Keeper, who would have leapt to tell me what I wanted to hear. You gave me the limits of your office instead. I find that rare. I find that *trustworthy*."

*I find that trustworthy.* Juniper held the gray eyes and nodded, and understood that he had just passed a test he could not afford to fail twice, and that the price of passing was that Voss now believed him safe. Let him believe it. The hidden ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floor stones under the stair, and the fog had indeed covered the cove that night, and both things were true, and only one of them would survive the winter.

"And you, Mistress Hallow." Voss turned the warmth like a lamp on a gimbal. "Your ledgers show the ration issue faithfully. Remarkable bookkeeping for a farmwife."

"I had a good teacher," Maren said. "My husband counted everything. Apples, years, sins." She smiled at the dead man's memory, and Juniper saw her hand tighten on the bread knife a half-second longer than carving required. "Counting keeps grief orderly, Captain."

"Doesn't it just." Something crossed Voss's face — fast, gone — that was almost recognition, almost respect, and Juniper thought: *he has lost someone too, or he has practiced losing someone.* "Though I'll confess, madam, counting can become a habit that outlives its usefulness. Some figures are better left as estimates."

"Some grain," Maren said pleasantly, "is better left as flour."

Brandt's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. Pell looked up. For one heartbeat the kitchen balanced on a knife-edge, and then Voss threw back his head and roared with laughter.

"*Better left as flour!* Oh, mistress, they warned me islanders were blunt. They undersold you." He dabbed his eyes. "But you're right, of course. Flour feeds children. Figures feed clerks. And I promise you —" and here his voice changed, dropped, became the reasonable voice, the voice of a tired public servant among friends — "my whole purpose here is that the flour reaches the children. That is why I ask questions. That is why I ask *who else* asks questions. Because if someone on this island is spreading stories — grain diverted, stores sold off the books, soldiers grown fat while farmers grow thin — those stories do damage. They frighten folk. They make my work harder. So when I hear that certain persons have been comparing the ration ledger against the storehouse tallies — purely as gossip, you understand — I want to know who, so I can *reassure* them."

He let it fall into the warm air and waited, smiling, the way a man waits at a hole he has dug and covered with leaves.

Tam, bless him, chose that moment to be dull. "Captain," he said, "if a hundredweight is eight stone to the bushel, why does the garrison schedule reckon it as ten?"

The question landed like a dropped plate. Pell blinked. Brandt stared at the boy with naked alarm. And Voss — Voss looked at Tam for a long moment, and Juniper watched the charm hold and hold and then, at the edges, show what it was lacquering over: a cold, patient attention, the attention of a man marking a name.

"Why indeed, young master Hallow," Voss said softly. "Perhaps you should come count for me sometime. I do admire a boy who checks the arithmetic." He rose, drawing the evening up with him like a net. "Mistress Hallow, you have fed me better than I deserve. Keeper, I look forward to reading your log for many years. Sergeant — Lieutenant. We have rounds."

At the door he paused, hand on the lintel, and looked back at the table — at the goose bones, the honey pot, the family shapes around it — with an expression Juniper would think about for weeks afterward, because it was almost tenderness, and tenderness from such a man was worse than threat.

"One housekeeping matter," Voss said. "Beginning next week, all official correspondence leaving the island — logs, ledgers, petitions, letters — passes through the garrison post for review before the packet boat takes it. Nothing sinister. Wartime measure. Paper discipline." He tapped the doorframe twice, lightly, like a benediction. "Good night, all."

* * *

Maren stood in the yard long after the hoofbeats faded, arms wrapped against the cold, listening to the sea make its slow argument with the rocks.

Inside, Tam was clearing plates with too much force, and Juniper had not yet left, though his coat was on. She went back in. The three of them stood in the wrecked magnificence of the kitchen — a whole goose consumed by wolves who had thanked her for it — and nobody spoke.

"Paper discipline," Juniper said at last.

"He means to read everything," Maren said. "Every log, every letter, before spring."

"Aye." Juniper looked at her, and she saw him decide something — saw the decision move through his face like weather. "Then whatever gets written on this island this winter had best be written twice."

Their eyes met over the dead fire of the candles, keeper and farmwife, and Maren understood that he was telling her — obliquely, in the language of two careful people — that somewhere he was keeping a second account. She almost told him then. Almost said, *I've known since October, I've counted every missing hundredweight, I stayed silent for Tam.* But Tam was standing right there with a stack of plates, watching them with eyes too old for his face, and some walls should not come down in front of children.

"Twice," she said simply. "Aye. That's sensible."

Juniper nodded once, shrugged into the cold outside his coat, and opened the door onto the dark. Then he stopped, half-turned.

"Captain," he said quietly, to neither of them and both. "When he looked at Tam — 'come count for me sometime.' That wasn't courtesy."

"I know," Maren said.

"That was a hook."

"I know," she said again, and her voice cracked on the second word despite everything, despite the wall, despite two months of practice, and Juniper stood in the doorway with the wind pulling at him and did not leave, and did not speak, and finally reached out and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way a man steadies himself on a rail in rough water.

Then he was gone up the cliff path, a dark shape climbing toward his light, and Maren turned back to her kitchen, to her son, to the long winter, and began — silently, in her head, where no review could reach it — to write everything twice.
