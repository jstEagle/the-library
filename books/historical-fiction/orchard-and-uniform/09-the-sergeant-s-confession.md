## 9. The Sergeant's Confession

The knock came at half past eleven, and it was wrong in every particular.

Juniper knew the sound of a fist on his door the way he knew the sound of wind in the lantern room — by weight, by rhythm, by what it meant. The garrison knocked twice, crisp as a drill call. Maren Hallow knocked three times soft, then waited, because she had learned that he came to the door slowly on bad nights. Tam knocked like a boy, all elbow and apology.

This was none of those. This was a single blow, then silence, then another, delivered with the flat of a hand rather than the knuckles, as though the man on the other side could not decide whether he wanted an answer or wanted to be turned away.

Juniper set down the wick-trimmers and stood a moment in the round stone room below the lamp, listening. The light turned above him with its patient mechanical breathing — *tick, sigh, tick* — and outside, November pressed its grey face against the glass. He had not expected anyone tonight. He had not expected anyone at all, which was the condition of his life now: the bridge closed, the island sealed, the season stretching ahead like a sentence being read aloud.

He took the lamp from its hook and went down.

The man on the step was Sergeant Brandt, and he was drunk in the way soldiers are sometimes drunk — not loud, not laughing, but hollowed out, as if the drink had been poured into a wound instead of a stomach. His greatcoat was buttoned wrong, one side riding higher than the other, and his cap was gone entirely, so that the rain beaded in his cropped hair and ran down a face that looked ten years older than it had at the dinner table three weeks past.

"Keeper." His voice came out sanded flat. "I need — I don't know what I need."

"You need to come inside before you freeze," Juniper said, and stepped back from the doorway, because whatever else the man was, he was cold, and cold was something Juniper understood how to answer.

Brandt hesitated on the threshold like a dog that has been kicked too often to trust an open hand. Then he came in, dripping, and stood in the middle of the room as if he'd forgotten what rooms were for.

"Sit," Juniper said. "There's tea. There's also whisky, if tea won't do."

"It'll do," Brandt said. "The tea." And then, when Juniper had set the kettle over the small stove and turned back: "No. The whisky. God forgive me, the whisky."

Juniper poured two fingers into a tin cup and put it in the sergeant's hands. Brandt drank half of it at once, closed his eyes, and shuddered — not from the spirit but from something underneath it, some load shifting in the hold of him.

"I shouldn't be here," he said. "If Voss knew I was here—"

"He doesn't know?"

"He thinks I'm walking the cove road." Brandt laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "He trusts me. That's the worst of it. He trusts me like a man trusts a good boot. You don't thank a boot, Keeper. You just keep wearing it until it wears through."

Juniper sat down across from him and said nothing. He had learned long ago — first from his wife, Ede, who could wait out any silence, and then from thirty years of keeping a light for ships that never signaled back — that most men did not need questions. They needed a witness. They needed one person sitting still in the dark while they said the thing they could not unsay.

The kettle began its low complaint. Juniper made the tea anyway and set it within reach, and the two cups sat together on the table, whisky and tea, like two versions of the same night.

"They're starving us," Brandt said at last. It came out without preamble, without the long approach he had clearly rehearsed on the walk up from the village. "That's not — I want to say it plain, because I've been saying it crooked to myself since September, and crooked is how they get you. The grain comes off the supply sloops. It goes into the garrison stores under requisition orders. And then it goes out again, at night, down to the cove, onto boats that aren't on any schedule I ever saw. Mainland brokers. Voss sells it, Keeper. He sells the island's own bread while the ration ledger says we ate it."

Juniper kept his hands still around his cup. Inside, something turned over with the slow heavy motion of ballast shifting in a hull.

"How much?" he asked. Not *is this true*. He had already logged the unlit sloop in October; he had already watched crates go down the cliff path in the dark; he had already copied pages into a second ledger hidden behind the oil store. The question was only scale.

"Enough that the village children are hungry." Brandt stared into the tin cup. "Enough that Maren Hallow's boy asked me — asked *me*, a uniform — why the garrison had meat twice a week when his mother was boiling orchard apples for supper. Fourteen years old, and he asked me polite, like I might not know the answer." He drank again. "I knew the answer. I've known since the second shipment. I counted the crates myself. Two hundredweight of barley, marked *spoiled — condemned*, and I carried it down the path on my own shoulder and helped load it, and the broker's man paid Voss in banknotes right there on the shingle, and I stood at parade rest like a good soldier and looked at the sea."

"Why are you telling me this?" Juniper asked quietly.

It was the question Brandt had been walking toward and away from all evening, and it landed on him visibly, like a stone dropped from height. He set the cup down. His hands were not steady.

"Because you're the only man on this rock who isn't afraid of me," he said. "And because—" He stopped. Started again. "My father was a tenant farmer. Outside Harrowgate. When the blight years came, the landlord sold the grain off the estate same as this, and my mother boiled nettles, and my little sister—" His jaw worked. "She didn't die of the blight, Keeper. There wasn't any blight. She died of arithmetic. Somebody's ledger balanced better without her fed."

The stove ticked. Above them, faint through two floors of stone, the great lens turned on its bath of mercury, sweeping its beam out over the black water, patient as conscience.

"And now I'm the landlord's man," Brandt said. "Do you see? I swore an oath to the Crown, and the Crown sent me here, and the man wearing the Crown's coat is doing exactly what starved my sister, and I held the crates for him. I held them steady. Good soldier. Good boot." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and ashamed, all three at once, and Juniper thought: *there is the wound the drink was poured into.* "I'm not telling you because I'm brave. I'm telling you because I can't carry it anymore and stay standing."

"What do you want me to do with it?" Juniper asked. It was not a challenge; it was an honest question, and he meant it honestly. "You've told me. Now what?"

"I don't know." Brandt passed a hand over his face. "I don't know. Report it? To whom? The bridge is shut. The post is shut. Every officer above me eats at Voss's table or fears him, and every man below me follows my lead, and my lead has been *eyes front, Sergeant, nothing to see*. If I go to the mainland in spring and say a word, I'm a perjurer against my commanding officer with no proof but my word against his reports. If I say nothing—" He gestured vaguely upward, at the storm-season sky, at the whole sealed world of it. "Then I'm what I am now. Just cleaner about it."

"There's the manifests," Juniper said.

Brandt went very still.

"The shipping papers," Juniper continued, evenly. "Grain doesn't move without paper. Requisitions in, sales out, payments received. A man who counts crates would know where those papers live."

"A man who counts crates knows they live in Voss's strongbox," Brandt said, "under Voss's eye, guarded by men whose bread Voss controls. A man who touches those papers is a thief before breakfast and a corpse before noon." But he said it the way a man reads a map of a country he is already planning to enter.

Juniper rose and fetched the teapot and refilled the untouched cup, more for something to do with his hands than anything else. While his back was turned he felt the old habit rise in him — the measuring habit, the weighing habit, the habit of a man who had spent a year after Ede's death deciding what of her life could be carried and what must be left in boxes. He weighed this man now. Drunk, frightened, complicit — and yet here, on this doorstep, in the rain, choosing to say the crooked thing straight to the one person on Merrow's Reach who wrote everything down.

"Sergeant," he said, sitting again, "I'm going to tell you something, and you may throw your tea at me for it."

Brandt almost smiled. "Go on."

"I keep a logbook. Every keeper does. Weather, lamp hours, vessels sighted. It's a legal record — sworn paper, eventually. What I log stands." He let that sit a moment. "In October I logged a sloop at the cove that isn't on any schedule. I've logged the crate traffic on the cliff path. I've logged the garrison boats going out dark. I haven't reported it, because reporting it means handing it to Captain Voss, who is the subject of the report. So it sits in my book, waiting for someone with rank enough to read it and power enough to act."

Brandt was staring at him. "You've been building a case in a weather log."

"I've been keeping an honest log," Juniper said. "It happens to be a case. There's a difference, but it's narrower than you'd think."

For a long moment the sergeant said nothing. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere below the point, the sea said its one long word over and over.

"My testimony plus your log," Brandt said slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "That's still not manifests. That's a keeper's sightings and a sergeant's say-so. Voss will call it mutiny dressed as bookkeeping. He'll call you a mad widower and me a drunk, and he'll be half right, God help me, look at me."

"Yes," Juniper agreed. "He will. Which is why the papers matter. Paper doesn't get drunk. Paper doesn't grieve. Paper just sits in a strongbox being true until somebody carries it out."

"And you're asking me to be the somebody."

"I'm not asking anything," Juniper said. "I'm telling you what's missing. What you do about it is between you and your oath. But I'll say this much, Sergeant, and then I'll say no more on it." He leaned forward. "You told me your sister died of arithmetic. Well. There's a boy in the village named Tam Hallow who is learning the same arithmetic this winter — how many ounces of barley a body needs, how many days a sack lasts, how much a mother can skip so her son doesn't have to. Every week the papers sit in that strongbox, the sum gets worse. You can't un-carry the crates you carried. But you can decide what you carry next."

Brandt looked at his hands. They were big hands, rope-scarred, soldier's hands, and they shook slightly, whether from the drink leaving him or the truth arriving, Juniper could not tell.

"If I'm caught," Brandt said, "they'll hang me. Or worse — they'll post me somewhere warm and quiet and pretend I never existed, which for a soldier is the same thing."

"Yes," Juniper said. "Probably."

"That's it? *Probably?* No speech about duty and honor?"

"I buried a wife, Sergeant. I've done my speeches." Juniper stood and took both cups to the basin. "Duty is a heavy thing and honor is heavier, and neither one helps a man climb. All I can offer you is what I know: things weigh less once you've decided to pick them up. It's the deciding that breaks your back. After that, it's just carrying."

Brandt laughed — a real laugh this time, short and cracked, startled out of him. "You're a strange man, Keeper Ashgrove."

"So I'm told." Juniper glanced at the clock on the mantel. Nearly one. "It's a long walk back down the cliff path in the dark, and the rain's turning. You're welcome to the chair by the stove till morning, and I'll wake you before first light so you're home before the garrison stirs. Or you can go now. Either way, tonight never happened — except in my logbook, where it happened exactly as it happened, minus names."

"Minus names," Brandt repeated. He looked at the chair, at the stove, at the window full of rain, and something in his face gave up a fight it had apparently been fighting since the doorstep. "One hour," he said. "Just till I can walk straight. And Keeper—" He paused at the chair, half-turned, and the firelight caught the shame still living in his face, not burned away, maybe never to be burned away, but no longer alone with itself. "The next shipment goes out Thursday week. Big one. Before the equinox tides. If a man were going to do something foolish, that would be the night the strongbox is open longest."

He sat down heavily and was asleep within minutes, boots and all, his head tipped back, his mouth open, snoring with the terrible innocence of the exhausted.

Juniper banked the stove, climbed the stairs, and stood a while in the lantern room with the light turning above him. Below, the sergeant slept the sleep of a man who had set down something heavy without knowing yet whether he would pick it back up. Beyond the glass, the cove lay invisible in the rain — the shingle, the cliff path, the strongbox waiting in the flooded garrison hall with its paper heart of arithmetic.

Thursday week.

Juniper opened the logbook to the day's page and wrote, in his small exact hand: *Wind SW fresh, rain continuous. Visitor at 2330, duration one hour, no names. Subject: weights and measures.*

He read it back. It was true, and it was nothing, and it was everything, depending on who read it and when — and spring, and the historian's pen, were still a long way off across a winter of storms.

He closed the book. Below him, faint through the floor, Sergeant Brandt slept on, and dreamed, perhaps, of crates, or of nettles, or of a little sister waiting at a gate for a brother who had finally decided what to carry.

* * *

Three mornings later, the answer came — though not in any form Juniper had imagined.

He was trimming the lamp at dawn when he saw the figure on the cliff path below the tower, standing very still in the grey light, looking up at the lighthouse. A soldier's posture even out of uniform, shoulders squared, cap in hand. For a moment Juniper thought it was Brandt come back with an answer.

Then the figure raised one arm and pointed — deliberately, unmistakably — down the coast toward the garrison, and Juniper's stomach went cold, because standing beside the man on the path, small and thin against the wind, was Tam Hallow.

And Tam was looking up at the lighthouse too, and his face, even at that distance, wore the expression of a boy who has seen something he was never meant to see.
