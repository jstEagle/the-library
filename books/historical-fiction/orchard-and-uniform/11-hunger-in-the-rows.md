## 11. Hunger in the Rows

The frost came down from the moor in the last week of November and did not leave again. It lay on the orchard rows like a shroud laid over sleeping men, silvering the bare branches, sealing the earth so hard that Maren Hallow could not have turned a spade into it if her life had depended on it. Perhaps it did. She walked the rows each morning before light with a lantern she could ill afford to burn, counting what remained: six barrels of apples gone soft at the core, half a crib of turnips, a sack of barley meant for seed. She counted them the way Juniper Ashgrove counted oil — because numbers did not flinch, and she needed something in this world that would not.

Behind her, the village slept badly. She could tell by the smoke. In October there had been a dozen chimneys breathing fat, easy plumes by dawn; now the smoke came thin and gray and late, and some chimneys did not breathe at all until midmorning, because a fire banked overnight cost less than one kept alive, and a cold house made for slower hunger than an empty pot. Widow Creel's chimney. The Prynne boys' cottage, where two brothers under ten now lived alone since their mother went coughing to the churchyard in the spring. Old Heddy Sorrel's crooked stack, which smoked only when Heddy remembered to eat, which was less and less.

Maren stopped at the third row, where Tam's small handprints were pressed into the frost on the gate rail from the evening before, and she let herself feel, for exactly the length of one breath, the full weight of what she was carrying. Then she put it back down where she kept it, behind the ribs, and went inside to make breakfast stretch.

* * *

Breakfast that morning was barley porridge thin enough to see the bottom of the bowl through, sweetened with a spoonful of apple butter Maren had been saving since September and told herself she wasn't saving for anything in particular. Tam ate without complaint, which was worse than complaining. A boy who complained still believed the world owed him better. A boy who ate silently had already learned the arithmetic.

"Mrs. Prynne's boys were at the shore yesterday," Tam said, into his bowl. "Digging for limpets. The tide pools are all scoured out from the storms. Davy Prynne said they got eleven."

"Eleven limpets."

"For all three of them." He said it carefully, the way he'd begun saying careful things lately, watching her face over the top of the words. "Mum. The garrison had a roast yesterday. I saw the cart come up from the cove. Two whole carcasses, and Brandt driving, and he wouldn't look at anybody."

"I know," Maren said.

"The ration cart came Tuesday and it was flour again, and half of it's weevils, and Mr. Quill says the ledger shows—"

"Tam." She kept her voice even. "Eat your porridge."

He ate his porridge. But he looked at her while he did it, fourteen years old and already too old, and Maren understood that whatever she decided in the coming days would be decided in front of him, and that he would carry it the way children carry everything — longer, and closer to the bone, than the adults who handed it to him ever would.

After he'd gone out to check the snares — they had three wire snares along the hedgerow, and the rabbits had grown cunning, or perhaps simply scarce — Maren took down the ration ledger from its shelf behind the flour bin and opened it to November and read it again, though she knew every line by heart.

She had copied it herself, in fair hand, from the figures posted at the garrison storehouse door each week. That was the arrangement: Voss's quartermaster posted the official issue, the village council recorded receipt, and the stores were distributed from the tithe barn on market days under Sergeant Brandt's eye. On paper, the island of Merrow's Reach had been fed all autumn. On paper, four hundredweight of wheat had come ashore in October, and three hundred and fifty in November, and oats besides, and salt pork, and dried peas. On paper, no child on this island had missed a meal since the bridge closed.

Maren ran her thumb down the column and did the sum she had done forty times. The wheat issued in October should have filled the tithe barn's near bay past the crossbeam. She had stood in that bay herself with a measuring rod. The grain had reached, generously, the second rung.

Somewhere between the garrison storehouse and the tithe barn, more than half the island's bread was evaporating. And the smell of roasting meat that drifted down from the garrison hill on Sunday evenings was not the smell of men eating their own rations.

She closed the ledger and sat with her hands flat on its cover, and above her, through the ceiling beams, she could hear the wind worrying at the orchard, patient as a creditor.

* * *

The village council met that evening in the church vestry, which was the only room on Merrow's Reach large enough to hold them and warm enough — barely — to keep their breath from showing. There were nine of them: Maren, Tom Quill the schoolmaster who kept the receipts, Widow Creel, the fisherman Aldous Prynne, Heddy Sorrel, the cooper Bram Fell and his wife Ilsa, the young stonemason Ned Carraway, and old Reverend Lyle, who had retired to the island years ago and found that retirement on a sealed island meant you never really left the work. Juniper Ashgrove sat at the end of the bench nearest the door, having come down from the light on the pretext of drawing oil rations, which fooled no one and offended no one either. He had a way of arriving at things without announcing himself. Maren had noticed it the first supper he'd eaten at her table: a man who entered rooms the way water entered a hull, quietly, and then was everywhere.

Quill had the ration ledger open on the table between two candles, and around it the meeting arranged itself like iron filings around a magnet.

"You've all seen the postings," Quill said. He was a thin man with ink-stained fingers and a voice built for a schoolroom, pitched to reach the back bench. "You've all done your own sums, or you've heard your neighbor do them. Four hundredweight in October. I'll not insult this room by pretending otherwise. We are being robbed."

"We're being starved," Ilsa Fell said. Her voice was quieter than her husband's would have been, and for that reason it landed harder. "There's a difference, Tom. Robbery takes your purse. This is taking the winter out from under the children. The Prynne boys are down to a meal and a half a day. Heddy hasn't been to market in three weeks because she says there's no point standing in the cold for a pound of weevily flour."

"There's point enough," Heddy said from the corner, "in being seen. They count us, you know. At the distributions. Brandt counts heads. I mind my grandmother telling me about the famine year on the mainland, before any of you were born, and the first thing that happened was folk stopped coming out. Stopped being counted. And after that nobody had to answer for them." She folded her hands in her lap. "I go to market. I stand where they can see me."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the wind found the loose shutter on the vestry window and worried it, tick, tick, tick, like a clock counting toward something.

"So we confront him," Ned Carraway said. He was twenty-three and had shoulders like a doorframe and the particular courage of a man who has never yet lost anything he couldn't rebuild with his hands. "We go up the hill, all of us, every soul in the village, and we stand in Voss's yard and we ask him to his face where our bread has gone. He's one man and thirty soldiers, but there's four hundred of us, and soldiers' stomachs get uneasy when the crowd is bigger than the bayonet line."

"And then what?" Maren said.

They all looked at her. She hadn't meant to speak yet; she had meant to hold it, the way she held everything, until the shape of it was sure. But Ned's face had the shine on it of a man about to do something irreversible, and she had seen that shine before, on Tam's father, the year he decided to take the boat out in the dropping barometer because the herring were running and pride is heavier than weather sense. Her husband had been right about the herring. The sea had been right about everything else.

"Ned. Say we march up the hill. Say we stand in his yard and shout the truth at him. What does he do?"

"He gives it back," Ned said. "Or he can't very well refuse in front of—"

"He denies it," Maren said. "That's the first thing. He stands on his own step with the garrison flag over his head and he tells four hundred hungry people that the ledgers are correct, that the shortfall is spoilage, rats, wet storage, the storms — pick your reason — and half the crowd will believe him, because hungry people want to believe whoever feeds them next. And the other half gets angrier. And then, Ned, then he does the second thing, which is the thing he's been waiting for someone to give him a reason to do. He declares assembly unlawful under garrison authority. He arrests the loudest of us. Maybe he arrests you. Maybe he arrests Quill, who keeps the receipts, and then we have no record and no voice and no man on this council who isn't frightened. And the day after that, the ration carts come up short again, only this time nobody dares say so."

"They can't arrest the whole island," Bram Fell said.

"No," Maren agreed. "But they don't need to. They need one example. One man dragged off to the mainland in irons, and the rest of us will stand in our kitchens and count our limpets and say nothing, and he will have bought another quiet season for the price of one loud farmer." She looked down at her own hands, chapped from the frost-walking, and made herself lift her eyes again. "I'm not saying the man doesn't deserve to hear it to his face. God knows he does. I'm saying that right now, tonight, the only weapon we hold is proof, and if we spend it on anger we'll have nothing left to spend on justice."

"And what proof?" Ned demanded. "A ledger with a shortfall in it? He'll call it spoilage, like you said. Rats."

"Rats don't load crates onto boats at night," Maren said, "and rats don't roast two carcasses on a Sunday."

The words came out before she could weigh them, and the moment they were out she felt the room change temperature — felt Quill go very still, felt Widow Creel's sharp intake of breath, felt Juniper at the end of the bench stop being furniture and become a witness. She had just said aloud, in a room with eight other people, the thing she had carried alone since October. The thing she had not even fully said to Tam. The cove. The unlit sloop. The crates.

"Who told you that?" Ned asked slowly.

"A widow sees a good deal from her kitchen window," Maren said. "And I'm not the only one on this island who's seen boats in the cove that aren't on any schedule. Am I?" She looked, deliberately, down the length of the benches, and her gaze rested last on Juniper Ashgrove.

The lighthouse keeper met her eyes. His face, seamed and wind-burned and habitually closed, did something she had learned to read over a season of suppers: a fractional settling, like a man shifting weight onto firmer ground. He didn't nod. He didn't need to. Everyone in the vestry watched the silence between them and understood it for what it was — two people confirming a shared account without spending a word of it in front of the room.

"There's a difference," Juniper said at last, in his low, unhurried voice, the voice of a man accustomed to being heard over storms, "between knowing a thing and being able to prove it. Mrs. Hallow is right about that. I log what passes my light. I've logged boats in the cove that answered no hail and showed no papers. But a logbook entry is a keeper's word, and a keeper's word against a garrison commander's report—" He shook his head slightly. "In spring, when the bridge opens, there will be an inquiry. There may be men from the mainland whose trade is records. If we come to them with fury, they'll write down that the island was restless. If we come to them with figures, dates, witnesses, manifests—" he glanced at Maren, "—they'll write down the truth. Fury fades by springtime, Ned. Ink doesn't."

"Ink doesn't feed the Prynne boys tonight," Ned said hotly.

"No," Maren said, and her voice cracked on it despite everything, because that was the wound under all of it and she refused to pretend otherwise. "Nothing we do in this room feeds them tonight. That's the truth of our position and I won't dress it up. We are sealed on this rock with a thief, and spring is a long way off, and every week between here and there costs us. So the question is not whether we act. The question is what we can afford to lose acting too soon." She stood, because her knees wanted to and because a seated woman argues and a standing one testifies. "Here's what I propose. We watch. All of us, quietly, the way Heddy watches at market and I watch from my window and—" she allowed herself the smallest glance at the lighthouse keeper, "—and others watch from higher windows still. Dates, times, boat names, crate counts. Everything written down, everything kept safe. And meanwhile we pool what food is left. Properly pooled, not charity — nobody eats alone and nobody eats proud. Bram's smokehouse, Creel's salt, my orchard's soft apples, whatever the snares bring. If we share, we stretch. If we storm the hill, we bleed, and the bleeding buys nothing."

"It buys attention," Ned said.

"It buys graves," said Heddy Sorrel, from her corner, in her dry old voice, and the room went quiet around the word.

They argued for another hour. That was the nature of councils, Maren thought wearily, watching candle wax build stalactites down the sides — that the truth had to pass through every mouth before it could be swallowed. Bram feared the pooling would breed resentment. Ilsa backed Maren. Quill, precise to the last, proposed a shadow ledger kept in cipher, separate from the official receipts, and Maren agreed so quickly that Quill raised his eyebrows at her over his spectacles. Ned Carraway held out longest, arms crossed, jaw set, until finally Reverend Lyle — who had said almost nothing all evening — spoke from his chair by the stove.

"When I was a young curate in Fennmouth," the old man said, "there was a mill owner who shorted his workers' meal sacks, and the workers marched on his house, and he had them fired upon, and eleven died, and the inquiry afterward found for the workers to a man. Every account agrees he was guilty. Not one account suggests the eleven came back." He looked around the vestry, mild and terrible. "Justice delayed is a grief. Justice purchased with blood before its time is a waste of both. Mrs. Hallow has the patience of an orchardist, and orchardists know what the rest of us forget: that you cannot hurry fruit, you can only keep it from rotting. I'll stand with her proposal."

Ned uncrossed his arms. "Then I'll keep the watch on the harbor path," he said, grudgingly, which from Ned was a surrender complete enough to sign.

* * *

They filed out into the frozen dark one by one, breath smoking, lanterns bobbing away down the lanes toward thin chimneys. Maren stayed to snuff the candles, and Juniper stayed too, gathering his oil chit from the table, and for a moment they were alone in the vestry with the smell of tallow and cold stone.

"You said the historian's letter," Maren said quietly. "Spring. Men whose trade is records."

Juniper paused with his cap in his hands. In the last candlelight his face was all planes and shadow, a face like the headland itself. "You remember that."

"I remember everything anyone tells me in my own kitchen. It's the orchardist in me. Things planted tend to grow." She folded the shadow ledger — Quill's cipher copy — into the inner pocket of her coat, against her heart, where she had decided it would live. "How many entries, Keeper? In your book. Of boats and nights."

"Enough," Juniper said. "Not yet enough to hang a man. Getting nearer."

"Then we're agreed," Maren said. "Patience and proof."

"Patience and proof," he echoed, and there was something in the repetition that was not quite agreement — something weighted, like a man testing ice before committing his full step.

She blew out the candles. In the dark, before their eyes adjusted, she heard him say it, very low, as much to himself as to her:

"But winter tests patience harder than it tests anything else. And I'm not sure how much proof is worth if we bury half the village getting to spring."

Outside, the frost lay silver on the orchard rows, and far off across the black water, where the cove hid its secrets below the headland, a lantern moved — brief, low, deliberate, going down toward the water where no honest boat waited at that hour.

Maren stood in the churchyard gate and watched it until it vanished, and then she turned up her collar and started home to write down the date, the hour, and everything she had seen.
