## 13. Betrayal at the Cove

The chalk fit Tam's pocket the way a secret fits a fist — warm, small, and always threatening to give him away.

He'd taken it from the schoolhouse slate box three days ago, when Miss Ellery-Ann had her back turned writing the date on the board. A stub of white chalk, no bigger than his thumb. He'd told himself it was nothing, that boys took chalk all the time, but his palm had sweated around it the whole walk home and it hadn't stopped sweating since. Because chalk wasn't for slates anymore. Chalk was for marking.

*Mark the boat*, Maren had said — no. Maren had said nothing. That was the whole trouble of it. It had been Juniper who said it, three nights ago in their kitchen, his voice kept low the way he kept everything low, like a man afraid of waking something. *If you can get close to her at the cove, mark her hull. Somewhere small. The waterline strake on the port side, if you can reach it. Then whatever name they've painted over, whoever asks about her, we'll know which boat we saw.*

*We*, Tam thought now, hunched behind the gorse at the top of the cliff path with the February wind trying to peel his cap off his head. *We'll know.* Except there was no we down here. There was only Tam Hallow, fourteen years old, with a stub of chalk in his mitten and a heart going like a gull's wings against a window.

Below him, Merrow's Cove lay gray as an unwashed dish. The tide was half out, dragging its lace of foam across the shingle, and the smugglers' sloop sat in the shallows where she'd grounded herself on the falling water — the same sloop Juniper had seen from the gallery, the one that came dark and unlit, off every schedule ever written. Up close she was smaller than Tam had imagined and uglier: patched sails furled tight, paint peeling in long gray scabs, a broad arrow roughly painted out on her stern and something else painted over the painting-out. She'd been a government boat once. Now she was nobody's boat, which meant she was Voss's boat.

Four men worked on the beach, rolling barrels down from the cart track to the waterline. Tam counted them twice, then a third time, because counting was the one thing he could do perfectly and it steadied him. Four. Two wore garrison greatcoats with the collars up. Two wore fishermen's oilskins that fooled nobody, because one of them had sergeant's boots on under them — Tam knew those boots, had seen them stamping through the village square a hundred times. Brandt's boots. Brandt himself, big and stooped, hauling a barrel like it weighed nothing, his face turned away.

And standing apart from all of them, at the edge of the wash where the foam ran up to touch his boots and retreated, stood Captain Ellery Voss.

Tam's mouth went dry. Voss was supposed to be at the garrison. Voss was supposed to be anywhere but here, in person, watching barrels of the island's own grain go down a private beach in the last gray light of a February afternoon. In the stories Tam told himself — and he told himself stories the way other boys whittled, constantly, to keep his hands busy — Voss stayed in the fort on the hill, a shape behind lit windows, a voice giving orders. Stories were safe because they kept the wolf in the woods. Here was the wolf standing ankle-deep in the sea, hands clasped behind his back, watching his men work with the patience of a man watching a clock.

*Go home*, Tam told himself. The sensible part of him, the part that sounded like his mother, said it plainly: *You've seen enough. Go home and tell them what you saw and let the grown men do the rest.*

But the chalk was in his pocket. And the sloop was right there, grounded, helpless, her port side tilted toward the shingle close enough to touch.

He told himself the story one more time, the good version: he would creep down the far side of the cove along the rocks, reach the boat while the men were busy at the cart track, put one small white mark low on her hull where the sea would half-hide it, and be back up the cliff path before anyone was the wiser. One mark. One small, brave, permanent thing in a winter full of things that wouldn't stay put. His mother rationing flour until her knuckles went white on the scoop. The Kestrel children crying at supper because there was nothing to cry *with*. Mr. Ashgrove's face when he talked about the logbooks, careful as a man carrying water in both hands.

One mark. For all of them.

Tam went down the rocks.

* * *

The rocks were kind to him, which should have warned him.

They gave him handholds and footholds worn smooth by a hundred years of fishermen, and they hid him from the beach, and they delivered him to the sloop's shadow without so much as scraping his knee. Up close the hull rose over him like a barnacled wall, weeping cold, smelling of tar and bilge and something sweeter underneath — grain gone musty in a closed hold. He put his mittened hand flat against the planking just to feel it real.

The port side sloped away from him into the shallows, and there, exactly where Juniper had said, ran the waterline strake, bared by the ebb, green-slimed and reachable. Tam knelt on a shelf of rock, dug the chalk from his pocket, and pulled off his right mitten with his teeth. His bare hand ached instantly in the cold, the fingers going stiff and stupid, but he could hold chalk better bare. He reached out and drew — quickly, before his nerve went — a single vertical stroke, then a second crossing it. An X, small as a coin, white against the weed-dark wood.

It seemed too small. All that terror, and this was what it came to — two strokes of chalk a gull could have made. But Juniper had said marks don't need to be big, they need to be *findable*. Tam pressed harder and dragged the strokes longer, and the chalk squeaked against the wet wood, a thin sound that seemed enormously loud in the quiet cove—

"Boy."

The voice came from above him. From the deck. From *directly overhead*.

Tam's whole body went to ice. Slowly — there was nothing fast left in him — he tipped his head back. A sailor leaned on the rail six feet above his head, a stranger with a week's beard and eyes that had already moved past Tam's face to the chalk in his bare hand, and then beyond, to the fresh white X gleaming on the hull.

"Well," the sailor said, not loudly. "Look at that."

* * *

Afterward, Tam would remember strange details and lose important ones. He remembered that the sailor's hands were cracked at the knuckles. He remembered a barrel stave lying in the shingle shaped like a crooked letter J. What he could never reconstruct, however hard he tried, was the moment between the rock and the beach — how he got from one to the other, whether he walked or was carried or simply fell downhill in pieces. He only knew that suddenly he was standing on the shingle in front of all four men plus the one from the rail, and Captain Ellery Voss was looking at him with an expression of mild, courteous interest, as though Tam were a curiosity brought to him on a tray.

"The Hallow boy." Voss said it softly, almost warmly. "Maren's son."

Every eye on the beach was on him. Brandt had gone a color Tam had never seen on a living man, gray-white like fish flesh, and the barrel he'd been rolling had come to rest against his boot. Tam's chalk hand — still bare, still holding the stub — felt like it belonged to somebody else.

"I was—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated himself. "I was looking at the boat."

"Yes," Voss said pleasantly. "You were looking very closely at the boat. With chalk." He came closer, boots crunching on the shingle, and held out one gloved hand. Not grabbing. Offering, the way you'd offer to take a coat from a guest. "May I?"

Some animal instinct told Tam to throw the chalk into the sea. His arm didn't obey. Fourteen winters of being an obedient boy on a small island, four months of soldiers and rations and the slow grinding lesson that obedience kept families fed — all of it lived in his arm, and his arm opened, and the chalk crossed the space between them in a little white arc, and Voss caught it out of the air like a man catching an apple tossed at a fair.

Voss turned it over once between his fingers. Then, without hurry, he walked to the waterline and looked at the X. He studied it for a long moment, head tilted, and Tam understood with sick clarity that the captain was admiring it. Judging it. Finding it, perhaps, adequate.

"A marksman's habit," Voss said, to no one in particular. "Surveyors do it. Naval men do it. Mark your position so you can report it later." He looked back at Tam, and his smile was the worst thing Tam had ever seen, because it was genuinely kind. "Who taught you that, I wonder? Who taught a farmer's boy to identify and mark a vessel?"

Tam said nothing. Saying nothing was the hardest work he'd ever done.

"No matter." Voss dropped the chalk into the sea. It made no sound at all, and somehow that was worse than if it had. "Here's what will happen now. Nothing. Tonight, nothing. Tomorrow, nothing. You will go home to your mother, and you will eat your supper, and you will sleep in your bed, and you will say nothing to anyone — not your mother, not the keeper, not the gulls." He stepped in close, and this near, Tam could smell bay rum and lamp oil on him, civilized smells, and see the fine stitching on his collar, and understand that the menace wasn't in any of it. The menace was in the mildness. "Because here is the arithmetic, boy, and I'll do it slowly so you can follow. Your mother draws rations for two. If anything — *anything* — reaches my ears about marked boats, or ledgers, or questions asked in kitchens, the Hallow house draws rations for none. Not less. None. There are families on this island who will tell you what none looks like by spring, if you'd like a reference."

Behind Voss, Brandt made a small sound, like a man swallowing broken glass. Voss didn't turn.

"Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Tam whispered.

"Louder. The sea's noisy."

"Yes, sir."

"There's a good lad." And Voss smiled again, warm as a hearth, and clapped Tam on the shoulder with a gloved hand that weighed like an anvil. "Run home now. Supper will be getting cold. And Tam — thank you for showing me the boat. She is a fine old girl, isn't she? We'll miss her when she goes."

* * *

He didn't run. Running would have meant breaking apart. He climbed the cliff path one deliberate step at a time, and the whole way up, the arithmetic rode on his back like a second boy: *two rations, zero rations, two, zero.* His mother had gone down half a stone since autumn feeding him first. He'd watched her do it — watched her ladle the barley soup and keep the pot tilted so the thick part came to his bowl, and he'd pretended not to see, because pretending was how they loved each other, quietly, with lies. And now he had handed Voss the lever and shown him exactly where it fitted.

At the top of the path he stopped and looked back down. The cove was emptying. The barrels were aboard; the sloop's patched canvas was waking; the men were small figures moving in the dusk. And the X — his X, the one brave true thing he'd done all winter — sat on her hull in plain view, and Voss had *seen* it, and Voss had let it stand.

That was the part that finally broke something open in Tam's chest. Not the capture. Not the threat. It was the understanding, arriving cold as the tide, that the mark didn't matter anymore. Voss had let him make it because a marked boat could be repainted overnight, and a frightened boy could not be unfrightened, and a threatened family was worth more than ten marks. Tam had brought a chalk stub to a war, and the enemy had picked it out of his hand, admired it, and thrown it away.

He was halfway across the top field, the Hallows' kitchen light a smudge of gold through the sea fret, when he made his decision. It didn't feel like courage. It felt like falling.

He couldn't tell his mother. Telling her was telling her the danger, and she would either march to the garrison — Maren Hallow did not scare, that was the terrifying truth of her — or she would look at him the way she'd looked at his father's empty chair all those years ago, and he could not survive that look. So he would tell the other one. The keeper. The man who measured everything, who wrote everything down, who had asked a boy to carry a piece of chalk into the dark without ever once saying *this is safe*, because it wasn't, and he'd known it wasn't, and he'd asked anyway.

Juniper would know what to do with it. Juniper put things in books where they became heavier and safer at the same time.

But even as he turned toward the cliff path that led to the lighthouse, even as he set his frozen feet moving, Tam heard Voss's voice again, mild as milk: *anything reaches my ears.* The boy had run straight from the cove to the lighthouse — of course he had; everyone knew the keeper's light burned all night; everyone knew where the keeper's sympathies lay, even if no one said it. Voss would know within the day. Maybe within the hour.

Maybe the warning was already the trap, and this — this walk, this telling, this hope — was the second half of it.

Tam stopped at the fork in the path, the village below him on one side, the lighthouse beam swinging its patient white arm above him on the other, and stood in the wind with his empty chalk hand opening and closing, choosing between the two people he could not bear to fail — and knowing that whichever door he knocked on first, someone he loved was going to pay the bill.
