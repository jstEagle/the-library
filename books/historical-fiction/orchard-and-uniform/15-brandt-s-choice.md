## 15. Brandt's Choice

The manifests were kept in the bottom drawer of Voss's field desk, beneath a false bottom of oiled canvas, and Sergeant Aldous Brandt had known about that drawer for eleven months without ever once opening it. That was the arithmetic of his life on Merrow's Reach: what he knew and what he did not permit himself to know, balanced like weights on either side of a scale that had long since stopped moving. He was a careful man. Careful men survived. He had survived Balaclava's aftermath, two fever seasons in the tropics, and a wife who had died waiting for him in Portsmouth, and he had done it by keeping his boots dry and his eyes forward and his hands clean of anything that could not be explained at muster.

Eleven months of grain going out of the cove by night. Eleven months of ration ledgers that said bread had been issued to families whose children he watched go thin as gulls. Eleven months of Captain Ellery Voss smiling at him across the mess table, calling him *the backbone of this post*, Brandt, absolutely the backbone, while the backbone did nothing but hold up the rot.

He opened the drawer on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before dawn, because Tam Hallow had looked at him.

That was all it took, in the end. Not the hunger, not the ledgers, not even the boy's father — no, the boy had no father, it was Maren Hallow and her brother's son, the Hallows of the orchard rows — not any grand outrage. Just the boy, fourteen years old and trying to be brave about it, standing in the garrison yard three weeks past with frostbite whitening his fingertips, looking at Brandt the way soldiers look at officers they have decided will not help them. Brandt had seen that look before. He had worn that look himself, once, as a private under a captain who sold the regiment's winter blankets downriver and called it logistics.

*You are the man they have decided will not help them,* he had told himself that night, lying in his bunk listening to the wind worry the shutters. *And you have decided to be him.*

So on Tuesday, in the grey hour, he opened the drawer.

* * *

The manifests were beautiful work. That was the thing that undid him most, he thought afterward — not the theft itself but the craftsmanship of it. Voss kept two sets. The official ledger recorded grain received from the mainland stores, weighed, inspected, issued to the village against signed chits. The second set — thinner paper, finer hand, Voss's own writing — recorded the same tonnage received and then *disposed*, with dates and quantities and initials, consigned to a broker named Callum Reeve out of Wexmouth who paid, if Brandt's eye for figures still served him, something north of four hundred pounds across the season. Four hundred pounds while the island ate nettle soup and boiled orchard apples and called it supper.

There were escort details listed. His own name appeared nine times. *Sgt. A. Brandt, cove watch.* Nine nights he had stood at the waterline with a lantern shuttered, watching crates go down the shingle into Reeve's boat, telling himself each time that it must be sanctioned somewhere above Voss, some quartermaster's arrangement, some necessity of the blockade season. He had been so diligent about not knowing. He had built a fortress of not-knowing with walls thick enough to sleep behind.

He read until the sky went from grey to pearl, then put the papers back exactly as they lay, oiled canvas folded the same way, drawer closed to the same finger's width, and went out into the yard where the sentry — young Pell, nineteen, chilblains on his ears — touched his cap and asked after his health, and Brandt said *fine, thank you, Pell* in the voice of a man whose health was fine, and walked the wall walk with his heart going like a drum under orders.

They would hang him for this. Not for the theft — for the knowing. If Voss ever suspected that Brandt had seen the second ledger, Brandt would not face trial; he would face an accident on the cliff path, a misstep in the surf, a fever. Voss was not a man who left witnesses. The island was full of graves and short on stones that told the truth about how their tenants had come to them.

But the boy had looked at him. And somewhere below the wall, in the village, children were eating boiled apples for the third meal of the day, and calling it porridge so the little ones wouldn't cry.

* * *

He waited six days. He told himself the waiting was prudence — choosing the hour, the route, the pretext — but he knew it was cowardice wearing prudence's coat. Each morning he rose intending to act and each evening he found a reason the morrow would serve better. On the fourth day Voss dined ashore at the Hallows' and came back smiling, and asked Brandt at mess, pleasantly, over the mutton, whether anyone on the island had been *curious about the cove lately*, and Brandt heard his own voice answer *not that I've heard, sir*, steady as a parade-ground reply, and understood that he had crossed some line without noticing — that he was now a liar in service of a thief, which was worse than being merely a thief, because at least a thief stole for himself.

On the sixth night there was weather coming. He felt it in his knee, the old one, the one the surgeons in '81 had promised would tell him true about storms better than any barometer. The pressure sat on the island like a thumb. The garrison battened down early, and Voss took his brandy to the map room and closed the door, and Brandt walked out of the fort through the sally gate with his greatcoat collar up and the manifests — he had copied the essential pages in his own hand over three nights, burning each draft page in the stove of the guardroom, feeding the fire sheet by sheet like a man feeding a dangerous animal — folded flat inside his shirt, against his chest, where they warmed slowly to the temperature of his body until he could not tell which was beating.

The cliff path to the lighthouse took forty minutes in fair weather. In the rising wind it took an hour, and he spent the whole of it composing and discarding speeches. What did one say? *I've brought you evidence, keeper, though I helped make the crime it proves.* He was not delivering truth; he was delivering his own confession, bound in another man's handwriting. Every step uphill was a step away from the army he had served twenty-two years, and toward — what? An old widower with a lamp and a logbook. A village that owed him nothing. A gallows, possibly, or whatever Voss used instead of gallows when gallows drew questions.

At the turn above the cove he stopped and looked back. The fort showed one light at the map-room window. Below, the village showed none — dark by ordinance, oil rationed — except a single candle glow in an upper window of the Hallow farmhouse, the kind of light a woman keeps lit for someone expected home. The sea beyond was black and beginning to talk to itself in long, deep syllables. Somewhere out there, in weather like this, boats had made that crossing all season with stolen bread aboard, and men had been paid, and boys had gone hungry, and he had held the lantern.

He climbed the rest of the way.

* * *

Juniper Ashgrove opened the lighthouse door before Brandt finished knocking. Perhaps he had seen the lantern coming up the path; perhaps the old keeper simply heard footsteps the way he heard everything, with that patient attention of a man who lived alone with machinery and had learned to listen to the smallest sounds. He stood aside without a word and let Brandt in, and closed the door against the wind, and the sudden quiet rang in Brandt's ears like the silence after guns.

The lamp turned overhead, steady, its beam sweeping round and round through the glass panes, laying bands of light across the stairwell and lifting them away again. Brandt had never been inside the tower. It smelled of oil and brass polish and, faintly, of salt soaked so deep into the stone that no amount of scrubbing would ever find it.

"Sergeant." Juniper's voice was quiet, unhurried. "You'll want tea. Sit by the stove."

"I've not come for tea."

"No," the keeper said. "I don't imagine you have. But you're wet through, and whatever you've carried up that hill, it'll keep five minutes while you warm."

It was such a small kindness, so plainly given without calculation, that Brandt nearly lost his nerve entirely. He sat. He drank the tea, strong and bitter, holding the mug in both hands because they shook and he didn't want the keeper to see it — though of course the keeper saw everything; that was evidently his profession, seeing things and writing them down honestly, which was more than Brandt had managed in eleven months.

Then he took the folded pages from inside his shirt and laid them on the table between them, and the lamp's turning beam swept over them, bright, then shadow, then bright.

"Shipping manifests," Brandt said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Copied in my hand from Captain Voss's private ledger. Grain received from mainland stores, grain sold to a broker in Wexmouth — a man called Reeve — with dates, tonnages, and payment amounts covering the whole season. The official ration ledger shows the same grain issued to your village. It wasn't. It was sold. Four hundred pounds and more, and your people eating apple broth." He made himself go on. "My name is in it nine times. Cove watch. I stood the watch myself. I want that understood — I'm not bringing you a stranger's crime. I'm bringing you mine."

Juniper did not touch the pages at first. He looked at Brandt the way he might look at a chart, reading depths. Then he drew the lamplight closer — a small brass lamp on the table, independent of the great lens above — and began, page by page, to read. He read the way he did everything, Brandt realized: thoroughly, precisely, missing nothing. When he reached the entries with Brandt's own initials he paused, and glanced up, and there was no triumph in his face and no contempt either. Only a kind of grave acknowledgment, one drowning man nodding to another.

"You understand what happens to you now," Juniper said. "If Voss learns these left his desk."

"I've thought of little else since Tuesday."

"And you brought them anyway."

"The alternative," Brandt said, "was to keep being the man that boy looked at in the yard. The one who won't help." He turned the mug in his hands. "I've a daughter buried in Portsmouth churchyard. She'd be near Tam Hallow's age. I keep thinking — I know it's foolish, I know the dead don't watch us — but I keep thinking she'd ask me why I stood nine nights at that cove holding a lantern for stolen bread, and I haven't got an answer that fits in my mouth."

The keeper was quiet a moment. The beam swept the room, and swept, and returned.

"There's a historian coming in spring," Juniper said at last. "To write the official account. From Voss's reports alone, unless there's something else for him to read. These—" he rested his hand flat on the copied pages, "—are something else. With my logbook and the villagers' testimony, this is a record no one can burn. You've done the hardest part, Sergeant. The rest is keeping it safe until spring."

"Keeping it safe," Brandt repeated. He almost laughed. "Keeper, I've kept things safe for twenty-two years. Rations. Ammunition. Other men's secrets." He met the old man's eyes. "This is the first time I'll have kept something safe worth the name."

* * *

He was back inside the walls before midnight, through the sally gate with the sentry changed and no one the wiser, and for two days nothing happened, and he permitted himself the fatal luxury of believing that nothing would.

On the third day, Voss sent for him.

It was midmorning, grey and close, the storm-pressure heavier than ever — his knee sang with it, a dull hot wire of pain — and the captain's orderly led him down the corridor to the map room where Voss stood at the window with his back turned, hands clasped behind him, the picture of command contemplating the sea. Two other men stood against the far wall. Corporal Finch and Private Dole. Brandt had seen them used before, on a drunk gunner last autumn who'd talked too loudly in Wexmouth's hearing about what went down the cove. They had escorted the man to the mainland on a disciplinary transfer. No one had ever heard from him again.

"Sergeant Brandt." Voss turned. He was smiling — Voss smiled often, easily, a smile like polished brass, decorative and cold to the touch. "Walk with me to the wall, would you."

They walked. Below the parapet the sea worked at the rocks, green and black. The village smoke rose thin and straight in the dead air.

"Curious weather coming," Voss said conversationally. "The barometer's falling like a stone. Might be the worst of the season. I do hope our people are prepared." He stopped at the wall's corner, out of earshot of the sentries, and his voice dropped into a register Brandt had heard only twice before, both times just before men disappeared. "Now. Someone entered my quarters on the sixth. The lock was forced — clumsily, but forced — sometime between the hours of two and five. And I am given to understand that certain documents no longer lie where they lay." He studied Brandt with mild, terrible interest. "You were on the rota for those hours, Sergeant. Yours was the only name on it."

Brandt's mouth had gone dry as gunpowder. Twenty-two years of drill held his spine straight and his face blank, and underneath, where no one could see, everything fell with a long slow rush, like scree down a cliff face. The lock. He had locked the drawer; he had not thought of the *lock*. He had thought himself so careful, and he had left a broken lock behind him like footprints in snow.

"I reported all secure that night, sir," he heard himself say. "I made no entry to your quarters."

"Mm," said Voss. "No. Of course not." The smile widened a fraction. "I believe you, Aldous. Truly. You've been the backbone of this post." He put one hand briefly, almost affectionately, on Brandt's shoulder — and squeezed, once, hard enough to mean it. "Which is why it grieves me that I must now search every man's effects, starting with the senior men. Tonight. Thoroughly. And should anything... irregular... surface, well." He released the shoulder and stepped back, and his voice lifted to parade brightness. "Discipline is the soul of armies. Dismissed, Sergeant."

Brandt saluted. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. He turned and walked the length of the parapet on legs that belonged to someone else, and at the stairhead he looked back once. Voss remained at the wall, watching him, smiling, making no attempt to hide it.

The search would begin tonight. The copies were hidden — he would not say where, even to himself, even in his own head; he had buried them off the cliff path beneath a cairn of beach stones only he could find again — but the originals, the true manifests, still lay in Voss's drawer, and Voss would move them, or destroy them, or produce them falsely, and the record Juniper meant to carry into spring would be gutted before the first thaw.

Unless there was something else. Unless the historian arrived to find not one ledger but many — the keeper's logbook, the villagers' testimony, the sworn words of a sergeant of the garrison who had seen the second ledger with his own eyes and could recite its contents from memory, dates and tonnages and payments, because he had spent three nights copying them and a lifetime wishing he hadn't.

They would arrest him tonight. He understood that now with perfect clarity. Voss's search would find something — planted if necessary; Finch and Dole were not ornamental — and Brandt would be transferred to the mainland for discipline, or would suffer an accident on the cliff path in the coming storm, and either way his voice would be sealed before it ever testified.

So it would have to testify first.

* * *

He went down to the village in the last light, openly, along the cliff road, in full view of the sentries — a sergeant stretching his legs before bad weather, nothing remarkable — and he knocked at the Hallow farmhouse door, and when Maren Hallow opened it he removed his cap, which he had not done indoors or out before any civilian in years, because it seemed suddenly important to stand before her uncovered.

"Sergeant," she said. Her eyes went past him to the darkening road, wary; she had reason enough. Behind her, at the table, Tam looked up from mending a net, and Brandt saw the boy's shoulders square, braced.

"Mrs. Hallow. I've not much time, and less right to ask what I'm about to ask. There's a search tonight at the fort — of my effects among others — and after it, I expect I'll be leaving this island in a manner I won't choose." He said it evenly, the way he'd report a position overrun. "Before that happens, I need your household to carry word to Keeper Ashgrove. Tell him the original manifests stay in Voss's possession, but I have the contents entire, here—" he touched his temple, "—dates, tonnages, sums, the broker's name, all of it, and I'll swear to every line under any oath they care to administer, whenever and wherever there's authority left on this island to hear me. Tell him to keep his book hidden and his lamp lit, and to trust no soldier who comes to him claiming I sent them — because after tonight, no one will be able to claim that honestly but me."

Maren Hallow looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face, it settled something, because she stepped back from the doorway and said, "You'd best come in and say that once more slow, Sergeant. And you'll take soup. Don't argue — you'll want your strength, and I'll not send a man to what's coming on an empty stomach."

It was, absurdly, the first food anyone had offered him on Merrow's Reach in eleven months. He sat at their table among the smell of apples and woodsmoke and ate soup while a fourteen-year-old boy memorized his message with the fierce concentration of a soldier receiving orders, and he thought: *this is what we stole from. This table.* He finished every drop.

The arrest came an hour later, at the top of the village lane, in the first spits of the storm. Four men, Finch and Dole among them, lanterns swinging, and Voss's written order in Finch's gloved hand: *Sergeant A. Brandt relieved of duty pending inquiry, to be confined aboard the garrison cutter.* They did not strike him. They did not need to; the order was the blow, and they all knew it. But as they took his arms, Brandt looked past them down the darkening street to where a small figure stood at the edge of the lamplight — Tam, breathless from running, having come the whole way rather than trust the message to memory alone — and Brandt gave the boy a single nod, slow and deliberate, the nod of one soldier confirming to another that the order stands.

Then they walked him down toward the cove, into the teeth of the rising wind, and behind him the first rain came slanting off the sea, and far above on the headland the lighthouse beam swung round and round through the murk, sweeping, sweeping, holding its patient circle over the island — over the fort, over the village, over the cove where they put him in a boat — as if the light itself were counting everything, and would forget nothing, and was waiting to see what the morning brought.
