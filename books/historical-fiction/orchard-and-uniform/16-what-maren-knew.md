## 16. What Maren Knew

The confession had been waiting in her chest since October, and it came out the way an abscess breaks — sudden, foul, and with a relief that frightened her more than the sickness ever had.

Maren Hallow said it standing at her own kitchen table, hands still damp from kneading the morning's barley bread, flour ghosting her knuckles white as frost. Juniper Ashgrove sat across from her where he'd been sitting for three days now, sleeping in Tam's old cot by the fire since the soldiers turned the lighthouse inside out looking for a ledger they hadn't found. His hands were wrapped around a cup of chicory he hadn't touched.

"I've known since the harvest," she said. "Since before you wrote your first hidden page."

Juniper looked up. In the grey light through the east window his face was all angles and exhaustion, the face of a man who had spent a winter carrying something heavy and was only now being told someone else had been carrying its twin.

"Say that again," he said quietly.

"The grain ledger." She wiped her hands on her apron because she could not bear to say the next part with flour on them; it was a foolish vanity and she knew it, and she did it anyway. "I keep the village ration book. You know I do. Every head of household, every measure issued, every hand it passed through. And in October — the second week, when Voss requisitioned the harvest stores — I copied the figures into my own book before I entered them in the official one. The same way you did. Because numbers lie more convincingly than people do, and I wanted a copy that couldn't be amended."

Juniper set down the untouched cup. "You never said."

"No," Maren said. "I didn't."

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, the wind was working itself up again out of the southwest, the way it had been doing for a week now, worrying the shutters like a dog with a bone. Somewhere down the lane a garrison boot crunched on frozen mud, paused outside their gate, and moved on. They had all learned to hear boots the way sailors hear weather.

"Why?" Juniper asked. It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. He asked it the way he trimmed wicks — carefully, precisely, wanting the truth of it.

Maren sat down across from him because her knees told her to and pride could wait.

"Because of Tam," she said. "Because Tam went up to the garrison in November with a petition in his pocket and came home with a sergeant's spit on his collar and a look in his eyes I'd never seen on a child's face before. Because after that, every time I thought about speaking — every time I stood in the council meeting with the ledger figures burning a hole in my apron pocket — I did the arithmetic. Not grain arithmetic. Mother arithmetic. One voice raised against Voss costs what? A family cut off rations entirely. And which family would he make an example of first? The widow who farms the orchard and keeps the books and has one son, fourteen years old, who has already once been caught too close to the cove." She spread her hands flat on the table. "I did that sum every night, Juniper. And every night it came out the same. My silence bought Tam his supper. That's what I told myself. That's what I *told* myself."

"And now?"

"Now Brandt is gone," she said. "Arrested by his own men for handing you those manifests. Now soldiers have torn apart your home and found nothing, which means Voss knows the evidence is somewhere he can't reach, which means he'll stop searching houses and start searching people. Now the storm season is turning, and I can smell the big one coming the way the horses can. And now—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she let it catch anyway because some things deserved the sound of breaking. "Now a man has lost everything trying to do what I was too much of a coward to do. Sergeant Brandt chose his conscience over his oath knowing what it would cost him. And I have been choosing my son over thirty starving families and calling it love."

"It is love," Juniper said.

"It's love with a blindfold on," Maren said. "And I'm taking the blindfold off tonight, before the weather takes the choice away from both of us."

* * *

She went to the dresser in the corner — the good oak one that had come across from the mainland with her grandmother — and pulled open the bottom drawer, beneath the folded linens, and lifted out a false bottom that Tam had made for her two winters ago with a chisel and a great deal of sawdust and a smugness he'd inherited honestly.

The notebook underneath was smaller than Juniper's hidden ledger, bound in oiled cloth against damp, and she set it on the table between them like something recovered from a wreck.

Juniper didn't touch it at first. He looked at it the way he must have looked at his wife's trunk, Maren thought — at the packed belongings he'd measured all autumn and never opened. With reverence and dread in equal parts.

"May I?"

"It's why I'm showing you."

He unwrapped the oilcloth the way he handled the lamp machinery: slow, exact, nothing wasted. Inside were pages of Maren's small, upright hand — ration figures, dates, the names of households, and beside each entry in a second column of ink, the discrepancy. What was recorded as issued against what actually reached a table. Three months of it. October, November, December. The gap between the official record and the empty bellies of Merrow's Reach, written down week by week in a farmwife's neat script while she ladled thin soup and told herself she was protecting her son.

"Here." Juniper's finger stopped on a page. "December the ninth. The garrison drew forty bushels for 'road repair crews.'"

"There are no road repair crews," Maren said. "There haven't been since the bridge closed. There's no road left to repair. But the requisition is signed, countersigned, and entered in the garrison ledger — which means on paper, forty bushels of barley went to men mending a road that doesn't exist. On paper, everything balances. That's the trick of it. Voss doesn't steal anything. He just feeds the island less than the island is owed, and the difference walks down to the cove at night in unlit boats."

"Forty bushels," Juniper murmured. He was flipping forward now, faster. "November the third — sixty. And here, the flour marked 'spoiled by damp' and written off—"

"Eleven hundredweight," Maren said. "Perfectly good flour. I saw the sacks myself, stacked dry as bones in the garrison storehouse when they had me in to inventory the infirmary linen in October. Damp, indeed."

Juniper closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them there was something in his face Maren hadn't seen there before — not the careful keeper's blankness she'd learned to read past over the years, but something rawer. Shame, she realized. Plain and human and terrible.

"All winter," he said. "All winter I thought I was the only one carrying this. I sat in that lantern room copying pages by candlelight feeling like — like the last honest man on a dishonest rock. And you were three fields away with your own ledger and your own nights of deciding, and neither of us said a word to the other because we were each too busy guarding our own fear."

"Aye," Maren said. "That's about the shape of it."

"We could have—"

"What?" she asked, gently, because she genuinely wanted to know. "What could we have done in November, together, that we can't do now? Gone to Voss? He'd have eaten us and called it dinner. Gone to the council? Half of them have sons or brothers in the garrison payroll one way or another, and the other half would have talked, and talk travels to the cove faster than boats do. No, Juniper. Two ledgers kept apart kept each other safe. If they'd found yours, mine survived. If they'd found mine, yours burned on. It was cowardice and it was wisdom and I have honestly never been able to tell which was which." She almost smiled. "Perhaps that's what courage is. Cowardice that keeps working anyway."

Juniper laughed — a short, startled sound, half grief, and then he put his head down on his crossed arms on the kitchen table, right beside her grandmother's notebook, and shook. Maren rose, put her hand on the back of his neck the way she'd done for Tam through fevers and nightmares, and stood there while the lighthouse keeper of Merrow's Reach wept as silently as he did everything else, into his own sleeves, in a farm kitchen that smelled of bread and lamp oil.

Neither of them heard Tam come in. But they heard the door, and the scrape of the boy stopping dead on the flagstones, and the particular quality of silence a fourteen-year-old produces when he understands he has walked into something private.

Maren lifted her hand. Juniper straightened, scrubbed his face once with both palms, and became — mostly — himself again.

"Mr. Ashgrove's staying till the weather clears," Maren said, which was true, and insufficient, and the best she could manage with her son watching her with those sharp Hallows eyes.

Tam nodded slowly. Then he crossed the room, took down the third bowl from the shelf without being asked, filled it from the pot, and set it in front of Juniper the way you feed a working animal or family — no ceremony, no comment. He sat down with his own bowl and began to eat.

It was, Maren thought, the most eloquent thing anyone had said in that kitchen all winter.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the barometer fell and the light thickened toward storm.

At the scrubbed pine table, under the one window, they laid the two records side by side: Juniper's hidden ledger, dense with dates and observations — the unlit sloop at the cove on the fourteenth of October, the night landing on the second of November, the crates he'd counted through the gallery glass, the manifests Brandt had smuggled to him in December, the shipping marks of mainland brokers — and Maren's ration book with its merciless second column. His was testimony of movement. Hers was testimony of absence. Together they made a single account, and the account balanced in the worst possible way: every bushel missing from a village table accounted for, bushel by bushel, boat by boat, in the private ledgers of Captain Ellery Voss.

"Read me the ninth of December again," Maren said, copying as Juniper dictated, transcribing his entries into the back of her book so that each record would carry a copy of the other. If one was seized, the other survived. If one burned, the other testified.

"'Ninth December. Observed from gallery, 0240 hours, six men unloading the cove, lanterns shuttered. Cargo consistent with grain sacks, approximately forty bushels by count of trips. Boat returned seaward before dawn. No entry in garrison log.'" He paused. "And your column for that date?"

"Forty bushels drawn for road repair crews," Maren said, writing. "Signed E.V., countersigned by Lieutenant Harrow, whose brother-in-law, I might add, owns a warehouse in Wexmouth."

"Wexmouth," Juniper repeated softly. "Brandt's manifests named Wexmouth twice."

"Then we have the boats, the cargo, the dates, the signatures, the destination, and the buyer," Maren said. "What we don't have is a court."

"The inquiry historian comes with the spring tide," Juniper said. "If the bridge opens."

"*When* it opens," Maren corrected. "Bridges always open. Storms always end. That's the whole arrangement of the world, whatever it feels like in February." She dipped her pen. "The question isn't whether spring comes. The question is whether we're still holding these books when it does, and whether either of us is still free to speak."

Juniper was quiet a moment. Through the window the sea had gone the colour of pewter, and the first real gusts were beginning to lean on the orchard's bare trees, making them bow and shudder like a congregation at prayer.

"Maren," he said. "There's something you should know about where my ledger lives now."

She looked up.

"After the search. After they went through the lantern room with bayonets and took apart my desk and read my official log line by line looking for discrepancies." His mouth twitched. "I moved it. Into the lens housing. Into the clockwork carriage, behind the fresnel panels, where the mechanism turns day and night whether I tend it or not. The most closely guarded thing on this island is the light itself, and the truth is sitting inside it, going round and round."

Maren stared at him. Then she laughed — really laughed, for the first time since autumn — a rough, startled bark of a laugh that made Tam look up from his woodcarving by the fire.

"You hid it in the *lamp*?"

"Where else?" Juniper said, and for a moment the weariness fell off him entirely, and she saw the man he must have been before the widowhood, before the island, some younger self who had once found the world funny. "Voss can search every house and hold every door. But he cannot take the light off the rock without killing ships, and even Voss needs the ships alive. As long as Merrow's Reach has a lighthouse, Merrow's Reach has a witness."

"And if the great storm comes," Maren said, quieter now, because the window rattled as if in answer, "and the mechanism jams, or the housing floods, or the glass goes—"

"Then I'll carry the book out of it with my own hands or go down explaining to my Maker why I was late," Juniper said. "But it stays in the light until spring. It belongs there. Everything else I ever tried to carry, I put down eventually. This one I mean to deliver."

Maren looked at him across the table — at the flour still on his sleeve where he'd leaned against her bread board, at the ink on his fingers, at the stubbornness that she recognized because she saw it in her own mirror every morning — and she understood suddenly, completely, what her silence had cost her all these months. Not just the risk. The company. She had kept her knowledge alone in the dark, and alone in the dark, knowledge rots into fear. Shared, lit, set turning in a lens with a stubborn man to tend it, the same knowledge became something else entirely.

"Partners, then," she said, and held out her ink-stained hand across the table.

Juniper took it. His grip was a keeper's grip, calloused and sure.

"Partners," he agreed.

From the fireside, without looking up from his carving, Tam spoke for the first time in an hour, in the flat, deliberate tone of a boy who has decided to say a thing and will not be deflected from it.

"When you testify," he said, "in the spring. When they ask who kept the records. You say both names. Hers and yours. Both."

Maren's throat tightened.

"Tam—"

"You stayed quiet for me," the boy said, setting down his knife and meeting her eyes with a directness that undid her more thoroughly than any accusation could have. "Fine. That was your choice and I'm not ungrateful. But when the telling happens, I'm not going to be a secret you protect anymore. I'm fourteen, Mum. Old enough to carry sacks. Old enough to be threatened by sergeants. Old enough to stand up in a flooded hall and say what I saw at the cove with my own eyes." He picked the knife back up. "Both names. Or I'll say mine myself, and embarrass you both."

The wind chose that moment to hit the house properly — a long, groaning shove against the north wall that made the shutters jump in their frames and sent the fire flaring. Out beyond the orchard, the sea was getting its voice up.

Juniper rose and went to the window, and Maren watched his face as he read the horizon the way only a lifelong keeper reads it.

"That's not a squall building," he said. "That's a season changing. By tomorrow night we'll know whether this is the one." He turned back to them, and the lamplight made his eyes bright and steady. "Whatever comes — the books stay separate until it passes, then we merge the last pages together. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Maren said.

Outside, the first hard rain began to fall, and somewhere far off across the darkening water, she thought she heard — or imagined, or perhaps did not imagine at all — the deep, grinding complaint of the sea getting ready to break something.
