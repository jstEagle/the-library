## 17. The Raid on the Lighthouse

The warning came by way of a child.

Juniper was oiling the clockwork when he saw her come up the cliff path — a small figure in a red shawl, running with the particular urgency of a child who has been told to run and is frightened of what will happen if she stops. He knew the shawl. It belonged to the Pellow family down in the Rows; the girl wearing it was the youngest of four, a solemn creature named Bets who had once left a jar of honeycomb on his doorstep without a word.

She did not stop at the door this time. She hammered on it until he opened it, breathless, cheeks whipped pink by the wind.

"Maren says," she gasped. "Maren says the soldiers are coming up the hill. Six of them. With Sergeant Hale leading. And they've got a barrow."

A barrow. Tools, then. Or something to carry away.

Juniper crouched so that his eyes were level with hers. "Did Maren say anything else?"

Bets screwed up her face, dragging the words back out of memory the way one drags a bucket from a deep well. "She said — *tell him it's time to trim the wick.*"

He felt the words settle into him like a stone dropping through water. Trim the wick. A keeper's phrase, an innocent phrase — and from Maren Hallow, who had sat across his supper table for three months now and learned to speak in the language of lamps, nothing innocent at all. She was not telling him to tend his light. She was telling him the searchers were close, that there would be no second messenger, that whatever he meant to do with the truth he had been hoarding page by page since autumn, he must do it now.

"Thank you, Bets," he said. "Run home the low way, mind the ice on the steps."

She went. Juniper stood in the doorway a moment longer, watching the path below, where — yes — six figures in garrison blue had already begun the climb, moving at the unhurried pace of men who knew exactly how much time they had and needed none of it to spare.

* * *

The ledger lived behind the third board in the stairwell, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged in the gap between joists where the salt air had long since eaten everything else soft. Juniper had chosen the spot in October because it seemed safe: dry, dark, unremarkable, reachable only if a man knelt and knew which board to lift. Three months of copying entries into it — dates, tonnages, tide tables, the sloop's landings at the cove, Brandt's half-drunk confession transcribed the morning after while his hands still shook — had made the hiding place feel less like concealment than like a grave he tended.

But graves could be opened. Boards could be pried. Six men with a barrow and a sergeant's orders could turn the lighthouse inside out in an hour.

He took the stairs two at a time.

The lantern room smelled of brass polish and burnt oil and, faintly, of the sea itself, for the glass never entirely kept the storm out. The great lens stood at the center of the room like the heart of some patient animal — a beehive of cut crystal and bronze, eight feet tall, its panels ground and angled so that a single flame became a beam visible twenty miles out. Juniper had tended this machinery for eleven years. He knew its weight, its temper, its tolerances. He knew that inside the pedestal beneath the lens assembly there was a void — a hollow in the cast iron base where the counterweights ran, a space no larger than a loaf of bread, accessible only through a panel that required a special key, and only when the rotating carriage was locked in its maintenance position.

No soldier would look there. No soldier, looking, would understand what he saw. The interior of a lens housing was to them what the interior of a church was to a mackerel — a place of awe and total ignorance.

But first the panel had to come off, and the carriage locked, and all of it done before boots reached the gallery door.

His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady. That surprised him. He would have expected them to shake as they had shaken the night after Brandt's confession. Instead they moved with the calm of long habit, and he understood, kneeling there on the iron floor with the wind moaning past the glass above him, that this was simply another form of the work. For eleven years he had kept the light burning against the dark. Now the dark had come up the hill wearing uniforms, and the work was the same work. Hold steady. Keep the mechanism true. Do not let the flame go out.

He locked the carriage. He fit the key to the access panel — a key he kept on the same ring as the lamp keys, disguised among them, meaningless to any man who didn't know — and swung the little door open onto the shadowed hollow where the weights descended.

The ledger came up from the stairwell wrapped in its oilcloth. Too bulky. He stripped the cloth away and considered the book itself — two hundred pages of his own cramped hand, the whole rotten season of Merrow's Reach bound in gray boards. It was too wide for the slot between the guide rails. Of course it was. Everything important was always slightly too large for the space allotted to it.

He thought of Maren's kitchen knife, the one she used to halve apples for Tam, and fetched it from his coat, and cut the binding free, and split the book into three sections. Each section slid flat into the hollow — one beneath the counterweight track, one against the far wall, one tucked into the gap where the grease channel ran. Then he closed the panel, turned the key, wiped every fingerprint of oil from the bronze, and unlocked the carriage so the lens would turn again as though nothing in the world had changed.

From below, faint but distinct, came the sound of boots on the rock stairs.

* * *

They knocked the way soldiers knock — not asking permission, announcing intent. Juniper counted five seconds, made himself walk to the door rather than hurry, and opened it.

Sergeant Hale stood in front, a broad man with a mustache like a wet brush and eyes that never quite settled on the thing they looked at. Behind him, six — no, seven; Bets had miscounted, or one had hung back — soldiers in blue, breath steaming, stamping the cold from their boots on his threshold without being invited.

"Keeper Ashgrove." Hale produced a paper with the flourish of a man performing ceremony. "By order of Captain Ellery Voss, commanding officer, garrison of Merrow's Reach, this structure is to be searched for contraband goods and unauthorized documents. You'll stand aside and permit us entry. You'll answer all questions put to you. Resistance will be noted in the official record."

"The record," Juniper said. The word tasted strange in his mouth, like a coin held too long under the tongue. "Yes, Sergeant. I know something about records."

Hale's eyes flicked up to his face, held there a moment. Whatever he found there gave him nothing to hold. "Stand aside, Keeper."

"I'll stand anywhere you like." Juniper stepped back into his own doorway. "Mind the oil on the second landing. It's slick in cold weather, and I'd hate for any of your men to break his neck carrying out my duties for me."

The search began at the bottom, methodical as erosion. They went through the storeroom first — turning over barrels of oil, probing the grain sacks Juniper kept for his own rationing, tapping walls with the flats of their bayonets. They went through his bedroom: pulled the quilt off the bed, ran fingers along the mattress seam, emptied the drawer that held Ellen's things — the hairbrush, the folded letters, the small tin photograph case — and scattered them across the blanket with the careless thoroughness of men searching a house they believed empty of meaning.

Juniper watched a corporal hold Ellen's hairbrush up to the light, frown at it as though suspecting it of sedition, and set it down bristles-up in the dust.

Something in his chest tightened and held. He let it tighten. He let it hold. There would be time after.

"You keep a neat station, Keeper," Hale said, appearing at his shoulder in the parlor. Two soldiers were prying at the floorboards near the hearth. "Neater than most. Almost as though a man were practicing for visitors."

"I keep a lighthouse, Sergeant. Neatness is the work. The sea doesn't forgive slovenly habits, and neither does the Board."

"No." Hale picked up the current logbook from its stand on the desk — the public book, the honest book, filled with weather and lamp-hours and the routine of tending. He leafed through it slowly, reading perhaps one line in ten. "You write a fair hand. Detailed. Tide tables, wind speeds, the comings and goings of boats." His thumb stopped on a page. "You logged a vessel in the cove on the night of the ninth of November. Unlit. Off schedule."

"I log everything that moves on that water. It's the duty. If I failed to log a vessel and she struck the rocks, the inquiry would ask why the light hadn't reported her."

"And did you report her? To the garrison?"

"To the garrison?" Juniper let the question hang a beat, mild as milk. "Sergeant, the sloop was flying no colors, carried no papers, and landed cargo at night in a cove used by no legitimate trader. Who at the garrison would I have reported her *to?*"

For a moment — a single, suspended moment — Juniper watched Hale's face do arithmetic. The sergeant was not clever, but he was not stupid either, and somewhere behind the wet-brush mustache a sum was being attempted: the keeper's calm, the phrasing of the question, the trap laid so neatly inside a courtesy. The sum refused to resolve. Soldiers are trained to obey orders, not to solve them.

"Captain Voss will want to see this logbook," Hale said at last, and tucked it under his arm.

"Then you'll want a receipt for it," Juniper said. "It's Board property. Take it without paper and you're a thief, Sergeant, and I'll say so in my quarterly report, which goes to Trinity House, which goes to the Admiralty, which — I'm given to understand — outranks Captain Voss by a considerable margin."

The silence stretched. Down in the lantern room overhead, the clockwork ticked on, and the great lens turned its patient circles whether anyone watched or not.

Hale wrote out the receipt.

* * *

They saved the lantern room for last. Juniper understood why: it was the least likely place, in their minds. A lighthouse keeper might hide grain in his flour bin and papers under his floorboards, but no one hid anything in the light itself. The light was the one part of the island that belonged to everyone — to every captain who ever read it from a pitching deck, every widow who scanned it from a headland waiting for a boat that might not come. It was sacred in the dumb, practical way that useful things are sacred, and even Voss's men felt the edge of that sanctity without knowing they felt it.

They climbed the stairs in a clatter of boots and emerged blinking into the glass-walled room, squinting against the lamp's steady blaze. The lens turned above them, throwing its slow white blade across the water, around, and around, and around.

"Careful," Juniper said, from the doorway, not moving. "That apparatus cost more than this entire island. Touch the crystal with a bare hand and you'll pit the glass. Touch the carriage while it turns and you'll lose the arm. I don't care whose authority brought you here — I answer to the Board for that machine, and I'll write down the name of any man who damages it."

The soldiers hesitated. Men who would shove an old woman into the mud for a dropped ration card paused before a piece of brass and glass as though before an altar rail. One of them actually removed his cap.

"Search it anyway," Hale said. But his voice had lost some of its ceremony.

They circled the lens at a respectful distance, peering into the pedestal's outer housing, tapping the bronze skin of the base. Juniper stood with his arms folded and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs, and let none of it show on his face. The panel was shut. The key was hidden among twelve identical keys. The pages lay flat in the dark beside the counterweights, inches from iron that swung and groaned as the carriage turned, guarded by the very mechanism that swept the sea with its light.

One young soldier — a boy, really, no older than Tam — leaned close to the crystal and flinched as the beam swept across his face. "It's like it's watching," he said, half-laughing, uneasy.

"It is watching," Juniper said quietly. "Every night. Every hour of every night, for eleven years. It has seen ships saved and ships lost and men doing things on this water they hoped the dark would keep. It forgets nothing, because it isn't a thing that can forget. It only shines, and what it shines on is seen."

Nobody answered him. The boy stepped back from the lens as if from a stranger who knows too much.

Hale stood a long moment in the center of the room, the confiscated logbook under his arm, looking at the machinery, at the keeper, at the sea beyond the glass where the beam marched out across the black water and vanished. Juniper met his eyes and offered him nothing — no guilt, no fear, no flicker of the wild drumming joy and terror that filled him to the fingertips. Just the flat professional patience of a man who had stood in this room through hurricanes.

"Nothing here," Hale said finally.

They clattered back down the stairs. Juniper heard the barrow wheels grinding away down the cliff path, heard Hale's voice giving some order that the wind shredded before it arrived, and then heard nothing but the sea and the tick of the clockwork and the soft continuous rush of the flame.

He waited a full quarter-hour. Then he climbed to the lantern room, fitted the key, and opened the panel, and put his hand flat against the pages in the dark, feeling the texture of them, the slight damp chill of iron and salt, the solid fact of them existing still.

They had taken his logbook. Let them. Let Voss read every honest line of weather and lamp-hours in it and find nothing but a diligent servant of the Board. The true record — the sloop, the crates, the manifests Brandt had died — no. Not died; had been arrested for — the true record lay inside the light itself, where no order signed by Ellery Voss could reach it without reaching through twenty miles of shining.

He closed the panel and stood up, and that was when he saw the light below.

Not the lamp's own beam — a smaller light, climbing the cliff road toward the village. Lantern-light, moving fast, and with it, faint on the wind, a sound he had heard only twice before in his life and never wanted to hear again: the tolling of the garrison bell rung hard and urgent, alarm-fashion, over and over, as though the whole night had caught fire.

Down in the village, doors were opening. Figures were spilling into the street. And beyond the harbor mouth, out on the black water where the beam swept and swept, the barometer's needle — Juniper had checked it an hour ago and not dared believe it — had fallen further than it had fallen in living memory.

The worst storm of the age was coming down on Merrow's Reach.

And somewhere out in it, he would learn within the hour, was a ship full of soldiers sent to carry Voss's secrets safely to the mainland.
