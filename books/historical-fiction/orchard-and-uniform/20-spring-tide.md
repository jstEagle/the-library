## 20. Spring Tide

The ice went out of the channel on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the sound of it could be heard from the lantern room — a long groaning, like a door opening in the floor of the world. Juniper stood at the gallery rail with his coat collar turned up against the wind and watched the floes shift and tilt and break apart in the grey water, each one carrying a rim of white light at its edge. Below, the tidal bridge showed its spine for the first time in six months: black stones emerging from the retreating tide-line, weed-hung, gull-marked, but standing.

Standing. He said the word to himself the way he checked a wick — twice, and then once more for honesty.

Six months. He had kept count not in days but in oil. Forty-one tins burned since the bridge closed, forty-one entries in the logbook that lived openly on his desk, and beside them the second ledger — the true one — still sleeping inside the lens housing, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged among the brass prisms where no hand but his had ever reached. He had not opened the housing since the night of the raid in February. He had not needed to. But every evening when he lit the lamp he felt the weight of it turning with the clockwork above him, the truth revolving through the dark like a second, slower flame.

The first boat came on Friday. Not a cutter, not a gunwale of uniforms — a fishing smack out of Pellow's Harbour, its skipper a red-faced man named Codd who had run the crossing twenty years and had wept openly, so the village said, when word came that the Reach was sealed. Now he stood at the foot of the lighthouse path with his cap in his hands, looking up at Juniper as though the tower were a relation returned from the dead.

"Thought you'd be taller," Codd said.

"Everyone does." Juniper came down the last steps. The wind off the water smelled different now — salt and mud and green things rotting into growth instead of salt and iron and nothing. "You've post?"

"Three sacks of it. And flour. And a crate marked glass, which I'm told is windows for your garrison hall, though what's left of the garrison to put them in is anyone's guess." Codd hesitated, and something moved behind his weathered face that Juniper had learned to read on this island the way one reads weather over water. "There's men coming behind me, Keeper. Not soldiers. Gentlemen. From the mainland inquiry."

"I know," Juniper said. "I wrote asking for them in January."

"Aye. Well." Codd put his cap back on, jammed down hard, and looked out at the bridge, at the water running clean between its piers for the first time since autumn. "They'll want to talk to you first, I expect. Being the light and all. Being—" he gestured vaguely at the whole island, the village, the orchards, the drowned fields just beginning to show their winter damage, "—being everything, I suppose."

"They can talk to whoever they like," Juniper said. "In whatever order they like. The truth doesn't keep a queue."

Codd laughed at that, short and surprised, and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and went down the path whistling.

* * *

The gentlemen arrived four days later on the mail steamer *Alderney*, and Juniper watched them come ashore from the gallery because watching arrivals had been his trade for thirty years and he saw no reason to stop now that the arrivals finally mattered. There were three of them. Two wore good coats and carried leather satchels and stepped carefully across the wet stones of the landing as if the island might lie about its footing as readily as its people were supposed to have lied about its grain. The third was younger, ink-stained, carrying a portable writing board under one arm like a shield held sideways.

The historian. Juniper felt his stomach tighten, an old rope drawing taut. Chapter fourteen of the winter, the letter that had changed everything: *a mainland historian will arrive in spring to compile the official account of the season.* All winter that sentence had hung over the Reach like the storm-glass falling. Voss's reports were already written, already filed, already glowing with the commander's efficiency and the garrison's sacrifice. Whatever account the man compiled would begin from those reports unless someone stood in front of him with something heavier than memory.

Juniper had spent five months making sure that someone would be.

He went down to the village that evening. The Hallows' kitchen was full — full in a way it had not been permitted to be all winter, full with people who had walked across the island through the thaw-mud because word travels faster than boats and everyone knew the steamer had landed gentlemen with satchels. Maren Hallow sat by the fire with Tam beside her, the boy grown half a head taller since autumn and thinner than Juniper liked, though the flour from Codd's boat had already begun to repair what the ration ledger had undone. Old Piety Ross was there, and the ferryman's widow, and a dozen others pressed along the walls, and when Juniper came in dripping, the talk stopped — not the hostile silence of suspicion but the held breath of people waiting to see which way a heavy thing will fall.

"They'll start tomorrow," Juniper said. "At the garrison hall, I'd guess, or the chapel if the roof held better there. They'll want statements. Sworn ones, written down, signed."

"And what do we say?" asked the ferryman's widow, whose husband had died in November of what the garrison doctor had recorded, with a straight face, as *seasonal decline*.

"The truth," Maren said quietly. She looked up at Juniper, and between them passed the whole of the winter — the ledger she had found wrong in October, the silence she had kept to protect her son, the night in February when she had brought her own reckoning books to the lighthouse and spread them beside his hidden copy and the two records had matched line for line like two halves of a broken plate. "All of it. Even the parts that shame us. Especially those."

Piety Ross grunted. "Some of us stayed quiet too long."

"Some of us did," Juniper agreed. "And the historian will write that down too, if we give it to him honestly. Better it comes from our mouths than from Voss's reports."

"Where *is* Voss?" Tam asked. It was the question under all the others, and the boy asked it the way boys ask things — directly, without the cushioning adults wrap around dread.

"Garrison hall," Juniper said. "Under arrest, by his own adjutant, since the night the supply ship went down. He dines alone now, I'm told. Though I hear he's stopped dining altogether." He paused. "Brandt takes him his meals."

That name moved through the room like a draft. Sergeant Brandt — drunk in October, confessing at the lighthouse door; arrested in February for bringing Juniper the shipping manifests; escaped during the chaos of the wreck in March and vanished into the flooded lanes of the village like water finding a crack. No one had given him up. Not even when rations were threatened. That particular threat had died the night Voss's authority drowned in the garrison hall, and everyone in this room had helped drown it.

"He'll testify?" the ferryman's widow asked.

"He'll testify," Juniper said. "I've spoken with him. He wants it. Wants it more than pardon, I think. Some men need the record to show they ended on the right side of it, even if they started on the wrong one." He looked around the kitchen, at the faces firelit and wary and tired beyond telling. "Tomorrow, speak plainly. Don't dress it up and don't leave anything out. We've kept this island alive on exact measures all winter. Let's not fail the truth by rounding."

Maren almost smiled. Almost. "Go home, Keeper. You'll want sleep. Tomorrow you'll be giving away five months of your life, and you'll want both hands free."

* * *

They took his statement in the garrison hall, which Juniper thought was fitting in a way he could not have explained to anyone without sounding cruel. The hall had flooded to the third stair in March; the tide-line still showed as a brown hem along the wainscoting, and the smell of soaked plaster and old smoke had not left the place. The long table where Voss had once dined visiting officers now bore an ink blotter, a decanter of water, and the historian's writing board. Rain ticked against windows that had not yet received their new glass.

The historian was called Mr. Aldous Renwick, and he was younger than Juniper had expected, and more careful. He did not open with grand questions. He opened with small ones.

"Your name for the record."

"Juniper Ashgrove. Keeper of the Merrow's Reach light, nineteen years this spring."

"You kept the light through the whole season of closure."

"Every night. One hundred and seventy-one nights the lamp burned. I can show you the log."

"I should like that very much." Renwick dipped his pen. "Begin anywhere you like, Mr. Ashgrove. Begin where it began for you."

So Juniper began where it began — with the unlit sloop at the cove in October, riding dark on a night when any honest vessel would have shown lights, and the entry he had made in his log, and the hesitation afterward that lasted eleven minutes by the clock and cost him more sleep than any storm of the following winter. He gave Renwick the dates. He gave him the tide tables, because smugglers choose their tides and the tides never lie. He described Tam's errand in November and the sergeant's threats, and he watched the young man's pen falter slightly when he described a fourteen-year-old being backed against a crate wall by a grown soldier, and Juniper did not soften it, because softening was how histories like Voss's got written in the first place.

Then Brandt was brought in — escorted, pale, sober-looking in a way that seemed to cost him physical effort — and gave the manifests, and the dates matched, and the tonnage matched, and the names of the mainland brokers matched the accounts Maren had traced backward from the ration shortfall. Then Maren herself, calm as a millpond, reading aloud from her own household book while the two senior commissioners compared it against the garrison's requisition figures and grew quieter with every line. Then Piety Ross, and the widow, and the ferryman's cousin, and a dozen voices of the village, one after another, each one laying a stone into the causeway.

It took three days. On the second day, Juniper retrieved the second ledger from the lens housing in full view of the commissioners — he had offered to do it privately, and Renwick had said, with a small fierce smile, "No, Mr. Ashgrove. If it has lived in your light all winter, let us see it in daylight." And so they climbed the hundred and twelve steps together, all of them, and Juniper opened the housing and drew out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped it before them, and the pages — copied fair through December nights, cross-referenced in January, indexed in February — lay open in the cold clean air of the lantern room with the sea going silver and blue beyond the glass.

Renwick touched the cover with one finger, gently, as if it might be hot. "You understood," he said, "that this might be the only record to survive."

"I understood that reports are written by victors," Juniper said, "and that I didn't intend to find out in spring who the victors were."

* * *

They took Voss away on the ninth of April, aboard the steam frigate *Wexford*, under guard, in a plain coat because they had taken his uniform with the rest of his effects. Juniper watched from the gallery again — he seemed fated to watch departures from this rail — and the strange thing, the thing he would carry longer than the relief, was how small the man looked on the quarterdeck. Captain Ellery Voss, who had requisitioned a harvest with a signature and starved an island with arithmetic and nearly buried the truth of it under a historian's respectful prose, stood at the rail with his hands folded behind him like a passenger, like a man going somewhere ordinary.

Tam stood beside Juniper at the rail. The boy had insisted on climbing the tower, and Juniper had let him, because some things a person needs to see from high ground.

"Will they hang him?" Tam asked.

"Probably not," Juniper said. "Prison, likely. Years of it. Trial first, though — a real one, on the mainland, with all our statements read aloud." He considered. "Which means everything we said gets said again, in front of strangers, and this time nobody can call us liars, because it's all written down in three hands and witnessed."

Tam was quiet a while. Below, the launch pulled away from the frigate's side, and the frigate's engines began to turn the grey water white.

"I used to think he was grand," Tam said finally. "When he first came. The uniform and all. I thought that's what important looked like."

"It's what *costume* looks like," Juniper said. "Important looks more like your mother's ledgers. Slower. Heavier. You have to carry it every day or it stops being true."

Tam glanced up at him with that quick, unsparing look children have, the one that weighs a man and finds his true displacement. "You carried it all winter."

"Somebody had to hold the light steady," Juniper said. "That's the whole job. Everything else is trimming the wick."

* * *

The inquiry commissioners left on the last day of April, and the morning they sailed, the island did something Juniper had not seen in nineteen years of keeping the light: it turned out. Not in celebration — there was no band, no bunting, no speeches, and thank God for it — but the whole population of the village stood along the shore road as the steamer took them off, silent, hats off, watching until the hull shrank to a smudge and then to nothing. A witness, Juniper thought. That's what we are, all of us, standing witness to our own testimony going out into the world. It was the strangest kind of vindication: not a shout but a hush, not triumph but the settling feeling of a load set down exactly where it was meant to go.

Afterward, Maren found him on the path below the tower. She had flour on her sleeves — the first real bread in months had gone into the ovens that morning, and the smell of it still hung over the village like a benediction — and she walked beside him in silence for a while, the two of them matching strides without discussing it, the way they had learned to do everything that winter.

"You'll stay," she said. Not a question. She had stopped framing it as a question sometime in March.

"I'll stay," Juniper said. "There's a season of maintenance overdue on the lens. And Tam wants to learn the clockwork. And—" he looked out at the bridge, running clear, the tide moving through it like blood returning to a limb, "—and it seems a poor time to leave a light just when people have remembered why it matters."

"Mm." Maren looked straight ahead, at the orchard rows just breaking bud, the pink-white haze of blossom starting at the sheltered end near the wall. "There's a room over the kitchen. Has been since February, if you're wondering how long. I aired it weekly."

Juniper stopped walking.

She stopped too and faced him, and her expression was the one she wore for ledgers and for truths — level, patient, immovable. "You buried your wife's trunk in March," she said. "Unopened. I helped you dig. Nobody buries a trunk like that unless they've decided the past stays past. And nobody stands witness the way you stood unless they mean to belong somewhere." She brushed the flour from one sleeve, briskly, as if the matter were settled by housekeeping. "Supper's at six. Bring nothing. You've carried enough."

She walked on toward the village. Juniper stood a moment in the wind with his heart doing something unmeasured and imprecise, and then he laughed — a short, startled sound the gulls answered — and followed her down toward the smell of bread.

Behind him the tower rose white against the clearing sky, and inside it, on his desk, the logbook lay open to a fresh page, waiting for the evening's first line.
