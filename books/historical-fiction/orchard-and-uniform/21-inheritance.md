## 21. Inheritance

The spring came to Merrow's Reach the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

Maren Hallow stood at the edge of the orchard on a morning so clear it seemed the winter had been someone else's story. The rows of apple trees were waking; pale buds clustered along the black branches like small held breaths. Below, the sea had gone from slate to a blue that hurt to look at, and the tidal bridge — open again these three weeks — carried carts and voices and the ordinary commerce of an island remembering itself. Somewhere down in the village a hammer was knocking. Someone was rebuilding what the storms had taken. Everyone was rebuilding something.

She had come out early to count the trees. It was a habit from her father's time, this counting — not because the number ever changed, but because counting them meant believing they would still be there when autumn came. Forty-one trees in the long row, twelve in the crooked row by the wall, and the old king tree at the center that her grandfather had planted and her father had grafted and she herself had nearly lost twice to blight and once to lightning. She counted them now with Tam's help, or rather with Tam's company, for he had stopped helping and started climbing, perched in the lowest fork of the king tree with his boots swinging, watching her work with the particular patience of a boy who has been through a winter that aged him.

"Forty-one," Maren said, marking her slate. "Twelve."

"Thirteen," Tam said. "You always forget the one behind the wall."

"I don't forget it. I don't count it. It's half dead."

"It's half alive." He said it with such simple stubbornness that she laughed, and the laugh felt strange in her chest, like a door opening into a room she'd shut in November.

They worked in silence after that, or near enough. The island was full of silences still, but they had changed their character. In January the silences had been the silence of hunger, of ration lines, of neighbors too proud to ask and too honest to pretend. Now the silences were only the ordinary quiet of people getting on with things. The garrison hall had been scrubbed. The inquiry men had come and gone, then come again, filling their ledgers with statements taken by lamplight. Captain Ellery Voss sat in a cell on the mainland awaiting trial, and Sergeant Brandt — thin as a winter reed but walking — had been seen twice at the churchyard, standing a long while before the graves of the two sailors who hadn't been pulled from the water.

And Juniper Ashgrove was still at the lighthouse. That was the thing Maren turned over every morning like a stone in her pocket. He could have gone. When the bridge opened, when the first ferry crossed with its cargo of officials and news and mainland bread, everyone had assumed the keeper would take his leave — pack up his wife's trunk, collect whatever pension the Trinity men allowed him, and go back to wherever widowers went to be lonely in comfort. Instead he had trimmed the lamp, wound the clockwork, swept the gallery stairs, and stayed. As if the light were simply a duty he had not yet been relieved of. As if he did not know — or would not let himself know — that no one was going to relieve him of anything, and that the choice of staying was his alone to make.

It was Tam who said it first, in March, in the low voice he'd learned to use that winter, the voice of a boy who understood that some truths are dangerous until enough people agree to carry them.

"Mum. Why doesn't Mr. Ashgrove just live here?"

She had been mending a net by the fire. She had not answered right away. Some questions you answer with words, and some you answer with a season's worth of thinking, and this one had taken her the better part of six weeks.

* * *

The supper she made that evening was not a feast, because feasts were for occasions and Maren had decided — in the way she decided things, quietly and completely — that this should not feel like an occasion. Occasions put weight on a man. This needed to feel like Tuesday.

So: fish chowder, the way her mother had made it, heavy on the potatoes because the potato stores had held. Brown bread. A jar of the damson preserve she'd hoarded all winter against exactly this kind of need, though she couldn't have said what kind of need until now. And cider from the barrel at the back of the cellar, the last of the pressing, saved through the hungry months when drinking it would have been an extravagance the village council would have rightly frowned upon.

Tam laid the table without being asked. He set four places, then looked at the fourth place, then looked at his mother, and took the fourth plate away again and put it back in the cupboard — but carefully, she noticed, where it could be reached.

Juniper came down from the light at dusk, as he had come perhaps thirty times since the storms ended, drawn sometimes by an errand, more often by nothing he could name. He arrived the way he did everything: precisely, apologetically, carrying something. Tonight it was a sack of lamp oil rations returned to the communal store — the garrison's requisitions had been reversed, the stores redistributed, and Juniper had apparently spent a week personally returning to each household what had been taken from it, keeping accounts in that small neat hand of his, leaving nothing unrecorded.

"You'll have kept the whole island's books by summer," Maren said, taking the sack.

"Somebody has to know where things stand," he said. "Otherwise we're only guessing."

"And you can't abide guessing."

"No," he admitted. "I can't."

He ate the way he did everything else — thoroughly, gratefully, without waste — and told them about the lighthouse: the new glass ordered from the mainland, arriving on the June boat; the Fresnel lens cleaned and sound; the logbook for the storm year complete and copied, the original already in the hands of the inquiry, the copy — the hidden ledger, the one that had lived inside the lens housing through the raid — bound in oilcloth and stored now in a dry drawer, though Maren suspected he would never quite stop sleeping beside it. He spoke of these things the way other men spoke of weather. But underneath, Maren heard what she had been listening for all spring: the flatness going out of his voice. A man talking about next month. A man making plans that assumed a future.

When the plates were cleared and Tam had been sent — over loud protest — to see to the hens, Maren poured the cider and set the cup in front of Juniper and sat down across from him at the scrubbed table, in the lamplight, and folded her hands, because this part required formality even if the supper had not.

"Juniper," she said. "I want to talk to you about the orchard."

Something flickered across his face — the wariness of a man who has learned, over one long terrible winter, that conversations which begin calmly do not always stay that way.

"The orchard is sound," he said carefully. "The trees came through."

"The trees came through. The orchard didn't." She held up a hand before he could argue. "I'm not speaking of blight. I'm speaking of hands. My father ran this orchard with two hired men and me. The hired men are gone — one dead, one gone to the mainland and not coming back. I have Tam, who is fourteen and grows a hand's width every month but is still one boy. I have forty-one trees in the long row and twelve in the crooked row and the king tree, and every one of them wants pruning in a way I cannot do alone, and grafting in the spring after, and picking in the autumn, and carrying, and pressing, and storing. An orchard is not land, Juniper. It's a marriage between land and labor, and I am short one partner."

He was very still. She watched him understand, watch him refuse to understand, watch him understand again — the same sequence she'd seen on his face the night he'd shown her the hidden ledger, page after page of dates and tonnages and cove-landings in his precise hand, evidence gathered alone in the dark because he had believed, until that night, that he was alone.

"You're offering me work," he said at last.

"I'm offering you a share."

"A share of the harvest."

"A share of the orchard." She let the word sit between them. "A sixth part. Written fair and witnessed by the council, same as any inheritance. Not wages — wages end. A share. Your name in the deed alongside mine and Tam's."

"Maren." His voice had gone rough. "I'm a lighthouse keeper. I know oil and wicks and the turning of tides. I don't know the first thing about apples."

"You know how to keep a schedule. You know how to keep records. You know how to do the same necessary thing every day whether anyone thanks you or not, which is nine-tenths of orchard work and all of it, most winters." She leaned forward. "And you know how to stay. That's the rarest knowledge on this island, Juniper. Half the young ones leave the first chance they get, and I don't blame them. But staying — staying through a bad season, staying through a bad *year*, staying when staying costs you — that's not a skill you learn. It's a nature. And I've watched yours all winter."

He looked down at his cup. The cider trembled slightly in it, and she saw that his hand was not steady, and she looked away to give him the dignity of the moment, out the window toward the darkening orchard where the king tree stood like a patient elder among its children.

"There's more," she said gently. "There's the room."

"Maren—"

"Hear me out. The east room. Tam's grandmother's, before she passed. It faces the orchard and gets the morning sun, and it's been shut up two years and needs a coat of paint and a man's decision about whether the bureau stays or goes, and I'm done deciding everything in this house alone." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she pressed on. "I'm not asking you to stop being the keeper. The light needs you and the island knows it and God knows it. Keep the light. But a keeper walks down from his tower at the end of his watch, and a keeper ought to have somewhere to walk *to*. You've eaten at this table thirty times since the thaw. You've fixed our gate and re-hung our door and taught my son to read the weather off the water. You've been family since November, Juniper Ashgrove. All I'm proposing is that we say it out loud and write it down, because I know you, and you won't believe a thing until it's written down."

The silence stretched. Out in the yard, Tam's voice rose in argument with a hen, and the sound was so ordinary, so absurdly domestic, that Maren saw Juniper's eyes go bright and had to look hard at the table.

"My wife packed a trunk," he said finally.

Maren waited.

"Before she died. Before — the last winter. She packed a trunk of her things. The good shawl, the letters, the little mirror her mother gave her. Things she wanted carried, if the house was ever left." His thumb moved along the rim of the cup. "I've carried that trunk eleven years. Three houses, two postings, and then here. I've measured it into every room I've ever had. I've never opened it. I keep thinking there will be a right time to open it, and there's never been a right time, and I begin to think the trunk isn't for opening. It's for carrying. It's the last thing I know how to carry."

"What will you do with it?" Maren asked, though she thought she already knew, because she had watched him all winter, watched him lay down one weight after another — the guilt, the secrecy, the solitary burden of the ledger — and stand straighter each time.

"I'd like to bury it," Juniper said. "If that's permitted. Under the king tree, maybe. Where the roots are." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and clear at once, the way the sea looks after a storm has finished with it. "Not throwing her away. Never that. Just — setting down what was never mine to keep carrying. She'd have liked your orchard. She grew things. She'd have said a tree was a better grave-goods than a cupboard."

Maren found she could not speak for a moment. She reached across the table and put her hand over his, and his hand turned under hers and gripped, hard, like a drowning man gripping a rope that turns out to be solid ground.

"Saturday," she said. "We'll do it Saturday morning. Tam can dig — he'll be honored, though he'll complain. And afterward there'll be chowder, and it won't be an occasion, and you'll eat too much, and that's the whole of the ceremony. Is that agreed?"

"That's agreed," Juniper said, and his voice broke on the second word, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

Saturday came in mild and gold, with a wind out of the southwest that smelled of open water and turned earth.

The whole of it was smaller than Maren had expected and larger too. Word travels on an island the way water finds cracks, and by midmorning there were a dozen people at the orchard wall — not a crowd, nothing so formal as a gathering, just neighbors with reasons to be passing: old Mrs. Tregale with her basket, the ferryman Cobb leaning on the gate, two of the councilmen who had signed the petition last winter and never spoken of it since, and Sergeant Brandt, standing apart at the edge of the trees in civilian clothes that fit him badly, holding his cap in both hands as though it were the only thing anchoring him to the ground.

Tam dug. He dug well, complaining magnificently, and the hole went down through the dark loam to the clay beneath, and Juniper knelt at its edge with the trunk between his knees. It was a modest thing — oak, brass corners gone green, a leather strap worn to a shine by eleven years of one man's grip. He did not open it. He ran his palm along the top once, the way you'd touch the shoulder of someone sleeping, and said something so quiet that only Maren, standing nearest, caught the shape of it: *Home now, love. I found the place.*

Then he lowered it into the earth, and Tam filled it in, and Maren pressed the soil firm with her own hands, and old Mrs. Tregale, who had buried a husband and two sons and knew the office, spoke the few words that needed speaking — not scripture, just the plain blessing of one grower over the roots of a tree: *"What's given to the ground is not lost. It feeds what comes after."*

And then, because Maren had promised no ceremony and intended to keep worse promises than that, they went in to chowder.

But at the door, Juniper stopped. He turned back to the orchard — to the long row and the crooked row and the king tree with its fresh-turned earth at its feet — and then to the people scattered through his yard, his yard, theirs — and Maren watched him try to find the words for it and fail, and watched him give up trying, which for Juniper Ashgrove was surely the deepest fluency of all.

"The deed reading is Thursday," Maren said into the pause, practical as ever. "Council chamber, seven o'clock. Wear something that isn't covered in lamp oil."

"I'll wear my good coat," he said. "Elsie made it. It's in the trunk."

The laughter that went around the yard was gentle and surprised him into joining it, and Maren saw the moment land — saw him realize he had made a joke about Elsie, about grief, about the buried and the living, and that the world had received it kindly. Belonging, she thought, was not a door that opened once. It was a muscle. And his had been wasted a long time, and would ache now, being used.

That night, long after Tam slept and the lamps were out, Maren stood at her window looking down at the orchard. The moon laid the tree rows out silver and shadow, and at the foot of the king tree the new earth showed as a darker patch, already settling, already becoming ground. Above and beyond it, on the black headland, the lighthouse turned its steady wheel of light across the water — one, two, three, and dark, and one, two, three — the rhythm she had fallen asleep to for twenty years and would fall asleep to for twenty more, though now, when she counted the flashes, she would be counting for a man who was hers: her partner, her boarder, her brother-in-all-but-ink.

Thursday, the deed. And after Thursday—

She was still at the window when she saw the lantern coming up the lane. A small bobbing light, moving fast, and behind it the unmistakable urgent knock of Tam at her door, breathless, half-dressed, a letter crushed in his fist.

"Mum — Mum, wake up — there's a boat just come across from the mainland — the ferryman brought post out of hours — Mum, it's the historian. The one from the inquiry. He's here *early* — and he's asking for Mr. Ashgrove. He's asking for him *tonight*."
