## 22. The Record Stands

The parcel came on the first cutter of the new season, wrapped in brown paper and tied with the kind of string that frays at a touch, and Juniper Ashgrove carried it up the hundred and nine steps of the tower without opening it.

He had learned, over the years, to let things arrive before he decided what they were. Oil arrived in casks and became light. Grief had arrived unbidden and become, eventually, a habit of the hands — the way they still reached for Maren's sister-in-law's name when he meant Maren Hallow's, the way they still set out two cups some mornings until he caught himself and laughed, quietly, at the old arithmetic. The parcel was heavy in the way paper is heavy: not much, and everything.

He set it on the chart table in the lantern room, where the morning sun came through the glass in long amber panes and lay across the brass like poured honey. Below him the island of Merrow's Reach turned through its autumn business — carts on the cliff road, gulls arguing over the fish sheds, the orchard rows on the south slope gone the color of struck flint. A year ago those same rows had been stripped bare by requisition, the trees themselves notched by soldiers' hatchets for firewood while the village counted its children's ribs. Now there were apples again. Not many. Enough.

He cut the string with his trimming knife.

Inside was a book. *The Merrow's Reach Inquiry: An Official Account of the Winter of 1893–94*, printed in London, its spine stiff with new glue, its pages smelling of ink and machine heat. On the title page, beneath the imprimatur of the Board of Trade, a line he had read four times already since the historian's clerk had sent word it was coming, and which he read a fifth time now as though it might have changed overnight:

*Compiled from sworn testimony, shipping manifests, and the keeper's log of the Merrow's Reach Light.*

His log. His hand. In an official book, in London, where anyone who wanted to know could know.

Juniper sat down slowly on the stool by the chart table, the way a man sits when he has been standing watch too long and only just permits himself to feel it.

* * *

Tam Hallow came up the tower stairs two at a time, because Tam did everything two at a time, and burst into the lantern room with his collar askew and his breath smoking in the cold.

"Maren says if you're reading it without us she'll—" He stopped. He saw the book open on the table, and whatever threat he'd carried up the stairs died in his throat. "It came."

"It came."

"Is it—"

"Sit down, Tam."

The boy dragged the second stool over. He was fifteen now, taller than he knew what to do with, all elbows and sudden silences where the chatter used to run straight through. His hands, when he folded them on the table, were a working man's hands already — rope-scarred, nail-bitten at the edges. Juniper had watched those hands mark a smuggler's hull with chalk in the dark of a cove, watched what that chalk had cost, watched the boy carry the guilt of it like ballast all winter. The hands looked different now. Steadier. Or perhaps it was only that Juniper had learned to see.

"Read from the beginning," Tam said. "The proper beginning."

"The proper beginning is a list of dignitaries and a preface about methodology."

"I want the proper beginning."

Juniper turned past the dignitaries. Outside, the light behind them stood cold and clean, its great lens sleeping in its daytime shroud, waiting for dusk the way it always waited — patiently, without opinion. He found the page where the preface ended and the account began, and he cleared his throat, and he read aloud.

"*In the autumn of 1893, following storm damage to the tidal bridge, the island of Merrow's Reach was placed under the provisional authority of Captain Ellery Voss of the Coastal Garrison.*"

He paused. Even now, even a year on, the name landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"*During the months of the island's isolation, official returns recorded the full issue of grain rations to the civilian population. This account will demonstrate that no such grain reached the village tables; that between November 1893 and February 1894, no fewer than eleven shipments of requisitioned stores were landed at night at Marrow Cove and transferred to private vessels bound for mainland brokers; and that these operations were conducted under the direct order and personal profit of Captain Voss.*"

Tam was very still. Juniper glanced at him and saw the boy's jaw working, the way it did when he was holding something down.

"Keep going," Tam said. "The part about the lighthouse."

"There are several parts about the lighthouse."

"I know. I've read the bits you let me see. I want to hear how they wrote it."

Juniper turned pages. The afternoon light shifted along the brass rail. He read:

"*That the truth of these months survived at all is owing to a chain of ordinary persons who declined, each in their own season, to look away. To Sergeant Aldous Brandt of the garrison, who surrendered the shipping manifests at the cost of his liberty. To Mrs. Maren Hallow of the orchard keepers, whose private accounting of the ration ledger exposed the discrepancy the official returns concealed. And to Mr. Juniper Ashgrove, keeper of the Merrow's Reach Light, whose daily log — kept, in his own words, 'because a record is a thing that can be carried' — preserved against every attempt at suppression the plain account of what this island endured.*"

Juniper stopped. His voice had gone somewhere on the last sentence and he had to find it again before he could go on.

"That's you," Tam said, unnecessarily.

"That's a lot of people."

"It's you." The boy leaned over and tapped the page with one scarred finger, right on the printed name, as though to make the ink admit it. "*Juniper Ashgrove*. In a real book. My grandchildren are going to be sick to death of hearing about you."

"You're fifteen."

"I'm planning ahead."

Juniper huffed something that was almost a laugh, and turned the page, and read on — through the testimony of the shipwrecked sailors, through Brandt's deposition taken in the flooded garrison hall with seawater still running across the floorboards, through the paragraph that described, in language so careful it was nearly a eulogy, the night the supply ship *Providence* broke her back on the northern rocks and the light above them burned steady until dawn, and thirty-one men who had come to silence the keeper were carried instead into his keeping.

"*It is the finding of this inquiry,*" he read, "*that the conduct of the keeper of the Merrow's Reach Light throughout the winter of 1893–94 reflects the highest tradition of the service, and that the light itself — maintained without interruption through the worst storm in living memory — stands as witness to what this account can only set down in words.*"

He closed the book.

For a while neither of them said anything. The wind moved around the lantern room, testing the seams the way it always did, finding nothing loose. Far below, a cart creaked along the cliff road toward the village, and someone — Maren, probably, though he couldn't have said how he knew — was singing one of the orchard songs with more enthusiasm than accuracy.

"They got it right," Tam said at last. "Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"Well." The boy pulled the book back toward himself and opened it, hunting. "Here. It says Brandt 'surrendered the manifests.' It doesn't say he sat in your kitchen for three hours first, crying into his tea, saying he'd sworn an oath to a man who was selling children's bread." Tam's voice had an edge on it now, old anger finding its familiar groove. "And here — it says Maren's accounting 'exposed the discrepancy.' It doesn't say she knew from November and lay awake every night deciding whether saying it out loud would get Tam's rations cut. It makes it sound tidy. Like everyone just did the brave thing at the right time and went to bed."

"People write it tidy," Juniper said, "because living it wasn't."

Tam looked up at him. Fifteen years old, and he held the look a long time, weighing it, the way he'd learned to weigh things that winter. Then he nodded slowly and closed the book.

"Voss's trial starts next month," he said. "On the mainland. Maren got a letter."

"I know. She showed me."

"Are you going?"

Juniper was quiet. Through the glass, the sea ran silver and slate to the horizon, unbroken now by any unlit sloop, any cargo that wasn't cargo. The bridge stood open across the channel, mended, busy with the season's first ferries. He could be on one inside the week. He had thought about it — he would not pretend to Tam that he hadn't. There was a version of him that wanted to stand in a mainland courtroom and watch the man in the uniform finally small.

"No," he said. "I gave my testimony. It's written down. It doesn't need me standing over it." He touched the closed book. "This is my courtroom. It always was."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he gave everything now, then nodded once, satisfied, as though a verdict had been entered.

"Maren also says," he added, with the air of a boy delivering terms, "that supper's at six, and if you're late again she's giving your portion to the dog, and the dog is imaginary, so think about that."

"She has an imaginary dog now?"

"We got a real one in spring. Her name is Biscuit and she is a menace and she loves you best, which Maren pretends not to mind."

Juniper smiled. It still surprised him sometimes, how easily the thing came now — how the muscles of it had remembered their work after so many years of disuse, like a lamp trimmed and relit after a long dark.

"Tell her I'll be there," he said. "Tell her I'm bringing the book."

* * *

Tam clattered down the stairs with the volume tucked under his arm, off to parade it through the village like a captured flag, and Juniper was alone in the lantern room with the last of the light.

He did his rounds. It was habit older than grief, older than the winter, older than anything on the island except the rock itself. He checked the oil reservoirs, full and clean. He checked the clockwork that turned the lens, oiled and true. He climbed the little ladder and put his hand flat against the great lens housing, warm from the day's sun, and felt — as he had felt every day since a soldier's hands had searched this tower and found nothing — the faint weight of history sleeping in the metal. The hidden ledger had come out of the lens housing a year ago, sworn and copied and entered into evidence, but his palm still knew where it had lain. Some weights you carried so long they left a shape.

He took out his own logbook — the current one, the fourth since that winter — and opened it to the day's page.

Dusk was coming down. Out west the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, gold at the seam of it, and the first stars were pricking through above the channel. He lit the lamp. He trimmed the wick with the small knife, the same knife that had opened the parcel, the same knife his wife had once used to pare apples in a kitchen three hundred miles away and a whole life ago. The flame stood up straight and sure, and the lens gathered it, and turned it, and sent it out — one flash, and another, and another — across the water to every boat that needed finding.

He uncapped his pen. Beneath the day's entries — *wind SW fresh, sky clearing, ferry traffic normal, oil received 40 gallons, condition good* — he wrote the last line of the day, and then, because the moment asked it of him, he sat with the pen hovering a moment longer, and added one more.

*All accounted for.*

He looked at it a long time. Four words. They weighed nothing. They carried everything — the grain that had returned to the tables, the sergeant mending nets now in the village rather than drowning in a cell, the boy coming up the stairs two at a time, the woman singing badly on the cliff road, the trunk of his wife's belongings resting quiet under the orchard's oldest apple tree where he had buried it in spring, roots around it, apples above it, nothing in it that needed carrying anymore.

Outside, the light turned. Inside, the keeper of the Merrow's Reach Light closed his logbook, wound the clockwork, and went down the hundred and nine steps to supper — leaving the lamp burning behind him, as it burned tonight, as it would burn tomorrow, steady over the water, over the island, over the record that stood.
