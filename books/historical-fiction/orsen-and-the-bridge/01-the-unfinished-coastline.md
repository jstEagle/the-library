## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

Three months after burying a man, you learn his habits better than you ever learned them while he lived. That is either a cruelty or a gift of the trade, and I have not yet decided which.

I rise at half past five because Aldous Corven rose at half past five, and I light the lamp at the north window because he lit it there, and I grind my ink to the consistency of cold honey because he once told me—only once, and with the gravity of a man handing down scripture—that ink too thin was a lie told lazily and ink too thick was a lie told desperately. He had opinions about ink the way bishops have opinions about God. I miss them. I miss being scolded by a dead man's standards every morning before breakfast.

The shop smells the way it has always smelled: linseed oil, copper shavings, the vinegar bite of the etching bath, and underneath it all the faint sweet rot of old paper that has been loved too long. In December it also smelled of coal smoke and Aldous's cough. In March it smells only of itself, and I confess there are mornings when the absence of the cough is louder than any sound in the room.

My name is Silas Brightwater. For three months now it has also been, unofficially and against every reasonable expectation, *Master Corven's successor*, which is a title like a coat cut for a taller man—I wear it and it hangs past my hands.

* * *

The atlas lay open on the great table as it had lain every morning for ninety-one days, and I will admit here what I would not admit to anyone in Vellmark: some mornings I spoke to it. Not words, exactly. More the sort of noises a man makes when he drops something—small involuntary sounds of apology. Because an unfinished atlas is a reproachful thing. It sits there with its blank spaces like a guest who has been interrupted mid-sentence and is waiting, politely and forever, for you to let him finish.

Aldous had been at work on it eleven years. Eleven years for a river city and its coastline—the Vellmark estuary, the salt marshes east of the embankment, the shipping channels down to Greywater Point, and then the coast proper, forty miles of it running north toward the cliffs at Tarn Head. The plates completed were beautiful in the way that only patient work is beautiful: his hatching on the mudflats so fine it looked like breathing, his lettering for the depth-soundings set in that small confident hand that made numbers feel like facts rather than guesses. Nine plates done. Four remaining. And the four that remained were not merely undone—they were *absent*. No sketches. No drafts. No penciled margins where a man thinks aloud in graphite. The final section of the atlas was simply a row of empty folio pages, each one labeled in Aldous's hand with its intended subject—*The Inner Channels; The Embankment Surveys; The Floodline; The Northern Coast*—and beneath each label, nothing.

That nothing had a texture to it. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the man's religion and I am trying to practice it even in grief. A page left blank out of ignorance feels one way. A page left blank out of exhaustion feels another. But a page labeled carefully, titled, dated in the corner—and then left utterly untouched, year after year, by a man who could not leave a crooked line uncriticized—that page feels *deliberate*. It feels like a door someone closed quietly so you wouldn't hear it.

I traced coastlines instead. That was the routine. The northern coast plate needed finishing regardless—it was nearly complete when he died, needing only the last stretch of shoreline below Tarn Head and the soundings along it—and so every day I sat at the transfer desk with the survey sketches pinned above me and drew a country I had never seen and would likely never see. Aldous had walked that shore himself in '39 and again in '44, and his field notebooks recorded it in his compressed shorthand: distances, bearings, the character of the ground, notes like *"gulls hostile"* and *"innkeeper honest, ale not."* I knew the coast through him. I knew where the cliff path washed out in a storm winter and where a fisherman named Brandt kept a boat he'd painted green for reasons never explained. I knew it the way you know a place from letters—from a correspondent who loved it. And like any correspondent's friend, I had begun to grieve for the place as well as the man, which is a strange doubled mourning, and heavier than it sounds.

That morning I was working the stretch below Gull Neck, where the tide runs hard around the point and Aldous had taken soundings at half-hour intervals across a full ebb, which meant he had stood in a bobbing boat in February weather taking measurements every thirty minutes for six hours, presumably arguing with the boatman the whole time. The numbers came up through his shorthand clean as coins. I transferred them to the plate, laid the shading for the deep channel, and paused—as I paused every morning at this exact spot—to admire the way the estuary opens there, wide and shallow, the river spreading out like a hand letting go of something.

"Six hours in a February boat," I said to the empty shop. "You were mad."

The shop said nothing. The shop had gotten very good at saying nothing since December.

* * *

At nine I opened the shutters, because Aldous had always opened them at nine regardless of weather or season—"customers keep no calendar," he said, though in eleven years the customers had kept such a sparse calendar that we might reasonably have published it as a rarity. There were fewer still now. Word travels fast in Vellmark when a master mapmaker dies, and faster when the man who inherits the shop is young and unproven and carries a name that opens doors in drawing rooms but not, apparently, in counting houses. I sold a chart of the harbor to a barge captain who remembered Aldous and bought it, I think, out of pity priced at two crowns. I re-hung a framed survey of the mill district that had gone crooked on the wall sometime in January, and straightening it gave me more satisfaction than any work I'd done all week, which tells you something about January.

Mid-morning, Mrs. Halloway came in with bread and gossip in equal portions, which is how she delivered everything, including once a summons from the magistrate.

"They're saying the ice'll go early this year," she announced, setting the loaf on the counter as if it were evidence. "Warm spell coming up from the south. My sister's husband says the fishermen are already talking about it."

"The ice goes when it goes," I said. "It's a river, not a committee."

"That's exactly the sort of talk that gets a river angry." She looked past me at the atlas on the great table, the way everyone looked at it—hungry and slightly afraid, the way people look at a large dog or a debt. "You'll finish it, then? His grand book?"

"I mean to."

"He was a strange one, your master. Kind, but strange. Kept everything locked up like a moneylender. You ever find his private papers?"

This was Mrs. Halloway's genius: she asked questions that sounded idle and landed like grappling hooks. I gave her the answer I had given everyone. "His effects went to me, such as they were. Mostly instruments and paper. Nothing private about any of it."

"Mm," she said, which is the sound a woman makes when she does not believe you but is willing to let you find out why at your own pace. She took her leave with half the loaf still mine, and I stood a moment in the doorway watching her go down Copper Lane, and I noticed—for perhaps the hundredth time—that the neighbors still crossed to the far side of the street when passing the shop, and I wondered whether they avoided the dead man's ghost or the living man's name. With a name like Brightwater, in this city, it could honestly go either way.

* * *

Here is what I had found in three months of going through Aldous's effects, and I record it plainly because plainness was his creed: instruments, immaculate. Notebooks, forty-one of them, spanning fifty years of surveys, all in the same tireless shorthand. Correspondence, modest—a few letters from fellow surveyors, an invoice from a copper supplier in the capital, a Christmas card from the printers' guild with a printed signature that fooled no one. And the working drafts of the atlas, complete except for the final section, whose drafts existed nowhere in the shop.

Nowhere. I had looked the way only an apprentice looks—under floorboards, behind paneling, inside the false bottom of the plan chest that every mapmaker builds and pretends he built for security rather than sentiment. I had found, over those months, a tin of buttons, a child's wooden horse that predated my apprenticeship by decades, and a receipt for a funeral wreath dated 1809. I had found everything except the thing that was missing.

And here is the thought I did not say to Mrs. Halloway, and barely said to myself: a man who hides nothing leaves drawers full of clutter. A man who hides *one thing* leaves a clutter that is arranged. Aldous's clutter was arranged. I could not prove it. But when I held his notebooks, certain ones felt read more than others—handled, returned, squared to the shelf edge with the particular care a man gives to things he wants found in good order. Or perhaps wanted *not* found, and squared them anyway because disorder offended him more than exposure did. He was contradictory that way. He was contradictory most ways, once you knew him, and I had known him eleven years, and I had thought that meant I knew him entirely.

Ninety-one days is long enough to learn otherwise about anyone.

* * *

In the afternoon the light turned, as March light does, from silver to the color of weak tea, and I gave up the tracing board for the cleaning bench, because copper plates do not forgive neglect and neither, in my experience, does anything else worth keeping. I was scraping old wax off the proofing roller when I heard the bell over the door and looked up expecting Mrs. Halloway's second invasion or a lost sailor.

It was neither. It was a woman I did not know: old, upright, dressed in black that had been good once and was maintained now with the fierce economy of someone who intends her mourning to outlast her money. She stood just inside the door and looked at the shop—the plates, the charts, the atlas—and then at me, and her face did something complicated that ended in a kind of settled dislike, the way weather ends in frost.

"You're the Brightwater boy," she said. Not a question.

"I'm Silas Brightwater. Master Corven was my teacher. Can I help you, madam?"

She looked at me a moment longer. Whatever she was weighing, she weighed it fully, and then she said, "No," with a finality that closed the matter like a ledger, and she turned and went back out into the street, and the bell rang behind her with what I swear was a note of judgment.

I stood there holding a waxed roller, feeling like a man who has been measured for something without knowing what garment was intended. It occurred to me later that evening, and it occurs to me now, that she had looked at my name the way you look at a stain on good cloth—wondering whether it will come out, or whether the cloth was always spoiled and only pretending.

But that was evening's conclusion. The afternoon still had hours in it, and routines are the only furniture grief leaves you, so I went back to them.

* * *

It was while reshelving the survey folios—Thursday's chore, done Thursdays because Aldous had done them Thursdays and I saw no reason the calendar should lose its religion just because its keeper had—that I found it. Or rather, I found the *wrongness*, which is how finding usually begins in my trade.

The folios lived in the plan chest, third drawer, in order of date. The 1811 folio sat between 1810 and 1812 as it had always sat, and as I lifted it out I felt it catch—slightly, wrongly, the way a drawer catches when something behind it has shifted. I drew it into the lamplight and turned it over, and there, along the spine, I saw it: a seam in the leather that should not have been there. Stitching, close and expert, running the length of the binding like a scar. Someone had sewn the spine shut. Someone had sewn it shut *over* something—the leather bulged along that seam with the faintest asymmetry, the kind of irregularity no eye would catch and no finger would miss.

I want to tell you I felt dread. What I actually felt, standing there with a fifty-year-old folio in my hands and the lamp guttering, was the specific thrill of the craftsman encountering craftsmanship: *someone stitched this well.* The dread arrived a heartbeat later, punctual as ever, wearing the question like a coat.

Because I knew that stitching. Close-set, double-locked, the thread waxed—there was one pair of hands in Vellmark that sewed like that, and I had watched them do it a hundred times over eleven years, mending chart covers, binding proofs. Aldous Corven had sewn something into the spine of the 1811 survey folio, flat and thin and rectangular, and then shelved it in order among sixty other folios where it looked, to any eye in the world, like exactly what it appeared to be.

An ordinary book. An ordinary year. A flood year, if you knew your history—the winter the river took the old bridge and eleven men of the bridge-builders' guild with it, the disaster every child in Vellmark learned before they learned their letters. A year nobody would pull from the shelf without reason.

Unless, of course, they were the sort of person who reshelved folios every Thursday and paid attention to seams.

I set the folio on the bench. I did not open it. I stood there a long time with my hand flat on the cover, feeling through the leather the small hard rectangle of whatever slept inside, and outside the window the river ran dark and quiet under its ice, keeping, as rivers do, every secret it had ever been given.

"To whoever finishes my work," the label on the front had always read, in Aldous's small confident hand.

I picked up my shears.

* * *

I did not cut the stitches that night. Let the record show I am capable of restraint, though history may judge it the last restrained act of my life. I told myself it was respect—he had sewn it shut, and it seemed fitting that opening it should be a decision made in daylight, soberly, the way he would have wanted a survey confirmed: twice measured, once cut. This was true. It was also true that my hands were not steady, and that a man should not open his dead master's secrets with shaking hands, and that some part of me already understood—some part that had counted the empty plates, and marked the ordered clutter, and watched an old woman look at my name like a stain—that whatever lay in that spine had been sewn there to wait for exactly this trembling, and deserved steadier treatment than I could give it at ten o'clock at night alone in a dead man's shop.

So I put the folio back in the third drawer, in order, between its neighbors. I banked the fire. I blew out the lamp at the north window, which I had taken to leaving lit some nights, though I never admitted that to anyone either.

And I lay upstairs in the narrow bed under the eaves, listening to the river, thinking about the woman in the good black dress and the word she hadn't said. Because she hadn't said *Brightwater* the way people say a name. She'd said it the way you spit out a stone.

Three months I had believed the missing plates were the mystery—four blank sections, a puzzle of absent paper, grief's little riddle to keep a bereaved apprentice occupied. Tonight, with the memory of that seam under my fingers, I understood for the first time that the blank pages might not be a gap in the work at all.

They might be a wound. And wounds, in my experience, are rarely left open by accident.

Tomorrow I would cut the stitches. Tomorrow I would find out what my master had judged worth hiding from the world, and worth trusting, somehow, to me.
