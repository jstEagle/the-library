## 2. A Letter Under Wax

The trouble with tracing coastlines you will never see is that your pen learns to lie.

I had discovered this on a Tuesday in the third month of my solitude, somewhere around the hundredth mile of a shore I could not name without Master Corven's notes to tell me its character — whether the cliffs there were chalk or granite, whether the fishing villages clung like barnacles or stood back from the water with the wariness of people who had been flooded once and meant to remember it. Without him, the coastline became an argument between ink and guesswork, and guesswork was winning.

I set down the pen and rubbed my eyes until the map swam.

Three months since we had buried Aldous Corven under a February sky the color of dishwater, and I still caught myself turning to speak to him. Not aloud — I was lonely, not mad — but that half-turn of the head, the gathering of a sentence, the little collapse when I remembered the bench by the south window was empty except for his magnifying glass, which I kept there because moving it felt like a second funeral and leaving it felt like a vigil, and I hadn't decided which was worse.

The shop smelled as it always had: linseed, lamp-black, the ghost of tobacco he'd sworn off eleven years before dying but whose stain still lived in the ceiling beams like a portrait nobody commissioned. Outside, Vellmark went about its business. Carts over the cobbles. A fishwife arguing with heaven. The river sliding past beneath its lid of ice, patient as a debt collector.

I picked up the pen again. That was the comfort of routine, I'll grant it that much — grief may empty a room, but it cannot empty a task. There were fourteen plates finished, six sketched, and two missing entirely, and if I did nothing else that winter, I would at least know exactly how unfinished the atlas was. Precision in loss. It's a mapmaker's vice. We measure everything because we can hold nothing.

* * *

It was while re-binding the old survey folios — a job I'd been postponing since the funeral on the principle that dead men's papers should be disturbed only by their heirs, and then reluctantly — that I found the letter.

The folio in question was a survey of the upper river reaches, dated 1811, which should have told me something right away. 1811. Every child in Vellmark knew the year the way children elsewhere know plague years or coronations. The flood winter. The winter the bridge came down and took nine guildsmen with it, and the city built its memorial stone and told itself a story about cruel ice and crueler God, and everyone agreed it was very sad and very long ago and absolutely no one's fault, which is how cities prefer their tragedies served.

But I wasn't thinking about any of that. I was thinking about binding glue.

Aldous had taught me the trade's small liturgies: how to stretch vellum so it doesn't buckle, how to cut a quill so it sings instead of scratching, how to sew a folio's spine with linen thread waxed against damp. And he'd taught me that a well-made book is a box with pretensions — that anything flat enough can hide inside one. He'd hidden banknotes in atlases during the hard years. He'd once hidden a love letter in a nautical chart for a client who lacked the courage to deliver it himself, and charged him double for the discretion.

So when my fingers found a ridge along the inner spine of the 1811 folio — a thickness where no thickness belonged, a small firm pulse under the leather like a secret heartbeat — I didn't gasp. I reached for the bone folder.

The stitching was masterful. Of course it was. Someone had unpicked the spine sewing, laid a packet flat against the boards, and resewn the whole length so cleanly that thirty-odd years of readers had turned these pages without ever feeling the letter sleeping behind them. I say thirty-odd years because the paper, when I finally eased it out, had gone the amber color of old varnish, soft at the edges, and the wax seal — unbroken, thank God, though I confess my thumbnail hovered over it longer than dignity allows me to admit — bore a device I recognized before I understood it.

Not Corven's mark. His mark was a compass rose, plain and honest.

This was a bridge. An arched stone bridge, drawn small and precise, the way the guild used to sign their plans. Before the guild stopped existing.

The front read, in a hand I almost knew — a version of Aldous's writing from before it had settled into the cramped, dry script I'd learned under:

*To whoever finishes my work.*

Well.

I sat down on his stool, which was the wrong height for me and always had been, and I held the letter up to the window light, as if illumination might make it less heavy. It weighed perhaps an ounce. It weighed considerably more than that.

Here is what you must understand about me, whoever you are reading this — and yes, I know how it sounds, a man narrating his own life as if someone might someday consult the record; blame the profession. We mapmakers believe everything should be legible to strangers. But here is what you must understand: I was raised in the comfortable religion of the present tense. History, I believed, happened elsewhere. It happened to other cities, other centuries, other families — families with names carved into courthouse lintels, families with portraits in the guild hall, families who *mattered* enough for the past to bother with them. The Brightwaters were merchant money, three generations deep and none of it dramatic. My grandfather had bought flour cheap and sold bread dear, and that was the whole epic. Our history was arithmetic. Safe. Boring. *Ours.*

A sealed letter sewn into a survey from the flood winter, addressed to the finisher of Aldous Corven's atlas — that was somebody else's story. Some other family's skeleton. Mine was still in the cupboard where skeletons belong, which is to say, nowhere, because we had none, because we were dull, and dullness, I believed then, was a kind of innocence.

I put the letter in the drawer of Aldous's desk. The deep drawer, the one with the false bottom he used for debts he didn't want Maren to see. Then I locked the desk, put the key in my waistcoat pocket, and went upstairs and made tea I did not drink, and came back down, and opened the drawer, and looked at it, and closed the drawer again.

Curiosity, I find, is like river water under ice. You can stand on it all winter. The question is what happens in spring.

* * *

My aunt Maren arrived on Thursday, which was her day, as it had been her day for twenty years, announced not by knock but by the particular scrape of her umbrella being furled against our doorframe, a sound I could distinguish from all other umbrellas in Vellmark the way a shepherd knows his own bellwether.

"Silas." She kissed the air near both my cheeks, inspected the shop with the expression of a woman auditing a warehouse, and removed her gloves finger by finger, each removal a small verdict. "You've let the windows go."

"I wash the windows."

"When? Epiphany?"

She hung her coat on the hook that had held Aldous's coat for forty years and did not look at it, which was how she mourned: by refusing to look at things. Maren Brightwater was seventy-one years old and built like a well-made chair — upright, economical, designed to bear weight without complaint. She had buried a husband, outlived most of her friends, and survived my uncle's bankruptcy by the simple method of never having liked him. She frightened me in the way only the truly practical can frighten the merely clever.

"Tea?" I said, knowing the answer.

"You know I don't drink tea in the afternoon. It keeps me awake, and I'm too old to afford sleeplessness." She sat in the customer's chair, arranged her skirt, and fixed me with the look she reserved for negotiations she intended to win. "I've had a letter from the Harrow agent."

"Have you." The Harrows. Old money, older than ours, dockside money, money with warehouses named after it along the east embankment. Edmund Harrow had been at school a year ahead of me and had shaken my hand at graduation with the grave courtesy of a boy already practicing for his own statue.

"Harrow & Sons want the shop," Maren said. "Land and building. They're offering four hundred marks."

Four hundred marks was — I did the arithmetic, because in our family arithmetic was inherited along with the chin — roughly double what the shop was worth on an open market, and triple what it would fetch in February, when nobody buys anything except coal and regret.

"That's generous," I said carefully.

"It's obscene," Maren said. "Which is why you should take it before they come to their senses." She leaned forward. "Silas. Listen to me. I know what this place is to you. It was Aldous's life, and now you've made it your mourning, and mourning is respectable, I won't argue with mourning. But you cannot eat respectability, and come April the creditors will be at that door, and Harrow's offer will have evaporated, and you'll be selling maps to tourists at half price until the bailiffs carry out the presses."

"The atlas—"

"Will be finished by someone else or not at all," she said, not cruelly. Maren was never cruel. She was simply incapable of saying a thing gently once she'd said it accurately. "You're one man, and the plates are half done, and engraving takes years you don't have funding for. Sell the shop, keep the plates, finish the atlas in a rented room like every honest poor artist in this city. Take the four hundred marks. Pay what we owe. Keep the name clean."

*Keep the name clean.* I would hear those words again, later, in worse weather. But that Thursday they sounded like sense, the way sensible things always do when spoken by someone who has already survived more winters than you.

"Maren," I said, "why does Harrow want a mapmaker's shop? They have docks. They have ships. What do they want with—" I gestured at the shelves, the drawers, the smell of linseed. "This?"

Something crossed her face then. Small. Quick. A cloud passing over a field — you'd swear afterward you imagined it.

"They're buying up the riverfront," she said. "Everyone knows that. Land is land."

It was a true answer. It was also, I noticed even then, not the answer to the question I'd asked. But Maren had risen, retrieved her coat, and begun the glove ceremony in reverse, and my aunt's departures were not to be delayed with follow-up questions any more than tides are.

"Think about it before spring," she said at the door, and touched my cheek — actually touched it, flesh to flesh, which she did perhaps twice a year. "You have his stubbornness, you know. Aldous's. It's a fine quality in a craftsman and a ruinous one in an heir."

Then the umbrella scraped, and she was gone, and the shop settled around me like a dog returning to its corner.

* * *

That night I did not sleep.

I told myself it was the offer — four hundred marks rattling around my skull like coins in a tin. I told myself it was the creditors, whose names and sums I knew too well. I lay in my room above the shop listening to the wind worry the signboard — CORVEN & BRIGHTWATER, MAPS AND SURVEYS, the ampersand doing all the work of holding two lives together — and I told myself a great many things, and around two in the morning I gave up telling myself things, lit a candle, and went down the stairs I knew by heart into the dark that smelled of my dead master.

The desk key was cold. The drawer opened with the same reluctant whisper it always made. And there it lay in the candlelight: the amber packet, the unbroken wax, the little bridge pressed into it like a promise someone had forgotten to keep.

*To whoever finishes my work.*

I am finishing your work, Aldous, I thought. That's me. That has been me for three months, badly, alone, tracing coasts I'll never see. If this letter is part of the work, then it's addressed to me, and if it's addressed to me, then not opening it is — what? Respect? Cowardice wearing respect's coat?

I thought of Maren's cloud-crossed face. *They're buying up the riverfront. Land is land.* I thought of the folio's date. 1811. I thought of the memorial stone by the embankment stairs, which I had walked past ten thousand times, with its nine names worn shallow by weather and thumbs, names I could recite the way recitations are recited — smoothly, meaninglessly, the way you recite a psalm.

Ferran. One of the names was Ferran.

I sat on the stool that was the wrong height and held the letter over the candle and turned it, and the light came through the paper just enough to show me the shadow of folded pages within, dense with writing, front and back, a great deal of writing, the writing of a man who had needed many words to say whatever it was.

And I found — this is the honest part, the part I'd rather skip — that I was afraid.

Not of ghosts. Ghosts are easy; they only want remembering. I was afraid of something with better manners than a ghost. I was afraid that the letter would be exactly what it appeared to be: a piece of the past that had traveled thirty-six years through flood and fire and forgetfulness to arrive at my desk, addressed to me, and that once opened, it would refuse to stay in 1811. That it would climb the stairs and sit at breakfast. That it would look out of my own face at me in the mirror and say, *here is what your name has been doing while you weren't watching.*

Because that is the crack I felt open that night — the first hairline fracture in a belief I'd carried like load-bearing masonry all my life: that history happens elsewhere. To dukes, to guilds, to cities with better chroniclers. To anyone, in short, but a middling merchant family called Brightwater, whose sins, if they had any, were surely small ones — a sharp bargain here, a cold shoulder there — the ordinary peccadilloes of ordinary commerce, dust-scale, forgivable, *boring.*

The ice on the river groaned that night — it does that in late winter, settling, shifting, testing its own strength — and I heard it through the floorboards and thought: that's the sound of something deciding whether to hold or break.

I did not open the letter. Not that night.

I wrapped it instead in a square of oilcloth, the way one wraps a thing meant to survive water, though there was no reason on earth to think it would meet water, and I hid it — not in the desk, anywhere but the desk — inside the hollow spine of the great folio itself, sewn loosely, retrievable, and I put the folio back among its brothers on the shelf, indistinguishable, one survey of the 1811 river among many surveys of the 1811 river.

Then I blew out the candle and climbed back to bed and lay listening to the ice groan below, and I slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of a coastline I did not recognize, drawn in a hand almost like mine, running along the edge of a map toward some country the cartographer had refused to name.

Two days later, I took the folio down again, ostensibly to check a sounding against the new draft plate.

Ostensibly.

I took it down because curiosity is river water, and the thaw, whatever the calendar claimed, had already begun.
