## 5. Maren's Arithmetic

My aunt Maren had a way of arriving that made the shop feel smaller. She did not knock so much as announce herself with the door, letting it swing wide and stand there in the frame like a creditor, which in a sense she was—though the debts she collected were of the moral sort, and she never sent a bill.

"Silas." She said my name the way she said *February*: as though it were something to be endured annually.

"Aunt Maren." I set down my pen before she could see how much of the coastline I'd inked that morning, which was none. Three hours at the drafting table and nothing to show for it but a smudge where my thumb had rested too long on the vellum, worrying the same stretch of riverbank as if pressure alone could make Aldous Corven rise from his grave and finish it for me.

She crossed the shop without looking at anything in it. This was her particular genius, I think—Maren could walk through a room full of evidence and see only what she'd already decided was there. She passed the survey folios, passed the drying inks, passed the half-finished atlas lying open under its weights like a patient mid-surgery, and settled onto the stool by the stove as if the stool had been holding her place since morning.

"You haven't sold the shop," she observed.

"No."

"It's been three months."

"It has been three months and eleven days," I said, "but who's counting."

"I'm counting." She unwound her scarf with the deliberation of a woman uncoiling an argument she'd rehearsed on the walk over. "Silas, we need to talk about money."

"We always need to talk about money. It's our family's love language."

"Don't be clever. Cleverness is what your father had instead of sense, and look where it got him." She held her hands toward the stove, though the fire was low; I suspected the gesture was more rhetorical than thermal. "I've been through the accounts. All of them. The ones you don't look at because they're kept in the green ledger with the broken clasp, behind the sample frames."

"The green ledger is mostly Aldous's notes on pigment costs."

"The green ledger is a eulogy," she said flatly. "The Brightwater money is gone, or nearly. What's left wouldn't keep us through summer even if you closed the shop tomorrow and took in washing."

I should have known. Part of me, I admit, *had* known—the way you know a floor is rotten beneath the carpet, from the slight give of it underfoot. The Brightwater name had always sounded richer than the Brightwater purse. My father had lived like a man expecting an inheritance from somewhere, and when it didn't come he'd simply gone on spending as though credit were a form of faith. Faith, in our family, was always someone else's problem eventually.

"How nearly?" I asked.

"Nearly enough." She fixed me with the look she used on tradesmen and bishops, both of whom she considered varieties of the same animal. "There are creditors. Quiet ones, so far, but quiet creditors are like quiet rivers—you want to know why they're quiet before you trust them."

"And you've come to tell me the solution, because you always have one."

"I do have one." And here she paused, and I saw—for the first time that morning, and possibly for the first time ever—that my aunt was nervous. Maren nervous was a thing I had no category for. She smoothed her skirt twice, which she does when buying time, and looked into the stove rather than at me. "Edmund Harrow has made an offer."

The name landed in the room and sat there between us like a dropped tool.

"Harrow," I repeated.

"Edmund Harrow. He came to me last week—he knows I hold the family interest in this property—and he wants the shop. The building, the stock, the plates, and the atlas. All of it. He's offering more than it's worth, Silas. Considerably more. Enough to clear every debt and leave us comfortable. Comfortable," she said again, tasting the word, "for the first time in twenty years."

I stood very still. Outside the window, February was doing its usual impression of a city that had given up on color. A cart went by on the cobbles, and the sound of it seemed to come from very far away.

"Why," I said, "would Edmund Harrow pay above value for a mapmaker's shop?"

"Because he's young and rich and eccentric, and young rich eccentrics collect things."

"Maren."

"Because he admires Corven's work," she tried.

"Maren."

She sighed then, long and theatrical, the sigh of a woman who has decided honesty is cheaper than another round of fencing. "Because the Harrows and the Brightwaters have old business together, and Edmund would like that business to stay old. Buried. However you like your metaphors."

*Old business.* The phrase turned over in my mind and showed me its underside, and I thought of the letter sewn into the binding of the survey folio upstairs—addressed *to whoever finishes my work*, still sealed, still waiting, weighing on my conscience these weeks like a stone I carried in a pocket I kept forgetting I had. I hadn't opened it. I told myself I was honoring Aldous's privacy. I told myself a great many things before breakfast.

"What old business?" I asked, though some part of me already knew the shape of the answer, the way you know the shape of a thing under a sheet.

Maren gave me a look that said she knew I knew, and that she resented being made to say it aloud regardless. "Eighteen-eleven," she said. "The flood winter. Your grandfather and Josiah Harrow were partners in it—whatever it was. Nobody in this family says plainly what it was, which tells you everything, doesn't it? Families only go quiet about the past when the past owes somebody an apology."

"And you've known this all along."

"I've known that there's a hole where the story should be. That's not the same as knowing what's in the hole." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the performance fell away and she was just a tired woman of fifty-eight who had buried a brother and outlived her usefulness to everyone but one nephew. "Silas. Listen to me. I watched your father die ruined. Not poor—ruined. There's a difference, and the difference is that poor men can hold their heads up. He died owing people who smiled at his funeral and then picked over the estate like gulls. I will not watch you do the same out of loyalty to a dead man's unfinished drawings."

"They're not just drawings."

"They're coastlines you'll never see! Rivers you'll never sail! Ink on sheepskin!" Her voice cracked upward and she caught it, wrestled it back down, and continued in the low, reasonable register that was somehow worse. "Aldous Corven left you a shop that eats money and an atlas no one has commissioned. Edmund Harrow is offering you a life. Take the life."

I walked to the window. It's a habit of mine when I need a moment—I pretend to be interested in weather. Across the street, the chandler's boy was sweeping snow off his step with the grim efficiency of the very bored, and beyond the rooftops, invisible but present, the river lay locked in ice, holding whatever it holds until spring.

"The atlas isn't mine to sell," I said finally.

"It's absolutely yours to sell. You inherited it."

"That's not what I mean."

"Then what do you mean?"

And there it was: the question I couldn't answer without opening the letter, and couldn't open the letter without answering questions I wasn't ready to ask. I thought of Odile Ferran's face when I'd mentioned the 1811 surveys—no, I hadn't yet been to her door; that was still ahead of me, though I confess the plan had been forming in me like frost on glass. I thought of the way Aldous, in his last year, had taken certain folios down from the shelves at night when he believed I was asleep, and the way the lamplight under his door had burned later and later, and how I had told myself it was his eyes going, needing more light for less work.

"I mean," I said carefully, "that a man who leaves you a sealed letter probably left it for a reason, and selling his work to the Harrow family before reading it feels like the kind of decision a man regrets in a more interesting century than this one."

Maren stared at me. "Sealed letter."

I hadn't meant to say it. It slipped out the way truth does—sideways, while you're guarding the front door. My aunt rose from the stool with the slow inevitability of a tide.

"There is a letter," she said, "from Aldous Corven, that you have had in your possession for weeks, and you have not mentioned it to me."

"It's addressed to whoever finishes his work. I haven't finished his work."

"So you're waiting until the atlas is done to read the instructions? Silas, that's not diligence, that's cowardice wearing a nice coat."

"Cowardice rarely wears such a well-tailored coat," I admitted.

"This is not amusing." She crossed to me and took my wrist—not gently, Maren has never done gentle when firm would serve—and turned me from the window to face her. Up close I could see what the years had been doing to her while I wasn't watching: the fine lines around her eyes had become canyons, and there was a tremor in her grip that I chose, charitably, to attribute to anger. "Whatever is in that letter, it changes nothing about the arithmetic. Debts don't care about letters. Creditors don't care about legacies. In six weeks, maybe eight, the shop goes to auction and the atlas with it, and you'll have nothing but a story about the noble thing you almost did. Is that the inheritance you want? Because I promise you, it's the inheritance your father left, and I stood in the wreckage of it, and I am telling you—" her voice did crack now, fully, and she let it, "—I cannot stand in it twice."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs, the house settled against the cold with a groan like an old man changing position.

"Maren," I said quietly, "what exactly did Edmund Harrow offer? The number."

She told me the number. It was, as advertised, generous—generous the way a snare is generous with rope. It would clear the debts, buy her the small house in Hillward she'd denied herself for two decades, and leave me free to begin again anywhere, as anyone, unencumbered by a name that was apparently worth less than the paper it was engraved on.

It was a good offer. That was the terrible thing about it. It was a genuinely, sincerely good offer, extended by a polite young man with soft hands, and every rational bone in my body voted yes, and the vote was unanimous except for one small stubborn vertebra at the base of my skull that had begun, in recent weeks, to ache whenever the year 1811 was spoken aloud in my presence.

"I'll consider it," I said.

"You'll consider it." She released my wrist and stepped back, and the relief on her face was so naked that I felt like a thief for having caused it. "That's all I ask. Consider it properly. With the ledger open. Not with your head in the clouds where Corven kept it."

"He kept my head in the clouds? I thought that was the family estate."

"Your head," she said, gathering her scarf, "is the only asset in this family that has never once appeared in the accounts." But she said it softly, and at the door she paused, hand on the latch, and did not quite turn around. "Silas. Whatever's in that letter—read it before you decide anything. If there's one piece of advice I'd trade the whole Harrow offer for, it's that. Decisions made blind are how families end up owing apologies."

Then she was gone, and the cold came in behind her and found me standing in the middle of my own shop like a stranger taking inventory.

* * *

I went upstairs after dark.

This deserves some description, I suppose, because I had been avoiding it with the discipline of a professional. The letter had lived for three weeks in the drawer of Aldous's old desk, sewn—as I'd discovered while repairing the binding of a water-damaged survey folio—into the spine itself, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax that bore no crest, only a thumbprint pressed deep, as if the seal were less a lock than a signature. *To whoever finishes my work.* I had read those five words perhaps four hundred times. They were both an invitation and an escape hatch: as long as the atlas remained unfinished, the letter remained, technically, not mine.

But Maren's arithmetic had changed the terms. A man can ignore a letter indefinitely when ignoring it costs nothing. Ignoring it while deciding whether to sell it to the Harrow family—that was a different transaction entirely, and my conscience, that fussy bookkeeper, refused to sign off on it.

So: after dark, lamp lit, desk cleared, I cut the stitching with Aldous's own bone folder, because it seemed right that his tools should open his last word.

The wax broke cleanly. Inside, several pages, closely written in the small fierce hand I knew better than my own by now. I read the first line, and then I sat for a long time without reading anything else, because the first line was:

*If you are reading this, then you have not yet learned what your name cost, and I am sorry to be the one to teach you.*

I read it all, then. Twice. By the third reading the lamp had burned low and my hands had gone cold, and outside the window the river lay silent under its ice, keeping, as it had kept for thirty-six years, the secret of what it had been paid to hold.

The Brightwaters, Aldous wrote. And the Harrows beside them. The flood winter of 1811 laid out in plain ink, no metaphors, no mercy—plans bought cheap from drowning men, depths falsified, a bridge's failure dressed up as fate. My grandfather's signature on documents I suddenly needed to see. Josiah Harrow's beside it.

I put the letter down and looked at the atlas through the open doorway below, pale under the moonlight coming through the shop's front windows, its final plates empty and waiting.

And I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after midnight and bad news, that Edmund Harrow's generous offer was not generosity at all.

It was a bid at auction—and I had just learned what was being sold.
