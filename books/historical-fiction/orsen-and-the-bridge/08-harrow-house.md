## 8. Harrow House

The summons arrived on a Thursday, which I mention only because Thursdays had become my day for cleaning the engraving plates, and the messenger's knock came at the exact moment I had my sleeves rolled in lamp-black to the elbow. There is no dignified way to answer a door with black hands, and I have since concluded that this was the point. The Harrows did not send for people; they arranged for people to arrive slightly diminished.

The card was heavy cream stock, edged in gilt, and read: *Mr. Edmund Harrow requests the pleasure of Mr. Silas Brightwater's company at Harrow House, Saturday, two o'clock. Matters concerning the late Master Corven's estate.* The word "pleasure" was doing a great deal of work on that little rectangle of paper, the way a keystone does work holding up an arch it never chose.

I stood there with the card in one blackened hand and thought about what Maren had told me over supper a week prior — that the Harrow name sat alongside mine in the ledger of 1811 like two hands folded over a stolen purse. I had not yet told her about the letter. Some knowledge you carry the way you carry a cracked plate through a crowded room: carefully, and pretending it isn't there.

"Shall I say you'll come?" the messenger asked. He was a boy of perhaps fourteen, dressed better than I was, which told me something about Harrow money and nothing good.

"You may say I'll consider it," I said, which was a lie so transparent even the boy smiled.

"He said you'd say that," the boy said. "He said if you did, I'm to wait."

* * *

Saturday came gray and cold, the river still wearing its ice like a coat it refused to give up. Maren insisted on seeing me to the door with my collar straightened and my boots brushed, fussing the way she did when she wanted very badly to ask a question and knew I would not answer it.

"The Harrows don't invite mapmakers to tea," she said. "They invite debts to tea. Try not to sign anything."

"I'm a Brightwater," I said. "We don't sign things. We inherit them pre-signed."

She laughed despite herself, then caught the laugh and held it prisoner, and I went out into the cold before either of us could say something true.

Harrow House stood on the high side of Cathedral Hill, where the city kept its money above the flood line — a fact I found myself noticing with new eyes now, having spent nights reading about a winter when the water took the low districts and everyone who built bridges there. The house was pale stone, four stories, with windows tall enough to hang a man in, and I mean that as architecture criticism rather than metaphor, though by evening both readings would apply.

A servant met me in the entrance hall, which was floored in marble the color of frozen cream, and led me up a staircase wide enough to drive a cart up, past portraits of Harrow men who all shared the same expression: satisfaction with the world as they had arranged it.

Edmund Harrow received me in a study lined with books he had almost certainly not read, though to be fair to him, neither had most of their owners before him. He was younger than I expected — five-and-twenty perhaps, with his father's jaw and none of his father's certainty yet, the way a coat hangs differently before the shoulders fill out. He rose when I entered. He shook my hand with a grip calibrated to suggest equality without conceding it.

"Mr. Brightwater. Thank you for coming." He gestured me into a chair angled toward the fire. "You'll take tea. My mother insists the household serves it at two regardless of whether anyone drinks it, so we may as well drink it."

"That's very nearly philosophy," I said.

"It's the only kind we practice here." He poured himself, then me, from a pot that had been waiting with the patience of hired help, which it was. "I understand congratulations are owed. Master Corven's atlas. Half the drawing rooms in Vellmark are talking about it, and the other half haven't heard yet but will pretend they always knew."

"There's not much to congratulate. A dead man's unfinished work is less an inheritance than a homework assignment."

"Spoken like someone who actually does the work." He settled back and looked at me over the rim of his cup, and I saw that behind the pleasantness there was a mind taking measurements, the way Aldous used to sight along a coastline. "Tell me about the atlas. What remains?"

"Four plates. Two coastal, one hydrographic, and the final composite — the master chart of the river approaches."

"And the missing papers? Corven's private surveys. His drafts. Whatever he kept locked in the shop that wasn't part of the commissioned work."

I let a moment pass. Tea has many uses; one of them is buying time. "I wasn't aware anything was missing."

"No?" Edmund smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Then you won't mind my asking how you plan to complete the final plates without them."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the next hour, and I set it down plainly because the trap deserves an honest description, having been built by craftsmen:

Edmund Harrow wanted the atlas finished. That much was genuine — he spoke of it with a collector's hunger, the desire to own the thing every drawing room was beginning to whisper about. But beneath the collecting instinct ran something colder and more specific. He wanted the *papers*. All of them. Aldous's drafts, folios, marginalia, correspondence — "for the archive," he said, "a proper Harrow-Corven collection, catalogued and preserved." And he wanted them *before* anyone else understood what might be in them.

The offer itself was beautiful, the way a snare is beautiful if you admire knotwork. Three hundred pounds — three times what the shop would fetch in an honest market — payable against delivery of the completed atlas plus the entirety of Corven's private materials. He would fund the engraving, the printing, the binding, everything, through his family's printers. The Brightwater name would appear on the title page. The Brightwater fortunes would be restored in a single stroke. Maren could stop lying awake doing arithmetic she hated.

"And in exchange," I said, "the papers come to you."

"To the collection, yes." He refilled my cup unasked. "Corven had no heirs, no guild standing worth mentioning, and a workshop that will leak rain until it falls down. Those papers are rotting, Mr. Brightwater. Paper doesn't keep. Ask any archivist — damp, mice, fire, theft. A private collection under professional care is the only responsible thing." He said *fire* lightly, the way a man says a word he hasn't yet decided the weight of.

"Master Corven struck me as particular about who handled his work."

"Particular, yes. Dead men generally are." Edmund laughed at his own joke, and it was such an easy, charming laugh that I understood why people underestimated him, and understood also that he cultivated being underestimated the way his grandfather had cultivated shipping lanes. "Look — I'll be direct, because I suspect directness is the only language you trust. My family has interests in the river trade. Dredging contracts, towage rights, the new embankment scheme. Charts matter to us. Accuracy matters to us. If Corven left behind survey material that contradicts the published record — old soundings, superseded drafts, that sort of thing — it would be *awkward* to have it circulating loosely while we're negotiating with the Corporation. Not scandalous. Just awkward. You know how paper is. It starts arguments."

"Arguments," I repeated.

"Facts start arguments," he corrected mildly, "which is worse."

And there it was, laid on the tea table between us next to the sugar tongs: the shape of the thing I already half-knew. The letter sewn into the folio. The falsified depths. 1811 sitting under the floorboards of both our houses like a corpse neither family would admit to burying.

I should tell you what I felt, because it surprised me. I expected fear, and there was some — the animal kind, the kind that counts exits. But mostly what I felt was a strange, cold steadiness, as though I had been walking on the river ice for weeks wondering if it would hold, and here at last was a man drilling a hole in front of me to show me exactly how thin it was. There is a horrible comfort in that. At least the hole is honest.

"The offer is generous," I said.

"It's fair," he said. "There's a difference, and I suspect you know it. Generosity expects nothing. Fairness expects exactly what it pays for." He set down his cup. "Take your time. A fortnight. I know grief keeps its own schedule — my father died two years ago and I'm still finding his opinions in drawers I don't open."

For a moment — just a moment — the mask slipped, or rather turned, and I saw the other face of Edmund Harrow: a young man sitting in a dead man's study, surrounded by a dead man's books, holding a dead man's debts, looking across the table at another young man in precisely the same position. We were two heirs circling the same wound from opposite sides, each convinced the other was carrying the knife.

"My condolences," I heard myself say, and meant it more than I wanted to.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Well." Then the pleasantness reassembled itself, seamless as a printed border. "A fortnight, Mr. Brightwater. Send word to this address. And do give my regards to your aunt — my mother speaks of Mrs. Brightwater often. They move in the same circles, or did."

* * *

The servant showed me out through corridors I memorized out of habit — Aldous trained me to memorize routes the way other masters trained apprentices to sweep. Past the portraits again. Past a display case in the gallery containing, of all things, a brass sounding-lead mounted on velvet like a relic, engraved with a date: *1811*. A trophy. Someone had hung the instrument of the drowning winter on the wall and called it heritage.

I stopped in front of it longer than was polite, and the footman waited with the infinite patience of a man paid to watch strangers grieve.

On the walk home I did arithmetic of my own. Three hundred pounds. Maren's creditors. The roof of the shop, which leaked onto the press. The final plates, which could not be finished honestly without the papers Edmund wanted swallowed. And underneath all of it, the question I had been avoiding since the night I broke the wax seal: *who else knows what Aldous knew, and how long has the list been?*

Because here was the detail I carried home like a stone in my boot. Edmund had said *superseded drafts*. He had said *old soundings*. He had known, specifically, the shape of what lay hidden — not the contents of a general archive, but contradictions. Soundings that argued with the published charts. Which meant either that Aldous's secrets had leaked before his death, or that the Harrows had always known what needed contradicting, because their grandfathers were the ones who'd done the contradicting.

Either way, the trap was not closing around me. It had closed years ago, around my whole name, and Edmund's tea was merely the first time I'd been invited to sit inside it.

The shop, when I reached it, smelled of lamp-black and cold iron and looked, for once, like what it was: a fortress with a leaky roof. I lit the lamp, unlocked the drawer where the folio slept, and put my palm flat on its cover just to feel that it was still real.

Three hundred pounds, or the truth. A restored name, or a redeemed one.

"A fortnight," I said aloud to the empty shop, and began, right then, that same evening, to make copies of everything.
