## 10. Working for the Enemy

There is a particular kind of humiliation in being paid well for work you'd have done anyway. I discovered it on a Tuesday, when Edmund Harrow's clerk arrived at the shop with a leather folio fat with banknotes and a contract drafted in a hand so elegant it seemed ashamed of its contents.

I signed it standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender. My name went onto the line—*Silas Brightwater*—and I watched the ink dry and thought about how a signature is just a map of a person, a coastline traced in one continuous stroke, and how easily coastlines can be redrawn.

The terms were generous to the point of insult. Full funding for the atlas's completion. New copper plates from the Harrow foundry. A stipend that would make Aunt Maren weep into her arithmetic. In exchange: all of Aldous Corven's private papers, delivered to Harrow House "for scholarly preservation," and my assurance that the final plates would follow whatever surveys the family archives provided.

"Scholarly preservation," I said to the clerk, testing the phrase like a bad tooth.

"Mr. Harrow is a great friend of history," the clerk said, with the flat conviction of a man who has never once wondered what history thinks of his employer.

I gave him Aldous's correspondence—the letters, the invoices, the dull driftwood of an old man's business. I kept back three things: the letter sewn into the folio binding, now hidden inside the hollow leg of my drafting table; the 1811 drafts with their falsified depths; and the scrap of paper where I'd copied out the shorthand symbols Aldous used in his margins. The clerk counted my surrender, found it sufficient, and left me alone with the smell of banknotes and the peculiar lightness of a man who has just sold something he cannot yet name.

Maren came by that evening and actually did the thing I'd predicted. Wept into her arithmetic. Then she cooked me a dinner involving more butter than our household had seen since before Father died, and said, "You see? It was only ever a matter of asking properly."

It was only ever a matter of kneeling, she meant. And she was right, and the dinner was delicious, and I hated every bite in a way I couldn't explain to her without explaining everything, which I could not do, because the truth would frighten her more than poverty did.

So began the double life.

* * *

By day, I was the Harrows' mapmaker. I worked at the front bench, in full view of the street, engraving plates from the official surveys—the ones with the river depths that lied. It was careful, tedious work, and I did it well, because there is no better camouflage for treachery than competence. Edmund's man called twice a week to inspect progress, a narrow fellow named Quill who smelled of ink and suspicion in equal measure. He would lean over my shoulder, hum through his nose, and say things like *"Harrow House prefers precision"* as though precision were a brand of soap they had purchased in bulk.

By night, I was someone else entirely.

I would bar the door, shutter the windows, light two candles instead of one—not for brightness but so a shadow falling across one flame would announce itself—and bring out Odile Ferran's husband's soundings. Anselm Ferran had measured the Vellmark reach in the autumn of 1810 with a lead line, a notebook, and the patience of a man who loved the river the way other men loved wives. His numbers told a story the published charts had strangled in its cradle: the channel under the old bridge pilings ran eleven feet deep where the official record claimed six. Eleven feet of water does not freeze shallow enough to shift a bridge's footing. Six feet does. Six feet made the collapse look like God's arithmetic rather than a guild ledger's.

Someone had needed the river to be shallower than it was. Someone had needed the drowned builders to look like victims of nature instead of victims of a bargain. And so the maps—my trade, my religion, my inheritance—had been made to kneel too.

Every night I copied the true soundings into the final plate.

Here I must confess to a feeling I am not proud of, though honesty is supposedly the theme of this entire miserable enterprise: there were moments, bent over the copper with my graver, when the work felt *good*. Not righteous-good, not virtue humming like a hymn, but the animal pleasure of doing your craft perfectly while hiding it from everyone. There is adrenaline in secrecy. Your hearing sharpens. Every carriage in the street becomes a Harrow agent; every knock is the watch. And when the door stays barred and the candles stay lit and the plate comes out of the acid bath with the true channel gleaming in it—eleven feet, *eleven*, you liars—you feel briefly like the cleverest criminal in Vellmark.

Then morning came, and I sat down at the front bench to engrave the lie for money, and the cleverness curdled into something with a taste. Guilt, if you want the word. It braided through everything. I ate breakfast with one hand and checked the lock with the other. I answered Maren's cheerful letters in a hand steadier than I felt. I dreamed, three nights running, of drowning builders who spoke in Aldous's voice and asked me, very politely, which version of the river I intended to draw today.

* * *

Odile came on Thursdays.

She arrived always after dark, always by the alley door, always with something wrapped in cloth—a loaf, a jar of pickled eel she insisted I liked, once an entire goose that filled the shop with the smell of roast for two days and made me feel watched by the whole street. She moved through the workshop like a woman returning to a house she'd been evicted from, touching Aldous's instruments with two fingers, reading his margins over my shoulder without asking.

It was Odile who cracked the shorthand.

Aldous had written his private notes in a cipher of his own devising—little hooks and crosses tucked between survey lines, invisible unless you knew they were words. I had spent weeks assuming they were decorative flourishes, the idle pen of an old man. Odile looked at them for perhaps four minutes and snorted.

"He learned this from Anselm," she said. "Guild marks. Before your family helped dissolve the guild, every builder signed his soundings with these." Her finger tapped a small anchor-like hook. "Depth below datum. This one"—a cross with a bar—"current direction at flood tide. And this—" she stopped.

"What?"

"This one means *measured against instruction*. Anselm used it when the guild ordered a reading taken one way and the river said another." She looked at me. "Your master used it forty times in the 1811 margins."

Forty times. I pulled the drafts out and we went through them together, her arthritic finger and my magnifying lens, and slowly the margins stopped being decoration and became testimony. Aldous had annotated the falsified charts the way a coroner annotates a body: *not as claimed*, *depth altered*, *here the record lies by five feet*, and once, in a margin beside the collapsed east pier, a single symbol repeated three times like a hammer striking—*against instruction, against instruction, against instruction.*

He had known. For thirty-six years he had known, and he had written the truth in a language only dead guildsmen read, and then he had died and left it to a Brightwater, of all people, because apparently God has a sense of humor after all.

"Your master was a coward," Odile said quietly. It was not an accusation. It was a diagnosis, delivered with the weary fairness of a woman who had buried a brave man and had thirty years to compare the two.

"Yes," I said.

"But a careful one. Cowards who write everything down are the only reason history isn't entirely written by the winners." She turned the page. "My Anselm wrote nothing down that mattered. He put his truth in the river and let the river hold it. Look what the river got to keep."

We worked until the candles guttered. She decoded; I engraved. By the third Thursday we had a rhythm, and by the fifth we had something neither of us named aloud, which was friendship built entirely on the shared project of robbing two great families of their sleep.

One night, near the end, she paused over the final plate and studied the names I was cutting along the southern bank—small, precise letters, each one a drowned guildsman, each one placed at the spot where the river took him.

"You're putting my Anselm last," she observed.

"Alphabetical."

"Liar. You're putting him last because you're afraid of what I'll say." She leaned closer, reading the letters. Then she straightened up, and her voice did something I hadn't heard it do before—it softened. "Cut him deeper than the others. He'd want to be readable from the far shore."

I cut him deeper.

* * *

The trouble with living two lives is that eventually they meet, usually at the worst possible crossroads, wearing each other's clothes.

Quill came on a gray Friday near the end of the month, earlier than his usual day, and caught me at the front bench with the false chart half-engraved—which was fine, expected, harmless. What was not fine was the state of the room behind him. I had been working late the night before and had committed a cardinal sin: the true plate was still in its oilcloth on the back bench, and the shorthand key was lying face-up beside Aldous's drafts.

Quill's eyes went to the back of the shop exactly once. It was the glance of a bookkeeper, quick and total, cataloguing. Then he returned to inspecting my depth lines, humming his little nasal hum.

"Harrow House asks," he said pleasantly, "whether Master Corven kept any working notes beyond the papers already delivered. Marginalia, drafts, that sort of thing. Mr. Edmund has taken an interest in the master's methods."

"Mapmakers scribble," I said. "Most of it's gibberish."

"Of course." He smiled. "Though Mr. Edmund says gibberish is often just writing done by someone with something to hide. His father used to say the same about ledgers." He gathered his hat. "You'll forgive me, Mr. Brightwater. It's only that a commission this size makes a family attentive. Attentiveness looks like suspicion from the outside, I know. From the inside it feels like care."

He left. I stood at the window and watched him walk down Rope Street, unhurried, pausing to speak to a fishwife, glancing back at the shop not once but twice.

That night I moved everything. The true plate went up into the ceiling cavity above the acid room, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged between joists where only a man who knew to look would find it. The shorthand key I burned and rewrote from memory, in my own invented variation, in case the original ever surfaced and someone compared hands. I slept—or lay awake pretending to—in the cot behind the drafting table, listening to the river.

And here is the hook on which the next disaster hangs, though I didn't know it yet: the river sounded wrong.

I had grown up on that sound the way Odile's husband had grown up on its measure. All winter it had muttered and groaned under its ice, the deep complaining voice of a frozen thing. But lying there in the dark, I heard something new underneath it—a loosening, a wet shifting, ice settling against ice in a way that meant the water beneath was moving faster than it should in February.

I remembered, absurdly, the almanac in the corner of the shop. Warm spell forecast through the month, the printer's boy had said when he delivered it, warmest February in twenty years, mark me.

The ice on the Vellmark had held for a century past any calendar. But rivers keep their own books, and I had spent enough nights copying Anselm Ferran's soundings to know that the river, unlike the charts, had never once been persuaded to lie.

I got up and lit a candle and looked at my work schedule pinned above the bench—the neat columns where I'd apportioned the remaining engraving across the coming six weeks, finishing well before the traditional thaw in April.

Outside, water ticked against a pilings somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

Six weeks, I had given myself. Lying there, listening, I began to suspect the river intended to give me fewer.
