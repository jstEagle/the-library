## 11. The River Rises Early

The first sign that winter meant to abandon us early was not the ice at all. It was the smell.

I noticed it on a Tuesday morning in late February, standing at the workshop door with a cup of tea going cold in my hand. Vellmark in February smells of coal smoke and frozen mud and the particular sourness of a city that has been holding its breath since November. But that morning there was something underneath it—something green and wet and impatient, like the river had exhaled after three months of holding its breath itself.

By noon the eaves were dripping. By evening, the gutters ran.

I should have celebrated. A warm spell meant I could heat the workshop without burning through my dwindling store of coal, could keep my fingers supple enough to hold an engraving burin for more than an hour at a stretch. Instead I stood at the window and felt the cold arrive in a place no stove could reach.

The ice was leaving. And the ice had been my clock.

* * *

Let me explain the arithmetic of a guilty man, because it is stranger arithmetic than Maren's ever was.

When I had accepted Edmund Harrow's commission—his generous, poisonous, perfectly polite commission—I had set myself a private deadline alongside his public one. He wanted the atlas finished by spring, when the printers' guild would take up its annual contracts and his family's money could buy its way onto the title page. I intended to finish it by then too, but not for him. I intended to finish *the other thing* by then: the final plate, the true plate, the one with Odile Ferran's soundings cut into the copper where the official charts said only smooth water and honest depths.

Spring, I reasoned, would be my moment. The guild hall would fill. The printers would gather. And if I timed it rightly—if I unveiled the plate before Edmund's lawyers could descend like well-dressed crows—the truth would be loose in the city before anyone could catch it and stuff it back in the drawer.

But spring, in Vellmark, arrived when the ice went out. Everyone knew it. The whole calendar of the river city hung on that single event: the shudder, the groan, the great white plain below the bluffs cracking like a dropped plate and sliding seaward in grinding bergs. Until the ice broke, the guild hall stayed half-empty, the printers stayed cautious, the Harrows stayed patient. The ice was a wall between me and my reckoning, and I had counted on every day of it.

Now the wall was melting six weeks early.

I sat down at the workbench and looked at the final plate, half-engraved, resting under its cloth like a patient who hadn't yet decided whether to live. The true river took shape across it line by line—the deep channel hugging the north bank where the published charts showed shallows, the false shallows marked near the south piers where the Brightwater surveys had lied men into their graves. Odile's husband's numbers. Forty years old and still more honest than anything the city had paid for since.

Six weeks of work remained in that plate. Perhaps five, if my hands held steady and my nerve did likewise. And the river, apparently, intended to give me neither.

"Stop staring at it," I told myself, "and start cutting."

It is a measure of how far I had come from the man who had spent three months tracing coastlines he'd never see that this advice actually worked.

* * *

Odile came to the shop twice that week, moving slower than usual, her breath short on the stairs. She was seventy if she was a day, and she crossed Vellmark in all weather because her own house had become unbearable to her—not the house itself, but the district. Salters' Row sat low along the archive quarter, in the crook of the river where the current slowed and dropped its silt and had been dropping it for two hundred years. Every spring the meltwater found it. Usually the floods were a nuisance: water in the coal cellar, furniture up on bricks, boats in the street for a festive week.

"You've seen the ice?" she asked, unwinding her scarf with fingers that had once wound sounding lines off the stern of her husband's boat.

"I've smelled it."

"My grandfather used to say you can smell a bad breakup before you see it." She lowered herself into the chair I kept for her now, the good one, near the stove. "He said the river gets a temper in a warm February. Thaws by day, freezes by night. Ice on top of water on top of ice. Then one morning the whole mess decides to move at once."

"And you think this will be one of those mornings."

"I think," Odile said, "that I have watched sixty breakups from that window on Salters' Row, and I have never heard the ice talking this early. It talks at night now. Groaning. Like an old man getting out of bed." She fixed me with eyes that missed nothing, least of all the way my gaze kept drifting to the covered plate. "And I think you've stopped sleeping, Silas Brightwater, which makes your judgment about as reliable as a wet chart. How much is left?"

"Five weeks of engraving. Less if I rush, and rushing ruins copper."

"Then don't rush. Hide it instead."

I looked at her.

"You heard me." She leaned forward. "If the water comes to Salters' Row, it'll come to the low streets after—Ferry Lane, the archive cellars, everything below the embankment. Where is the safest ground in this city? The bluffs. Harrow House sits on the bluffs." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "The rich always build where the river can't reach them. It's the one wisdom they've never needed to fake."

"So I move the plate."

"You move the plate, and the soundings, and Aldous's folio, and every scrap of paper that matters, up out of this shop and into somewhere dry. You finish the work wherever the river can't argue with you. Because I promise you, boy—" and here she used the word the way she reserved for moments of real affection or real alarm, and I couldn't always tell which— "if that plate drowns, it doesn't drown alone. It takes Tomas's name with it. It takes forty years of my waiting. The river has drowned enough truth in this town. Don't hand it yours."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I did next, because it shows precisely how a conscience operates when it has been living a double life for long enough: I panicked about the wrong things first.

My first thought was not of the plate. It was of the letter—or rather, of the absence of the letter, since I had burned the copy weeks ago and kept only what lived in my memory. My second thought was of the drafts, months of work, the fair copies of plates one through nine that constituted my visible, commissionable, Edmund-approved labors. My third thought was of the shop itself, the presses, the type, Master Corven's tools that I had no right to love as much as I did.

Only fourth came Odile's house. Only fifth came Odile.

That ordering shames me now, and I set it down here without varnish, because the whole lesson of that winter was that corruption does not announce itself with trumpets. It arrives as triage. It whispers that some things are simply more important than others, and it lets you do the ranking yourself, and it never tells you what the ranking costs until the bill comes due.

So: I spent two days moving papers.

I carried the true plate wrapped in oilcloth to the loft above the print floor, wedged behind a false panel Master Corven had built years ago—for what purpose, I wondered now, and didn't wonder hard enough. I moved the soundings. I moved the folio. I made lists of what could be replaced and what could not, and I discovered that the list of irreplaceables was shorter than I feared and heavier than I could lift: the plate, the soundings, Odile's memory, my own.

On the third day I walked down to Salters' Row to see the river for myself.

* * *

You grow up beside a frozen river and you forget it is alive. That is the trick of ice. For three months the Vellmark is a road, a fact, a white silence you can walk across. Children slide on it. Men cut blocks of it for the ice houses. It seems as permanent and as obedient as stone.

It is none of those things. It is a held breath, and that week I watched the breath start to go.

The channel nearest the current had gone black—open water, steaming faintly in the cold air like something breathing. Out from the banks, the ice had rotted to the color of old candle wax, honeycombed, sagging. And everywhere, under everything, the sound: a deep irregular groaning, like a giant shifting in sleep, punctuated by cracks that rang off the bluffs and sent dogs barking in every yard of the low town.

Men stood along the embankment watching it the way men watch a horse they've bet against.

"Early," said one, a bargeman I half-knew. "Damn early. '11 was early too."

I turned. "What did you say?"

"The flood year." He spat over the rail, an old habit aimed at nothing. "'11. Warm spell in February, just like this. Ice went out ahead of itself and took half the low town with it." He squinted at the black water. "Course, that year the bridge went too. Bad foundations, they said." He glanced at me sidelong, and something flickered there—recognition, maybe, of my name if he knew it, or just the instinct of a working man scenting a gentleman asking questions. "Your people would know. Brightwaters had the contracts."

"They did," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me more than the engraving ever had.

"Bad luck, all round," he said, and turned back to watch the ice talk.

Bad luck. There it was again, the word that had been doing forty years of work in this city, load-bearing as any pier. Bad foundations. Bad luck. Bad ice. Everything bad except the men who had bought the plans cheap while the builders drowned, and sold them back dear, and called the arrangement commerce.

I walked home along the embankment with the river's groaning following me like a conscience with a voice.

* * *

That night the temperature fell, as Odile's grandfather's law demanded, and the refreeze made everything worse. Water that had begun to run found itself trapped under new ice, and the pressure built in the dark, and the sounds coming off the river were no longer groans but reports—sharp cracks like distant cannon, one after another, all night long. I lay in the loft above the workshop listening to them and doing sums I couldn't make come out right.

Five weeks of engraving. Perhaps ten days of ice remaining, if the warm spell returned, and every sign said it would. The gap between those numbers was where my whole design lived or died.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself say plainly, even to myself: I had planned to choose my moment. To stand in the guild hall with the plate finished and polished and the room full, and to speak. Now the river was going to choose for me. If the ice went out early, the guild would convene early, the printers would settle their contracts early, and Edmund Harrow's money would close around the atlas like a fist—and my unfinished plate would be a piece of engraved copper worth exactly nothing, because testimony delivered late is just gossip with better penmanship.

Or worse. If the flooding came to Salters' Row—

I sat up in the dark.

Odile's house sat in the crook. Odile's house held the original soundings—no, I had those. But it held Tomas Ferran's letters, his instruments, forty years of a widow's careful keeping. It held Odile herself, seventy years old, on a street that turned to canal most springs, in a year promising to be no most-spring.

I dressed, lit a lamp, and sat at the bench until dawn, not engraving but planning, which is the coward's version of work and the planner's version of prayer.

* * *

The warm spell returned on Friday and did not leave. By Sunday the eaves ran all night. By Monday the river had risen four feet, brown and muscular between its shrinking shelves of ice, carrying whole trees southward like kindling. The bargemen pulled their boats out. The cellar-keepers along Ferry Lane began hauling stock upstairs and joking about it in the way people joke when the joke has stopped being funny but quitting it would be worse.

Tuesday, I went to Salters' Row with bread, a bottle of the decent brandy I'd been saving for a victory that kept receding, and an argument I'd rehearsed the whole way down.

Odile opened the door before I knocked.

"You're here to tell me to leave," she said.

"I'm here to help you pack," I said. "The leaving was going to be your idea. I find that works better with you."

For a moment I thought she'd shut the door on the argument if not on me. Then she stepped aside. Inside, the house was already half-prepared—she had beaten me to it, of course, had probably beaten me to it by days. Furniture up on bricks. Carpets rolled. A trunk packed with photographs and papers, sitting by the door like a dog that knows.

"But," she said, before I could gloat, "I'm not going to the relief rooms on Chapel Hill, so save your breath. I'm going to my sister's in Marlow Ford, and I'm going tomorrow, and I'm taking Tomas's letters with me, and that is the end of it."

"Good," I said.

She looked at me suspiciously. "Good?"

"You expected a fight. I can't afford one. I need ten days, Odile. Ten days of steady hands and no distractions, and the plate is done. So yes—go to Marlow Ford, take the letters, be safe, and let me work."

She studied me for a long moment, this woman who had spent forty years reading water and had lately taken up reading me. "Ten days," she said. "And then?"

"And then the ice goes out, and the guild convenes, and I stand up in front of this city with the true river in my hands."

"And if the Harrows get to the printers first? If Edmund's money buys the room before you can fill it?"

"Then I'll fill it anyway. Truth doesn't need a lease, Odile. It just needs a door open long enough to walk through."

Something moved in her face—hope, or fear for me, or possibly indigestion; at her age she claimed not to distinguish. She put her hand flat against my cheek, briefly, the way you'd touch a son. "Ten days," she said. "Work fast, boy. And Silas—" she paused at the door, scarf half-wound, suddenly looking every hour of her age and every year of her waiting, "—when the ice breaks, you'll hear it from here. Whole city hears it. Sounds like the sky cracking open. When you hear it, don't stop to admire it. Whatever state the plate is in—finished or not—you bring it to high ground. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar," she said fondly, and went inside to finish packing.

* * *

I worked. God knows I worked. Eighteen hours a day at the copper, burin singing, acid biting, the true river emerging line by line from the metal like something surfacing from a long dive. The names of the drowned guildsmen went in along the north bank in small clear letters, each one checked against Odile's memory and the parish rolls: Ferran, T., first among them. Dole, M. Ketterick, J. and J., brothers. Eleven names. Eleven places where the water was deep and the charts said shallow and the Brightwater surveys said *fate*.

On the ninth day, with the plate three-quarters done and the river lapping at the top of the embankment stairs, a boy in Harrow livery appeared at the workshop door with a note sealed in green wax.

I opened it with hands still smelling of acid.

*Mr. Brightwater — My agents report the river rising faster than the almanacs promised. I trust your work proceeds safely and above the waterline. I confess some curiosity as to the final plate's progress; my grandfather always said the last page of any book tells you what the author truly believed. Do call at Harrow House at your convenience. — E.H.*

I read it three times. It was courteous. It was generous. It was, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the weather, the note of a man who had begun to count.

Outside, the river groaned against the ice, and somewhere far upstream, with a crack that rolled across the city like the opening of a very long account, something gave way.
