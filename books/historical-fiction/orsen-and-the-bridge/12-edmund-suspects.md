## 12. Edmund Suspects

My father taught me two things about paper before he died: that it burns faster than reputations, and that a man who understands ledgers never needs to raise his voice. He said this while tapping the Harrow accounts with one finger, the way another man might tap a horse he was deciding whether to buy. I was nineteen. I thought he was being profound. Later I understood he was simply reciting the family catechism, the one we don't write down because writing things down is precisely the problem.

I have inherited his desk, his accounts, and his habit of counting. It is the third inheritance that has ruined my sleep.

It began, as most disasters in this city do, with a map.

* * *

The commission had seemed simple enough in February. Silas Brightwater — the mapmaker's orphan apprentice, if you'll forgive the phrase; he is neither orphan nor truly apprentice anymore, but Vellmark enjoys its shorthand — had accepted my terms. He would complete Aldous Corven's atlas under Harrow patronage. In exchange, Corven's private papers would come to us for "archiving," which is what we call burying when we want to be polite about it.

I confess I liked him. That was my first error. A man who likes his adversary starts making allowances for him, and allowances are how empires leak.

The second error was curiosity. Father warned against that too, though he framed it as thrift: every question costs something, Edmund, so only ask the ones you can afford to answer. But there are questions that arrive uninvited, like creditors, and by March I had several.

The first arrived on a Tuesday, in the form of a sounding.

Silas submitted his working drafts to me monthly — a condition of the funding, ostensibly for review, actually for custody. I am not a cartographer. My knowledge of rivers extends to knowing which side of one our warehouses sit on and which side of one certain guildsmen drowned on. But I can read numbers. Numbers were my father's language and they are mine, and when a man who measures rivers for a living submits a table of depths that disagrees with itself by eleven feet at the Weir Bend, I notice.

Eleven feet. Not two, not six — not the sort of discrepancy that sloppiness produces. Sloppiness scatters. Eleven feet is a decision.

I set the draft beside the official chart — the 1812 survey, engraved and blessed and sitting behind glass in the Exchange as though it were scripture. The official chart said the channel at Weir Bend ran deep and true, fourteen fathoms at low water, plenty of depth for the great bridge piers. Brightwater's new draft said nine. Nine fathoms. Shallow enough to ground a barge. Shallow enough, I thought, feeling something cold walk up my spine, to crack a pier in a flood year.

But here was the thing that kept me at the desk past midnight: Brightwater hadn't corrected the old survey. He'd reproduced it faithfully in the historical plates — the ones covering 1809 through 1811 — exactly as published, errors intact. And then, in the modern plates, the river simply *changed*. No note. No correction. As though between 1811 and 1847 the Weir Bend had quietly silted itself into a different shape out of embarrassment.

Maps do not lie by accident. They lie on instruction.

* * *

I did what any sensible heir does with an inconvenient suspicion: I took it to dinner and pretended it was conversation.

My mother presided over the table with the serenity of a woman who has never once wondered where the money comes from, which is either ignorance or discipline of a very high order. My uncle Gaspard held forth on the new tariffs. And across from me sat my cousin Lysbet, who married into the Verlaine shipping money and therefore knows everything, because shipping families keep score of everything, including sins.

"The Corven atlas," I said, to no one in particular. "You remember the old man? Surveyed half the river before his hands went."

"Remember the scandal, more like," Uncle Gaspard said. "Guild man gone strange at the end. Kept to himself. Died alone over his drawings."

"He wasn't always strange," Lysbet said. She has a way of saying things flatly that makes them land harder than accusations. "He worked for Odile Ferran's husband, before the flood. Tomas Ferran. One of the soundings men."

"Ferran," I repeated, and heard my own voice go carefully neutral, which in my family is the same as going pale.

"Young Harrow doing history now?" Uncle Gaspard laughed. "Careful. History in this town is like the river mud. Best left where it lies or it stinks up the house."

Everyone laughed. I laughed too. I have a good laugh; I practiced it during the years Father drilled me in the art of hearing bad news without moving my face. Inside, the cold thing walked further up my spine and settled between my shoulder blades.

Because I knew the shape of 1811 the way every Harrow knows it — not the facts but the furniture of them. The flood winter. The bridge down. The guild broken, its men scattered, its plans and surveys sold off cheap in the spring to men with ready cash. My grandfather had been twenty-six that year and had come out of it holding the river's papers. The family story — told at firesides in the tone reserved for providence — was that the Harrows had *saved* the engineering. Rescued the plans from chaos. Bought them, yes, but bought them honestly, from desperate men, and the desperate men should have been grateful.

Desperate men. I had never once asked why they were desperate, or why the desperation clustered so conveniently around the winter our fortune crystallized.

That night I opened the locked drawer my father had shown me once and never again — the drawer he tapped when he gave his sermon about paper. Inside were the 1811 correspondence files, tied in ribbon gone brown as tobacco. Grandfather's hand, brisk and pleased with itself. I read until the candle drowned in its own wax.

Most of it was ordinary commerce. Some of it was worse than ordinary. And one letter — from a name I didn't recognize, a survey clerk named Pell, writing in the nervous italics of a man who wants to be paid for his conscience rather than exercise it — mentioned "the Ferran soundings." Mentioned that the original winter measurements had been "amended for the record." Mentioned that Master Ferran had objected loudly and been, in Pell's careful phrase, "no longer consulted."

Amended for the record. Eleven feet amended, I would have bet the warehouse district.

I put the letters back, locked the drawer, and sat in my father's chair in the dark listening to the ice grind against the embankment pilings. Somewhere out there, a mapmaker was engraving the truth into copper plate, plate by patient plate, and had chosen to reproduce the lie faithfully in the historical sections — not because he believed it, but because a corrected lie is a confession, and Silas Brightwater wanted the whole world to see both versions side by side and do the arithmetic themselves.

He wasn't finishing an atlas. He was building an indictment and calling it a gift, and I was paying for the copper.

* * *

You will want to know what I felt. People always do, afterward — they want the villain to have felt villainous, or better yet, to have felt nothing, because nothing is easier to condemn. What I felt was recognition, which is far more uncomfortable.

I thought of my father's finger on the ledger. I thought of the glass case in the Exchange where the 1812 chart hangs like an icon. I thought of my mother's serenity and understood, suddenly and completely, that it was not ignorance. Serenity like that is built. It is maintained daily, the way a dike is maintained, by people who know exactly what the water wants.

We are not liars, the Harrows. That was the astonishing discovery of that dark week in March. We are archivists. We keep the truth meticulously — wrapped, ribboned, locked in drawers — and we simply decline to let it circulate. There is a word for people who preserve a document while preventing anyone from reading it, and I suppose historians have it, and I suppose the word is not flattering.

And Brightwater — I found myself thinking about him at odd hours, which was its own kind of trouble. Silas Brightwater, whose family name sat on the wrong side of this same wound. Because that was the other thing the letters showed me, the thing I turned over like a coin worn smooth: the Brightwaters hadn't merely profited. They'd brokered. Old Josiah Brightwater had been the friendly face between the drowning guild and the hungry buyers, and had taken his percentage in perpetuity. The Brightwater fortune and the Harrow fortune were not parallel fortunes. They were braided. One rope.

Two heirs, then, standing on the same buried thing. He had dug. I had been handed a shovel and told it was a garden trowel.

* * *

I tested him. I'm not proud of the method, but pride is a luxury I suspended around the time I started reading my grandfather's mail.

I returned one of his monthly drafts with annotations — innocuous ones, mostly, the kind a patron makes to justify his patronage — and one deliberate provocation. On the Weir Bend plate, in the margin, in my best imitation of idle curiosity, I wrote: *"Shall we reconcile these depths with the 1812 record for consistency?"*

Reconcile. A gentle word. A bookkeeper's word. What I was really asking was: shall we make sure your atlas agrees with the lie?

His reply came back within the week, and I have it still, because I keep everything now — I've become my father's drawer. He wrote:

*"With respect, Mr. Harrow, reconciliation is the surveyor's task and the historian's temptation. The 1812 record is what it is; my soundings are what they are. An atlas that reconciles them has stopped observing and started editorializing. I prefer to let the river disagree with itself. Rivers usually have their reasons."*

I read that sentence perhaps forty times. Let the river disagree with itself. Do you see the elegance of it? He had answered my question without answering it, confirmed my suspicion without confirming anything, and managed, in three lines, to tell me that he knew I knew, without ever once risking a word that could be quoted back at him.

I sat with the letter and I laughed — my real laugh this time, alone in the study, the ugly one — because it was magnificent, and because I had just realized I was no longer funding an atlas. I was paying protection money to a manuscript.

* * *

March loosened. The eaves began dripping in the mornings. Down by the archive district, the shopkeepers stacked sandbags with the resigned competence of people who do this every year, and the river rose a hand's width and grumbled about it.

And I made my rounds. This is what an heir does: he visits. The printers' guild hall, to renew our standing (and to note, idly, which presses could run a folio atlas and how fast). The Exchange, to stand in front of the glass case for ten minutes while clerks pretended not to watch me watch the chart. And once — I tell myself it was route efficiency, and I have never believed it — past the Ferran woman's street in the low quarter, where the water stood in the gutters already and an old woman's window glowed the color of a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

Odile Ferran. Widow of the man whose soundings had been "amended." Keeper, if Pell's letter meant what I feared, of whatever the amendment had been amending away.

If she talked to Brightwater, the atlas would not merely imply. It would testify.

I could have sent someone. Of course I could have; we are the Harrows, sending someone is practically our coat of arms. A quiet visit, a rent increase, a fire hazard noticed, an offer of a room in a drier part of town that would somehow never quite materialize. The machinery exists. I grew up inside it the way fish grow up inside water, and the horror of that month was discovering that I could feel the water now — could see it, taste it, describe its temperature — and still not know how to breathe anywhere else.

Instead I wrote another note to Silas Brightwater requesting the next batch of drafts a fortnight early, citing printer scheduling. Which was even true. That is the trick of it, the thing nobody warns you: you can be honest right up until the moment honesty becomes the weapon, and then you deploy it, and it works beautifully, and you understand that you are your grandfather's grandson after all.

* * *

The drafts arrived early, which told me something too. A guilty man delays. A man with nothing to hide complies promptly. A man who wants you to look closely at a particular page sends it early and lets you find it yourself.

I found it myself.

The final plate section — the modern river, the honest river — had grown annotations. Tiny ones, marginal, in a cramped hand I now recognized as Corven's. Dates. Initials. A shorthand I couldn't read but could count: seven sets of initials along the Weir Bend margin, each followed by a year, all years 1811 or earlier, none of the men carrying surnames that appeared anywhere in the guild's surviving rolls.

Seven names that had been subtracted from every record I could access. Seven initials that Silas Brightwater had added back.

I spread the drafts across my father's desk — across the desk where he had taught me that paper burns faster than reputations — and I understood at last what was actually being built in that shabby workshop on the lane, and who it was being built against, and that "against" included a young Harrow who had signed a generous contract in good faith, or in what he had then believed was good faith, or in what he now suspected had been vanity wearing good faith's coat.

Father's voice, in memory, tapping the ledger: *A man who understands ledgers never needs to raise his voice.* But Father had never faced a ledger that fought back — one where the columns were drowned men and the sums were names, and the audit had been running, patiently, for thirty-six years.

The candle guttered. Outside, the river made a sound I had never heard it make in March — a long, splintering groan, like a door being forced somewhere far downstream — and I looked at the initials on the margin, seven small witnesses marching along the bank of the Weir Bend, and I thought:

He is going to print them. And when he prints them, every name in this city will have to choose which side of the plate it stands on.

Including mine.

I blew out the candle, locked the drafts in my father's drawer — beneath his letters, where the two truths could get acquainted — and went to the window. Far off toward the low quarter, the ice was talking to itself in the dark, cracking and settling, cracking and settling, like a very patient man clearing his throat before he speaks.

There were, I calculated, perhaps three weeks left before the thaw took the choice out of everyone's hands.

Three weeks, and two inheritances, and one river coming up fast under all of us.
