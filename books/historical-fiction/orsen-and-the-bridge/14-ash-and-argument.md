## 14. Ash and Argument

My aunt found the copies on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days she came to dust things that were not hers and disapprove of things that were.

I had made a mistake, the kind of mistake a careful man makes only when he has stopped sleeping properly. I kept the true soundings in three places: one set sewn into my waistcoat lining, one memorized — I could recite them like scripture by then, and often did, silently, on the walk to Harrow House, like a rosary of drowned men — and one set copied onto drafting vellum, hidden beneath the false bottom of Aldous's old plan chest. The plan chest had a false bottom because Aldous Corven had been the sort of man who believed every honest object deserved a dishonest drawer. I had assumed the same principle would extend to its protection.

It did not extend to Maren Brightwater. Nothing ever had.

She discovered it at eleven in the morning while I was at Harrow House producing beautifully fraudulent drafts for Edmund Harrow's inspection, and she was waiting for me when I returned at four, sitting in Aldous's chair with her hands folded in her lap and the vellum spread across her knees like an accusation she had been rehearsing all afternoon.

"Silas," she said, in the voice she used for funerals and for telling me my collar was crooked, "sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit *down*."

I sat. There is no arguing with a woman who has your treason spread across her knees.

"You've been copying the old surveys," she said. "The real ones. The ones from the widow's house." She turned the vellum over as though hoping to find something kinder printed on the reverse. "And you've been doing it while taking Harrow money. While eating Harrow bread, Silas, in Harrow's own house."

"That's roughly the shape of it."

"Do you know what they do to forgers?"

"I'm not forging anything. That's rather the point."

"You're forging *trust*." She said it the way other people said *fire*. "You sit in that young man's parlor and let him believe you're working for him, and the whole time—" She pressed her lips together. I watched her decide to be reasonable, which in Maren was always more frightening than when she decided otherwise. "It isn't too late. That is what I keep coming back to, Silas. It isn't too late. Give these to me."

"No."

"I'll take them to Edmund myself. I'll tell him you were verifying the old records out of scholarly diligence and that you found nothing. I'll tell him whatever needs saying. I have been lying to creditors since before you could write, child; I can lie to one soft-handed boy in a good coat."

"They're not forgeries, Maren. They're evidence."

"*They're paper.*" She stood up, and the vellum slid off her lap and she caught it against her chest, clutching it the way she once clutched our father's ledgers during the year the bailiffs came. "Paper can be burned. Paper can be given away. Paper does not have to hang anyone. Do you understand what happens to us if this comes out? Not to the Harrows. To *us*. To the name on your shop door."

"It's Aldous Corven's name on my shop door."

"And yours under it! In smaller letters, yes, but ink is ink!" She was pacing now, between the drafting table and the window, a tight circuit, a dog pacing a kennel it had outgrown. "I have spent forty years keeping this family upright. Forty years of arithmetic, Silas. Of knowing exactly how much humiliation we could afford in any given quarter. And you want to spend everything we have on—on *dead men*. On a flood nobody living remembers."

"Somebody remembers," I said quietly. "Odile Ferran remembers."

Maren stopped pacing. For a moment I thought I had wounded her past speech, which would have been a mercy. It was not a mercy.

"Give them to me," she said again, softer now, and softness from Maren was the most dangerous register she owned. "Or I will give them myself, and I will tell Edmund Harrow where I found them, and I will tell him you've been deceiving him since the day he shook your hand. And then you may explain yourself to his lawyers, and to the printers' guild, and to whoever else asks, because I will not be standing beside you when you do it."

I want to be fair to her. This account will not be fair to her unless I make it so deliberately, and fairness does not come naturally to a man whose family fortune was built on a flooded bridge.

Here is what I knew about Maren before that afternoon: she had raised me after our father died, on money that arrived late and left early; she had a genius for making a shilling stand up straight and salute; she had never once, in my entire life, told me a story about herself that ended with her wanting something. Here is what I learned that afternoon: none of that was generosity. All of it was fortification.

"Maren," I said. "Why? Truly. Not the arithmetic. You've done the arithmetic out loud already. Why does this frighten you more than debt ever did? We've been poor. We've been poor together, for years, and you never once looked like this."

She sat back down. Slowly, the way old women sit down when their body has voted ahead of their pride.

"You don't remember the winter your father died," she said.

"I was seven."

"You were seven and I was twenty-three and I remember every hour of it." She looked at her hands. They are good hands, strong, a bookkeeper's hands, hands that had held a household together with string and stubbornness. "Do you know why Father owed money at the end? Everyone says he gambled, or drank, or was simply weak. He wasn't weak. He was *honest*. He was the honest Brightwater, Silas. The first in two generations. And honesty, in our family, is expensive."

"What do you mean?"

"After 1811, after the bridge and the guild and all of it, there were debts. Real ones. The family had bought the plans cheap and sold them dear, but the buying itself cost money, and the men who helped — clerks, aldermen, a magistrate or two — they had to be paid to forget. Grandfather paid them. And then Grandfather died, and those same men came to Father with the notes, and Father read them, and he understood what they were for. What they'd *bought*." Her voice went very flat, which I had learned long ago meant the feeling underneath was too large to be permitted above stairs. "He said he wouldn't pay another shilling to bury it. He said the family had already paid enough in the only coin that mattered."

"And?"

"And they called the loans. Every one of them, within a season. The house on Cordwainer Street, the warehouses, the annuities — all of it structured so that one refusal toppled everything. Your father spent nine years being ruined slowly and publicly, Silas, and everyone in Vellmark watched and nodded and said what a shame, such a promising man, and not one soul told him why. Because he wouldn't say why either. He took the ruin rather than confess what the ruin was *protecting*."

I said nothing. The workshop clock ticked. Somewhere outside, the river did its patient work under the ice.

"He died in February," Maren said. "In a rented room above a chandler's, because we'd sold the last of the houses in November. He died owing eleven pounds to the chandler and he made me promise to pay it, and I did, it took me two years, and I paid it *because a promise was the only thing we had left that was clean*." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were dry, which somehow was worse. "So do not speak to me about clean things. I have seen what happens to a Brightwater who reaches for one. I buried him. I was twenty-three years old and I buried my brother because he wanted to be honest, and the world charged him full price."

"The world," I said, "charged *him*. The men who called the loans walked away rich. That's not the price of honesty, Maren. That's a fine levied on it, by the guilty."

"Don't you dare philosophize at me."

"Someone has to. You've been doing arithmetic for forty years and calling it wisdom."

That landed. I saw it land — a small flinch, quickly mastered, but I had spent a childhood reading her face the way I now read soundings, and there was no mistaking where the line ran deep.

"I am trying," she said, each word placed down like a heavy crate, "to save the last thing your father left."

"He left a promise too. Did you ever wonder what it was?" I hadn't known until I said it; the words came up from somewhere below craft, below strategy. "He wouldn't pay to bury it. He took the ruin instead. Maren — he was on our side of this argument. He lost everything rather than hand the papers over, and we're sitting here deciding whether to hand them over for a great deal less."

She stared at me. And then she laughed — one short, awful syllable of it, without any humor in it whatsoever.

"You think he was brave." She shook her head. "Oh, Silas. He wasn't brave. He was *tired*. There's a difference, and the difference is that tired men die and leave the bill to their sisters." She gathered the vellum off her lap, squared its edges with a bookkeeper's precision, and held it out to me — not offering it, exactly. Returning it. A test, maybe. Or a surrender she couldn't yet pronounce. "Take them."

I took them. Our fingers didn't touch.

"If you go to Harrow House tomorrow," I said, "if you carry anything of mine to that house, then everything Father paid — the whole ruin, the rented room, the eleven pounds — goes toward nothing. It buys the same silence Grandfather bought. Another generation of interest."

"And if you keep them?" she whispered. "What do they buy *you*, except the same room above the chandler's?"

"Maybe. Probably." I looked down at the true river in my hands, every depth recorded, every drowned man's name waiting along its banks in Aldous's shorthand. "But the room would be mine. Chosen. There's a difference, and I've finally learned the difference, and I got it from watching you."

She didn't answer. She put on her coat with her back to me, and her shoulders were very straight, and at the door she stopped without turning around.

"The ice is going out early this year," she said. "Everyone at market is saying so. Three weeks early, maybe more. The low streets will flood." A pause. "She lives on Tanner's Row, doesn't she. The widow."

"Yes."

Maren stood there a moment longer, and I understood — or believed I understood — that some part of her was fighting the same battle I was, on a different front, with older weapons. Then she pulled the door shut behind her harder than she needed to, which in Maren's language was a kind of blessing.

I sat alone in the workshop until the light went blue and then grey and then gone. The vellum lay on my knee. Below it, through the floorboards, the cellar where Aldous's press still smelled of ink and iron. Behind me, the plan chest with its honest drawer and its dishonest twin.

I should have felt victorious. I had kept the papers; I had won the argument; I had even, in some private ledger of my own, scored points off a woman who had scored points off me for thirty years.

Instead I felt the way I imagine my father felt in February, in the rented room: certain, and alone, and aware — with the terrible clarity of the nearly ruined — that the ice on the river was going out early, and that everything I loved was built on low ground.

I wrapped the vellum in oilcloth and put it in the cellar, and I banked the fire, and I did not sleep.

Somewhere across the dark city, on Tanner's Row, the river was already whispering against the underside of the ice. And somewhere nearer than that — though I would not learn it for two more nights — a match had already been struck.
