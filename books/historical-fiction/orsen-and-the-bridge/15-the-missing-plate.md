## 15. The Missing Plate

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a man does at four in the morning, hunched over a table with a cold lamp and a memory he doesn't trust. It is not the arithmetic of money, which I had long since failed at. It is the arithmetic of loss: how many lines did I lose to the river, and how many of those lines can I raise from the dead?

The flood in the archive district took eleven months of drafting. Not all of it — I'd kept the finished plates for the northern counties locked in a chest at Maren's — but the southern sheets, the ones I'd been reworking under Edmund Harrow's commission, went soft as bread in the water. Paper remembers water the way a widow remembers a wedding. You can dry it, press it, iron it flat, and still it holds the shape of its drowning.

I had hung the ruined sheets on a line across the workshop like laundry, forty pages of ghost-river fluttering when I opened the door. Odile Ferran stood among them the morning after the flood, touching one with a fingertip as if greeting an acquaintance.

"They'll do," she said.

"They're ruined."

"Ruined isn't gone." She turned to me, and her eyes had the flat shine of river ice — something that looks solid until you test it. "You think my husband's soundings survived sixty years by staying dry? Josiah wrote them twice. Once in ink for the guild, once pricked into tin so the ink could rot and the truth couldn't. Ink is a promise. Tin is a fact. Learn the difference, Mr. Brightwater, or your atlas will be another promise."

* * *

We worked that way for nine days.

By day I was Edmund Harrow's hired hand, producing clean, honest-looking drafts of a dishonest river for a client who suspected — I could feel him suspecting through every polite note his secretary delivered — that the work in my shop contained more than he'd paid for. By night I sat with an eighty-year-old woman at a table lit by two candles and learned to read Josiah Ferran's tin.

The pricked sheets were beautiful in the way that only necessity is beautiful. Every sounding — every measured depth of the Vellmark channel from Greyling Bend down to the Salt Gate — was a pinhole in metal, arranged in columns, annotated in a shorthand Aldous Corven had evidently borrowed and expanded. Letters within letters. A *d* crossed with a tide-mark meant depth at ebb; the same mark un-crossed meant flood. A cluster of three dots below a number meant *measured by line*, and two meant *guessed by lead from the bank* — and Josiah, Odile told me, never guessed.

"He said a mapmaker who guesses is a liar with better handwriting."

"Aldous used to say almost exactly that."

"I know," she said, and let the silence sit there between us like a third person at the table. "That's why I employed him. And that's why what he did after — what he *didn't* do — cut deeper than anything the Harrows ever touched. The Harrows were always wolves. Aldous was a sheepdog who lay down with them."

She didn't say it cruelly. That was the worst of it. She said it the way she read the tins: precisely, without decoration, because decoration was for people who had time to be believed gently.

* * *

Reconstruction is not copying. That was the first lesson of those nights, and it took me three days to stop fighting it.

My instinct, trained into me by ten years at Aldous's elbow, was to reproduce — to make the lost page identical to the page that existed before the water took it. But the water hadn't taken a page. It had taken a version. The drafts I'd lost were the commissioned drafts, the ones drawn to Edmund's specifications, which were themselves drawn — though Edmund perhaps didn't fully know it — to the specifications laid down in 1811 by men who needed the river shallow at the wrong places and deep at the convenient ones. To reconstruct those faithfully would have been to rebuild the lie in finer type.

So I rebuilt differently. I started from Josiah's tin and worked forward instead of backward, letting the true soundings dictate the contours rather than the other way around. Where the official charts showed six fathoms off Coalheap Point, Josiah had found nine and a half — deep enough, in 1811, that a bridge pier set properly on the guild's original plan would have held. The published charts showed a gravel bar where Josiah's line showed open channel; they showed bedrock where he'd found silt; they showed the current running east where he'd logged it stubbornly west, the whole river bent a degree or two out of true, just enough to make the bridge's collapse look like the river's temper rather than anyone's arithmetic.

Someone had edited history, and then mapped the edit. And the edit had been signed — I'd found the initials weeks before, in the survey folio's margin, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

*B.H.* Bartholomew Harrow. My family's partner. My grandfather's benefactor. The man whose money had bought the Brightwater name out of a debtors' list and into a house on Lantern Hill.

"You keep looking at that margin," Odile said one night, "the way my husband looked at a knot in good oak. Like you're deciding whether to plane it out or build around it."

"I'm deciding whether to engrave it."

"Engrave what?"

"The initials. In the plate itself. Along the bank where the falsified chart begins."

She put down her magnifier. For a long moment she studied me the way she might study a sounding — testing whether the bottom was real or whether I was guessing.

"Do it," she said finally. "But if you're going to indict a man on copper, Mr. Brightwater, you'd best be prepared to stand in front of the plate while strangers judge your hand. Because that's what happens. A map is testimony. Whoever reads it will believe they were there. You will be making them *all* witnesses."

* * *

Engraving copper is slow work even when your hands are steady, and mine were not steady. Nine days of reconstruction, weeks of double life, a night of waist-deep floodwater — the tremor lived in my fingers now like a tenant who paid no rent. So I did what Aldous taught me to do with difficult plates: I worked in passes. First the outline, light as breath. Then the hydrography, the true depths, each figure checked against the tin twice and once against my memory of Aldous's own 1811-era drafts, which I now understood had contained the corrections all along, buried under later overwriting like bones under a paved road. Then the lettering, which is where a map stops being science and becomes argument.

I gave the river its true channel. I gave the banks their true names — not the names the city had papered over them with after the flood, the tidy commemorative ones, but the working names the guildsmen had used. Ferryman's Reach. The Widow's Cut. And along the eastern bank, where the official record dissolved into fiction, I engraved the roll of the drowned.

Fourteen names. Odile recited them to me from memory, no paper needed, in the order the ice took them, and I cut each one into the copper at half the height of a normal map label — small enough that a casual reader would take them for decorative script, large enough that a careful one would read every letter.

Ferran, Josiah. First, because she asked, though she asked as if requesting a favor she hadn't earned, and I told her that the first name on an indictment belongs to whoever paid most dearly, and she turned her face to the candle and said nothing for a while.

Then Quill, Ambrose. Marsh, T. and Marsh, H., brothers. Odo Fenwick. Little Sam Pell, who was seventeen. And eight more, some with only partial names surviving — *Vos*, *Renick*, *the elder Bray* — gaps in the record that Odile filled with what the widows' fund ledger remembered, which was sometimes only a first name and a widow's initials.

"That's not enough for a stone," she said, watching me engrave *Widow M.V.'s man, given name lost.*

"It's enough for a map. Maps forgive gaps. They draw the coastline and leave the interior blank and say, honestly, *here be the limits of what we know.* It's the charts that lie who claim to know everything."

* * *

On the twelfth night, I cut the last line.

It was not a sounding or a name. It was the margin itself, the border rule that frames every plate in the atlas, and I broke it — deliberately, cleanly — at the lower left corner, where the frame opens onto nothing, onto the blank paper beyond the map's edge. Aldous had taught me that a closed frame is a claim of completeness, and completeness, on a river, is a lie you tell with a straightedge. An open corner says: *this continues past the edge of what is drawn. The river goes on. The consequences go on. Look up from the plate.*

Below the broken frame, in letters smaller than any I'd ever cut, I engraved the dedication I had been carrying around inside my ribs since the night I burned the copy of Aldous's letter:

*For the fourteen, whose depths were altered to excuse the drowning. Surveyed truly by J. Ferran, 1811. Suppressed by B. Harrow. Restored against interest, 1847.*

Against interest. Odile read it aloud and laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening for the first time in decades.

"'Against interest,'" she repeated. "You mean yours. You mean you'll lose everything for this."

"I mean the phrase works on several levels. That's what makes it honest."

"It makes it dangerous. Edmund Harrow has lawyers, Silas. Lawyers are how rich men engrave."

"I know." I wiped the plate with whiting and held it to the candlelight, and the true river came up out of the copper — every depth corrected, every bend restored, fourteen small names standing along the bank like a picket fence of the dead. It was the finest work of my life and possibly the last I'd ever sign with my own name intact. "But a plate in a drawer indicts no one. It has to be printed. Printed in numbers. And that means the printers' guild, and the printers' guild means going public before Edmund decides to be his grandfather's grandson again."

Odile gathered her tins into their oilcloth wrap with the unhurried care of a woman who had spent sixty years learning that everything can be taken except what you've memorized.

"Then we'd best not wait for the ice to finish melting," she said. "Once the ferries run, Harrow's men move faster than the river does."

* * *

I slept — or lay in the shape of sleeping — until the grey hour, and woke to a sound I knew too well to ignore.

Not fire this time. Footsteps.

Two sets, unhurried, on the cobbles outside the workshop. They stopped. A lantern's glow slid across the shutter seams, left to right, patient as a finger reading a page. Then a voice, low and courteous, the voice of a man asking directions:

"No lights. He's working nights, the old woman said. Leave the note under the door and come along."

A note slid through the gap beneath my door with a whisper of expensive paper. Boots receded toward the quay, where the ice groaned all night now, shifting, loosening, rehearsing its breaking.

I waited until the footsteps were gone, then picked the note up by its edge and read it by the grey light, because I couldn't bear to light the lamp and see the final plate sitting there on the bench, gleaming, finished, and suddenly very easy to steal.

The note was from Edmund Harrow. Four sentences, beautifully written:

*Messrs. Brightwater — The commission concludes Friday. Please deliver all plates, drafts, and papers to Harrow House by noon. My grandfather's correspondence has been reviewed, and certain questions have resolved themselves. I would prefer to settle accounts as friends, but I am advised the alternative is also available. — E.H.*

I stood there a long time with the paper in my hand, listening to the river grind against its winter cage, doing the only arithmetic left to me.

Friday. Noon. One printing run takes two days.

Which meant that whatever was going to happen between the Harrows and the truth would happen on the ice, and soon — before the thaw, before the ferries, before anyone's lawyers could outrun a copper plate wrapped in oilcloth and carried through a waking city at dawn.
