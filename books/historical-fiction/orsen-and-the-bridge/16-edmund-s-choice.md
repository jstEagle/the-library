## 16. Edmund's Choice

Edmund Harrow came to the shop on a Thursday, which I remember because Thursdays were when I cleaned the pens, and I was elbow-deep in turpentine and regret when the bell over the door rang.

I knew before I turned around who it was. There is a particular quality to silence that announces money—the way a rich man's footsteps hesitate at the threshold of a room he could buy three times over. The floorboards didn't creak under him. They wouldn't have dared.

"Mr. Brightwater." He removed his hat. He had good manners, Edmund Harrow; it was the most dangerous thing about him.

"Mr. Harrow." I kept my hands in the turpentine a moment longer than dignity required, mostly to give myself an excuse not to offer one. "We're closed."

"I know. That's rather why I came." He set his hat on the counter with the care of a man who had been taught that hats were expensive. "May we speak plainly? Or as plainly as either of us is capable."

There it was. Not a threat, not quite—a mirror held up with a slight smile. I dried my hands slowly, buying time the way I'd learned to buy it all winter: in small denominations, at ruinous interest.

"The back room has two chairs," I said. "One of them doesn't wobble."

He took the wobbling one. I noted that too. Men like Edmund Harrow always took the worse chair; it was how they told you they'd read every book about power without ever having needed any of its lessons.

* * *

Let me say something about Edmund Harrow that will sound like flattery and isn't—or isn't only. He was twenty-six, four years my junior, with the kind of face that made clerks underestimate him for exactly as long as it took him to ruin them. His father had built the Harrow fortune the old-fashioned way, which is to say the way fortunes were actually built everywhere, by standing on other men's shoulders while those shoulders drowned. And Edmund had inherited not just the money but the arithmetic behind it, and—and this was the part I couldn't forgive—he had done the sums himself, honestly, and arrived at an answer he didn't like.

I knew this because he said so.

"You've changed the depth soundings," he said, before I'd even sat down. "In the lower river plates. Sections seven through eleven. They don't match your earlier drafts, and they don't match the official survey of 1812, and they match—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before saying the word *cancer*— "something else entirely."

The stove ticked. Outside, a cart went by hauling ice, which the river was giving up reluctantly, in great groaning plates, weeks early. Everyone in Vellmark was talking about the thaw. Nobody was talking about what the thaw might uncover.

"You've been reading my drafts," I said.

"I commissioned them."

"You commissioned the atlas. The drafts are—"

"Yours, yes. I'm aware of the distinction. My lawyers wrote it." He smiled faintly. "My lawyers write everything. It's how I know when they're frightened."

"And are they frightened?"

"They're furious. Which for Mr. Quill amounts to the same thing." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the polish dropped and I saw underneath it a tired young man who hadn't slept well, possibly ever. "Silas. May I call you Silas?"

"No."

A flicker of genuine amusement. "Fair. Mr. Brightwater, then. I'm not here to stop you. I want you to understand that first, because everything after it will be easier to hear if you believe it. I've read the drafts. I've done the comparison against the guild records Odile Ferran lent you—I know about the visits, Vellmark is a village wearing a city's coat—and I know what happened in 1811 wasn't fate. It was arithmetic. My family did the arithmetic. Yours signed it."

The stove ticked again. I found I had nothing clever to say, which for me is a serious medical condition.

"So," he went on, quieter now. "I'm not here to stop you. I'm here to ask what it costs."

* * *

Here is what I should have said, if I were the hero of a better-written story: I should have said it costs nothing but lies, and lies are cheap, and look how many we can afford.

But I had spent the winter learning what things cost. I had burned a letter once—not the truth of it, only the paper—to keep it from Harrow's agents, and I remembered the smell of it, and how part of me had felt *safer*, and how that safety had tasted like the first sip of something I'd spend years trying to stop drinking. Corruption, it turns out, doesn't announce itself. It invoices you quietly, monthly, in portions small enough to swallow.

So instead of the heroic line, I gave him the honest one.

"It costs both our names," I said. "Yours and mine. If the final plate goes into the atlas as I mean it to go—with the true depths, and the guildsmen's names cut along the bank where they died—then the Brightwater name dies with it. My inheritance becomes evidence. And the Harrows—" I met his eyes. "Your seat, your estate, your mother's position, your sister's marriage prospects, the wing of the charity hospital with your father's name carved over the door. All of it rests on the same lie. You know that. You did the sums yourself, or you wouldn't be sitting in my worst chair."

"I did do the sums," he said. There was no performance in it. That was the worst part. "That's why I offered to fund the atlas originally. I thought—if the work existed, finished, beautiful, dedicated to Corven, no one would dig beneath it. A monument is the best place to bury something. I learned that from my father."

"And now?"

"Now I've read your section nine." He looked at his hands. "You drew the channel at Miller's Reach as it actually was. Four feet, not eleven. Any engineer looking at that plate will understand within a minute why the bridge failed, and whose fault it was that the builders were on it anyway. It's not even subtle. It's just true, sitting there, being true." He laughed, briefly, without humor. "Do you know how rare that is? Truth that doesn't argue? Most truths you have to chase. This one just waits on the page."

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "If you wanted the plate destroyed, you've had agents in my shop twice this month. Don't bother denying it; the second-floor lock was picked and relocked with more skill than the man who picked it possessed."

Something moved behind his eyes—annoyance, maybe, or respect. "Tobias and the other one. Yes. My uncle hires them; I don't control them. I've told you the truth about that much." He stood, went to the window, looked out at the street where the ice-carts rattled past under a sky the color of dishwater. "Because I wanted to ask you a question before anyone else asks it for me."

"Ask."

"If I stand beside you—publicly, at the reading, wherever you mean to do this—does it save anything? My mother? The hospital wing? Or does the flood take the whole valley?" He turned around. "You've lived inside this lie longer than I have, from the other end of it. Tell me what it costs. Truly. Because I'll pay a great deal, Brightwater, but I need to know the full invoice first."

And there it was. Not a villain's demand. An heir's question—the same question I'd been asking since the night I opened Aldous Corven's letter with shaking hands: *what am I, if the money is a wound?*

"I can't give you a smaller number," I said. "I wish I could. The honest answer is everything, or the lie continues. There's no version where the atlas comes out half-true and the families stay comfortable. Half-truths are just lies with better posture."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, the way a man absorbs a price he cannot pay and knows it.

"My father used to say," he said at the door, putting on his hat, "that a name is the only thing you can spend that your children have to earn back." He almost smiled. "I used to think it was sentiment. Now I think it was a warning he didn't follow himself."

Then he left, and the bell rang behind him, and I sat in my good chair feeling like a man who had watched someone else stand at the edge of the ice and couldn't tell him whether it would hold.

* * *

Odile came at dusk, as she always did now, bringing bread and taking shorthand. Her hands were bad this evening—the cold got into them, she said, though I suspected it was more than cold—and her copies of the marginal notes went slower. We worked by lamplight in the back room, her husband's original soundings spread between us like scripture, and I transcribed the true depths into the final plate in a hand steadier than I felt.

"He came to see you," she said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"You've got the face on. The one men get after talking to their own kind and finding out they're not their own kind anymore." She blew on her tea. "What did young Harrow want?"

"To know the price of the truth."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That it's everything."

She snorted. "Everything's cheap. Everything gets spent whether you buy or not. The only question is what you're left holding after." She tapped the sounding sheet with one crooked finger, right on the number for Miller's Reach. "Pieter held this. Four feet. He measured it twice because he didn't believe it himself, and they put him on that bridge anyway, and the chart in every office in this city says eleven." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got heavier, the way water does. "Eleven feet, Silas. Eleven feet is a nice safe number. Eleven feet says *the river betrayed us*. Four feet says *a man decided*."

I engraved another line. The burin bit into the copper and the shaving curled away, and I thought: this is what testimony looks like up close. Not thunder. Just a hand, a tool, a wrong number corrected.

"Edmund asked if standing with us would save anything," I said. "His mother. The hospital."

"What did you tell him?"

"That there's no half-price."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "Good. Because half-price is what killed them in 1811. Somebody offered somebody a discount on the truth, and thirty-one men paid the difference." She gathered her papers with painful slowness. "Tell the Harrow boy he can stand with us or stand on us. But he doesn't get to watch from the bank. There's no bank anymore. The thaw's come early."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when I found the first sign.

I'd gone upstairs for the oilcloth—the final plate slept wrapped in it now, a habit I'd developed along with checking the lock twice and sleeping with my boots on—and coming back down I noticed the smell. Tobacco. Not mine; I'd given it up the year Corven died, mostly. Someone had smoked in the shop within the hour, and recently enough that it still hung near the front windows like a guest who wouldn't leave.

I stood very still and listened. The river spoke outside, low and restless, working at the ice. Nothing else.

Then I saw it: a thread of candle wax on the counter, white against the wood, still soft at the edges. And beneath the drafting table where I kept the false drafts—the ones prepared for Harrow's eyes—a drawer pulled out one inch too far, and inside it, everything undisturbed except the dust, which bore the clean oval prints of fingers that had lifted each folio, weighed it, and replaced it.

They hadn't found the real drafts. They hadn't found the plate. Tobias and his friend had gone through my shop by candlelight while Edmund sat in my parlor asking me the price of honesty, and either Edmund didn't know, or—

Or he knew exactly, and the conversation in the back room had been the polite twin of the search happening around it, and I would probably never learn which. That was the thing about Edmund Harrow I was beginning to understand: he contained both possibilities at once, sincerely. He was the only genuinely honest dishonest man I'd ever met, and I could not decide if that made him better than his family or merely more interesting.

I relit the lamp and checked the hiding places. The plate was safe. The true drafts were safe. Odile's copies were safe, sewn where the letter had once been sewn, in the spine of the old survey folio, because Aldous Corven had taught me one lesson above all others: the best hiding place in any shop is the binding of a book nobody opens.

But my hands weren't steady, and I knew why. It wasn't fear for the plate. It was arithmetic.

The thaw had come weeks early. The river was already talking in its sleep, groaning and shifting, and when it broke—when it broke with that cannon-sound the old boatmen described, the sound you feel in your teeth—it would break the embankments too, and the low archive district would flood, and Odile's street was the lowest in Vellmark. And the atlas had to be finished before any of it, because an unfinished atlas is a rumor, and a finished one is a fact, and facts are the only things Harrow's lawyers can't drown.

I sat down at the engraving table, wrapped my hands around the cooling lamp base for warmth, and looked at the final plate lying in its oilcloth like a body I was keeping alive.

Two weeks. Maybe less. The river was counting down, and somewhere across town Edmund Harrow was awake too, doing the same sums, and neither of us had an answer yet.

Outside, far off, the ice cracked once—sharp, like a shot—and then settled into silence, the way a man settles when he's made up his mind.

I picked up the burin and went back to work.
