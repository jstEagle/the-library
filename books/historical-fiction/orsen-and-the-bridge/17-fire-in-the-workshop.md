## 17. Fire in the Workshop

I had developed, by that third week of February, a set of habits that would have made my old master proud and my aunt despair. I slept in the workshop now, on a pallet behind the drafting table, because the atlas lived there and I had begun to distrust every other building in Vellmark. I banked the stove low. I kept the finished final plate wrapped in oilcloth inside the iron strongbox that had once held Master Corven's silver nibs, and I kept the key on a cord around my neck, where it lay against my chest like a small cold conscience.

The plate itself was finished—truly finished—two nights before the fire. I had pulled the last burr of copper from the engraved line of the eastern bank with a burnisher at half past midnight, held it up to the lamp, and seen the river as it actually was: deep where the official charts said shallow, shallow where they said deep, and along the near bank, in letters small enough to be missed by a careless eye and large enough never to be denied by an honest one, the names. Thirty-one of them. The guildsmen of the winter of 1811. Odile Ferran had dictated them to me one by one, her voice steady until the ninth, which had been her husband's, at which point she had steadied herself by gripping my wrist hard enough to leave marks I did not mention.

"There," she had said. "Now the river remembers them properly."

I slept better that night than I had in months. This is the part I keep returning to afterward, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone: I slept well because the work was done, and I did not understand until the smoke woke me that finishing a thing is not the same as protecting it.

* * *

The fire announced itself the way fires do in old wooden buildings—not with drama but with a sound like someone turning a page. A dry, papery whisper. I came up out of a dream about coastlines and lay still in the dark, listening, telling myself it was the stove settling, the wind in the shingles, the river muttering under the ice. I was very good at telling myself things in those days. It may have been my only inherited talent.

Then the smell reached me. Hot pitch. Burning sizing. And under it, faintly, the particular sweetness of varnish going up—which is the smell of a mapmaker's life ending.

I was on my feet before I was properly awake, and the room was already orange. Not the stove corner. The far wall, the one lined with the survey folios—forty years of Master Corven's field notes, every sounding he'd ever taken, every draft that had survived his ruthless self-editing. The flames had a head start and the appetite of a creditor. Heat pressed against my face like a hand.

I want to report that I thought of the atlas first. I did not. I thought, absurdly, of the kettle, and whether I'd left it on the stove, and whether this was somehow my fault, the way one blames oneself for weather. Then I thought of Odile's soundings—the originals, her husband's own hand in iron-gall ink, the only physical testimony in the city that the published charts were lies. Those were upstairs in my room. The plate was in the strongbox six feet from me. And the atlas itself, the great unwieldy body of it, two years of Master Corven's labor and four months of mine, lay open on the drafting table like a patient who could not walk out of a burning house.

There was no choosing, really. There was only sequence. I wrenched the strongbox open—the key nearly slipped from my sweating fingers, and I have never in my life been so grateful for a cord and a knot—and pulled the plate out, oilcloth and all. It went under my arm. Then the atlas, which was too big for any graceful rescue; I crushed it against my chest with my chin and it fought me the whole way, shedding loose leaves into the fire like a bird molting in panic. The soundings from upstairs I got on the second trip, coughing, eyes streaming, the staircase already breathing heat down at me.

I did not save the folios. I did not save the drafting table my master had leaned on for forty years, or the lamp he'd bought in Amsterdam, or the sign from the door with the shop's name in his own painted letters. I stood in the street in my shirtsleeves in February, holding the truth of 1811 against my ribs, and watched the rest of it go.

A mapmaker's shop burns beautifully, I'm sorry to report. The paper catches all at once, and the light through the windows goes the color of the charts themselves—amber, sepia, the gold of old coastlines—and for a few minutes the whole building is illuminated like something in an atlas, a city on fire, rendered faithfully, scale one inch to the ruin.

* * *

The street filled, as Vellmark streets do, with people in nightclothes offering advice nobody wanted. A bucket line formed with the cheerful incompetence of a militia drill. The fire brigade arrived eventually, ringing their bell as though the fire might be embarrassed into stopping, and by the time they had water on it, the roof was gone and the second floor was a skeleton of black beams against the orange sky, and snow was falling into the wreckage and hissing like the whole street was whispering about it.

I stood wrapped in a neighbor's coat, the oilcloth bundle under my arm, and let the cold do its work on me. Someone asked if anyone was hurt. I said no. Someone asked what had started it. I said I didn't know, and it was the first lie I'd told in weeks that I didn't hate, because I did know—or I knew enough. Stoves do not reach across a room to find the driest, most valuable paper in the building. Lamps do not topple themselves into folio shelves in the middle of the night while their owner sleeps ten feet away. Somebody had come into my shop while I slept and set a fire meant to eat the atlas, the soundings, and the plate, and had very nearly succeeded in murdering me as a clerical error.

Edmund Harrow's face floated up in my mind, and then, uncomfortably, his voice: *I'm not my grandfather, Brightwater, but I am my grandfather's heir.* He had asked me, ten days ago in his father's library, what it would cost. I had told him: both our names. He had left without answering. And now my shop was ash.

But doubt is a diligent clerk, and it kept the ledger open. Harrow's men had been seen near the shop—Odile had warned me, had begged me to move the work to her rooms. I had refused, out of pride or stupidity or the mapmaker's superstition that a map belongs where it was drawn. If not Edmund, then one of his agents, acting on instructions he'd have the decency to regret. Or the lawyers. Or someone whose face I hadn't yet learned to fear.

I stood there cataloguing suspects the way I'd once catalogued river hazards, and that was when I saw her.

My aunt Maren stood at the corner of Cooper's Lane, half in the shadow of the chandlery, in her good gray coat—the one she wore to funerals and negotiations, and I had learned by then that she considered the two related. She was not running toward the fire. She was not calling my name. She was standing very still, the way one stands at a graveside one has perhaps visited before, and when she saw that I had seen her, she did not wave. She turned and walked away, quickly, into the falling snow, and the crowd closed behind her, and I was left holding a bundle of copper and oilcloth and a question with edges on it.

* * *

The neighbor's coat smelled of tallow and dog. I gave it back around dawn, when the fire was down to a red respiration in the ruins and the brigade had stopped pretending they were accomplishing anything. I walked to Odile's rooms on Ferris Row with the plate under my arm and the soundings in a satchel, and I must have looked the way I felt, because she opened her door before I knocked.

"The shop?" she said.

"Gone."

"The work?"

"Here." I set the bundle on her table. "All of it that matters."

She unwrapped the plate with hands that trembled slightly and then, touching the engraved names, went still as a plumb line. For a long moment neither of us spoke. Outside her window the river lay under its ice, white and patient, holding its breath the way it had for thirty-six years.

"They meant to burn it," I said. "The atlas. Me, probably, as a bonus. Someone came in the night with pitch and a taper and a clear conscience."

"Do you know who?"

I told her about the figure at the corner of Cooper's Lane. I told her the coat. I watched her face while I said it, and she gave me nothing, which was its own kind of answer—she had known Maren Brightwater longer than I had been alive, and she was not going to spend her opinion where I could see it.

"Your aunt," Odile said at last, "has spent forty years standing very still in good coats while this city burned other people's lives down around her. That doesn't make her an arsonist, Silas. It makes her a witness. Those aren't the same thing, but I'd forgive you for confusing them tonight."

"I don't want to confuse them," I said. "I want to know."

"Wanting to know is what got the shop burned." She said it without cruelty, the way she said most true things. "You finished the plate. You showed it to Edmund Harrow's face, in effect, the moment you told him what it cost. You walked through this city carrying the end of two families' lies under your arm and you slept *there*, above it, like a man sleeping on a powder keg because he likes the warmth." She looked at the plate, at the thirty-one names. "You did the right thing every step of the way and you did it loudly. Fire was the polite response."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out wrong, half a sob dressed in a laugh's clothing, and Odile put the kettle on, and we sat in her small warm room while the city outside went about its morning, and I understood that I had lost the argument with myself about whether history happened elsewhere. It had happened in my shop, in the night, with a taper.

* * *

By noon the ruins had cooled enough to walk through, and I did, with a constable's bored permission and a pair of borrowed boots two sizes too large. Ash to the ankles. The smell of wet char. Here and there a recognizable fragment: a brass hinge, the scorched corner of a drawing board, a sliver of window glass fused into something like sea glass. I found the iron legs of the drafting table standing upright in the wreckage like the ribs of something that had drowned.

And near the back door—the one that opened onto Cooper's Lane, the one whose bolt I had checked every night and found, this morning, hanging open—I found a glove. Kid leather, good quality, the kind Maren had worn for twenty years, scorched at the cuff. It might have been dropped by anyone. It might have been hers, lost on some earlier visit. It might have been placed there, though that required an arsonist with a taste for theatrical evidence, and everything else about the fire had been workmanlike, efficient, uninterested in drama.

I put the glove in my pocket, because I was apparently the kind of man now who collected evidence against his own family, and I stood in the ash of everything Master Corven and I had built and felt the terrible arithmetic of it. If Maren had done this—my aunt, who had raised me after my father's debts ate the family, who had pressed me all winter to sell, to be safe, to be practical, to let the past stay past—then she had done it to save me, or to save what was left of us, and I did not know which of those possibilities was worse. If she had not done it, then she had stood at the corner and watched her nephew's livelihood burn and walked away without a word, and I did not know which of *those* was worse. There were no good outcomes. There was only the truth, somewhere under the ash, and the truth had never once, in my experience, been kind to the Brightwaters.

The constable found me there and asked, not unkindly, if I had any idea who might have done it.

"I have an idea," I said. "I don't have a name I can prove."

"That's usually how it goes in this city," he said, and looked at the ruins, and at me, and at the open back door, and said nothing else, which in Vellmark is a whole testimony.

* * *

I went back to Odile's rooms as the light was failing. The plate lay on her table under a clean cloth, and she had set a place for me at her table as though I were family, which, given the state of my family, was the kindest available arrangement. I had the bundle, the soundings, the memorized drafts, and one glove. It was enough to rebuild the atlas. It was not enough to prove anything.

I was unrolling my sleeves, preparing to tell her about the glove, when there came a knock at the door—three soft raps, the knock of someone who did not want to be heard by neighbors.

Odile looked at me. I looked at the door. Through the frosted pane I could see a shape in a good gray coat, and the snow still falling past it, and I felt my heart do something complicated and unwelcome.

I opened the door. My aunt stood on the landing with snow in her hair and her hands bare—only one glove, I noticed, and my stomach turned over—and she looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in thirty years, an expression with no arithmetic in it at all.

"Silas," she said. "Before you say anything. There's something you need to know about the fire."
