## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide went out at dawn the way it always did on Kellin's Reach — reluctantly, grumbling, leaving the shallows bare as a stripped table. Hazel Ashgrove stood waist-deep in the receding water with her gathering basket hooked over one arm, waiting for the light to come up enough to show her the glint of shell among the coral rubble.

"You're standing in my spot," said Old Sefa, from ten feet to her left, where he had been standing since before she arrived.

"You're standing in *my* spot," Hazel said. "I've dived this patch since I was nine."

"I've dived it since before your father could swim." Sefa shifted his weight, which was the only concession he would ever make to age. His legs were still knotted brown rope below the knees, his chest still a barrel scarred white along the ribs where a grouper had bitten him in '94 — a story he told differently every time, and no two versions agreed on anything except that he had won. "Besides, the pearls are on the north side today. The current turned in the night."

"The current doesn't turn in the night."

"Everything turns in the night," Sefa said, "if you're old enough to watch it." And he slid under the water without so much as a splash, the way he did everything — as though the sea were an old friend whose house you entered quietly.

Hazel counted to forty, out of habit, because that was how long Sefa could stay down when he was showing off, and fifty when he was serious. She got to thirty-eight before he surfaced, spat a thin arc of brine, and opened his palm to show her a pearl the size of a barley grain. A company pearl. Everything on the Reach was a company pearl.

"North side," he said smugly, and paddled off toward it without inviting her.

She followed anyway. That was the arrangement between them and had been for years: he pretended not to teach her, and she pretended not to learn, and between the two of them they had made a diver who could hold her breath nearly as long as a woman twice her age and read the water better than most men ever would. None of which, Hazel reminded herself as she ducked beneath the surface, mattered one whit, because none of it was hers.

That was the thing about the Meridian Trading Company. It owned the beds. It owned the boats — such as they were, three hulls gone soft at the seams, patched so many times they were more pitch than wood. It owned the drying racks, the sorting shed, the scales, the little brass tally-stamps the clerks pressed into the ledger with a sound like a knuckle cracking. It owned the house Hazel lived in, or rather it owned the right to take the house back if Maren Ashgrove ever stopped diving quota, and everyone understood those were the same thing. And it owned Hazel herself, or eleven more years of her, written fair and square into a contract she had signed at seven years old with her father's hand closed over hers on the pen, guiding it through letters she couldn't yet read.

*Indentured: Hazel Ashgrove, term of eighteen years, wages against debt, debt against wages.* The clerk had explained it kindly enough at the time, the way you'd explain weather to a child. Her father had smiled and said it was a fine start in life. Her mother hadn't said anything at all, which Hazel had thought nothing of then and thought a great deal about now.

Hazel came up for air, tipped the morning's fourth pearl into her basket, and watched it settle against the others with a small dry click. Eleven of them. On a good day she might bring up thirty; on a bad day, five and a cut thumb. The pearls went into the company tin at sundown, the tin went into the company ledger, the ledger went into the company strongbox on the quarterly packet, and the packet went away across the water to places Hazel had never seen and, by her own firm accounting, would never see. That was fine. That was simply how the world was built. Big things happened elsewhere — wars, coronations, shipwrecks worth writing songs about — and here on the Reach what happened was tides, quotas, and the slow erosion of net-mending twine. You could live a whole life inside that arithmetic and never feel its walls, if you didn't press on them.

Hazel pressed on them sometimes. But only in her head, where pressing cost nothing.

* * *

By midmorning the sun had climbed high enough to flatten the sea into hammered tin, and Maren Ashgrove came down the shingle path with a mended net over one shoulder and a look on her face that meant she had counted the boats.

"You're diving the north bed," her mother said. It wasn't a question. Maren didn't ask questions she already knew the answers to; it wasted breath she saved for important things, like telling Hazel to eat, or telling clerks precisely nothing at all.

"Sefa says the current turned."

"Sefa says that every day the pearls are somewhere he isn't." Maren spread the net across two rocks and sat, drawing her mending needle from the coil of tarred twine at her belt. Her hands moved before the rest of her settled, the way they always did — quick, sure, scarred at every fingertip from thirty years of mesh. "Your father dove that bed the week you were born. Came up with six pearls and told me you'd be rich."

"And instead I'm indentured," Hazel said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because her mother's needle stopped. Just for a moment. Half a stitch. Then it went on, pulling the twine through with a small singing sound.

"There's rich and there's free," Maren said, "and the company will sell you the first all your life if it keeps you from wanting the second." She said it lightly, almost like a joke, the way she said most true things. Then, brisk again: "The clerk comes Thursday."

Hazel felt something cold slide down her spine that had nothing to do with seawater. "Which clerk?"

"The inventory one. For the estate." Her mother's eyes stayed on the net. "They do it for every diver who dies in company service. They count what he owed and what he left, and the difference goes on the account." She pulled a knot tight. "You'll want the house straightened. And the chest emptied — they'll want the chest inventoried too. Tools, weights, knives, anything brass. Company property, half of it, stamped on the underside."

Her father's chest. Hazel had been avoiding the thought of it the way you avoid a loose tooth — knowing it was there, knowing sooner or later your tongue would find it. Tam Voss had helped carry it down from the boat after the storm, both of them silent, the whole village silent, because there is a particular silence reserved for drowned divers' belongings, heavier than any other silence on the Reach. The chest had sat in the corner of the house since March. Hazel walked around it. Her mother dusted around it. Nobody opened it, because opening it was a kind of admitting, and neither of them had the stomach for admitting yet that Brann Ashgrove wasn't coming back up.

"He kept his good knife in it," Hazel said. "And the compass."

Maren's needle paused again. This time longer than half a stitch.

"Yes," her mother said. "The compass." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat — gone before Hazel could name it. Grief, maybe. Or something wearing grief's clothes.

"You should have that," Hazel offered. "When they inventory it. The compass. Tell them it's yours, tell them it was never his —"

"It'll be inventoried as it stands." Maren bent back to the net, and her voice was flat and final as a hatch cover closing. "Empty it Wednesday night. Before the clerk comes. Anything you want to keep, keep. Anything you don't understand—" and here she did look up, and held Hazel's gaze with a steadiness that had a tremor buried somewhere deep in it, like a hairline crack in glass, "—bring to me. Not to Sefa. Not to anyone. To me."

"Not to anyone," Hazel repeated. "It's a chest full of dive weights, Mother, not state secrets."

"Everything is state secrets until it isn't," Maren said, and laughed, short and dry, and went back to her mending, and Hazel decided — being seventeen, and tired, and salt-stung, and reasonably certain the world ran on tides and quotas — that her mother was being strange for grief's sake, which was allowed, and thought no more about it.

This was the last morning for a long time that Hazel Ashgrove would be permitted to think no more about it.

* * *

The afternoon quota came easier. The north bed gave generously, as Sefa had promised — small pearls mostly, but clean and round, the kind the sorters called *rice* and paid a fair pennyweight for. Hazel worked the reef shelf in long patient arcs, her world shrinking to the size of a held breath: the green-gold murmur of light, the flicker of dart-fish, the pale mouths of oysters wedged in coral. Down there the company didn't exist. Down there there was only her body and the water's body and the small bright work between them, and if she stayed down a little longer than she needed to — floating in the blue gap between breaths, letting the current comb her hair — nobody above could say a word about it.

Once, hanging weightless over the drop-off where the shelf fell away into deeper water, she looked down into the blue-dark and saw — or thought she saw — far below, a shape. Wreck-shape. Timber lines softened by years of weed, lying on sand that no quota diver ever touched. The Lorelei fleet, the old-timers called that stretch, though nobody dove it and nobody talked about it, because the Lorelei fleet had gone down forty years ago with thirty men and women aboard, and the company bought the beds after, and the two facts were never permitted to meet in the same sentence.

Sefa wouldn't even fish that water. She'd asked him once, years ago, why, and he had looked at her for a long moment with his ancient creased face and said, "Because some rooms you knock before entering, girl, and some rooms you don't enter at all," and had refused to elaborate, which from Sefa was practically a sermon.

Hazel hung there now, lungs beginning to burn, looking down at the drowned shape in the blue, and felt the feeling she never named — the fierce, itching, unadmitted curiosity that lived somewhere behind her breastbone like a swallowed coal. *What happened? Who were they? What else is down there that nobody tells us?* Big things happened elsewhere, everyone said so. But here was a big thing that had happened *here*, sunk sixty feet under her own feet, and the whole village stepped around it the way they stepped around her father's chest.

Her breath broke. She kicked for the surface, bursting up into the ordinary afternoon, gasping, the question sinking back down behind her like a stone.

"Hazel!" Maren's voice from the shore, sharp. "Come in! Tide's turning!"

Everything turns in the night, Sefa had said. Apparently it also turned in the afternoon. Hazel swam for shore with her basket heavy against her hip, twenty-six pearls clicking their small dry music, and tried to feel the way she was supposed to feel: tired, satisfied, resigned. Quota met. Account served. Another day of the world's plain arithmetic, safely inside its walls.

* * *

Wednesday evening came gray and windless, smelling of rain that wouldn't fall. Maren was at the drying racks until dark, and Hazel lit the lamp and set it on the floor beside her father's chest, and knelt in front of it, and put her hand on the lid, and for a long time did nothing at all.

It was just wood. Iron-banded, salt-bleached, dented on one corner where it had fallen off a cart eight years ago. It smelled of pitch and brine and underneath that, faintly, of her father — tobacco and coconut oil, the smell of his shoulders, of the crook of his neck where she'd ridden as a small girl. She breathed it in once, deliberately, the way you take medicine, and then lifted the lid.

Dive weights, as promised. His good knife wrapped in oilcloth. A spare mask plate. Coils of line, a broken lantern, a tin cup dented into a crescent. A bundle of letters from her grandmother in the Straits, tied with string, which Hazel set aside with a pang — she'd forgotten these existed, forgotten the grandmother's voice, forgotten there had ever been anywhere else. And at the bottom, wrapped in a scrap of sailcloth, the compass.

Brass, heavy, older than her. Her father had carried it on every boat he ever crewed, though he'd never once spoken of where it came from, and Hazel had grown up thinking of it the way you think of a scar — present, permanent, unexplained. She turned it in the lamplight. The needle lay still. Under it, wedged into the shallow tray where the manufacturer's papers would sit in a finer instrument, was a flat rectangle she took at first for a card of shell buttons.

It wasn't buttons. It was paper. Thick paper, folded twice, sealed shut with wax — and the wax bore a stamp pressed deep into it, a crest of some kind, a shield and something crossing it. Official-looking. Magistrate-shaped. The kind of seal that appeared on the tax notices the packet brought, the ones Maren read twice and then burned.

Hazel sat back on her heels. The house was very quiet. Outside, the tide was turning in the dark with its long slow grumble, and somewhere down the beach a dog was complaining about it, and none of it — none of the ordinary night sounds of the Reach — seemed to reach inside the room where she knelt holding a sealed letter that had been hidden in her drowned father's chest, beneath a compass, wrapped in sailcloth, like something a man hides and means someone to find only when he can't explain it himself.

He'd known. The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way ice cracks across a pond. He'd known it was there. He'd put it there. Her father, who signed contracts he couldn't read, who joked with clerks, who dove quota his whole life inside the walls — her father had been carrying something that needed hiding from the company, and had hidden it where only family clearing a dead man's chest would ever find it.

*Anything you don't understand,* her mother had said, *bring to me.*

Hazel looked at the letter. She looked at the door, through which lamplight spilled toward her mother's empty chair. She thought about knocking on some rooms, and not entering others.

Then she heard footsteps on the shingle path — early, wrong, too heavy for Maren — and blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark with the letter in her hand and her heart going like a diver's hammering pulse, while whoever it was came closer.
