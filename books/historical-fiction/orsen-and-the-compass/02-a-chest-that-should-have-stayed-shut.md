## 2. A Chest That Should Have Stayed Shut

The company clerk arrived on the Tuesday boat, which was fitting, because everything Meridian Trading did arrived on a schedule, and grief was expected to keep the same hours.

"Inventory of deceased assets," said Mr. Quill, setting his ledger on our table with the care of a man laying down a sleeping cat he did not particularly like. He was a small, damp-looking person with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that fogged whenever he came in from the sea air, so that for the first minute of any conversation he was talking to you blind, which I suspect he preferred. "I'll need you to lay out all effects of the late diver Ashgrove, miss. Everything. The company has a claim on tools of trade."

Tools of trade. My father had been dead eleven months and the company still thought of him as equipment.

"He had a name," my mother said from the doorway, not looking up from the net she was mending. Maren Ashgrove could say more with her hands busy than most women could with their mouths going, and what she was saying just then was *mind your manners, little man.*

"Of course, madam. The late — ah — *Diver* Ashgrove." Quill wrote something. Probably *hostile household.* "If we could begin."

We began.

The diving chest sat where it always had, at the foot of my parents' bed, which was now just my mother's bed, though neither of us had ever said so out loud. It was made of teak, salt-bleached to the color of old bone, with brass corners gone green at the seams. My father had bought it secondhand from a wrecker the year before I was born, and it had traveled with him through every season of his working life: his suit, his weights, his knife, his lines and hooks, the little tin of beeswax he rubbed on the gasket seals. When they brought him up — when the *Lorelei* men brought him up, I should say, because he hadn't drowned alone, whatever the company report said about *a regrettable accident in adverse conditions* — the chest was the first thing my mother had carried back into the house herself. She wouldn't let anyone else touch it. She hadn't opened it since.

Now she stood by the window with her mending while I knelt before it, and Mr. Quill hovered behind me breathing through his mouth like a man afraid the sea might get on him, and I put my hand on the lid and found I couldn't lift it.

It isn't heavy, I told myself. It's wood and brass and dead men's clothes. You've carried heavier coming up from forty feet.

But my hands knew something my head didn't want to know yet, which is that some chests are only boxes until you open them, and after that they are never boxes again.

"Hazel," my mother said quietly, without turning around. She'd seen me hesitating. Mothers have eyes in the backs of their heads; mine apparently also had them in the walls.

"I'm going," I said, and opened it.

The smell came up first — tar and beeswax and rubber, the particular cold smell of a diving suit hung away wet one time too many, and underneath it all something fainter that I can only call *him*, though I know better than to think a smell can be a person. Grief does that. It assigns meanings to things the way the sea assigns barnacles: indiscriminately, and forever.

"All right." Quill licked his pencil. "Suit first."

I lifted out the diving dress. It was heavier than it looked, folded the way he used to fold it, sleeves crossed over the chest like a man preparing for sleep. I laid it on the floorboards and tried not to see it as a body. I mostly failed. Eleven months, and I still mostly failed.

"Knife."

His knife went onto the suit. Bone handle, worn smooth.

"Weights."

The lead weights clanked down, four of them, each stamped with the Meridian mark — even the weight that held a man to the bottom of the world belonged to the company. That should have been funny. I suppose in a year or two it would be.

Quill kept calling items and I kept fetching them, and the chest grew emptier and lighter, and with every layer removed I felt worse instead of better, which nobody warns you about. You think clearing out a dead man's things will be like draining a wound. Sometimes it is. Other times it is like peeling an onion built entirely of onions, and at the center there is no pearl, only more onion, and your eyes are streaming, and the clerk keeps licking his pencil.

Then the chest was nearly empty, and I could see its bottom boards, pale rectangles where the suit had lain for years keeping the sun off them, and in the corner nearest the hinge, something flat and round.

The compass.

I had forgotten it was in there. That sounds impossible, but grief files things strangely, and I had somehow filed my father's compass under *lost along with him*, though here it sat, real and tarnished and heavy in my palm. Brass case, glass clouded with salt-frost on the inside, the kind you could read by lamplight on a pitching deck. Every diver on this coast carried one, though God knows why — no man underwater ever navigated by compass; you navigated by line and lung and prayer. But they carried them anyway, topside, the way farmers carry lucky coins. A thing that tells you where you are. Divers need that more than most.

"Diver's compass, brass," Quill said, writing it down without reaching for it. "Company issue?"

"It was his own. Bought it himself, off a wrecker." I don't know why I said it defensively. Perhaps because everything else in the chest had been company property in one way or another, and I wanted the clerk to know there had been one thing, one single thing, that Elias Ashgrove had owned outright.

"Mm. Personal effects." A note. "Valued at—" he squinted at the compass across the room, "—four shillings."

Four shillings. My father's compass, worth four shillings to Meridian Trading Company. I held it a moment longer than inventory required, and I noticed two things.

First: the needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at me. Well — at the floor beside me, dipping steeply down, straining against its pivot like a dog pulling a leash. I turned the compass a quarter-turn. The needle swung back to the same spot. Another quarter-turn. Back again. Steady as a churchgoer. I frowned at it, and Quill misread my face entirely.

"Sentimental value, I understand," he said, with the brisk kindness of a man who has catalogued ten thousand widows' treasures. "But the figure stands at four shillings. Unless you'd care to buy it out yourself? Company rate would be—" he consulted nothing at all, "—six."

Six shillings to buy back a compass my father had paid half a crown for. The arithmetic of indenture never surprised me anymore, but it still had teeth.

"We'll buy it," my mother said, from the window. "Hazel, put it on the table. What else is in there?"

What else was in there was the question, because the chest wasn't quite empty. Beneath where the compass had lain, wedged into the seam where the bottom board met the side — wedged deliberately, I realized later, wedged the way a man wedges something when he wants it found by the right hands and nobody else's — there was a paper.

An envelope, rather. Thick, yellowed at the edges, stiff with age and with something brushed over it that had once been waterproofing, wax or oil, a diver's precaution. And sealed — properly sealed, not with the cheap wafer stamps the company used on its wage notices but with a blob of sealing wax the color of dried blood, pressed with a crest: a shield, a bird upon it, and letters around the rim too fine for me to make out in the dim room.

"That will be all, then," Quill said, closing his ledger with a snap. "Unless—" His gaze had snagged on the envelope too. Clerks are trained to notice paper; it's the whole of their religion. "What's that, miss?"

"Nothing." The word came out of me smooth and quick, faster than thought, and I heard my own voice lie with a fluency that frightened me a little. "Old papers of my grandmother's. Family things. Not company property."

Quill's eyebrows performed a small negotiation above his spectacles. I watched him weigh the trouble of arguing with a diver's daughter against the trouble of being wrong about a piece of paper, and I watched sentiment lose, because it always does, in ledgers.

"Family things go unrecorded," he conceded. "Sign here, acknowledging receipt of inventoried effects: one diving suit, one knife, four weights, one compass sold to the estate—" the pen scratched, "—total credit against the Ashgrove account, nine shillings sixpence."

Nine shillings sixpence. For a life. My father had owed the company more than that the day he died, which was, I understood suddenly and completely, exactly how the company preferred its divers to die: owing. A diver who dies owing cannot be asked questions. A debt is a very efficient coffin lid.

My mother signed. Her signature was small and neat, learned in a mission school she never spoke of, and watching her write it I felt the strange vertigo of seeing my own handwriting anticipated — she had taught me my letters, and my letters were hers, and both of ours were being spent signing away my father's bones for nine and six.

The clerk packed his ledger, thanked us with a bow that managed to be an invoice, and let himself out into the grey morning. We listened to his boots diminish down the shell path. Somewhere below, the tide was turning. It sounded like the house exhaling.

* * *

My mother didn't ask to see the letter. That was the first strange thing.

She banked the fire, put water on for tea, fed the gull that had appointed itself our dependent sometime in the winter, and moved through the kitchen with the deliberate calm of a woman performing normality so hard it becomes a wall. I sat at the table with the envelope in front of me and the compass beside it, and the needle still strained toward the floorboards, toward the sea beyond them, toward something sunk and specific, and neither of us spoke for the length of a whole pot of tea.

Finally she sat down across from me.

"You lied well," she said.

I looked up. There was something in her face I couldn't read — pride and fear braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which.

"I didn't lie. It isn't company property."

"It's paper with a seal on it, in this house, and you didn't want that man reading the name on it. That's lying, Hazel, and you did it quick and clean, and I want you to know that I noticed." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "Your father would have noticed too."

"What does that mean?" I said. "Mama, whose crest is that?"

"I don't know."

"You do. You've been looking at it since before the tea."

For a long moment she studied the steam rising off her cup as if the answer might be written there in someone's careful hand. Then: "I know what I know. Your father sealed that chest the week before he died. I watched him do it. I asked him what he'd put inside and he said — " her voice did something complicated — "he said, 'Provisions, Mare. For after.' And I said, after what? And he kissed my forehead and went out to the boats, and that was the last conversation we ever finished." She set the cup down. "So don't ask me whose crest it is. Ask yourself who seals a letter with a magistrate's wax and hides it under a compass in a diving chest, and why he wanted it found by you and not by me."

"Why not by you?"

"Because I would have burned it." She said it plainly, without shame, the way she said true things. "Eleven months ago I would have burned it to keep you safe from whatever's inside it. Today I'm sitting here letting you hold it, and I honestly don't know if that makes me braver or more tired." She reached across the table and touched the back of my hand, once, briefly, the way she touched everything — as if affection were a currency that had to be spent carefully. "There's a man on the point who reads the difficult hand. Old Sefa. If you're going to open it — and Hazel, look at me — if you're going to open it, don't open it alone, and don't open it here."

"Why not here?"

"Because walls on this island have ears that work for wages," my mother said, and rose to mend her nets, and that was the end of the conversation, in the way that all her hardest conversations ended: not concluded, but handed over, like cargo passed between boats in open water.

* * *

I waited until dark, because I am my mother's daughter and caution is the family trade.

I sat on my bed with a candle stub and the envelope and the compass, and I turned them over and over. The wax crest: a shield, a bird — a tern, I decided, or a gull, some white bird — and around the rim, in letters the size of pinheads, a motto I couldn't read. The envelope itself bore no address. Whatever road this letter was meant to travel, it was meant to travel unseen.

Under the candlelight I noticed what I'd missed in the dimness of the bedroom: faint writing on the back flap, in pencil, in my father's square fisherman's hand. Not much. Just three words, pressed hard enough to groove the paper:

*Sefa knows. Go.*

I sat very still and listened to the sea work at the reef, patient as a clerk, keeping its own ledger of everything it took.

My father had been dead eleven months. In eleven months I had cried maybe twice, quietly, in the water where no one could tell tears from the sea, and I had believed — truly believed, the way you believe the tide will come in — that the worst thing the ocean had ever done to me was already done. That grief was a settled account. That what remained was only the long arithmetic of getting through: pearls to the company, food to the table, years to the indenture, and nothing in this world left that could surprise me.

I ran my thumb under the flap of the envelope, feeling the old wax resist, feeling the paper strain —

— and stopped.

Not because I was afraid of what was inside. Though I was. Because of the compass. Because the needle, all evening, every time I glanced at it, had been straining toward the same point of the horizon, steady past all reason, past all magnetism I'd ever been taught, pointing out toward the deep water past the point where the Lorelei fleet had gone down thirty years before I was born — pointing, as far as I could reckon it, at the very place my father had drowned.

A compass is a simple thing. It tells you where you are by telling you where north is. My father's compass had stopped believing in north.

And I understood, sitting in the dark with a sealed letter grooved with his handwriting and an instrument that had abandoned the whole discipline of direction, that my father had not simply died in the water. He had been carrying something. He had known he was carrying something. And whatever it was — whatever *provisions for after* meant — it hadn't drowned with him. It had been waiting eleven months in a teak chest for a pair of hands the company didn't know to count.

I blew out the candle. Some doors you want to open in daylight, with witnesses.

Tomorrow, I would find Old Sefa.

Tonight, I put the letter back beneath the compass in the empty chest, and lay listening to the tide, and did not sleep at all.
