## 3. What the Letter Says

The letter sat under my pillow for two nights, and both nights I dreamed of drowning.

Not my own drowning — I was past fearing that, the way a fisherman's daughter stops fearing the sea itself and only fears what it takes. No, in the dreams it was always someone else going down: a shape in the green water, arms wheeling, mouth opening on a scream that came out as bubbles. And every time I woke with salt on my lips that hadn't come from anywhere but sleep.

I told myself it was nothing. A piece of old paper, sealed in wax gone amber with age, wedged beneath a compass that didn't point north. Paper rots. Wax cracks. Whatever my father had hidden at the bottom of his diving chest, he had hidden it from the company clerk, not from his family — and if he'd wanted *me* to know, he would have said so before the sea said it for him, in its own indifferent way, six years ago.

But paper doesn't rot when it's been wrapped in oilcloth and tucked where only a diver would think to look. And wax that cracks has to be broken by somebody.

On the third morning, while Maren was down at the net racks arguing with Widow Pellu about the proper tension of a cod line — an argument that had been running, off and on, since before I was born — I unwrapped the letter again and sat with it on my knees in the grey light of our kitchen.

The magistrate's crest stared up at me from the seal. I had seen that crest exactly once before in my life, stamped in blue ink on the notice that had informed us of Papa's death and enclosed, as compensation, eleven shillings and a formal expression of regret. The letter in my hands was older than that notice. The paper had gone soft as cloth, and the writing on the outside was small and cramped, addressed in a hand that did not belong to any company man I knew:

*To the keeper of this chest, being the child of Tomas Ashgrove.*

I read it four times. My name wasn't on it. But *child of Tomas Ashgrove* is a fairly narrow address, and there were only ever two candidates.

The inside pages were another matter entirely. I got the chest open — the legal kind of writing defeated me the moment I looked at it. It wasn't like reading at all. It was like looking into deep water where you know something big is moving below you but can't make out its shape: long lines of close-set words, words like *hereinafter* and *aforementioned* and *witnesseth*, clauses nested inside clauses like fish traps inside fish traps, and somewhere in all of it — I could feel it, the way you feel a current before you see it — something that mattered enormously.

There were names. That much I could pick out, because names stand up straight even in tall grass. *Ashgrove* appeared more than once. So did a word I didn't know: *Lorelei*. And near the bottom, a string of names I half-recognized from the churchyard wall, from the memorial stones the company had never paid for but the village had carved anyway with money raised over three winters: *Okonkwo. Brightwater. The brothers Salm. Ilse Marrow.*

Thirty names. I counted them twice, because thirty seemed like too many, and then a third time because counting them again changed nothing.

I wrapped the letter back in its oilcloth, put it inside my shirt, and went out into the cold to find Old Sefa.

* * *

You have to understand about Sefa. Every village has someone who is less a person than a fixture — the leaning fence post everyone ties their arguments to. Sefa was ours. He was seventy, maybe eighty; nobody knew, including possibly Sefa. He had one clouded eye from an old pressure injury and a voice like gravel poured slowly, and he had spent forty years sitting on the same overturned boat hull by the slipway, mending things nobody had asked him to mend: crab pots, children's kites, other people's grudges. He had dived for the Lorelei fleet before Meridian existed, back when the beds were contested and the water was honest, or so he claimed, and he told stories about those days the way other men tell weather — constantly, inaccurately, and with enormous authority.

He also, importantly, could read. Not many in our village could, past the shipping labels and the hymnal. Sefa had learned as a boy from a Lorelei fleet surgeon who had died drunk and generous, leaving behind a crate of books that Sefa guarded like pearls.

I found him at his post, mending a net that belonged to nobody, whistling through the gap in his teeth.

"Hazel Ashgrove." He didn't look up. "You walk like your father. Like the ground owes you money."

"I need you to read something."

That got his eye — the good one. "Can't you read?"

"I can read. I can't read *this*." I glanced up and down the slipway. It was empty except for gulls and the tide doing its slow arithmetic against the pilings. Still, I lowered my voice. "And I need you not to tell anyone you read it. Including me, afterward, if it comes to that."

Sefa set down his net needle. In forty years of knowing him I had never once seen him stop mending anything voluntarily. Something in my face must have told him this was not a girl coming to complain about Widow Pellu's cod-line opinions.

"Show me," he said.

I gave him the letter. He weighed it in his palm first — actually weighed it, the way a diver weighs a shell to guess the pearl inside — and then he turned it over, and his thumb stopped on the wax crest, and stayed there.

"Where did you get this?"

"My father's chest."

"His diving chest? The one the company inventoried?" His voice had dropped to match mine, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Hazel. Did the clerk see this?"

"No. It was hidden under the lining, beneath the compass. Nobody knew but him."

"And now you." Sefa looked at the seal a long moment. Then he took a knife from his belt — a small, worn thing he used for everything from bait to bailing twine — and held it over the wax without cutting. "Once this is open, it's open. There's no sealing a thing twice and pretending the second time never happened. You understand that?"

I thought of my dreams. The shape in the green water, going down. "I understand that."

He cut the seal cleanly, the way you'd gut a fish, and unfolded the pages, and began to read.

For a while there was no sound but the tide and his breathing. I watched his lips move through the dense legal thickets, watched him go back and re-read passages, watched the color leave his face the way it leaves a diver's fingers in cold water — gradually, and then all at once.

"Sefa," I said. "What is it?"

He didn't answer. He turned to the last page, and his good eye tracked down the column of names, and stopped — I saw it stop — on one line near the bottom. And then Old Sefa, who had pulled bodies out of the channel after the storm of '98 and buried half the village's dead without so much as a tremor in his voice, put his hand flat against the boat hull to steady himself.

"The floor just dropped out of my world," he said quietly, "and I'm trying to work out whether it dropped out of yours yet."

"What does it say?"

"It says—" He stopped. Started again. "It says the Lorelei fleet didn't sink. It was sunk. Deliberately. By men working for Josiah Meridian himself, eight years before the company ever got its charter. They cut the moorings and the air lines of thirty divers working the deep beds off Lorelei Reef, and they let them drown, and then they claimed the disaster, and then they claimed the beds." His finger moved down the page. "This is a witness statement. Sworn testimony, taken by a magistrate — a real one, an honest one, God knows how they found him — and it lists every soul who saw it done or helped cover it after."

"And the witnesses—"

"You're in it, child. Your father is in it." He tapped the line his eye had caught, and I leaned in and made out the cramped letters: *Tomas Ashgrove, diver, of this village, who removed evidence from the wreck site.* "Your father dove the Lorelei wreck. After. He brought something up — papers, records, the fleet manifest, I don't know — and this letter says he kept it, and kept silent, and that this document proves what he carried and why it matters." Sefa's voice had gone very low. "It says he swore to hold it until the law could act. And then it says—" he squinted, turned the page toward the light, and read aloud, slowly, translating the lawyer's language into human speech as he went — "*the undersigned witnesses do acknowledge payment received from the Meridian Trading Company in consideration of their continued silence, and do further acknowledge that such silence constitutes a debt owed to the drowned.*"

The tide filled the pause. It's very good at filling pauses, the tide. It has practice.

"They were paid," I said. "All of them. To forget."

"All but your father, maybe. Or him too — I don't know. There's a clause here about disbursements, itemized..." Sefa's finger moved down a column of figures and stopped. "Eleven pounds, ten shillings. Paid to T. Ashgrove, diver."

I felt something crack, not painfully, more the way ice cracks when the thaw comes — a sound that means winter is ending whether you're ready for it or not. Eleven pounds, ten. Enough to buy out half an indenture, in those days. Enough to keep a widow and a daughter fed through two bad seasons. My father had taken the company's money, and then hidden the truth in his own chest anyway, like a man who swallows poison and keeps the bottle.

"He took it," I said.

"He took it." Sefa folded the pages with great care, as though they might bruise. "And he kept the letter. And he kept whatever else he found down there, if this document is to be believed. Men are not one thing, Hazel. Your father was the best diver I ever watched work, and a kinder man than I've managed to be, and he took eleven pounds ten from murderers and then spent the rest of his life sitting on the proof." He looked at me with his one clear eye. "Which is more than I did. I saw the smoke from the reef that day. Everyone did. We told ourselves it was a fire on a passing ship. Forty years, I've told myself that."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you asked me to read a letter, and I read it, and now we're both in it." He handed it back to me. His hands weren't quite steady. "You wanted to know what it says. It says the floor drops out of small worlds, girl. It says history isn't something that happens to other people. It happened here. It happened to us. It happened in the water your mother mends nets beside every morning, and it's been lying at the bottom of your father's chest this whole time, waiting for you to be brave enough or foolish enough to open it."

I took the letter. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the diving chest had.

"There's a route described in the last section," Sefa said, before I could speak. "Instructions. For delivering it."

"Delivering it where?"

"The colonial magistrate. On the mainland." He said it the way you'd say *the moon*, or *the bottom of the trench*: a real place, technically reachable, practically mythical. "Three checkpoints between here and his door, and every one of them crawling with Meridian men. Salt Quay. Grey Reach. Tern Gate." He recited them like a litany, like he'd already memorized them, and perhaps he had — perhaps some part of him had been waiting forty years for a reason to.

"That's impossible," I said.

"Mm." Sefa picked up his net needle and threaded it, and for a moment he was just an old man on an overturned hull again, mending what nobody asked him to mend. Then he looked at me, and there was something in his ruined face I hadn't seen before — not grief, and not fear. Resolve, hardening like candle wax.

"When do you want to leave?" he said.

* * *

That night I lay awake listening to Maren breathe in the next room, and I understood, finally, what my dreams had been trying to tell me. The shape in the green water had never been a stranger. It was thirty divers, going down while the village watched the smoke and called it a passing ship. It was my father, diving their grave alone in the dark, taking papers from the dead. It was the debt, sinking slowly through forty years, coming to rest at last at the bottom of a chest, waiting for a daughter strong enough to lift it.

I thought about leaving the letter where it was. I thought about it for a long time, honestly, the way you think about a comfortable bed when the alarm goes off.

Then I got up in the dark, and took the brass compass from the chest, and held it to the window where the starlight could reach it. The needle swung — not north. Not anywhere north. It strained toward the open water east of the village, toward the reef the old charts still called Lorelei, trembling like a living thing.

"Well," I said to it, to my father, to the thirty, "show me, then."

Outside, the tide had turned. It was going out, and I meant to follow it.
