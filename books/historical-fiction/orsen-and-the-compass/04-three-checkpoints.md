## 4. Three Checkpoints

Old Sefa had spent forty years teaching himself not to remember, and he had gotten very good at it. He could gut a fish and think of nothing. He could mend a net through an entire sermon at the mission church and hear not one word of it. He could sit on his porch in the evening, watching the light go gold and then copper and then that particular bruised purple over the water where the Lorelei fleet had gone down, and feel — nothing. A door, shut and painted over and painted over again.

Then a girl with salt-stiff hair had put a magistrate's seal under his nose, and forty years of paint had peeled off the door in the space of one breath.

She was still sitting across from him at his kitchen table, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't touched. Waiting. The letter lay between them like something dead that refused to stop breathing.

"You knew my father," she said. Not a question.

"I knew him." Sefa turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "He was the best free-diver I ever saw, and I have seen every kind there is. He could hold his breath so long the fish got bored waiting for him." The joke came out of him automatically, the way old men's jokes do, worn smooth from handling, and for a moment it almost worked — Hazel's mouth twitched — and then it didn't, because her eyes went to the letter again, and his did too.

"Then you'll tell me what happens now," she said. "Because I've read it four times and I still don't know what a person does with a thing like this."

Sefa looked at her — really looked, which he had been carefully avoiding since she'd arrived, because looking at Hazel Ashgrove was like looking at a younger, angrier version of a man he'd once owed his life to and never repaid. She had Tomas's jaw. She had Tomas's stubborn way of sitting, spine straight, as if the chair were a matter of personal policy.

What happens now. He let the question settle into him the way a stone settles into a riverbed, finding its old groove. Because the truth was he'd known the answer since the day Tomas Ashgrove drowned — or rather, since the day before that, when Tomas had come to this same table, wet-haired and wild-eyed, and said, *Sefa, if anything happens to me, my girl will need someone who remembers how to be brave.*

And Sefa had said, *Nothing will happen to you, you great fool, go home to your wife.*

The stone found its groove.

"Now," Sefa said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt, "you take it to the colonial magistrate at Port Calloway."

Hazel blinked. "That's it? That's your plan? Walk it to the magistrate?"

"That's the plan. The rest is the problem." He pushed his tea aside and reached for the jar of charcoal sticks he kept for marking net weights, and a sheet of brown wrapping paper. His hands, he noticed with distant annoyance, were not entirely steady. Forty years of steadiness, ruined by one girl and one letter. "Come here. Look."

* * *

He drew the coastline as he'd known it since boyhood: the long curve of the island's sheltered side, the channel, the mainland rising beyond it in hatched lines.

"There is exactly one road to Port Calloway that a person without wings can walk," he said, tapping the paper. "It runs along the coast, and between here and the magistrate's door it passes through three places, and all three belong to Meridian. Understand me, girl — not near Meridian. Not beside. *Belong.*"

He tapped the first mark. "Salt Quay. That's where the ferry leaves for the mainland crossing. It looks innocent enough — fishwives, crates, a man selling candied ginger who cheats everyone equally, which is a kind of honesty." Hazel made a small sound that might have been a laugh. Good. He would need her able to laugh before this was done; fear alone would crack her like an egg on a gunwale. "But the ferry office is leased by Meridian, and there's always a spotter aboard. Company man, plain clothes, paid by the head. He knows faces. He knows which village girls have fathers who drowned asking inconvenient questions."

"How would he know that?"

"Because," Sefa said gently, "it's his job to know that. Meridian doesn't waste wages. They hire men who remember things other people want to forget."

He moved his finger down the coast. "Second: Grey Reach. The customs house. Now, customs houses are boring everywhere in the world except where a trading company owns the trade, and then they're teeth. Every bag opened. Every coat turned out. Every traveler asked where they're going and why, and the answers written in a book. And here is the trick of it—" he looked up at her, and hated what he had to say next— "the trick is they don't need to find anything on you. If they suspect you, they'll *plant* something. A pouch of undeclared pearls sewn into your lining while the guards jostle you. Then you're not a courier anymore. You're a smuggler. And smugglers get held indefinitely, quietly, and sometimes they don't come out at all."

Hazel had gone very still. It was Tomas's stillness too — the stillness of a diver hanging in blue water, listening with his whole body.

"And the third?" she said.

"Tern Gate." Sefa set down the charcoal. His mouth tasted of old ash. "The company fort. Stone walls, sixty men, cannon that have never been fired at anyone but fishermen, and the only gate on the road wide enough for a cart — which means the only gate anyone actually uses. You cannot go around it. There's marsh to the north that will swallow a horse to the belly, and cliffs to the south that will swallow everything else. Everything going to the magistrate passes under Tern Gate's lanterns, past the guardhouse, through the sally port, and out the far side. And the commandant takes his orders from the company factor, not the Crown, whatever the flag over the wall says."

Silence. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would win. Sefa watched the girl read the map, and saw her do the arithmetic he'd done forty years ago, standing on a beach, counting boats that didn't come back.

"So it's impossible," she said.

"It's impossible," Sefa agreed comfortably, "for anyone sensible. Which is why nobody has ever tried. Which is why nobody will be watching for anyone foolish enough to try." He allowed himself a thin smile. "Foolishness, done properly, is nearly a disguise."

"That's not wisdom, that's just being old."

"Being old is *how* I know," he said. "Youth thinks courage means running at the thing. Age knows courage mostly means walking slowly toward it while looking bored."

But she wasn't laughing now, and neither was he, because the joke was scaffolding and underneath it was the true weight: three checkpoints, each watched by men paid in company silver, and a letter that named thirty drowned divers and the founder who'd drowned them. He thought about the weight of that paper. Paper weighed nothing. Paper had sunk a fleet, more or less, or the keeping-silent about paper had. Words were heavier than pearls. Pearls at least stayed where you put them.

* * *

Hazel traced the road with one fingertip, from Salt Quay through Grey Reach to the black square he'd drawn at Tern Gate. "You've walked it," she said. "Recently?"

"Twice a year. I sell smoked fish up the coast." He shrugged. "An old man with a fish cart is furniture. Nobody sees furniture."

"And my father? Did he walk it?"

The question landed precisely where she meant it to, and Sefa admired her for it even as it hurt. "Once," he said. "Years ago. Before you were born. He carried a petition — divers' grievances, wages, the diving season shortened to save coin. He got it as far as Grey Reach." He paused. "The petition disappeared. Your father came home with bruised ribs and a story about falling off a ladder. He never spoke of it after."

"He didn't fall off any ladder."

"No," Sefa said. "He didn't."

Hazel sat back. He watched her jaw set — Tomas's jaw — and braced himself.

"Then you'll come with me."

Ah. There it was.

"Hazel—"

"You know the road. You know the men. You knew the Lorelei fleet, you knew my father, you—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, and he pretended not to notice, because that was a kindness, "—you knew *everything*, Old Sefa. You knew what happened and you never said."

The words went in clean, the way a fish-gutting knife goes in, no resistance until the depth of it. Sefa looked at his hands. Diver's hands, once. Rope-scarred, knuckles like walnuts now, spotted with age. Hands that had gripped a floating spar in the dark while men screamed around him in the water, and had gripped silence ever since.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I knew, and I never said. And I have eaten every meal of forty years knowing it. You think that's a small thing to carry? It isn't. But it's mine, and I chose it, and I will not lecture a child about choices I made before her mother was born." He raised his eyes. "So yes, girl. I'll come with you. Not because you shamed me into it — though you did, and neatly — but because I owe a debt to a drowned man, and debts to drowned men gather interest."

Something in her shoulders loosened. Only slightly. Enough.

"But we don't leave tomorrow," Sefa went on, holding up a finger before she could speak. "We don't leave this week. A courier in a hurry is a courier noticed. We prepare. You learn the route until you could walk it blind. We learn the schedules of the spotters, the habits of the guards at Grey Reach, who drinks, who gambles, whose sister works in the fort laundry. And—" he hesitated, then decided, because half-truths had cost enough already— "and we find out what else your father left you. A man like Tomas didn't trust one document to one hiding place. He was careful the way careful men are careful. There may be more."

Hazel frowned. "There was the chest. The letter, the compass, his diving knife. That was all the clerk inventoried."

"The clerk inventoried what the chest showed him," Sefa said. "Chests show people what chests want them to see. Especially your father's chests. He built them himself — did you know that? Joinery learned from a wrecked ship's carpenter. Clever hands." He smiled faintly at some private memory. "Once he hid our entire crew's pay inside a false bottom of a rum cask, right under the purser's nose, and drank a toast with the purser while it sat there. The man never knew. Tomas laughed about it for a year."

Hazel was quiet. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto thin ice to test it. "His compass," she said. "In the chest. It's strange. The needle doesn't hold north. My mother says it's broken, but it doesn't wobble, it just... settles wrong. Same wrong direction, every time I check it."

The gulls outside went on quarreling. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Sefa felt the hair rise on his forearms, slow, like tide coming in.

"Same direction every time," he repeated. "Which direction?"

She told him. Out past the point. Down-coast, into deep water.

Toward the wreck of the Lorelei.

Sefa sat very still for a long moment, an old man with forty years of locked doors suddenly hearing keys turning in all of them at once. Tomas, he thought. You clever, impossible, maddening man. What did you hide, and where, and why does even your compass refuse to lie?

"Well," he said at last, and his voice was rougher than he intended. "It seems your father left you a map after all. One that only points at what was lost." He stood, joints announcing their opinions one by one, and took his oilskin jacket off its peg. "Get your diving gear, girl. And tell no one — not your mother, not the priest, not the cat. We go to the point at first light, before the company boats make their morning rounds."

Hazel stood too, letter in hand, eyes bright with something that was equal parts terror and the beginning of purpose.

"Sefa," she said. "What did my father bury out there?"

"I don't know," Sefa admitted, shrugging into the jacket. "But I knew Tomas Ashgrove, and I'll tell you this for free: he never pointed a compass at anything unless he wanted you to find it."

Outside, the evening had gone the color of old bronze, and far down the coast, past the point, the water lay flat and dark and keeping its secrets.
