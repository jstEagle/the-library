## 5. The Compass That Lies

The compass sat on the table between the salt dish and the lamp for three days before Hazel worked up the courage to touch it.

It wasn't that she feared it, exactly. It was that it had begun to feel like a guest — a quiet, brass-jawed guest who had arrived with her father's letter and refused to explain himself. She'd turn it over while eating, run her thumb along the worn hinge of its lid, and set it down again. Old Sefa had told her nothing about it. He'd read the letter twice, gone grey as fish belly, and sent her home with instructions she could recite in her sleep: *Tell no one. Trust no one on company pay. And girl — keep your hands busy and your mouth shut.*

Her hands were busy. Her mouth was shut. But her mind was a net with a hole in it, and everything kept slipping through toward one question: why had her father owned a magistrate's seal?

On the third evening, Maren went out to mend nets with the other wives under Sefa's widow's lantern, and Hazel finally picked the compass up with intent.

It was heavier than it looked. The brass case was dented at one corner, dented in a way that suggested a story rather than an accident — the kind of dent a thing gets when it's dropped from a boat into shallow water and fished out again by a man too stubborn to let it go. Inside the lid, scratched small and careful, someone had written: *E. Ashgrove.* Not her father's full name. Just the initial and surname, as if the compass might forget him otherwise.

Hazel opened the face.

The needle swung.

She frowned. She turned the compass east. The needle swung west. She turned it south; the needle strained north past the little painted N, quivering against the glass like a moth against a window.

"Stop that," she told it, which is what her father used to say to everything, including the sea.

She carried it out to the doorstep, away from the iron pot and the nails in the wall, because every diver knows metal lies to needles. Still it swung, steady and certain, pointing not north but northwest — out over the rooftops, over the drying racks, over the black line where the shallows gave up and the deep water began.

Northwest. Toward the Lorelei wreck.

Hazel stood very still on the step. A night heron laughed somewhere down the shore, that ugly, wonderful sound. Somewhere behind her, the women's voices rose and fell over their mending, ordinary as breathing.

Her father had died eleven years ago, when she was nine, in water no deeper than this table is tall. A cramp, they said. A good man taken by a silly accident. She had spent eleven years believing that death was small — that the sea took people the way gulls take bread crusts, carelessly, without meaning anything by it.

But now there was a letter naming him a witness to murder, and a compass that didn't point north, and both of them had been hidden in his diving chest beneath his spare mask strap, wedged where only someone clearing out that chest with a clerk standing over her shoulder would ever have found them.

Hidden, Hazel thought. Or left. There was a difference, and she wasn't sure yet which one frightened her more.

* * *

She waited two days, because waiting is a discipline divers learn early. You don't chase a pearl. You let it come to the basket.

She told her mother she was going to check the company lines off the north spit — true enough, dull enough. She took the small boat, the tide tables, and the compass, and if Maren noticed the compass riding in her apron pocket, she said nothing, which by then was its own kind of language between them.

The morning was the kind the island does best: pale gold water, wind soft as a hand smoothing a bedsheet. Hazel rowed until the village shrank to a smudge and the compass needle swung hard to port, straining like a dog on a lead.

Below her, at forty feet, lay the bones of the *Lorelei*.

Every child on the island knew the wreck the way children elsewhere know bogeymen. The rival fleet — three ships, the old folks said, before Meridian bought the beds and burned the ledgers — had gone down in one night, all together, in weather that shouldn't have sunk a dinghy. Thirty divers lost with them. The company called it a storm. The company called a great many things storms.

Divers didn't go down to the *Lorelei*. Not for pearls, not for salvage, not for anything. The company had posted a notice about it twenty years ago, and though the notice had long since rotted off its post, the prohibition had soaked into the island like brine into wood. Bad luck, they said. Haunted. Hazel had always privately thought *haunted* was a word grown-ups invented so they wouldn't have to say *afraid*.

Now she shipped the oars and looked at the needle pointing straight down through the hull of her little boat.

"All right," she said aloud, to the compass, to her father, to whoever was listening. "Show me."

* * *

The water at forty feet was cold in the way deep water is honest — it doesn't flatter you, it just tells you what it is. Hazel went down the anchor line hand over hand, equalizing, watching the wreck rise up out of the blue-green gloom.

She had expected a skeleton of a ship. What she found was almost worse: the *Lorelei* looked *interrupted*. Cargo nets still hung from her rails like laundry forgotten mid-chore. A ship's boat lay keeled over in the sand beside her, whole, as if it had simply lain down and given up. Pearl shells were scattered across the seabed in a fan, hundreds of them, opened and empty — the fleet's last harvest, spilled and abandoned. Fish had moved in. A grouper the size of a door regarded her from a porthole with the flat disapproval of an established tenant.

The compass pulled her along the deck. She followed it the way you follow a rope in bad visibility: trusting, resentful, unable to stop.

Near the stern, the needle spun once, twice — and stopped dead, quivering, aimed at a tangle of plating where the hull had split. Behind the plating, half-buried in sand and coral crust, was the captain's cabin.

Hazel worked the plates loose with her knife. It took longer than her air liked. Rust flaked off in orange clouds; a crab objected to her elbow and pinched her forearm through her sleeve, and she swore into her regulator, a rich and inventive oath her mother would have washed her mouth out for. The gap widened. Beyond it, in the cabin's drowned dark, something pale caught the light.

A strongbox. Small, iron, its lock eaten through decades ago. The lid came up with a groan of bubbles.

Inside: water, silt, and paper.

Her heart did something complicated. Paper didn't survive down here — everyone knew that. Except paper sealed in wax, wrapped in oilcloth, pressed inside iron, in water cold enough to slow the world down. Whoever had put it there had known the sea better than most, and had wanted it found by someone patient.

There were two bundles. The first was a ship's manifest — she could read that much, the columns of names and cargo, the *Lorelei*'s own hand. The second bundle was smaller, wrapped tighter, and bore a seal she recognized with a lurch: the same magistrate's crest as the letter in her father's chest.

She couldn't open either down here. Her chest was beginning to ache in that way that means *now*, not soon. She stuffed both bundles into the mesh bag at her hip, kicked free of the cabin, and rose.

Halfway up, she made herself stop, hang weightless in the green light, and look back down at the wreck.

Thirty men had drowned here. Her father's name was on a letter that said so. And her father had owned a compass that pointed here — a compass he'd hidden with the evidence that said so too. Which meant her father had come down here, or someone had come for him, or—

Or he had known, and carried it, and drowned in four feet of water with the truth locked in a box in his own house.

Hazel surfaced into the bright morning and gasped as if she hadn't been breathing the whole way up.

* * *

She didn't open the manifest in the boat. Some things want land under them.

She rowed back to the north spit instead of the village — the far side, the side without eyes — and beached the boat in the cutgrass above the tideline, where she and her father used to gut fish when she was small. The grass was dry and gold and whispered secrets to itself all day long. It felt like the right congregation.

The oilcloth came away stiff as bark. Inside, the manifest had fared remarkably well, the ink browned but legible, the pages stuck together like leaves after rain. She peeled them apart with wet fingers and read.

And then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had apparently lied to her.

It was the *Lorelei*'s final manifest, signed by her captain, dated three days before the sinking. And it listed, in neat commercial script, the cargo of the forward hold: *drill charges, blasting powder (cased), fuse wire — 40 coils.*

Blasting powder. On a pearl fleet. A fleet that harvested oysters by hand, by breath, by the patience of men holding their lives in their lungs. You don't blast a pearl bed; you'd shatter every shell for a mile and grind the pearls to grit. No captain alive would carry powder on a pearler unless—

Unless it wasn't his. Unless it had been loaded at port by men with money and a plan, and the captain had logged it because logging was the only protest available to a man who needed his wages.

The last page was different. Tighter handwriting, angrier, the letters pressed hard enough to tear. A note, unsigned:

*They will call it weather. I have ordered the holds inspected and cannot unship what was put aboard without losing my charter and my crew's pay. If we go down, let this page testify. Powder does not sink a fleet. Men do.*

Below that, a list. Names. Divers, mates, the cook, the boy who tended the air lines. Thirty-one names in all. One had been struck through carefully and annotated in the margin, in another hand entirely, older and shakier:

*Sefa Tulifau — survived. Witness.*

Hazel sat down hard in the cutgrass.

Old Sefa. Who drank one cup of tea every morning and complained it was too weak. Who mended the children's fish spears for nothing and told stories so gently that the horror in them arrived late, like thunder after lightning. Who had served the rival fleet, everyone knew that much — but everyone also knew Sefa never talked about the sinking, and everyone had politely agreed for forty years never to ask.

Survived by clinging to wreckage, maybe. Survived while thirty around him drowned. And the company — because who else? — had paid the survivors to forget. Her father's letter named witnesses. Witnesses who took coin and held their tongues. Her father had been one of them. Had he taken the money? Had he thrown it back? Is that why they'd been poor his whole life, poorer than the other indentured families, scraping when others scraped less?

The compass lay in the grass beside her, needle swinging lazily back toward the wreck, back toward the truth, faithful as a dog at a grave.

"He knew," Hazel said to it. Her voice came out strange, young-sounding. "He knew what sank them, and he sat across the table from me every supper for nine years and shelled prawns and never said."

But even as she said it, she heard how unfair it was. What would saying it have done? Gotten a nine-year-old girl drowned alongside him? Her father hadn't been silent because he was a coward. He'd been silent because he was *keeping* something — keeping it safe, keeping it whole, the way you keep a seed through winter, waiting for a spring you may not live to see.

He'd hidden the letter in the chest. He'd left the compass where a searching hand might find it. Maybe he'd meant it for Sefa. Maybe he'd meant it for whoever came next, whenever that was. Maybe he'd simply refused, in the only way left to a dead man, to let the sea have the last word.

Hazel looked at the manifest — the powder, the captain's furious testament, the thirty-one names — and felt awe move through her like a tide, and then fear arrive right behind it, the way the big swell follows the first wave.

Because this was worse than the letter. A letter named witnesses; witnesses could be dismissed as bitter old divers. But this was the *Lorelei*'s own log-paper, in the captain's hand, proving the sabotage from inside. This was the thing Meridian's founder had killed thirty men to bury. This was the thing the company would drown a whole coastline to keep buried — and now it was sitting in the cutgrass with a nineteen-year-old girl who, six months ago, had believed history was something that happened to other people.

Somewhere out on the water, a company patrol boat crossed the horizon, white wake stitched neat as a seam. Too far to see her. Probably.

Hazel wrapped the manifest in the oilcloth, wrapped the oilcloth in her spare shirt, and stowed it against her spine, where a knife would sit if she were the sort of person who carried knives. Then she picked up the compass.

The needle had settled. Pointing northwest. Toward Salt Quay. Toward the ferry. Toward the first checkpoint between here and the magistrate — the route Sefa had traced for her on the table with a blunt forefinger, three gates, each one watched, each one hungry.

"You're not a compass at all," she said. "You're just honest in a dishonest direction."

The needle trembled, agreeing.

She got up, brushed the grass from her knees, and stood a moment looking at the sea — at the beautiful, complicit, generous sea that fed them and hid bodies and kept paper fresh for forty years. Her father had carried this weight alone and drowned quietly under it. Sefa had carried it forty years and bent like a mast in a storm. And now it was hers, and she could feel exactly how heavy it was, and exactly how impossible it would be to set down again knowing what she knew.

The patrol boat's wake faded. The tide had begun to turn; she had perhaps an hour before it made the north spit awkward, and Maren would be home by dusk, asking questions with her eyebrows.

Hazel pushed the boat into the shallows and climbed in. The compass rode in her pocket, needle straining forward, pulling toward the ferry, toward the customs house, toward the fort — toward whatever waited at the end of a needle that didn't point north.

She dipped the oars and rowed.

Behind her, the *Lorelei* settled back into her silence, thirty-one names richer by one witness — and ahead, at Salt Quay, a ferry whistle sounded, thin and clear over the water, like something calling time.
