## 6. Company Men at the Door

Maren Ashgrove had mended the same net for eleven years, and she knew its every knot the way she knew her own heartbeat. So when she felt the tear near the cork line that morning — a tear she had not put there, in a net Hazel had sworn she hadn't touched — she did not curse it. She sat very still instead, with the hemp twine loose in her lap, and listened to her daughter humming in the other room in a key that meant *I am hiding something from you, Mother, and I think I'm doing it well.*

Hazel hummed badly when she lied. Always had. As a small child she'd hummed while stealing honeycomb off the shelf behind Maren's back, certain that the humming made her invisible. It had never once worked.

The letter had been in the house for four days now. Maren had not asked about it directly, because asking would require Hazel to decide whether to lie to her, and Maren did not want to watch her daughter's face while she made that choice. But she had watched everything else: the way Hazel carried the diving chest's old brass compass like a bird with a broken wing, cupped and careful; the way she'd taken to sleeping with her coat on, as though the cold might explain herself to her if she woke; the way she'd stopped going down past the second reef, which for a girl who swam like an otter was its own confession.

Something had come out of that wreck besides pearls. Maren didn't need to see it to know it. She'd married a man who came up out of the water once with his whole face changed, and she recognized the look on his daughter now.

She was still deciding whether to be frightened when the boat arrived.

It came around the point at half-past nine — too early for fish buyers, too fine for Meridian's usual collection men, who came in scows that smelled of bilge. This boat was white-hulled with a canopy, and the man standing in it wore a grey coat that had never been rained on in its life. Behind him sat two company guards with carbines laid across their knees like they were tired of holding them but not tired enough to set them down.

"Hazel," Maren said, without raising her voice. In this house you learned to pitch things so they carried through walls. "Company."

The humming stopped. There was a silence with a great deal of movement in it, and then Hazel appeared in the doorway with her hands dry and her face arranged into pleasant blankness, which was worse than any expression.

"Mother, we've got tea?"

"We have tea," Maren agreed, because what else does one say when the wolf asks to be offered something.

* * *

The man in the grey coat introduced himself as Auditor Pell, of the Meridian Trading Company, Office of Contracts and Inventory, and he said it the way other men said *your king* or *the tide*, as though it were a fact of nature rather than a job title. He was perhaps fifty, narrow as a pilchard, with spectacles that caught the light so completely that Maren spent a good minute trying to see his eyes before concluding he had arranged them that way on purpose.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." He did not offer his hand, which spared them both. "I regret to inform you that your husband's estate has been flagged for final settlement. The company's records show the inventory of his effects was never completed after the — " here he consulted a leather folder with two fingers, delicately, like picking something up by its tail — "the incident. Seven years ago."

"The accident," Maren said.

Pell looked up. For a moment the spectacles tilted and she glimpsed eyes that were pale and unhurried and entirely without cruelty, which she somehow found more frightening than the alternative. Cruelty could be read. This man was simply doing arithmetic.

"The incident," he repeated, mildly, and wrote something down.

Hazel poured the tea. Maren watched her daughter do it and marveled, distantly, at how steady the girl's hands were. Steadier than hers. Maren's own hands wanted to shake, and she kept them busy instead by turning the net in her lap, working a knot, undoing it, working it again — the ancient pantomime of a woman too occupied to notice anything, which every woman within forty miles of the sea could perform in her sleep.

"I should tell you," Pell went on, accepting his cup without drinking from it, "that the settlement is routine. The deceased's contract passes any remaining obligations to the household. Your daughter's indenture will be finalized — transferred formally into her own name, terms unchanged. A formality." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind sold in city shops. "Though I'm obliged to conduct a full inventory of the dwelling first. Company property has a way of wandering."

"Wandering," Maren said.

"Divers are sentimental people, Mrs. Ashgrove. They keep things. Rope, brass, papers." He sipped, finally, and grimaced faintly at the tea, which Maren noted with some satisfaction, since she'd made it weak on purpose. "You'd be astonished what turns up in divers' houses. Documents especially. The sea gives men paper and men forget to give it back."

"Well," said Maren, "there's the nets. And the pots. And my mother's chair, which isn't ours but he never gave it back either, so you may as well count it."

Hazel made a sound into her teacup that might have been a cough.

Pell regarded her for a moment over the rim of his cup. "I'll begin outside," he said, "and work in. You'll forgive the intrusion. Procedure."

"Procedure," Maren agreed warmly. "We're great believers in procedure."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the most peculiar theater Maren had ever attended, and she had once sat through a funeral where the priest fell asleep mid-rites and the widow finished the service herself out of spite.

Pell inventoried. He was thorough the way water is thorough — finding every gap, every seam, settling into every low place. He counted the cooking pots and wrote down their dents. He examined the roof beams. He weighed the anchor chain in his hands and asked where it was purchased, and when Maren said *from a man on a boat*, he wrote that down too, though heaven knew what use it was. One of the guards turned the diving chest out onto the floorboards with more force than the task required, and Maren's heart performed a slow, cold somersault — until she remembered, with a relief so violent it nearly made her laugh aloud, that the chest held nothing now but a spare knife, a rotted mask, and her dead husband's church clothes, folded, smelling of mildew and incense, which he'd worn twice and drowned in neither time.

Where was it? She had asked herself the question all morning and gotten no answer, and now the question sharpened to a point. Somewhere in this one room, one loft, one lean-to kitchen. Somewhere under this roof that Pell was circling like a gull over a fish shoal. She began cataloguing the room herself, frantically, behind her face: under the floorboard by the hearth — no, loose, he'd check that. The mattress. The salt barrel. The —

The net floats.

There was a basket of them by the door, waiting for the corks to be re-lashed. Hollow glass floats, blown green, each one big enough to hold a folded letter if a folded letter were slid in through the tie-hole and the hole stoppered with wax and twine. And sitting among them like a hen among eggs, the largest float of all, the one Hazel used for the deep sets, already stoppered against leaks with pitch.

Maren looked at the basket. Then she looked at her tea. Then she looked at Hazel, who was watching Pell measure the window shutters with an expression of such studied boredom that Maren understood, suddenly and completely, that her daughter had done exactly what she herself would have done, and had done it days ago, and had told no one, because telling people was how things got lost.

Well. Good girl.

That left only the performance.

* * *

"You'll want to look at the loft," Maren announced, when Pell emerged from beneath the bed with dust on his knees and nothing else. "That's where he kept the diving things worth keeping. Weights, belts, the good lamp. I can't climb the ladder anymore — knee — but Hazel can show you."

"Generous," said Pell.

"We're a generous family."

He climbed. Hazel followed. The guard stayed below with his carbine and his boredom, and for three minutes Maren sat alone in her own kitchen with her heart going like a pump in a fish hold, rehearsing.

Because here was the thing she had understood sometime during the counting of the pots, sitting with her hands full of net: they were going to lie to this man. Both of them. All day, if it came to it. And lying was not something the Ashgroves did — had never done. Tam Voss's mother used to say the Ashgroves would drown before they'd float on a lie, and it wasn't meant kindly but it was true. Maren had gone seven years answering every company man straight, every clerk, every collector, giving them the truth like rent, on time, in full — because truth was safe, truth cost nothing when you had nothing, truth was the luxury of people with nothing to lose.

And then her daughter had started sleeping in her coat, and a white boat had come around the point, and Maren had discovered, sitting quietly with her mending, that she had something to lose now. Two things. One of them was upstairs showing a company auditor where the lamp hung.

When Pell came back down he was carrying the good lamp, the brass one, and writing.

"Lamp," he said. "Marked as company issue, 1903. That stays. The belt stays. The weights stay." He paused over the list. "Your husband's logbooks, Mrs. Ashgrove. Divers keep logs. Where are they?"

And there it was.

The logbooks existed. Three of them, oil-stained, in Orsen's square diver's hand, full of depths and beds and weather and — Maren knew, because she had read them in the worst weeks after the drowning, hunting for some last word of him — remarks. Remarks about the Lorelei fleet. About the year the pearl beds went quiet and strange. Her husband had written down everything, because he was the sort of man who believed the water ought to answer to somebody.

"They burned," Maren said.

Pell's pen stopped.

"Burned?" he said pleasantly. "All of them?"

"After he died. Grief does foolish things." She met the pale glint of his spectacles and did not blink, and heard herself continue in a voice she barely recognized — level, sorry, slightly stupid, the voice of a woman who had indeed done something foolish and was ashamed of it. "I couldn't bear to read them. Every page was his hand. I put them in the stove the winter after. I've regretted it since. You spend a life wishing you had less of a man's writing around, and then—" She let the sentence die, and pressed her knuckle briefly to the corner of one eye, and it was not even acting, because it was true; she had regretted it, in a way, though not for the reasons she was giving.

The kitchen was silent except for the sea, which was doing its usual work against the rocks below, patient as a debt.

"That's unfortunate," Pell said at last. He made a note. "Logbooks destroyed by the widow. Winter of '04–05." He closed the folder. "You understand I have to record it as stated. If the books surface elsewhere — in another house, another village — the statement becomes a problem. Yours, specifically."

"If they surface," Maren said, "you may bill me for the ink."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Recalculation, maybe.

* * *

He was almost done. Almost. He stood by the table squaring his papers, and the guard waited by the door, and the boat rocked at its mooring with its patience wearing thin, and Maren permitted herself one breath of the particular relief that always arrives a half-second before the disaster.

Then Pell said, without looking up: "One more item. The manifest lists a brass compass. Maker's mark, London, 1888. Issued to O. Ashgrove upon certification." He glanced around the kitchen, the shelves, the windowsill. "I don't see it."

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. Maren lived a decade in them.

"He was buried with it," Hazel said.

Everyone turned. She had come down the ladder without a sound, and stood there with the dust of the loft on her sleeves and her chin up and her eyes bright and steady, and she said it again, simple as reporting the weather: "He was buried with it. Mother put it in the box. It was his hand it belonged to. It seemed right."

Maren stared at her daughter and thought, with a wild, swimming pride: *where did you learn to lie like that.* And then, immediately underneath, colder: *she didn't learn it anywhere. She was born knowing. I just never gave her a reason.*

Pell looked from one of them to the other — the mother with her mending, the daughter with her dust — and Maren watched him do the arithmetic, whatever arithmetic it was. A grave was a grave. Exhuming a diver for a compass was a request that traveled upward, required signatures, cost money. She could see the sum forming behind the glass and coming out not worth the carrying.

"Buried with him," Pell repeated. He wrote it down. "Compass: interred with contractor. Witnessed." He tucked his pen away and finally, finally, drank the rest of his terrible tea, all of it, as though finishing a chore. "Then we're concluded. The transfer documents will arrive within the week. Your daughter signs; the obligations become hers alone. Congratulations, Miss Ashgrove, on your majority in the eyes of the company."

He said it dryly enough that it might have been a joke, and no one laughed, and he gathered his guards and his folder and went down to the white boat, which pulled away around the point with its grey coat gleaming, taking the morning's terror with it like a tide going out.

* * *

They listened to the oar-beats fade. Neither of them moved. Then Hazel crossed the kitchen, lifted the big glass float out of the basket by the door, and set it in her mother's hands. It was heavier than a float should be. Through the green glass, folded small and tight as a seed, Maren could just make out the pale shape of paper.

"You knew," Hazel said.

"I knew you were humming," Maren said. "The hiding, I guessed at. The float was luck." She turned the thing over. Wax and twine, pitched stopper, her husband's daughter's cleverness sealed inside. "Whose crest is on it, Hazel? Tell me plainly."

Hazel told her. Not all of it — Maren could see the girl rationing, choosing which pieces a mother could carry and which she'd take herself — but enough. A magistrate's seal. A name of a fleet Maren had heard whispered of for years the way you speak of the drowned. Thirty men. Her husband's name among the living witnesses, written down somewhere official, which meant someone official had known, all these years, and the knowing had cost thirty families their men and the Ashgroves seven years of indenture and grief priced as an accident.

Maren sat down slowly in her mother's chair.

"I lied to that man," she said. It came out half wonder, half accusation.

"Yes."

"Twenty-six years in this house and I lied to a company man and he thanked me for the tea."

"Yes," Hazel said again, and there was the beginning of a grin on her, terrified and fierce at once, the exact face her father made before a deep dive.

Maren looked at the float in her hands — at the paper inside it, at the ghost of a fleet inside the paper, at the long gray road between this kitchen and wherever the truth was supposed to go — and found that the fear had not gone away. It had simply changed rooms in her, settled deeper, somewhere near the bone. But underneath it, unexpected as a pearl in a bad oyster, sat something else. Something that felt disgracefully like pride.

"He'll write it all down," she said. "The buried compass. The burned logs. It's in his book now, our lies, in ink, official." She shook her head. "Seven years I paid them the truth like rent. Now we owe them lies instead, and lies compound, Hazel. They don't sit still."

"Then we'd best get moving before the interest comes due," Hazel said, and took the float gently from her mother's hands, and began, right there in the kitchen, in the afternoon light, to unpick the wax seal — and paused, because down the path, footsteps. Light ones. Quick ones. Old Sefa's cane knocking double-time on the stones, faster than an old man had any business walking, and his voice ahead of him, breathless, calling Hazel's name before he'd even reached the door.

Maren rose, heart already climbing again. Whatever Sefa came running to say, it had made him run — and nothing in seventy years of tides had ever made Old Sefa run.
