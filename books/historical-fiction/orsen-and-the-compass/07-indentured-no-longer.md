## 7. Indentured No Longer

The pearls from the Lorelei sat in a salt-glazed jar beneath the floorboards of our kitchen, and for three days I did nothing but look at them.

I told myself I was being careful. A sensible girl checks her footing before she leaps — that was the kind of thing my father used to say, back when saying things like that made him seem wise instead of doomed. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: I kept lifting the jar into the morning light and turning it, watching the little moons inside shift against each other, and every time I did, some small cowardly voice in my chest said, *Put it back. Put them all back. Dive them up again if you must, but put them back.*

They were not beautiful pearls, most of them. They were work pearls, cloudy at the edges, the kind Meridian's clerks would weigh with a bored flick of the wrist before dropping them into a ledger line. There were perhaps sixty of them. Enough, by my rough counting, to buy out an indenture contract — barely enough, if the company's assessor was in a generous mood, which no Meridian man had ever been in my living memory.

Enough to make me free. Or enough to make me something worse than free.

* * *

"You're going to do it," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. He sat on his porch mending a net he had no boat to use it on, fingers moving through the knots the way other old men moved through prayers.

"I'm going to ask about doing it," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is not." He bit a thread clean with teeth that had outlived their warranty. "You have the look your father had when he'd decided a thing and was only waiting for the world to catch up. I hated that look on him too."

I sat down on his step. Down below, the tide was going out over the beds, leaving the flats shining and exposed, and somewhere on those flats Maren was gathering cockles because gathering cockles is what you do when you are indentured and the nets are mended and there is no other way to be useful to the company that owns the hours of your hands.

"Sefa. What happens after?"

"After what?"

"After I pay. After the clerk writes it in the book and stamps it and tells me I'm nobody's now. What then?" I had been circling this question for three days, the way a gull circles a fish it can't quite lift. "Does the contract burn? Do they give it to me? Does someone come to the house and take Meridian's mark off the door?"

Sefa's fingers stopped moving.

"Ah," he said.

"That's not reassuring."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He laid the net across his knees and looked out at the flats for a long moment, and I realized I was about to hear one of the stories from the part of his life he never fished up voluntarily. "Girl, how many freed divers do you know? Truly free ones. Bought-out, stamped, done."

I thought about it. I thought about it longer than I wanted to.

"Old Noli," I said finally. "She bought herself out eleven years ago with her husband's salvage money. And... Tam Voss's uncle, maybe. Though everyone says he left for the mainland."

"Everyone does say that," Sefa said mildly.

"What happened to Noli?"

"She fishes the far channel now. Alone. She speaks when spoken to, and mostly she speaks about weather." He resumed his knotting, slow and even. "And Tam's uncle — well. Everyone says he left for the mainland. Everyone says a great many things, and the beauty of what everyone says is that no one has to prove any of it."

My mouth had gone dry. "Freed divers who talk disappear."

"Freed divers who talk disappear," he repeated, as gently as a man could say such a thing. "Freedom here isn't a door opening, Hazel. It's a gate in a fence, and the fence is still there, and there are men paid to watch the gate. The moment your name comes out of their ledger, you stop being worth protecting and start being worth watching. An indentured diver is an investment. A free diver is a witness with legs."

"And I'm about to become a witness with legs carrying a magistrate's letter in her coat."

"Now you're learning arithmetic," Sefa said. There was no humor in it, though his mouth tried to make some.

* * *

The Meridian factor's office smelled of ink, camphor, and the particular staleness of rooms where men count things they've never touched. Mr. Pell — the assistant factor, a damp, precise man whose collar always seemed a size optimistic for his neck — received me standing behind his desk, because sitting would have implied I was owed eye level.

"Ashgrove," he said. "The daughter. You want a valuation appointment."

"I want a buyout."

His pen stopped above the ledger. "You want—" He set the pen down with great ceremony, as though it might otherwise do something reckless. "Ashgrove. Your mother's contract runs nine more years. Yours runs twelve. The standard redemption price is calculated against remaining term, projected yield, and — forgive me — current market condition of the asset."

*I am the asset,* I reminded myself, and found it easier to breathe if I pretended he'd said it about a boat.

"I know the arithmetic," I said. "I'm offering payment today. In pearls."

Something moved behind his eyes — not greed exactly, but its respectable cousin. "In kind settlements require assay. And provenance documentation."

"Of course," I said, and produced the story Sefa and I had built between us, smooth as oiled rope. "Salvage. My father worked the outer shoals before he drowned. I found his old marker buoys last month, low spring tides, and there's a patch of shell he never reported — he kept private beds, Mr. Pell, the way men keep private bottles. I've been working them since."

Pell looked at me for a long moment. I watched him decide that a pearl diver's daughter finding a dead pearl diver's hidden bed was exactly the sort of ordinary miracle his ledgers absorbed without comment. Greed does the thinking for men like him; all I had to do was leave the door open.

"The assay will determine everything," he said. "If the stones are clouded or undersized—"

"They're what they are. Weigh them."

* * *

He weighed them in the back room, under a lamp, with a loupe screwed into his eye socket so that he resembled nothing so much as a startled owl. I stood with my hands folded and my heart going like a drum in a festival parade while he lifted each pearl, breathed on it, held it to the light, and grunted in a code I could not read.

Sixty pearls. Fifty-one of them passed muster well enough to go onto his scale.

The scale tipped. The chalk squeaked. Pell wrote figures, crossed them out, wrote them again, and performed a small theater of sighing designed, I understood, to soften me up for whatever number came next.

"It covers yours," he said at last. "Barely. With the fee for early redemption, the assay charge, and the administrative stamp — the Company is not a charity, Ashgrove — your contract clears. Your mother's does not. Hers runs nine years, and these stones don't touch half of it."

"I know," I said. "Today it's mine. Only mine."

That was the bargain Sefa and I had made in the dark of his kitchen two nights earlier, and I had sworn to it, and still hearing it aloud felt like setting down a load I'd carried so long I'd forgotten it was heavy — and like abandoning a load someone else was still under. Maren had insisted. She'd taken my face in her two rough hands and said, *One of us walks out of their book, and it will be you, and you will not argue with me,* and when Maren says a thing in that voice, the tide itself argues less.

"Sign here," Pell said. "Here. Initial there. Here."

I signed. My hand shook on the first letter and steadied by the third, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that it was the first time in my life I had written my own name on anything that mattered. Every other paper I'd ever touched had already had my name written on it by someone else.

Pell blotted the page, stamped it twice — once in black for the Company, once in red for God knew what, probably the Company again wearing a different hat — and then, with visible reluctance, slid the original contract across the desk toward me.

Yours, the motion said. No one else's. It has your name on it in your own hand and no mark of ownership left upon you anywhere in this book.

"Collect your discharge stamp at the window," he said, already looking past me to the next item in his day. "And Ashgrove—" I paused at the door. "The Company notes divers who redeem early. It's unusual. Unusual gets remembered."

"Then I'll try to be boring," I said, and walked out before he could see what that cost me to say lightly.

* * *

The discharge stamp was a rubber affair worked by a clerk with ink-stained knuckles, and when it came down on the certificate it made a sound like a small kiss. *RELEASED*, it said, and beneath it, *Meridian Trading Company — Eastern District*, and beneath that a date, which was today, which was the strangest date of my life.

I walked out of the factor's office holding a piece of paper that weighed nothing at all, and I swear to you it was the heaviest thing I have ever carried.

The light outside was unbearable. Not cruelly bright — just unbearably *ordinary*. The same harbor. The same gulls conducting their eternal argument over fish heads. Old Noli's boat rocking at the far channel mooring, alone, as advertised. I stood on the office step and waited for freedom to feel like something — wings, wind, the great doors of the world swinging open — and what it actually felt like was the moment after you surface from a deep dive: ears ringing, lungs burning, alive, alive, and the whole grey sea still overhead.

A freed woman. Twenty years old, unowned for the first time since birth, and every hour of my day still belonged to a letter hidden under a floorboard.

* * *

Sefa was waiting at the top of the path, which meant he'd been waiting long enough to get tired of pretending he hadn't been.

"Well?" he said.

I held up the certificate.

He took it, read it twice, and did something I had never seen him do: he pressed the flat of it briefly against his forehead, eyes closed, the way a man touches a relic. Then he handed it back.

"Don't frame it," he said. "Don't hang it on the wall where callers see it. Papers like that are for keeping safe, not showing. You understand why."

"Because being free is suspicious."

"Because being *newly* free is suspicious," he corrected. "Give it ten years and you'll be dull as anyone. Until then, girl, you are the most interesting object in this district, and I include the Company's new weighing machine in that assessment." He started down the path, and I fell in beside him, and for a while we just walked, our shadows long on the packed clay, two silhouettes and one secret.

"Say it," he said finally. "Whatever you've been chewing since the office. You've been chewing it since the office; I can hear your jaw."

I laughed despite myself. "It's this: I spent three days afraid to spend those pearls. Afraid of the choice, I mean — really, properly afraid, sick-to-the-stomach afraid. And then Pell put the stamp down and I felt—" I searched for it. "Light. Frightened-light. Like the first drop off a boat when you don't know the bottom."

"And now?"

"Now I understand what the fear was for." I turned the certificate over in my hands as we walked. "It wasn't fear of losing the pearls. It was — once I spend them, once I'm free, then everything I do next is *mine*. If the letter goes wrong, if Tam Voss or whoever they send catches me at Salt Quay, no one can say the Company drove me to it. No master ordered it. It'll just be a free woman, choosing. That's the frightening part, Sefa. Chains explain a person. Freedom doesn't."

Sefa stopped walking. He looked at me for a long moment with the tide-flat light full on his ruined, patient face, and then he nodded slowly, the nod of a man confirming a sounding he'd suspected all along.

"Your father said nearly the same thing once," he said quietly. "Standing on this exact stretch of path, as it happens. About the weight of deciding things yourself." He started walking again. "Look where it got him."

"That's supposed to discourage me?"

"No," he said. "It's supposed to warn you that discouragement doesn't work on people like you, so I shan't waste more breath trying." He glanced sideways, and there — buried deep, under forty years of silt — was a gleam of the young diver he must once have been. "Salt Quay ferry leaves Thursday mornings, high water, seven bells. There'll be fishwives with crates, a customs spotter with a clipboard, and one seat by the stern rail that doesn't rock. You'll want the letter sewn flat against your ribs, not in a bag — bags get taken, ribs don't. And Hazel."

"Yes?"

"Wear your oldest coat. Nothing says 'search me' like a woman dressed better than her station."

We came around the bend above the village, and I saw our house below, smoke rising thin from the chimney, Maren's figure small and bent in the garden. Free, I thought. One Ashgrove free, one still in the book, and a dead man's compass pointing at a drowned fleet, and a letter in the floorboards heavier than all sixty pearls ever were.

Thursday, then. Three days.

Three days to learn how to carry freedom like it weighs nothing at all.

* * *

That night I took the compass from the chest and set it on the table beside the discharge certificate, and watched the needle swing away from north, faithful and stubborn, toward the reef where the Lorelei lay keeping its dead.

"All right," I told it. "You first. Then me."

The needle didn't waver. Outside, far off toward the quay, a lantern bobbed along the shore road — a watcher walking his evening round, or a man sent to walk past our house specifically, it was impossible to say — and I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to my own heart learn the rhythm of a free woman's fear.
