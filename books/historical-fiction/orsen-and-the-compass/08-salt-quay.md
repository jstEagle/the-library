## 8. Salt Quay

The letter had lived three days in the flour tin before Hazel decided the flour tin was a fool's hiding place.

It wasn't that she distrusted the tin. The tin had served her mother faithfully for years, guarding against weevils and the occasional thieving gull that came through the window. But any clerk worth his ink would look in the flour tin first. It was practically a law of nature, like tides or taxes: where there is something to hide, someone will check the flour.

So on the last morning before the ferry, Hazel sat at the kitchen table by lamplight with her father's old coat across her knees — the heavy one he'd worn to market in cold weather, too big for her in the shoulders but big enough to hide things in, which was more useful than a good fit just now — and she threaded a sailmaker's needle with waxed twine.

Maren watched from the hearth, mending a net she did not need to mend. Her hands needed occupation the way other people's minds needed quiet.

"You'll want the left side," Maren said. "Nobody pats a woman's left side. They pat the right, where a purse would be."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I have been patted by customs men twice in my life, and both times it was the right side, and both times they looked disappointed." She drew a knot tight. "Disappointment makes them lazy. A man who finds nothing gives up faster than a man who finds pearls."

Hazel moved the letter to the left lining. It was a strange thing, sewing your family's whole dangerous inheritance into a coat like a patch of extra warmth. The paper crackled under the cloth as she worked, and every crackle made her flinch as though the magistrate's crest itself might cry out.

*Fold it small,* Sefa had told her. *Small enough that it feels like nothing. A secret should sit against your ribs like a second heartbeat, not like a brick.*

She folded it into eighths. Then, because eighths still felt like a brick, she wrapped it in a scrap of oilcloth first, so the sea air couldn't reach it even if the stitching failed. Seven stitches an inch, the way her father had taught her for sails that had to hold against squalls. If the coat could survive a storm, it could survive a customs man's hands.

"There," she said finally, holding the coat up. From the outside, nothing. Just a shabby brown coat that smelled faintly of salt and pipe smoke and years.

Maren set down the net and came over. She ran her palm along the left side, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's chest to confirm breathing.

"It's there," Hazel said.

"I know it's there. I'm checking that it's *only* there." Maren's hand stopped at the hem, then the collar, then flat and empty again. "A woman carrying a secret walks differently. You'll want to practice walking like a woman carrying nothing but fish."

"Mama, I've been carrying secrets since the day I opened that chest."

"Yes, and you walk like it." Maren said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You walk like a girl with a stone in each shoe and a sermon in her throat. Tomorrow you walk like a bored fishwife whose feet hurt and whose husband is a disappointment. That's all. Can you do that?"

Hazel thought about it. Boredom. Disappointment. Fish.

"I can be bored," she said. "I've had practice."

* * *

Salt Quay earned its name honestly. Everything there was crusted white — the pilings, the mooring ropes, the low stone warehouses, the faces of old men who'd spent their lives hauling nets through brine. Even the gulls looked pickled. The morning Hazel arrived, the tide was out far enough to show the quay's foundations, all furred with salt like a jaw with teeth, and the ferry *Kestrel* sat waiting on the mud, leaning tiredly against its ropes until the water came back to lift it.

The fishwives were already gathering, and Hazel let herself be gathered among them.

This had been Sefa's advice, delivered in his loft with the gravity of a general planning a campaign: *Don't travel alone. Alone you are interesting, and interesting is dead. Travel with the fishwives going to market. Nobody searches forty women when they can search nobody. And for pity's sake, don't talk to them — you're from up-coast, remember, and up-coast speech will hang you faster than the letter will.*

So Hazel stood among women she half-knew by sight — Widow Brannic, the Tarrow sisters, big Osla with her laugh like a cleaver — and kept her mouth shut and her eyes dull and her shoulders rounded with imaginary fatigue. It was easier than she expected. Half of pretending to be a fishwife, she discovered, was simply being willing to smell like one, and after an hour wedged between Osla's basket of herring and Widow Brannic's crab pots, Hazel achieved authenticity beyond her ambitions.

The ferry took on cargo first: crates of dried fish, barrels of salt, a nervous flock of chickens who voiced their objections to travel in general. Passengers came aboard in ones and twos. A clerk with a leather case. Two farmers arguing about a fence. A woman with a baby who slept with the serene indifference of someone who has never heard of Meridian Trading Company.

And then, just as the gangplank creaked under its last loads, a man in a grey coat stepped onto the quay.

He wasn't dressed like company brass — no gold braid, no polished buttons — but Hazel knew the breed anyway. Something in how he stood, weight easy on his heels, hands loose, doing nothing except looking at everything. His eyes went along the line of boarding passengers the way a fisherman's eye goes along a net, checking the mesh for holes.

Her heart performed a maneuver that felt less like beating and more like trying to escape through her ribs entirely.

*Bored,* she reminded herself. *Bored fishwife. Disappointing husband. Aching feet.*

The spotter bought a ticket. He boarded behind a pair of oyster dredgers, unhurried, and took a place near the rail where he could see the whole deck.

Hazel stared at the herring.

* * *

The *Kestrel* got off the mud on the rising tide with a shudder and a complaint, and then the crossing began — two hours of grey water between Salt Quay and the mainland shore, with Grey Reach customs house waiting at the end of it like a stone fist.

Hazel found a seat on a coil of rope near the stern, as far from the grey coat as she could manage without making the distance itself conspicuous. She arranged her baskets around her. She pulled her hood up against the spray. And she waited for the inspection she knew must come.

It came sooner than she hoped. The *Kestrel* was barely clear of the harbor buoy when the spotter began working the deck.

She watched him sidelong from under her hood. He was younger than she'd first thought, thirty perhaps, with a face that might once have been friendly before the company got hold of it. He didn't flash credentials or announce himself. He simply drifted, chatting. He admired the Tarrow sisters' catch. He asked the oyster men where their beds were these days. He complimented Osla's herring and got a reply so colorful that two chickens fainted.

Then he reached the farmers, and Hazel saw him produce a folded paper — a manifest, a list, a warrant, something official enough to change the weather of a conversation. The farmers stopped arguing about the fence. One of them turned out his pockets without being asked.

Hazel's hands went cold inside her mittens.

There were maybe thirty passengers. He was moving methodically. He would reach her in ten minutes, fifteen at most, and what did she have? A coat with a heartbeat sewn into its left side, an up-coast accent she had to suppress, and no papers at all, because papers with a name on them were a rope and papers without a name were suspicious and either way —

*Think. Papa, think. What would you do?*

But her father was four years drowned and had never been searched by a company man in his life; divers carried nothing worth taking except their lungs. So instead she thought of Old Sefa, and of the story he'd told about the year the revenue cutter boarded his boat, and how he'd escaped questioning not by lying but by being, briefly and magnificently, impossible to interrogate.

Sefa was deaf in one ear. Had been since a blast charge went off too close in his diving days. And Sefa claimed — with a grin — that selective deafness was the finest tool ever invented, better than a knife, because a knife could be confiscated and bad hearing could not.

Hazel couldn't fake deafness. Deafness was total, and she'd already responded — hadn't she? — no. Had she spoken to anyone since boarding? She cast back frantically. No. She'd grunted at Osla. She'd nodded. She had not said one word aloud, and God forgive her, her silence was about to become a performance.

She let her shoulders curl further. She fixed her gaze on the middle distance with the mild, bovine patience of someone for whom the world is mostly rumor. When Osla passed her a herring and said something cheerful, Hazel smiled vaguely and pointed at her ear, then shook her head with an apologetic little shrug.

Osla's face went through a quick journey — surprise, sympathy, and then the brisk kindness of a woman who has fed deaf people before. She pantomimed *eat*, pointing at the fish, then at Hazel's mouth. Hazel nodded gratefully and ate the herring raw, bones and all, because refusing it now would break the whole architecture of her fiction.

It was, she reflected, chewing, the worst herring of her life, and she had grown up on herring.

The spotter reached Widow Brannic. Then the baby and its mother. Then the farmers' neighbor, a lean man with a crate of eels. Each conversation followed the same shape: pleasant words, the folded paper, a look at whatever the person carried. Each time, Hazel watched the spotter's hands, imagining those hands on her coat, finding the seam, picking the stitches with a thumbnail, unfolding oilcloth —

No. *Bored.* She rearranged her face into boredom. She counted the waves passing the hull. She got to two hundred and eleven before a shadow fell across her baskets.

She looked up.

Up close, the grey coat was older than it had seemed, patched at the elbows, and the man's eyes were tired in a way that company work seemed to manufacture wholesale. He held the folded paper. He said something — she caught only the shape of it, syllables like stones thrown into fog — and she gave him the shrug, the point at her ear, the shake of the head. *Sorry. Nothing there. There has never been anything there.*

He frowned. He tried again, louder, which was the universal instinct of the hearing confronted with the deaf, as though volume could bridge the gap.

She tilted her head. She spread her hands apologetically. She mouthed something she hoped read as *I don't understand, kind sir, I am a simple deaf fishwife with disappointing husband and aching feet,* and privately congratulated herself on the realism of the aching feet, which were genuinely aching.

His eyes narrowed. This was the moment, she understood, that everything balanced on. A fraud could be tested: shout suddenly, clap behind the ear, watch for a flinch. She prepared herself for the clap. She rehearsed not-hearing it. She reviewed the physics of not-flinching and decided that if it came to it she would simply be so profoundly bored that even thunder wouldn't interest her.

Instead, the spotter did something worse. He crouched slightly, bringing his face level with hers, and studied her — really studied her, the way Sefa had taught her to study water before trusting it. Looking for the lie underneath.

Hazel held his gaze with the placid emptiness of a cow regarding a fence post. Inside, her mind raced through a catalog of disasters: he asks me to write my name; he gestures me to stand and turn out my baskets; he simply takes my arm and steers me below to a proper search —

But her baskets contained only fish guts, a mending kit, and a cold potato, and her coat, her beautiful treacherous coat, hung on her like a sack, its left side silent as a grave.

The spotter straightened. He said one more sentence, softer this time, almost gentle, and though she couldn't make out the words she caught the tone, and the tone was: *Nothing here. Waste of time.*

He moved on to the chicken woman.

Hazel exhaled very slowly through her nose, the way her father had taught her to breathe off a mask, long and controlled, because a fast breath shakes and a slow one doesn't. Her heart was hammering hard enough that she feared it might actually be audible, that the whole ferry might turn and stare at the girl whose chest sounded like a drum corps.

She ate another herring. It helped. Misery is grounding.

* * *

An hour out, with Grey Reach's headland a dark smudge ahead, the sea decided to earn its passage money. Wind swung around into the channel and started throwing the *Kestrel*'s bow around like a toy, and spray came over the rail in cold sheets. The chickens revised their earlier position and began screaming. The baby woke and joined them in harmony.

Hazel discovered that seasickness and terror are excellent companions, each covering the symptoms of the other. She leaned over the rail with half the fishwives and contributed, and no one examined a vomiting woman's coat. Between heaves she checked the wind direction against the compass points in her head — northwest, pushing them south of the channel, adding time — and thought, absurdly, of her father's compass in its oilcloth wrap in her bundle beneath the seat. *Pointing at what was lost.* Right now she'd have settled for it pointing at anything, including home.

Widow Brannic, gray-green and gripping the rail beside her, patted Hazel's shoulder in fellowship. Suffering, Hazel noted, was also camouflage. She filed that away with the other tools she was accumulating: boredom, deafness, herring.

When the wind eased and the *Kestrel* steadied, she became aware that the spotter had gone below. She waited. He stayed below. Perhaps, she thought, he had gotten everything he needed topside. Perhaps the folded paper listed names and cargoes and none of them were hers.

Perhaps.

Grey Reach grew as they approached: first the headland, then the customs house itself, squat and black-roofed on its spit, with the company flag hanging damp and motionless above it like something drowned. Boats were tied up along the quay. Men in uniform moved along the dock with the unhurried confidence of people who own the ground they stand on. Beyond the customs house, the road climbed away toward the interior — toward Tern Gate, toward the magistrate, toward everything.

Hazel gathered her baskets. Beneath the seat, her bundle waited with the compass and the wreck-pearls wrapped in sacking, and she tucked it under her arm and felt the double weight of it: the fortune in her hand, the truth in her seams.

The ferry bumped the quay. Ropes flew. The gangplank dropped with a knock that went through Hazel's boots like a knell.

Ahead of her, the passengers funneled toward the customs shed. Ahead of them, at the shed door, stood four uniformed men and a table, and on the table lay open bags, and beside the bags a pair of long steel probes for prying into cargo.

Behind her, on the deck, a familiar voice said pleasantly, "After you, missus," and Hazel turned to find the grey coat smiling down at her, gesturing her toward the plank with a courtesy that made her stomach drop through the hull.

He was walking her in himself. Not letting her out of his sight.

*Why?* Her mind clawed at it as she shuffled forward with the crowd. *He marked me as nothing. Why follow—*

Then she remembered the herring. The raw herring she'd eaten, the first day of her life she'd eaten one raw, and Osla watching her do it, and Osla's eyes narrowing just slightly, just for a moment, before turning away—

No. No, surely not. A deaf woman could eat a herring however she pleased.

Or maybe he'd simply taken a liking to her. Maybe tired company men liked quiet passengers. Or maybe — and this thought arrived with the clarity of ice water — maybe he'd marked her from the moment she boarded, and the deafness performance had been theater he'd politely sat through, and the smile now was the smile of a man leading a fish to the net.

The gangplank swayed under her feet. The customs shed waited, and the probes, and the open bags.

And somewhere on the road behind her — though she could not have known it yet, could not have imagined it — her mother was already traveling, alone, with a bag full of planted pearls and a lie she'd never told before, walking toward the same checkpoint from the other side.
