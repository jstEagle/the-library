## 9. The Spotter's Name

Tam Voss had three rules for working the ferry crossing, and he recited them to himself every morning like a catechism: *Watch faces, not hands. Report what you see, not what you guess. And never, ever fall asleep on the leeward bench.*

He had learned the third rule the hard way during his first week, when he'd dozed off in the salt wind and woken with his notebook soaked and half its pages glued together into a brick of unreadable pulp. Mr. Calloway at the Salt Quay office had held the ruined brick up to the light, turned it over twice, and said, "Well. At least it proves you were *on* the boat." Then he'd docked Tam a day's wage for the ink.

That had been eight months ago. Eight months of watching fishwives and pearl brokers and one man who took the crossing every Tuesday with a crate of live crabs and a face like a closed door. Eight months of writing down names in his careful clerk's hand — names, ports of origin, cargo, anything that looked like more than it was. Eight months of company pay, which was nearly double what his father earned mending hulls, and which had bought his mother a proper winter coat and his little sister a pair of boots that didn't leak.

Eight months of telling himself it was honest work.

*It is honest work,* he told himself again now, standing at the ferry rail as the *Kestrel* pulled away from Salt Quay, her deck crowded with market-day traffic. *Somebody has to watch. That's all it is. Watching.*

The trouble with the catechism was that rule two kept snagging on him lately. *Report what you see, not what you guess.* But what if what you guessed was true? What if the quiet diver from Grey Reach whose name appeared in the ledger three times in one month wasn't smuggling pearls but something else? What if the letters Mr. Calloway burned unopened in the office stove were worth reading? What if —

"Mind your elbow, love," said a fishwife, jostling past him with a basket of silver mullet that smelled like it had opinions.

"Beg pardon," Tam said, and stepped aside, and made himself look at faces instead of thinking.

Faces. That was the job. A woman in a green shawl with salt-cracked lips — fishwife, no interest. Two brothers hauling rope coils, forearms like mooring posts — laborers, no interest. An old man asleep against the mast with his hat over his eyes — either very clever or genuinely asleep, worth noting either way. A girl near the bow, coat too heavy for the season, hands folded in her lap like someone who had been told by an expert how to look calm and was doing her best —

Tam stopped.

He knew that jawline. He knew the way she tucked her chin when she was concentrating, as though the whole world were a sum she was working out. He had sat behind that profile for six years of village school, throwing paper darts at the boy in front of him and pretending not to notice that Hazel Ashgrove finished every lesson before anyone else and then stared out the window at the water like it owed her money.

*Hazel Ashgrove,* he thought. *On the Kestrel. In a coat with new stitching along the seam. On the exact morning after the Ashgrove house got turned over by the auditor.*

His stomach performed a slow, complicated maneuver, like a crab deciding whether to fight or flee.

Because here was the thing about Tam Voss: he was not stupid. He had finished third in his class (behind Hazel, obviously, and behind a girl named Petra who had since gone off to be frighteningly successful somewhere), and he could add faster than Mr. Calloway, who claimed to have invented arithmetic. And when you added up the pieces — the auditor visiting the Ashgrove house last week, old Sefa seen walking the marsh road at odd hours, Hazel Ashgrove buying out her indenture with pearls nobody could account for, and now this: Hazel Ashgrove, alone, in disguise so thin you could see through it, heading toward Grey Reach —

Well. Even a crab could do that sum.

*Report what you see,* said rule two, in Mr. Calloway's dry voice.

*And what will you write?* asked another voice, which sounded suspiciously like Hazel herself, aged eleven, catching him copying her sums and saying, "If you're going to steal my answers, Voss, at least carry the ones properly."

Tam leaned on the rail and watched her and felt the strange vertigo of seeing two people in one body: the spotter with his notebook, and the boy who had once traded Hazel Ashgrove half his lunch for help with long division, because she did long division the way other people whistled.

She hadn't recognized him yet. He was almost sure of it. The company coat helped — it changed a person, apparently, putting a Meridian button on a man's chest. People looked at the button and stopped looking at the face behind it. He'd noticed that within a month of taking the post. Old neighbors crossed the street. His aunt had stopped inviting him to Sunday dinner. His mother said they were fools, every one of them, and served him extra potatoes to make up for it, and would not hear a word against the coat because the coat paid for the boots on Nell's feet.

The wind shifted. Hazel Ashgrove turned her head slightly, scanning the deck — badly, he thought; she scanned like someone checking for a dropped coin rather than a hunter — and her gaze passed over him, caught, and came back.

Oh no, thought Tam.

Her eyes widened a fraction. Not recognition, not yet. Suspicion first — the way you squint at a face you know wearing clothes it shouldn't wear. He watched the machinery turn behind her eyes: *company coat, young, familiar —*

Then her gaze slid past him, deliberately, to the horizon, and stayed there, and Tam let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

She knew. She didn't know it was him, but she knew what he was. She'd looked at the button.

He should go to her. That was the procedure, wasn't it? Approach, question, note the answer, log the name. He'd done it a hundred times — polite as a tax collector, harmless as weather. *Traveling alone, miss? Purpose of your journey? Any goods to declare?* Half the time people lied to him and he wrote down their lies and everyone understood the game perfectly and went home to supper.

But his feet wouldn't move.

Instead he heard himself say — quietly, to the rail, pitched just loud enough to carry — "Long division."

Hazel's shoulders stiffened.

"You carried the sevens wrong," Tam said. "Every time. Miss Priddy never caught it. I did."

A long moment. The *Kestrel* rose and fell on the grey swell. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed its opinion of the mullet basket.

"I carried them fine," Hazel said at last, without turning around. "You just wanted me to owe you something."

"And did I ever collect?"

"No." Now she turned, slowly, and looked at him properly, and there it was — the full weight of those grey-green eyes, the ones that used to make teachers feel underprepared. "Hello, Tam Voss. They gave you a coat."

"They gave everyone a coat," he said. "It's a very generous company. Coats, wages, a small booklet of rules." He patted his breast pocket, where the notebook lived. "I've memorized all nine."

"What are the other six?"

"'Don't feed the office cat from the ledgers,'" Tam said. "'Mr. Calloway's chair is Mr. Calloway's chair.' 'Tuesdays are crab day.'" He shrugged. "The important three are the same three I tell myself every morning. Watch faces, not hands. Report what you see, not what you guess. Never sleep on the leeward bench."

"The last one sounds earned."

"It cost me a day's wages and most of my dignity." He glanced past her, casual as anything, at the deck, at the fishwives, at the old man sleeping under his hat. "So. Traveling alone, miss? Purpose of your journey?"

"That's the script, is it?"

"That's the script."

Hazel looked at him for a long time. The wind pulled a strand of hair loose across her cheek and she ignored it with the same discipline she'd once applied to fractions. When she spoke, her voice was low and even.

"If I lie to you, you'll write down the lie. If I tell you the truth, you'll write down the truth. Either way it goes in the book."

"That's generally how books work," Tam said.

"So the only thing I can control," she said, "is which version of me ends up in it."

He hadn't expected that. He'd expected fear, or defiance, or tears — the usual currency of questioned passengers. He had not expected her to hand him the dilemma itself, wrapped neat as a parcel, and stand there waiting for him to open it.

"Hazel," he said, before he could stop himself. "What are you carrying?"

For a heartbeat — just one — something flickered across her face. Not guilt. Grief, maybe. Or the particular loneliness of a person who has decided to trust no one and finds it exhausting.

"Nothing that belongs to the company," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

* * *

They stood at the rail together after that, which was foolish of both of them and neither said so. A spotter and a suspect, shoulder to shoulder, watching Grey Reach resolve out of the haze ahead — the customs house with its slate roof, the crane arms, the company flag hanging limp in the wet air like something that had given up.

Tam talked, mostly, because silence felt more dangerous. He told her about the crab man (Tuesday, always Tuesday, never spoke, tipped in shells). He told her about the office cat, whose name was Ledger, and who had eaten an entire inventory report in March and thereby delayed a shipment audit by two weeks, and whom Mr. Calloway therefore secretly considered the most effective employee in the building. Hazel laughed at that — a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed, but real. It did something to Tam's chest, hearing it. He catalogued the feeling and filed it nowhere.

"Why?" she asked eventually. "The coat, Tam. Your father's shop, the boats — you were going to take over the repair berth. Everyone said so. You were good with your hands."

"Wages are double," he said. It came out flat, defensive. He tried again. "Nell needed boots. Mum needed — we needed things, and the company pays, and it's watching, Hazel. It's only watching. Faces and names. Nobody gets hurt by a notebook."

"Nobody gets hurt by a notebook," Hazel repeated, softly, tasting it.

"That's rule two. What you see, not what you guess."

"And what happens," she said, still watching the water, "to the people in the notebooks? After you write them down. Do you ever find out?"

He opened his mouth to say yes, of course, they get processed, cleared, released, whatever the word was — and found he didn't actually know. Names went into the book. Books went to Grey Reach. Grey Reach sent back instructions sometimes, and sometimes the instructions were *detain*, and the detained went into the customs house and came out — when they came out — quieter. He had never followed a name past the office door. He had never wanted to. That was the arrangement: he watched the water, the water stayed far away from whatever happened on land.

"I don't know," he admitted.

"That's the trick of it, isn't it," Hazel said. "Nobody knows. That's how it keeps working. Everybody watches one small piece and the piece never shows them the whole."

Ahead, the quay wall of Grey Reach rose dripping from the swell, and the deck began its small chaos of preparation — ropes thrown, baskets gathered, the fishwife arguing her way to the front on the principle that mullet waits for no man. Tam felt the minutes draining out of the crossing like sand through a fist.

There was still time to do his job. There was still time to walk to the wheelhouse, or simply wait until they docked and signal the customs men — a raised hand, nothing more, the smallest possible betrayal, barely a gesture. *Girl in a dark coat, traveling alone, Ashgrove by name.* Thirty seconds of work. Double pay this month. Boots for Nell through next winter.

Or there was time to do nothing at all, and let her walk down the gangway into whatever waited for her at the customs house, and spend the rest of his life wondering.

Neither option fit. Both options fit. He hated how well they both fit.

"Hazel," he said. "The customs search at Grey Reach isn't routine anymore. It got thorough last week. They're opening linings. They're weighing bags against their manifests." He kept his voice level, bored, the voice of a man discussing tide tables. "I'm telling you as a fact about procedures. Nothing else. Procedures."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he saw her decide — he could watch her decide — how much of her to show him.

"My coat lining is just coat lining," she said.

"I believe you," said Tam Voss, and discovered, with mild academic interest, that he meant it, and that meaning it constituted a firing offense.

* * *

The *Kestrel* kissed the fenders at Grey Reach quay, and the world resumed its noise. Ropes, shouts, the mullet woman winning her war. Tam took up his post at the gangway head, notebook open, doing his job with mechanical correctness — names, cargo, ports — while inside him two sums ran side by side and refused to agree.

*Girl in dark coat, Ashgrove, alone, coat recently altered.* That was what the book said if he wrote the truth.

*Boy from the village, helped with long division once, owes her nothing and owes his sister boots and cannot stop remembering the sound of her laugh at the crab story.* That was what his chest said, and there was no column for it in the ledger.

She passed him on the gangway. Close enough that he could have reached out and touched her sleeve.

"Purpose of your journey, miss?" he said, loud enough for the customs men to hear, official as Sunday.

"Visiting family," said Hazel Ashgrove, meeting his eyes, lying to his face with perfect serenity.

"Enjoy your visit," said Tam, and wrote in the notebook: *No persons of interest aboard.*

The lie sat on the page looking small and enormous at once. He watched her cross the quay toward the customs house doors, dark coat, steady stride, walking straight into the thorough search he had just warned her about, carrying God-knew-what sewn against her ribs.

Behind him, a voice said, "Anything, Voss?"

Mr. Dree, customs sergeant, red-faced from the wind, clipboard at the ready.

Tam looked down at the notebook. At rule two, worn smooth in his memory: *report what you see, not what you guess.* Well. Here was a loophole big enough to sail the Kestrel through. He had seen nothing. Everything else was guesswork, and guessing was against the rules.

"Quiet crossing," he said. "Fishwives and a crab crate. Same as ever."

Dree grunted and moved on down the quay — then paused, and jerked his thumb toward the customs house. "One thing. Word from Salt Quay this morning. The Ashgrove girl — the diver that bought herself out — she's believed to be moving south with contraband. Company property. If she comes through your crossings, flag her." He squinted at Tam. "You're from up that way, aren't you? Might know her face."

The gulls wheeled. The flag hung dead. Somewhere beyond the customs house walls, Hazel Ashgrove was handing over her bag.

"I might," Tam said.

His pen hovered over the fresh page. One word — *Ashgrove, spotted* — and it was done, and clean, and deniable, and Nell got her boots.

He wrote instead, in his best clerk's hand: *Crossing unremarkable. Weather deteriorating. Recommend increased vigilance re: crab crate, Tuesdays.*

And then he closed the book, and picked up his kit, and started walking toward the marsh road — because the Kestrel returned on the evening tide, but the marsh road ran parallel to it the whole way, and a man could walk it, and watch faces, and report what he saw.

Which, he suspected, was about to become considerably more than he was prepared for.
