## 10. Grey Reach Customs House

Maren Ashgrove had been lied to exactly twice in her life before that week. Once by her husband, who swore the storm would keep him ashore — it did not, and she had forgiven him for dying in it, mostly because he could not defend himself. And once by a fishmonger in Salt Quay who sold her a crab he claimed was full of roe and was instead, as she discovered at supper, full of sand and spite.

Now she was about to become the third liar, and she found the arithmetic of it strange: two lies against her in forty years, and now she owed the world one of her own.

The plan had been Hazel's. Of course it had been Hazel's. Maren had watched her daughter grow from a child who asked questions into a young woman who answered them herself, and somewhere along the way the questions had gotten dangerous.

"You'll travel separately," Hazel had said, the night before, in the low voice she thought adults couldn't hear through walls but Maren heard perfectly well through them, having spent twenty years listening for storms. "You take the coast road with the market women. I take the ferry."

"And what am I carrying?"

"Nothing," Hazel said. "That's the point. You're carrying nothing so they look at you and not me."

Maren had looked at her daughter across the lamp flame and understood, with the particular clarity of mothers, that this was both true and untrue, and that the untruthful part was the part meant to protect her. Mothers develop an ear for that tone. It is the tone children use when they say the porridge is fine, thank you, when the porridge has lumps in it like river stones.

"I'm too old to be interesting," Maren said.

"That's exactly why it works. Nobody searches a woman carrying mending baskets and pickled herring."

"They might search the herring. The herring is suspiciously good this year."

Hazel laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, the first in weeks — and Maren counted that laugh as payment in full for whatever came next. She had been paid worse for harder work.

* * *

The coast road to Grey Reach took two days, and Maren spent them in the company of five market women who talked the entire way as though speech were a bodily function they'd been warned not to suppress. Their names were Berta, Odile, Petra, another Berta (no relation to the first, and a standing grievance between them), and a tiny ancient woman called Nana Kess who was, by her own account, ninety-one, though her account changed between tellings and once dipped as low as seventy-four when she wanted help over a stile.

"So your girl's gone ahead?" Berta the First asked, on the second morning.

"She has business at the magistrate's," Maren said, which was true, and left it there, which was wise.

"Magistrate business." Odile sniffed. "My nephew went to the magistrate once. Came back three months later with a limp and a wife."

"Which caused the limp?"

"The wedding."

They all laughed, and Maren laughed too, and it occurred to her, walking with the herring baskets swaying on her hip, that this was what ordinary felt like. She had lived inside ordinary for forty years — tides, nets, mending, the small economies of a village where everyone knew how much salt everyone else used — and she had mistaken it for safety. Ordinary wasn't safety. It was just the water before the storm, and storms, in her experience, always knew where she lived.

Nana Kess fell into step beside her, which required no great effort since Nana Kess walked at the pace of continental drift.

"You've got the look," the old woman said.

"What look?"

"The look of a woman carrying something heavier than her basket."

Maren adjusted the basket on her hip. "It's the herring."

"Mm," said Nana Kess. "In my day we hid things in herring barrels all the time. Contraband, love letters, a runaway priest once. Nobody ever checks the herring."

"Then my herring is safe."

"Your herring is safe," Nana Kess agreed placidly. "It's you I'm wondering about."

* * *

Grey Reach announced itself by smell before sight — coal smoke and tar and the sour tide-flat stink of a town built on mud it refused to discuss. The customs house sat at the head of the quay like a fat man at a table, squat and pale and certain of its own importance, with the Meridian flag hanging above the door, damp and listless, as though even the cloth had grown tired of the arrangement.

The queue stretched down the steps. Market women, fishermen, a nervous-looking clerk with a leather case he kept shifting from hand to hand as if it were ticking. Two guards worked the door, and a third — a customs officer with a waxed mustache and the general air of a man who had been promised authority and received only paperwork — sat at a table just inside, stamping things.

Maren took her place in line and began, quietly, to rehearse.

She was Maren Ashgrove, widow, of the diving village. She carried mending, herring, and a bundle of her late husband's old netting to sell for scrap. She was going to Grey Reach because — because —

Because why? That was the hole in the plan. Hazel had given her nothing to say, only something not to say, and Maren realized, with rising irritation, that her daughter had sent her out as a decoy without providing a script. She made a mental note to raise this at the next family crisis. There would be a next family crisis. There always was, now.

The queue shuffled forward. Inside, she could hear the officer's voice, flat and bored: "Open the bag. Turn out the pockets. Next."

Berta the First went through with a wave of the hand — the guards knew her, or knew her herring, or simply lacked the courage to search a woman of her forearms. Odile passed. Both Bertas passed together, arguing. Then Nana Kess, who was waved through so quickly Maren suspected the guards believed searching her might be unlucky, or possibly fatal.

Then it was Maren's turn.

She stepped up to the table and set down her baskets, and the officer looked up from his ledger, and everything in Maren's chest went quiet and cold and very still, the way the sea goes before weather.

"Purpose of visit?"

Here it was. The hole in the script yawned open before her.

"My daughter," Maren heard herself say, "is being married."

The words came out smooth as oiled rope, and she stood behind them astonished, because she hadn't decided to say them. They had simply surfaced, the way drowned things surface eventually, whether you want them to or not.

The officer's eyebrows performed a small maneuver. "Married. In Grey Reach."

"To a magistrate's clerk," Maren added, building the ship as she sailed it. "We're to meet his family. I'm bringing the herring for the wedding feast. It's traditional."

"It is?" said the officer.

"In our villages," Maren said, with the serene confidence of a woman who had spent decades convincing skeptical divers that the pearl beds were fished out when they weren't. "You wouldn't know it here."

"No," the officer admitted. He glanced at the baskets. "And the mending?"

"A widow's hands don't rest," Maren said, and watched something flicker behind his eyes — not suspicion; recognition. His own mother had probably said the same thing, or his wife, or some woman whose hands never rested within a hundred miles of him. Men like this officer were moved less by documents than by the fear of seeming foolish, and Maren had raised a daughter and buried a husband and run a household on the wages of an indentured diver. She could smell foolishness-fear the way sharks smelled blood.

He waved her toward the side table. "Open the baskets. Just a formality."

"Certainly," said Maren, who had hidden nothing, and therefore opened the baskets with the easy grace of the truly innocent, which is a thing the guilty cannot counterfeit, and she knew it, and she let it shine off her like lamplight.

The guard went through the herring. He held up a jar of pickles. "What's in these?"

"Cucumbers," Maren said. "If they've turned into anything else, I want a new recipe."

The guard almost smiled. The almost-smile died on his face, because at that moment the door opened behind him and two men came in, and the temperature of the room changed the way it changes when a rich man enters a poor one.

They wore Meridian grey. Not uniforms — the company didn't believe in uniforms, believing instead in better boots and cleaner shirts than anyone around them — but every soul in the customs house recognized the breed instantly. The taller of the two carried a leather folio. The shorter carried himself like a man who enjoyed his work, which was infinitely worse.

"Ashgrove," the tall one said, reading from the folio without looking up. "Maren Ashgrove. Diving village. Widow of Tomas Ashgrove, deceased diver, deceased four years." He finally looked up. "That's you."

"I'm afraid it is," Maren said.

"We'd like a word. Privately."

"The herring—"

"Will keep," said the short one, smiling, and Maren understood that the wedding, the clerk, the entire ship she had sailed so beautifully, had never mattered at all. They hadn't come for smugglers. They had come for her name.

* * *

The back room smelled of ink and old tea. There was a desk, a chair for the officer, and no chair for Maren, which told her everything about how long they intended this to last.

"Your daughter traveled separately," the tall one said. It was not a question, so Maren treated it as weather talk.

"Hazel prefers the ferry. She gets seasick on the coast road. All those turns."

"Mm." He turned a page in the folio. "Her indenture contract was bought out last week. Paid in pearls. Do you know anything about pearls, Mrs. Ashgrove?"

"I grew up on a pearl boat."

"Then you know a wild-bed pearl from a farmed one. You know quality. You know provenance." He set the folio down. "The pearls used to buy out her contract match no licensed bed in company records. They match, however, the inventory manifest of a wreck in these waters. A wreck the company holds salvage rights to."

The silence stretched. Maren folded her hands, which was what she did instead of screaming, and had done since the day the company boat brought back her husband's compass and none of her husband.

"You're saying my daughter stole pearls," she said.

"I'm saying the company says so."

"Those are different sentences."

The short one laughed — a delighted, ugly little sound — and the tall one shot him a look that said, not now, which meant yes, later, which meant they were not the seamless machine they wished to appear. Good. Machines could be broken more easily when their parts disliked each other.

"Mrs. Ashgrove," the tall one said, leaning forward. "Here is what happens next. We're going to search your belongings properly. And then you're going to wait here while we send word down the road."

"Search away. You've seen my baskets. There's mending and herring and my husband's old netting, and if you find contraband in it I'll eat the netting."

The short one was already at her baskets. He went through them quickly, expertly — this was clearly not his first innocent woman — and Maren watched him with the detached interest of someone watching a tide come in. There was nothing to find. There was nothing to—

His hand stopped in the second basket. The mending basket.

When he drew it out, his fist was closed, and when it opened, there lay six pearls in his palm, round and grey-green and lovely, the kind of pearls that did not belong in a mending basket any more than a shark belongs in a soup pot.

"Well," he said softly. "Look what the widow's been hiding."

Maren stared at the pearls, and her mind — quick, sea-trained, forty years of reading water — did the terrible arithmetic in the space of a heartbeat. She had packed that basket herself. She had gone through it twice, because Hazel had taught her to be careful, because careful was the whole religion of their family now. Those pearls had not been in the basket when she packed it.

Which meant they had been put there after.

Which meant they had known her name before she ever reached Grey Reach, and the queue outside, the bored officer, the wedding story she'd been so proud of — all of it had been theater performed for an audience of one, while the play they'd actually written went on backstage without her.

"That's not mine," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"It was in your basket."

"It was put in my basket."

"By whom?"

"By you," Maren said, "or by whoever pays you, which I suspect is the same purse."

The short one smiled wider, enjoying this, actually enjoying it, and Maren thought: *there are men who drown people for a living and men who enjoy drowning people for a living, and the company hires both.* Her husband had died in water like that. Cold, deliberate, administered.

The tall one sighed, as though regretting a formality. "Maren Ashgrove. You're detained pending inquiry into possession of company property. Your goods will be held. You'll remain at Grey Reach until—" He paused, consulting the folio, and Maren saw, in the pause, in the flicker of his eyes toward the door, that the script here was written too. "Until your daughter presents herself."

There it was. The hook beneath the bait, the barb under the scale. They didn't want her. They wanted what she'd been carrying without knowing it: her own body, offered as ransom, walking down the coast road with herring and mending, drawing her daughter home like a lantern draws a moth.

They hadn't planted pearls to catch a smuggler. They'd planted pearls to catch Hazel.

* * *

They put her in a room upstairs with a barred window overlooking the quay. A guard brought tea that tasted of the customs house floor, and Maren drank it anyway, because a body needed warmth for thinking, and thinking was all she had.

Through the window she could see the coast road winding down toward the ferry landing, brown and rutted, empty in the afternoon light. Somewhere down that road, or across the water on it, was Hazel, with a letter sewn God-knew-where, walking toward a magistrate and a truth thirty divers had drowned for. And somewhere behind her, drawn along the same road like iron after a lodestone, would come her daughter — because Maren knew Hazel, knew her the way you know a current you've swum against all your life — and Hazel would come. Not because she was foolish. Because she was hers.

*Sacrifice,* Maren thought, watching the light go gold on the mudflats, *is only noble when somebody asks you for it. When nobody asks, it's just love wearing work clothes.*

Nobody had asked her. Hazel had handed her baskets and a role and a lie about weddings, and Maren had accepted all of it with a nod, understanding perfectly well and saying nothing, because saying it aloud would have made it real and unsaid it stayed survivable. Now it was real. She sat in a locked room smelling of bad tea, and the price tag hanging off her read: *one letter, one daughter, one truth.*

And the worst of it — the part that made her press her palm flat against the cold wall, the part she would never say aloud even to herself in the dark — was that she wasn't sorry. If the pearls bought time for Hazel to reach the magistrate, if her detention slowed the hunters even an hour, then the herring, the wedding lie, the six false pearls sitting in an evidence tray downstairs — all of it was cheap. She had paid more for less. She had paid forty years of silence for less.

A key turned in the lock. The guard leaned in.

"Visitor says she's your sister. Name of Berta."

Maren blinked. She had no sister named Berta. She had, however, met two of them on the coast road, either of whom would claim kinship with a fencepost if the fencepost looked like it needed feeding.

"Send her up," Maren said.

Nana Kess shuffled in, ninety-one years old and moving like a woman with a delivery to make, and pressed something small and hard into Maren's hand under cover of grasping both her palms in greeting. Maren closed her fingers around it without looking. She knew the shape. Every diver's widow knew the shape.

"Your sister sends her regards," Nana Kess whispered, "and says the herring was excellent, and says — her exact words — *if they've got you, stay got. Don't let them trade you for anything. She'll come anyway, and it's better if you're worth less when she does.*"

Maren looked down at the object in her palm: a single pearl, wild-bed, grey-green, one of the wreck pearls. A message in itself. *I know what they planted. I know what you did.*

"And where is 'she' now?" Maren whispered.

Nana Kess's old eyes, bright as a gull's, slid toward the window, toward the coast road, toward the gold light going down over the flats.

"On her way," she said. "Girl walks fast when she's scared, and that child is scared enough to outrun the mail."

Outside, far down the brown ribbon of the road, something small was moving. Coming on.
