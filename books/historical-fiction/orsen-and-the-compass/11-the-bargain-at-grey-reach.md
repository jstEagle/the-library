## 11. The Bargain at Grey Reach

The cell they put Maren in had a window the size of a hymn book, and through it Hazel could see her mother's hands folded in her lap as if she were waiting politely for tea rather than rotting in a customs house on a charge of smuggling pearls she'd never touched.

"Mrs. Ashgrove," the customs officer had said, holding up the little drawstring bag with theatrical disgust, as though pearls might bite. "Contraband. Company property, taken without license."

Maren hadn't flinched. She'd looked at her daughter across the crowded customs hall of Grey Reach and said, in the flat voice she used for gutting fish and delivering hard truths, "Well. That's that, then," as though being framed for theft were a weather event one simply dressed for.

Hazel had wanted to scream. Instead she had done what her father's compass was teaching her to do: she had gone still, and watched which way things pointed.

* * *

The officer's name was Pell, and he was the kind of man who polished his boots twice a day and believed this made him better than people who smelled of salt. He sat behind a desk scarred by decades of other men's rings and stamps, and he laced his fingers over his belly and looked at Hazel the way a cat looks at a mouse that has unexpectedly stopped running.

"The fine for pearl-smuggling," he said, consulting nothing, because men like Pell carried their numbers in their chests like medals, "is forty pounds. Or forfeiture of goods equal to value. Or—" he smiled, and it was a smile built from the same parts as a company ledger, "—the woman serves it out. Two years' labor at the company cannery. There's always room at the cannery."

Two years. Hazel thought of her mother's shoulders, already bowed from forty years of nets, and of the cannery women she'd seen once at Salt Quay — grey-faced, grey-throated, coughing brine.

"I don't have forty pounds," she said.

"No," Pell agreed pleasantly. "Divers rarely do. It's the great mystery of the trade."

The letter pressed against Hazel's ribs where she'd sewn it into her coat lining, three days ago now, on the floor of Old Sefa's kitchen while Sefa kept lookout and told jokes so bad they were clearly designed to keep her from thinking about what she was doing. *Why did the magistrate cross the road? Because Meridian paid the toll.* She had laughed anyway, because grief and laughter live in the same room and take turns answering the door.

She could feel the letter now the way you feel a splinter: not painful, exactly, but present, insisting. Thirty names. Her father's name among them. The truth about the Lorelei fleet, sunk in the dark by men who wore the same crest as the man currently smiling at her from behind a desk worth more than her house.

All she had to do was hand it over, and Maren walked free. Pell would never even know what he was holding. A magistrate's seal meant nothing to a customs man; it was just wax, just paper, just another document in a drawer full of documents that changed no one's life but the ones folded inside them.

It would be easy. That was the horror of it. The trade was sitting right there, priced and reasonable: one mother, one letter, one small betrayal that no one would ever have to know about except Hazel, forever, at three in the morning, for the rest of her life.

"Miss?" Pell prompted. "Your mother's charge requires resolution today. The company doesn't warehouse people out of charity."

*No,* Hazel thought. *You warehouse them out of profit.*

And then, underneath the panic, something her father had said surfaced, the way drowned things surface when the tide turns. She'd been maybe ten, complaining that the good shells were all picked over, and Tam Voss — before Tam Voss became a company boy, back when he was just a skinny kid who could skip stones nine times — had been gloating over a shell he'd found. And her father had laughed and said: *The sea gives up its treasure to whoever's willing to look where nobody else bothers.*

Where nobody else bothers.

Nobody bothered looking at divers. That was the whole architecture of the indenture system — you didn't watch people you considered furniture.

"Pell," she said, and was distantly proud that her voice came out level, "may I ask how long you've been stationed at Grey Reach?"

His eyes narrowed a fraction. "Four years. Why?"

"Because I've been diving these waters since I was six, and I'll wager my mother's freedom that there are beds within a mile of your station that aren't on any company chart." She leaned forward. "Unregistered beds. Wild oysters. The sort of thing that happens when a fleet sinks and nobody salvages its grounds."

Something moved behind Pell's eyes — not greed exactly, but the cousin of greed, the one that wears greed's coat when greed isn't looking. Customs men took percentages. Everyone knew it. It wasn't corruption; it was *fees*.

"I'm listening," he said.

* * *

Here is what Hazel had, laid out honestly on the desk of her mind:

From the Lorelei wreck — her father's wreck, her father's secret, her inheritance measured in nacre and dead men's cargo — she had brought away two kinds of treasure. The documents: the sealed letter with its magistrate's seal, and the manifest Sefa had wept over, the manifest proving thirty men had been sent to the bottom for the crime of working someone else's water. Those she would die before surrendering. She had known it since Salt Quay, known it in her bones the way divers know a storm coming before the sky admits it.

And then there were the loose pearls. Fourteen of them, pulled from the shattered hold of the Lorelei, still gleaming with forty years of dark, worth — Sefa's appraisal, delivered with shaking hands — somewhere north of sixty pounds.

Her plan had been to spend them carefully. Ferry passage. Bribes at Tern Gate, if it came to it. Food, lodging, the dull unglamorous arithmetic of staying alive and moving. Sixty pounds was a fortune and a lifeline and the only currency she had that wasn't made of paper.

Pell wanted forty for the fine. But Pell also wanted what every man in the company's orbit wanted, which was more than he was owed, and Hazel had learned enough in four days of running to understand that you never offered a hungry man a meal. You offered him a feast and let him think he'd stolen it.

"I can't pay forty pounds," she said. "But I can show you where sixty lives."

* * *

They went out at low tide, Pell in his absurd polished boots picking his way down the shingle like a man crossing hot coals, two of his deputies trailing with lanterns, Hazel leading them along a stretch of coast she had genuinely dived since childhood — though the bed she named was one her father had shown her and sworn her to secrecy about, because even honest divers kept one pocket of the sea to themselves.

She felt the lie of it like a stone in her shoe. Not the location — that part was true, the oysters were real, she'd make sure of it, she'd dive until her ears bled. The lie was smaller and worse: the pretense that she was helping him. The performance of a girl selling out a family secret to save her mother, when in fact she was spending her father's last gift down to the last coin.

*You're spending the inheritance,* said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like Sefa. *Pearl by pearl. What'll be left when you reach Tern Gate?*

*The truth,* she answered. *The truth doesn't tarnish. That's the whole point of it.*

But her hands shook as she waded into the shallows, and the cold of the water came up her arms like a warning.

* * *

She dove four times. On the first, she brought up nothing, buying time, letting the cold settle into her, letting her mind go quiet and blue as the deep water. On the second, an oyster, empty. On the third and fourth, the real thing — wild oysters fat with pearls, from a bed her father had tended secretly for years, his own small rebellion against a company that claimed to own the ocean.

*He kept secrets too,* she thought, surfacing, gasping, hair plastered to her face. *He kept this bed off every chart. He kept the compass. He kept the letter under the floor of his chest like a coal banked for winter.* She was her father's daughter after all. The whole family business was hiding things from Meridian.

Pell counted the pearls by lantern light on the beach, holding each one up, and Hazel watched his face do the arithmetic of a man realizing he'd caught something far bigger than a smuggled drawstring bag. Eleven pearls, good ones. His deputies hovered. She saw him weigh the temptation to arrest her too, take everything, report whatever number he liked upward.

She saw him decide against it, because she had been careful. She had let him find the bed himself, in front of witnesses. She had made him a partner, and partners couldn't be arrested without paperwork, and paperwork was the one sea Pell had never learned to swim in.

"Call it settled, then," Pell said at last, with the magnanimity of a man who believes he has won. He produced a key. "The woman goes free. The pearls are... seized as evidence." He paused. "Evidence of the bed's location having been properly registered. Which I shall do first thing tomorrow. There will be fees."

"There usually are," Hazel said.

* * *

Maren came out of the cell squinting, and the first thing she did was look at Hazel — really look, the diver's look, reading depths — and Hazel knew she knew. Mothers always knew. Her gaze went from Hazel's face to the wet coat to the absence of weight in the satchel, and something passed over her expression that was not gratitude, exactly. It was older than gratitude. It was the look of a woman watching her child pay a debt she'd have paid herself, and hating it.

"You gave them the pearls," Maren said quietly, once they were clear of the customs house, walking the marsh road in the last copper light.

"Some of them."

"All of them that mattered." Maren stopped. "Hazel. Look at me."

Hazel looked. Her mother's face was lined like old rope, and her eyes were the exact grey-green of the sea before weather, and Hazel loved her so much it felt like drowning.

"That money was your father's," Maren said. "It was yours. Your start. Your way out."

"It bought you out. That's a way out."

"It bought me out and left you poorer than when you started." Maren's voice cracked, just once, like ice on a pond. "That's not a bargain, love. That's just the company taking from us with extra steps. You've learned their arithmetic too well."

"I learned *your* arithmetic," Hazel said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because her mother flinched as though struck.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere out in the marshes a curlew called, that rising, falling note like a question nobody could answer.

Then Maren reached out and took Hazel's face in both hands — hands rough as whetstones, hands that had mended ten thousand nets — and held her there, forehead almost touching forehead, the way she used to when Hazel was small and feverish.

"My arithmetic," Maren said, "was always wrong. That's what your father loved about me. I spent everything on the wrong things and called it living." Her thumb traced Hazel's cheekbone. "Don't you dare apologize for spending on me."

"I'm not apologizing."

"Liar. You've got your father's tell. You go quiet and noble." Maren snorted, and it was such an ordinary, human sound, so wildly out of place on a smuggler's marsh road at dusk, that Hazel laughed despite everything, and the laugh broke something loose in her chest that had been clenched since the customs hall.

"He left us a fortune," Hazel managed, "and I'm trading it away piece by piece to a bunch of boot-polishers and toll-collectors. By the time we reach Tern Gate I'll be paying the ferryman in buttons."

"Then we'd best hope the truth arrives before the purse runs dry," Maren said. She shouldered the satchel — lighter now, nearly empty — and started walking. Then, over her shoulder, with terrible casualness: "How much is left?"

Hazel counted. Three pearls. One for passage, one for food, one for whatever devil waited at Tern Gate.

"Three."

Maren nodded slowly. "Three pearls and a letter that can hang a founder." She glanced back, and for the first time since the cell door had opened, she smiled — a real one, crooked and tired and fierce. "You know, most inheritances come with houses."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a drystone wall, sharing a blanket, watching the moon come up over Grey Reach like a coin somebody had dropped and couldn't afford to retrieve. Maren fell asleep first, breathing slow, her face in sleep suddenly young again, or perhaps just finally honest about how tired she was.

Hazel stayed awake, holding the compass.

Its needle swung in the dark, steady and patient, pointing not north but out toward the water — toward the Lorelei, toward the fourteen pearls still lying in her father's wreck that she hadn't taken, toward the thirty men whose names she carried stitched against her ribs. She had spent eleven of her father's pearls today. She turned the arithmetic over and over, trying to make it come out differently, and it wouldn't.

*You're spending the inheritance,* Sefa's voice said again. But tonight it sounded less like a warning and more like a question: *and what, exactly, are you buying?*

And Hazel found, turning it over, that she knew the answer. She was buying a mother's freedom. She was buying a road. She was buying the chance to stand on a magistrate's steps and read thirty names aloud into daylight. You could not spend a truth and have less of it. That was the difference between pearls and justice: pearls diminished when you gave them away.

Truth was the one treasure that grew in the spending.

She closed her hand around the compass and slept.

* * *

They were two miles down the marsh road at dawn, walking fast, when Hazel heard the whistle behind them — three notes, sharp and deliberate, the signal every village child knew meant *company rider, get off the path.*

She turned. A figure stood on the rise against the pink edge of the morning, and even at that distance she knew the silhouette: tall, narrow-shouldered, standing with the careful stiffness of a boy wearing a uniform he wasn't sure fit yet.

Tam Voss.

In his hand, unfolded, fluttered a paper. Orders. And he was looking directly at her.

"Hazel Ashgrove!" he called, and his voice carried strangely across the flat water, too loud, the voice of a boy shouting to convince himself. "Stop where you are! In the name of the Meridian Trading Company!"

Beside her, Maren went very still. Hazel's hand found the compass in her pocket, and the needle trembled against its glass, pointing — as it always pointed now — straight at the trouble coming for them.
