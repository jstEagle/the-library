## 12. Tam Catches Up

The marsh road smelled of rot and rain, the way it always did after a storm — a sweet, sour breath rising off the reeds like the ground itself had been drinking. Tam Voss walked it with his boots squelching and his orders folded in his breast pocket, and every hundred paces he told himself the same thing: *it's only paper. You're retrieving paper. Men have done worse for less.*

It was true. Men at Meridian did worse for less before breakfast. He'd watched them do it. He'd even held the lamp while they did it, once, back when holding the lamp seemed like honest work compared to gutting fish.

But the orders said *Ashgrove, Hazel* in Mr. Corliss's tight little hand, and under that, *stolen company property, recover by any means,* and "by any means" was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence, the way it always did in company writing. Tam had learned to read between the lines of Meridian documents the way other men read weather. The space between the words was where people got hurt.

He spotted her a mile on, where the causeway narrowed and the marsh opened out gray-green to either side, flat as spilled tea. She was walking with her coat collar up and her chin down, moving fast but not running, the way you move when you know running draws eyes.

His stomach did something complicated.

He had known this moment would come from the second he'd seen her name on the requisition sheet, and he had rehearsed it in the dark of his bunkhouse more times than he cared to admit. In the rehearsals, he was firm. Professional. A man of the company doing company work. In one version — the shameful one, the one he kept returning to anyway — she talked her way past him, because she'd always been cleverer than him, everyone in Saltmere knew that about Hazel Ashgrove, and then he could go home and tell himself there'd been nothing he could do.

In none of the rehearsals did he imagine how small his voice would actually sound.

"Hazel."

She stopped. Didn't turn around right away. When she did, her face was doing something he recognized: the careful blankness of someone deciding what you were going to be allowed to see.

"Tam Voss." Her voice hadn't changed since they were twelve. Still had that dry edge to it, like she found everything slightly funny and slightly disappointing at once. "They give you a hat yet? Or is the wage still too new?"

"They gave me a sash," he said. "For inspections. It's yellow."

"That's not a real color."

"It's a *company* color," he said, and immediately hated himself, because it sounded like a defense, and defending the yellow sash was not why he'd walked four miles through a marsh.

She turned fully now, hands in her pockets, and looked at him the way she used to look at boys who cheated at dice — patiently, giving them room to hang themselves. "You're here about the letter."

"I'm here about—" He stopped. Started again. "There's property missing from an inventory. Company property."

"Company property," she repeated. "In my father's diving chest."

"The chest is company inventory. Everything in it is—"

"Everything in it is company inventory," she agreed. "Including my father's compass? Including his spare knife? Including, I suppose, his teeth?"

"That's not how I meant—"

"How did you mean it, Tam? Because I've been wondering all day how you meant it." She took a step closer. The wind off the marsh pushed her hair sideways and she didn't bother with it. "Did you mean to search me? Because I'll save you the trouble. There's nothing in my pockets but lint and a button. Did you mean to arrest me? Do you know how to arrest somebody? Have they taught you that yet, or are we both going to stand here pretending?"

"I have orders," he said, and pulled the paper out, and hated the sound of it unfolding, crisp and official, like the whole company clearing its throat.

She didn't look at it. She looked at him.

"Read it to me," she said. "Go on. Out loud. 'Stolen company property.' Say those words to my face and tell me which part of my father's death belongs to them."

The wind moved through the reeds. Somewhere out in the flats a curlew cried, three notes, falling.

"You don't understand what's in it," Tam said finally, and heard his own voice crack on the word *understand*, fourteen years old again, caught out at something.

"Then tell me what you think it is."

"It's evidence," he said. "Against the founder. Against Meridian. Sefa told me—" He stopped.

"Sefa told you," she said softly.

"He came to the bunkhouse. Two nights ago. Bought a round for the whole room, which he has never once done in his life, and then sat down next to me like an old friend and asked me if I remembered my father." Tam's jaw worked. "My father dove for Lorelei. Before the fleet went down. Before Meridian bought the beds for nothing and called it fair trade."

Hazel had gone very still. "I didn't know that."

"Nobody knows that. That's rather the point of everything, isn't it?" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Sefa sat there and asked me if I remembered my father coming home with pearls in his pockets and salt in his hair, and whether I remembered what year the fleet sank, and I said I was four. And he said, *yes, and do you remember your mother crying, or do you just remember being told she was sad about the weather.*"

"What did you say?"

"I said I remembered the funeral. Everyone got fish. Free fish. From Meridian." His voice had gone flat and strange in his own ears. "I ate the fish, Hazel. I was four years old and I ate the fish and I thought it was kindness."

* * *

She didn't say anything for a long time. The marsh breathed around them. Then she sat down, right there on the causeway stones, and after a moment he sat too, because standing over her felt like a thing a company man would do.

"My father's name is in that letter," she said. "As a witness. Him and eleven others. Divers who saw the Lorelei scuttled — who saw the crew drowned, thirty men, Tam, thirty — and were paid afterward to forget it. Paid in indentures and silence. My whole life was the payment." She turned the words over like stones. "And I've been carrying it toward the magistrate for six days now, and at every checkpoint I keep thinking, *someone will stop me, someone should stop me, this can't really be happening to me.* Because history doesn't happen to us, does it? It happens to other people. To people in books. Not to pearl divers' daughters on a marsh road."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe history happens to everyone," she said. "Maybe it's only ever been a question of who gets to write it down. And Meridian writes it all. Every ledger, every record, every funeral notice. So this—" she tapped her chest, where the letter rode sewn into the lining, though she didn't say so, and he didn't ask, and they were both careful, so careful, "—this is the first thing anyone's ever written down the other way."

Tam looked at the orders in his hand. Mr. Corliss's handwriting, neat as stitching. *Recover by any means.* He thought about the yellow sash hanging on his bunkroom nail. He thought about his mother, who had taken Meridian's fish at a funeral and never once told him what she was crying about, and who was dead now herself, and couldn't tell him anything anymore.

"If I let you pass," he said slowly, "they'll send someone else. Someone who won't sit down on the road and talk. Corliss doesn't send the same man twice on a failure."

"I know."

"And when they ask me where you went, I'll have to tell them something."

"I know that too."

"So you're asking me to lie to the company. On my third week of wages." He heard how it sounded — young, frightened, and underneath it, decided. He'd known it was decided since the bunkhouse. Since the fish. Maybe since forever; maybe some part of him had been waiting forty years, in the shape of a boy who wasn't born yet, for someone to hand him the bill for thirty drowned men.

"I'm not asking," Hazel said. "I wouldn't put it on you. But I'm telling you the truth, because you're the first person they've sent who might carry it without dropping it. That's worth more than a safe passage, Tam. Truths get heavy. Most people set them down somewhere convenient."

He stood up. Brushed off his trousers, which did nothing against the marsh mud, which never brushed off anything. He unfolded the orders and read them properly for the first time, aloud, in the empty middle of nowhere, his voice steady enough to surprise him:

*"Subject: Ashgrove, Hazel. Property: documents, quantity unknown, origin suspected rival fleet. Instruction: recover by any means. Report to Tern Gate garrison upon completion."*

"Upon completion," he repeated. "Which means if I report nothing recovered, they'll know I failed. And if I report you recovered—" he looked at her, "—you'd be in a cell by tomorrow night."

"Yes."

"So I need a sighting that's true enough to check and false enough to hold." He started walking, thinking aloud, and she fell in beside him, because apparently they were doing this now, two children of Saltmere planning a lie in the open air like it was a schoolyard game. "There's a carrier cart comes through Marsh End at dusk. Driver named Pell. He owes me money, which means he'll do what I say, which is the only kind of loyalty that never disappoints. If I report you took the north track toward Grey Reach ferry—"

"But I'm not going north."

"—then they'll watch the north track. Corliss will pull his men off the marsh road to cover the ferry crossing, because Corliss trusts arithmetic more than people. He'll count the checkpoints and put two men where you ought to be instead of one man where you are." He stopped and faced her. "It'll buy you a day. Maybe two. After that, someone competent comes looking, and Hazel — the next one won't be me. Corliss keeps a man called Bracken for the ones he doesn't trust to his own clerks. Bracken doesn't talk. Bracken doesn't eat with anyone. I've seen him take a dog behind the warehouse and I've never asked what happened."

She was quiet a moment. "Why are you telling me this? The part about Bracken. You could just lie and walk away clean."

"Because if they catch you anyway," Tam said, "I want you to run sooner rather than later. And because—" he rubbed his face, "—because I've eaten their fish my whole life, Hazel. Everybody in Saltmere has. And nobody ever once said grace."

* * *

They parted where the causeway forked, north track and marsh road splitting like a wishbone. She stood on the marsh road side, thin and mud-skirted and utterly unafraid of him, which was somehow the most frightening thing he'd encountered in three weeks of employment.

"Tern Gate," she said. "That's where you're posted next?"

"After this report, yes. Corliss wants trusted hands at the last checkpoint." He smiled without any humor in it. "Congratulations. You've made me trusted."

"Tam." She hesitated, and for a second he saw the girl who'd once shared stolen plums with him behind the salt sheds, back before wages and widows and letters in wax. "Whatever you report tonight — make it good. Make it true enough that they believe you, and false enough that it costs you nothing you can't pay."

"There's no such lie," he said. "That's what I've learned today. Every lie costs. The only question is the currency." He pocketed the orders. "Mine's going to be expensive. Go on. Walk. Don't look back — it's bad luck and worse craft."

She went. She didn't look back, which stung a little, and then he understood it was the kindest thing she could have done, because looking back would have made him a person she was leaving, and he needed to be a detail. A false note in a report. Nothing worth remembering.

He watched until the marsh swallowed her, then turned north himself, toward Marsh End and the dusk cart and Pell, who owed him money.

* * *

The garrison lamp burned late at Tern Gate, and Mr. Corliss read reports the way he did everything, quickly and without mercy.

"North track," he said. "Toward the ferry?"

"Confirmed by the carrier driver Pell, independent of my own sighting." Tam stood at attention and let his voice stay level through sheer muscular effort. "Subject traveling alone, coat hood up, moving quickly. I broke off pursuit at the ford to report rather than lose time — she had a lead of nearly two hours."

Corliss's pen scratched. "Two hours. On foot."

"She's Saltmere-born, sir. They walk like gulls fly."

"Hm." Corliss signed the order without looking up. "Pull Hendricks and Moore off the marsh road. Double the ferry watch. And Voss—" the pen paused, "—you're reassigned to Tern Gate effective immediately. I want reliable eyes at the last gate. You've shown judgment."

"Sir."

Judgment. Tam walked out into the fort yard with the word sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. Behind him, lamplight spilled across Corliss's desk, where a second file lay open — one Tam hadn't been meant to see, its tab marked in red: *Bracken — deployment pending, target unconfirmed.*

Target unconfirmed. Which meant Corliss believed the north-track story exactly as far as it suited him, and no farther, and had already begun spending money on the possibility that Tam Voss was something other than reliable.

That night Tam wrote his mother's name on the inside of his wrist in pencil, the way he did when he wanted to remember whose son he was, and slept badly, and dreamed of free fish.

At dawn, a rider came down the marsh road with news that made Corliss smile for the first time in weeks: a woman matching Maren Ashgrove's description had been detained at Grey Reach customs house, contraband pearls planted in her bag — and the clerk who'd planted them reported a girl asking after her, alone, headed south.

South. Toward Tern Gate. Toward him.

Corliss rang for Bracken.

Tam stood in the yard, watching the marsh light come up gray and gold, and understood with perfect clarity that his lie had bought Hazel a day and cost him everything after — because the next person walking that road toward him might be her, and Bracken was already saddling a horse.
