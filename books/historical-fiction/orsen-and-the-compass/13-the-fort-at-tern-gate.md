## 13. The Fort at Tern Gate

Sefa had not climbed a hill in eleven years, and he intended to inform the hill of this grievance at every switchback.

"You could have told me," he said, "that the smuggler's path went *up*."

"It doesn't go up forever," Hazel said from above him, which was the whole problem with hills — everyone you were walking with was always above you.

"Everything goes up forever if you're seventy-one."

Maren, behind him with her bundle of mended nets and her talent for saying nothing at great length, offered her arm. He took it mostly out of courtesy and partly because his knees had begun drafting a formal petition for retirement. The marsh road fell away below them, stitched grey and silver where the tide came back in over the flats. Somewhere on that road, he knew, a boy named Tam Voss was telling Meridian's men a lie about a girl seen heading west toward the clay pits when she was actually standing three feet behind Sefa eating dried fish. It was a good lie. Sefa hoped it would hold. Good lies were like good nets: they held until they didn't, and then everything spilled at once.

They crested the ridge near midday, and there it was.

Tern Gate.

Sefa lowered himself onto a rock shaped by some ancient patience into a bench and looked at the thing he had been dreading since Salt Quay. The fort sat across the neck of land where the island road narrowed to a single stone causeway between the fort's seawall and the black water of the channel. It was an ugly building, as forts are — all function and no apology — with walls the colour of old teeth and a gatehouse that squatted over the road like a toad waiting for flies. A company flag hung limp above it, too humid to bother being proud. Through the gate, past the checkpoint shed with its slatted window and its queue of carts, the road ran on toward the magistrate's town, visible only as a smudge of roofs and a church spire that seemed, even from here, to be looking away from the fort rather than toward it.

"That's the last door," Hazel said quietly.

"That's the last door," Sefa agreed. "And we're going to have to knock on it."

* * *

They watched through the afternoon, taking turns with a fisherman's spyglass Sefa had bought off a man who swore it once belonged to a pirate, a claim Sefa disbelieved but paid extra for anyway, because hope should be indulged where it's cheap.

What he saw did not improve his mood.

The queue moved slowly. Every cart opened. Every bag turned out onto a table while a company clerk in shirtsleeves made marks in a ledger with the leisurely cruelty of a man paid by the hour. Two guards at the gate itself, one at the shed window, one walking the seawall with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod, and — this was new, this was the detail that put frost down Sefa's spine — a fourth man doing nothing at all. Just standing by the gatehouse door with his hands clasped, watching faces. Not hands. Not bags. Faces.

"They've changed how they're watching," Sefa murmured.

"What do you mean?"

"Used to be they searched things. Now they search *people*. That fellow by the door isn't looking for contraband. He's looking for somebody who walks like she's carrying something." He passed the glass to Hazel. "Look at his eyes. They follow the ones who don't look nervous."

Hazel studied the gate a long time. "So what do we do? Look nervous?"

"Heavens, no. Then they'd know we're amateurs." He took the glass back. "We look like nothing at all. Which is harder."

He said it lightly. Inside, the arithmetic was running whether he wanted it or not. Three checkpoints. One passed. One passed at a price that still sat in Hazel's face like weather — the pearls gone, every one of them except the documents, traded for Maren's freedom at Grey Reach. And here was the third, the fat one, the one with a garrison behind it and the magistrate's town just beyond, close enough now that on a clear evening you could smell woodsmoke from its chimneys drifting out over the channel.

One door left. And Sefa had promised them a key.

* * *

The key's name was Doro.

Corporal Doroteo Lamm, though nobody had called him Corporal in thirty years, had served with Sefa aboard the Lorelei before either of them had opinions worth the name. After the sinking, after the silence, Doro had done what Sefa had not: he'd gone into the company's own uniform, taken their pay, eaten their salt — and spent those years, so he claimed whenever he was deep enough into rum to claim anything, keeping one eye open for the day the ledger might be righted. He was the reason Sefa had ever dared plan this route at all. A man inside the wall. A man who owed the drowned.

Sefa had sent word ahead from Grey Reach. A fishwife going east with a message folded small as a moth: *Old friend coming through Tern Gate within the fortnight. Leave the lamp in the east window if the way is clear.*

The east window of the gatehouse faced the marsh road.

Sefa looked at it now through the spyglass, and looked again, and lowered the glass very slowly, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not give them the satisfaction.

No lamp.

"Maybe he's off duty," Hazel said.

"Maybe." Sefa settled the glass against his eye socket again, the brass cold and familiar as an old debt. "Maybe he's been transferred. Maybe he's sick. Maybe the fishwife never got word through." He paused. "Maybe they caught him."

Nobody said anything to that. Below them the gate creaked open for a cart of tar barrels, closed again. The face-watching man by the gatehouse door shifted his weight and resumed his vigil of ordinary people.

"We wait," Maren said.

It was the first thing she'd said in an hour, and both of them turned to look at her, because Maren's silences were usually load-bearing and her words, when they finally arrived, tended to be the beam that held up the roof. She nodded at the cluster of huts strung along the shore road below the ridge, half a mile east of the fort. Fisherfolk. Net-driers. A tavern whose sign — Sefa squinted — depicted a mermaid holding what appeared to be a loaf of bread, an artistic decision he intended to investigate personally.

"There'll be someone in that village who knows the fort's roster," Maren said. "There always is. Wives talk. Cooks talk. Laundry talks louder than magistrates."

Sefa looked at her with the respect one sailor pays another's navigation. "Maren Ashgrove," he said, "you have been wasted on pearl beds."

"I have been *spent* on pearl beds," she corrected. "It's different." And she started down toward the village without waiting to see if they followed, which was how Maren led.

* * *

The tavern with the bread-holding mermaid was called the Soaked Crumb, which explained the sign and raised further questions it declined to answer. By late afternoon it held a modest congregation: two eel fishermen arguing about bait with the passion other men reserved for religion; an ancient woman by the fire knitting something with no discernible purpose or shape; and a barkeep built like a capstan who polished glasses that appeared already clean.

Sefa ordered three cups of whatever passed for tea here and applied the oldest tool in his kit, older than any compass: he began complaining about the weather, and let the room correct him.

Within ten minutes he had learned that the rains were late, the eel run was early, the fort's garrison had doubled its bread order in the past month ("they're feeding somebody new," said an eel man darkly), and the knitting woman was deaf in one ear and pretending in the other. Within twenty, guided by Maren's quiet genius for asking questions that sounded like sympathy, he learned what he'd feared:

"The big officer with the grey whiskers?" The barkeep shrugged. "That'd be Major Corrigan. Came six weeks ago. Company's own man. Before that it was just a customs detachment — twelve men, sleepy as Sunday. Now it's—" he counted with a rag — "thirty, maybe more. And the major doesn't drink with us, doesn't trade with us, and had the east wing of the gatehouse refitted for his office. East wing." He tapped his nose. "Where the old duty officer kept his rooms."

"And the old duty officer?" Sefa asked, casually, casually, casually.

"Gone." The rag stopped. "Transferred, they said. Left in the night, no goodbyes. Doro Lamm, his name was. Quiet fella. Fed scraps to my sister's cat." A pause, and then, lower, with the particular caution of a man deciding how much truth the room can afford: "His boat's still tied up at the landing. Nobody's touched it. You don't touch a transferred man's boat. But nobody's seen him neither."

Sefa thanked him, paid too much for the tea as penance for the questions, and walked out into the failing light feeling the thread he'd been following go taut, then thin, then — almost — to nothing.

Doro gone in the night. No lamp in the east window. New major. Doubled garrison. And a face-reader at the gate, which meant somewhere in the company's tall offices, someone had finally begun to suspect that paper could be more dangerous than pearls.

* * *

They climbed back to the ridge in the blue hour and made camp in the lee of the rocks, cold food, cold wind, the fort below them lighting its lamps one by one until it glowed like a lantern set to guard a road instead of light it.

Hazel laid out the map she'd drawn from memory — Salt Quay, Grey Reach, the marsh road, and now Tern Gate, marked with her quick pencil strokes. She'd gotten good at maps on this journey. She'd gotten good at a lot of things on this journey, most of them things Sefa wished a girl of seventeen had no call to learn.

"So we find another way around," she said. It wasn't really a question. It was her jaw setting itself, which he'd learned to read the way sailors read cloud.

"Over the channel? Around the causeway?" Sefa shook his head. "The magistrate's office is guarded by clerks, child, not soldiers. The only thing guarding the road is that gate — because Meridian knows that whoever controls the gate controls what the magistrate ever hears. There is no other way through. That's the whole design of it. That's what forts are *for*."

"Then we go through."

"With your face? With your mother's?" He gentled it. "You saw the man at the door. He reads walkers. And I'm a known quantity, girl — forty years ago I dove these same beds for the Lorelei, and Meridian's old men remember faces better than they remember mercy. If I walk up to that gate, I'm not a fisherman. I'm a ghost they thought buried."

Hazel stared at the map. Then at the fort. Then at the map again, and Safa watched her do the thing she did — the thing that had frightened him from the first day and impressed him ever since — which was to refuse the shape of the problem as given.

"The festival," she said suddenly.

Sefa blinked. "What festival?"

"Maren heard it in the village. The lantern festival. For the safe-return-of-the-fleets — it's an old custom, before the company, they still keep it here because the company couldn't be bothered stopping it." Her voice had picked up speed, that current under her words he'd learned to respect. "Hundreds of people on that road, Sefa. Hundreds. Fishwives, children, whole families walking to the shrine past the fort, carrying lanterns, carrying baskets, carrying—"

She stopped. Sefa watched her arrive at the rest of it and watched, also, the fear come in right behind the idea, as it always did, faithful as a dog.

"—carrying papers," she finished softly.

"Carrying papers," Sefa agreed, "past a major who has doubled his garrison and hired himself a man who reads faces." He poked the fire with a stick, buying time, feeling hope pull thin as wire. "It might work. Audacity beats stealth nine times in ten — I've stolen boats on that principle and only been caught twice. But hear me, Hazel. Hear me before you fall in love with it. If that fails, there's nothing after it. No fourth chance. The letter burns in your pocket or it hangs us all, and I am too old to hang gracefully."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. Below them, the fort's lamps burned steady. Somewhere beyond it, past the gate, past the causeway, past the dark miles of road, the magistrate slept in his fine house, not knowing that history was walking toward him one checkpoint at a time.

"Then we make sure it doesn't fail," she said.

And because Sefa was seventy-one, and tired, and had spent forty years owing a debt to thirty drowned men, he heard himself say: "Aye. We make sure."

Though privately, looking at the fort glowing on its causeway like a coal in the world's dark throat, he thought: *we will need more than audacity.*

We will need a miracle with a wick in it.

* * *

It was full dark when the boy came up the path — a fisherman's son from the village, breathless, sent by the barkeep's sister, who apparently talked even louder than laundry.

"Message," the boy panted, "for the old diver. From the landing master." He thrust over a scrap of paper, then stood shifting foot to foot, wanting to be paid in story if not coin.

Sefa struck a match and read.

Three words, in a cramped hand he hadn't seen in thirty years, and his heart went from lead to lightning in the space of a single breath:

*Lamp soon. Trust me. — D.*

He read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had made him certain he'd misread it, and the third confirmed the miracle: Doro Lamm was alive, was *near*, and was promising a lamp in the east window — which meant the old duty officer had not fled at all. He'd gone to ground. He'd burrowed. He was working his way back toward the gatehouse the way a man works a splinter out of his palm: slow, careful, and in absolute silence.

"Well?" Hazel demanded.

Sefa handed her the note and watched her face take the light of it, and felt — for the first time since the ridge — the thread beneath his feet hold weight.

But then the boy, lingering, kicked at a stone and added the part he'd clearly been told to add last, the part that made Sefa's blood remember it was water after all.

"Oh — and the landing master says to tell you quick-like. The major's posted new orders today, painted up on the fort gate for everyone to see." The boy recited them with the relish of a messenger who knows his news matters: "*By order of Meridian Trading Company: all persons entering Tern Gate during the festival period will surrender all lanterns, parcels, and carried goods for inspection. No exceptions.*"

He grinned, pleased with himself, unaware of what he'd just said. Then he scampered off downhill toward the village lights.

Hazel looked up from Doro's note, and Sefa watched the miracle and the wall collide behind her eyes.

"All lanterns," she repeated quietly.

Below them, the fort burned its own lamps against the coming festival, and waited.
