## 14. What Survival Cost

The smuggler's loft above the chandlery smelled of tar, dried fish, and forty years of men deciding not to talk about things.

Sefa knew the smell intimately. He had helped build this loft, back when his knees worked and his hands were worth more than their memories. Old Brannock — dead now, God rest him, and probably complaining about the accommodations — had kept the loft for runners and smugglers and the occasional fugitive, and Sefa had carried half the timber up those stairs on his own back, showing off for a girl named Ilse who had married a rope-maker instead. That was the way of it. You showed off for one life and lived another.

He sat now in the corner where the lamplight didn't quite reach, because old habits were the last thing a man owned outright, and he listened to the girl breathe.

Hazel Ashgrove slept sitting up against the wall, her chin dropped to her chest, one hand still curled around the satchel that held the letter. She'd done that for three days straight — sleep with one hand on the documents, like a woman holding a door shut against the sea. She ate standing up. She checked the window by looking at it without turning her head, which was a trick he'd thought only company spotters knew, and here was a pearl diver's daughter doing it naturally, the way other girls did needlework.

She had her father's stubbornness and her mother's face, and neither of those things was going to save her. What might save her was what she'd shown at Grey Reach: the willingness to put everything she loved down on a table and bargain with it. Sefa had watched her pour out forty years of pearls to buy Maren's freedom, and he had seen something in her face that frightened him more than any Meridian man could.

Relief. She'd been *relieved* to spend it. As if the pearls were a debt she'd been glad to pay, and as if there weren't another debt behind that one, and another behind that.

*You don't know yet,* he thought, watching her breathe. *You think the price is paid in pearls.*

He looked down at his own hands, spread open on his knees. Diver's hands, or what remained of them. The fingers had gone knuckle-heavy decades ago, and the joints ached when rain came, which on this coast meant always. He had spent forty years telling people the aches were from cold water. It was a good lie. Cold water explained everything on this coast — the aches, the silences, the men who woke shouting and pretended they hadn't.

Cold water did not explain what he was about to do.

* * *

They had come to Tern Gate two days ago and found the door shut in every sense that mattered.

Sefa's contact — a fort clerk named Odell, who owed Sefa for a debt so old it had interest growing on it like barnacles — was simply gone. Not dead, as far as anyone could tell. Transferred. Which was worse. Men who died were tragedies; men who were transferred were messages. *We noticed your correspondence, old man. We moved your friend. Do you understand us now?*

Sefa understood them. He had understood them for forty years, and understanding them had kept him alive and small and quiet, and quietness, he was beginning to grasp, was not the same as survival. Quietness was just a slower drowning.

"We can't go through the gate," Hazel had said, standing in the reed-shadow across the road from the fort, watching the guards with that unturning-head trick of hers. "And we can't go around it."

"The marsh goes around," Sefa had said, "and the marsh has eyes too, and the marsh doesn't have a magistrate at the end of it. There's only the gate."

"Then we need the gate to be busy."

He'd looked at her then. She was nineteen years old and she said *we need the gate to be busy* the way her father used to say *the tide will turn*, with the calm of someone who has decided the world is obliged to cooperate.

It was Maren, quietly mending a strap on the satchel, who mentioned the lantern festival. Of course it was Maren. Mothers always know when the calendar turns; they keep whole years inside their heads the way ships keep ballast.

That had been yesterday. Today they waited, and waiting, Sefa had learned long ago, was when the truth got restless and climbed out of its chest.

* * *

"You're staring at me," Hazel said, without opening her eyes. "I can feel it. Like a gull over a fish stall."

"I'm an old man," Sefa said. "Staring is all I have left. My hearing's going, my knees are going, but I can stare at things like a champion."

One eye opened. Nineteen years old, and she could already do the flat look that made grown men explain themselves. He'd seen Maren do the same look to the inventory clerk in chapter — no, in *life*, in the ordinary unnumbered life before all this, and he'd laughed about it with Tomas Ashgrove over beer, *your wife could shame a customs officer into apologizing*, and Tomas had glowed like a lantern.

Tomas. That was the name under everything, the way the wreck lay under everything. Tomas Ashgrove, who had signed witness to something terrible and carried it sealed for twenty years and drowned before he could decide what to do with it. Leaving it to his daughter like an anchor passed hand to hand.

"Ask me," Sefa said.

"Ask you what?" Both eyes open now. Suspicious. Good. Suspicion would keep her alive longer than courage would.

"Whatever you've been not asking me since Grey Reach. You've been circling it for three days like a crab around a bait pot. You look at my hands, and then you look away, and then you ask me if I want tea." He snorted. "Tea. As if I came out of retirement and walked a hundred miles on these knees for *tea*."

"I'm being polite."

"You're being kind, which is different, and kinder than I deserve, which is why I'm going to spoil it." He shifted himself upright against the wall, joints announcing each movement to the room like a town crier. "Sit properly, girl. This isn't a story you'll want to hear slouched."

She sat properly.

* * *

"In 1871," Sefa began, "I was twenty-three years old, and I was the best free diver in the Lorelei fleet. Don't make that face. It's true, and modesty at my age is just vanity wearing its funeral clothes. I could hold four minutes. I could read the bottom by feel alone — sand, shell, mud, the difference between a rock and a sleeping turtle, blind, in bad light. The Lorelei captains fought over me. They paid me in pearls of my own finding, one in ten, and I had a belt with eleven pearls on it that spring, and I was saving for a boat."

The loft creaked. Somewhere below, Brannock's nephew argued cheerfully with a customer about the price of pitch. The ordinary sounds of the living world, going on around the dead ones.

"There were thirty-one divers in the Lorelei fleet that season. Thirty-one men and women, because the Lorelei took women divers when the companies wouldn't — which is one of the reasons the companies hated them, though they dressed the hatred up as concern for propriety." His mouth twisted. "Meridian's founder — I won't say his name, names are what he wanted, names are how a man becomes a legend instead of a criminal — Meridian's fleet worked the northern beds and we worked the southern, and the southern beds were richer, and had been richer for a hundred years, and everyone knew why everyone knew, and nobody said it."

"And then one night—" Hazel started.

"One night in June." His voice went flat, the voice he used for tide tables and prices, because if he let anything else into it he would stop and never start again. "There was a squall due. A real one, black-green in the west, the kind you tie everything down for. Our boats anchored in the lee of the shoal like always, because the shoal breaks the worst of the wind and the water's shallow enough that even a swamped boat is a wading pool. Safe harbor. Everyone knows it. Everyone has known it for a hundred years."

He stopped. Hazel waited. She was good at waiting; diving teaches patience or it kills you.

"That night the storm came early, and it came wrong. Wind from the north-northeast, which never happens in June, driving our own anchors across the bottom, dragging the whole fleet onto the shoal in the dark. And the water rose faster than a squall explains, and there was a swell running through the channel that no squall explains, and I was in the water already — we'd gone down late, racing the weather, greedy for one last basket — and I saw it."

"Saw what?"

"A chain." The word came out of him like a splinter coming out, slow and tearing. "A great ship's chain, laid across the channel mouth, weighted with stone. Somebody had sunk a chain across our safe harbor, and the storm surge was piling through the gap it left, and the current through that gap was strong enough to pull a man off the bottom. To drag a boat sideways onto coral. We weren't caught in a storm. We were caught in a *trap*, and the trap had been built by men who knew the tides better than we did, and it cost them maybe fifty crowns in chain and stone, and it bought them the southern beds."

Hazel's hand had gone white on the satchel strap.

"I got loose of the current," Sefa said. "I don't know how. I've thought about it for forty years and I don't know how, except that I was young and I was the best and being the best is a kind of luck until suddenly it isn't. I surfaced inside the wreck of the *Maren-Luise* — our flagship, Captain Oduya's boat — and the hull was on its side and there was an air pocket in the turn of it, knee-deep of air, and I climbed into it like a rat into a wall. And I heard them."

The loft was very quiet. Below, the argument about pitch had ended. Even the sea outside seemed to be leaning in.

"Divers hold their breath for a living, girl. We know what drowning sounds like from the inside. It isn't screaming, mostly. Screaming wastes air. It's — thrashing, first, and then a kind of knocking, men knocking on hull planks, on rocks, on each other, wanting to climb up something, anything, and then less thrashing, and then the knocking gets slower, and further apart, like a heart slowing, and then it stops, and the stopping is the worst sound there is, because it isn't loud at all. It's the absence of a sound. Thirty people, and the last thing most of them did was knock politely on the underside of the world."

His hands had closed into fists without his permission. He opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way he'd once taught apprentices to equalize pressure. Let it out slowly. Let it out slowly or it breaks you.

"I stayed in that air pocket until dawn. I heard the storm end. I heard the water drop. I heard the silence after, which was bigger than the storm. And when I finally swam out, the sun was coming up pink and beautiful over the beds we'd all bled for, and the water was full of — things floating. Baskets. Belts. People. And I grabbed the biggest piece of wreckage I could find, and I hung on, and I floated south, and a fishing boat picked me up near midday, the only one. One of thirty-one."

Hazel's eyes were wet, but she wasn't looking away. He gave her that. Most people looked away.

"What happened after?" she asked softly.

"After." He almost laughed. "After is the part I'm actually ashamed of, if you can believe there's a ranking system. The Meridian men came within the week. Very sorry, very solemn, hats off, such a tragedy, the sea is cruel. And then a clerk sat down with the survivors — there were four of us washed ashore alive, and two of those died of the fever inside a month, so really three, and then really two — and this clerk had a ledger, and he offered us money. Compensation, he called it. For our loss. And there was a line in the paper, and the line said we affirmed the fleet was lost to natural storm, and if we signed, there was money, and if we didn't sign—"

He shrugged, an old man's shrug, taking in the whole loft, the whole coast, the whole forty years.

"—there was nothing. No work on any company bed from here to the capital, because Meridian owned them all. No credit at any store, because Meridian owned those too. And the clerk was very friendly about it. He said, you're a young man, Sefa, you have a boat to buy. And I had a belt with eleven pearls, except the belt was at the bottom of the channel with everything else, and a man with empty pockets hears the word *compensation* like a hymn."

"You signed," Hazel said. Not accusing. Just placing it, the way she'd place a shell in a sorting tray.

"I signed." He nodded, slowly, giving it its full weight. "Eighty crowns. I bought a boat. I named her the *Second Chance*, which I thought was clever at the time, and fished her for six years, and sold her, and never went hungry again, and every single night for forty years I have lain down on a mattress bought with eighty crowns and listened for knocking."

* * *

"The others?" Hazel asked. "The witnesses. The letter names witnesses — Papa, and a magistrate's clerk, and—"

"Tomas Ashgrove, who was a deckhand on the *Maren-Luise* that season and saw the chain himself when he dove for salvage the next spring. Yes." Sefa rubbed his face. "The clerk didn't get everyone the same day. He harvested us. Some signed for money. Some signed because they were afraid. Some signed because their families needed the beds to stay open, and Meridian made sure they understood the beds' future depended on everybody remembering the same storm. Your father — Tomas held out longest. Years. But he had a wife, and then a daughter, and indentures to feed, and one day the arithmetic beat the conscience, the way it usually does. I don't say that to excuse him. I say it because you should know your father was not weak. He was *outnumbered*. There's a difference, and the difference matters, because weak men break and outnumbered men bend, and bending men can straighten again if somebody gives them a reason."

"He hid the letter anyway."

"He hid the letter anyway." Sefa smiled, and it hurt his face, because smiling and grief use the same muscles after a while. "A bent man, keeping a straight thing hidden inside him for twenty years. Waiting. Hoping someone braver would come along and take it off his hands. And then the sea took him instead, and he never got to choose. So the choosing fell to you, which is unfair, but the sea keeps its own accounts and fairness has never been one of its ledgers."

"And you? Why are *you* here? You signed. You kept your head down for forty years. Nobody would blame—" 

"Stop." He raised a hand, gently. "Don't do me the kindness of saying nobody would blame me, because I have spent forty years making absolutely certain that plenty of people would blame me if they knew, starting with myself. I am not here out of bravery, Hazel. I am here because I am *tired*. There's a difference between those too, and mine is the smaller share of the two."

He leaned forward, and the lamplight found his face at last, all the lines of it, the map of a long cowardice.

"When you brought me that letter and I read Tomas's name in it, I felt the floor go out from under me — you saw it, you thought I was ill. I wasn't ill. I was *relieved*. Forty years I'd been waiting for someone to come and make me do the right thing, because I could never make myself do it alone, and here comes a nineteen-year-old girl carrying her dead father's courage in a satchel, asking nothing, just walking toward the magistrate like it's a Tuesday. And I thought: either I help her, or I die exactly as I lived, which is to say quietly, with the knocking still in my ears."

"So we finish it," Hazel said. "Together."

"Together," he agreed. "But hear me now, girl, because this is the part you don't know yet, the part I've been circling the way you circled me. At Tern Gate, whatever happens — and something *will* happen, Meridian doesn't move clerks around for nothing — you may have to choose what to spend. Pearls. Freedom. Safety. Maybe worse. And when that moment comes, you'll remember that I traded my silence for eighty crowns, and you'll be afraid of becoming me."

He reached out and covered her hand where it gripped the satchel.

"Don't be afraid of becoming me. Be afraid of becoming the clerk. The clerk never lost a minute of sleep in his life, I'd wager, and he's the one I'd rather drown than be. As for me—" he squeezed her hand, and his ruined old diver's grip still had some iron in it, "—as for me, I intend to stand somewhere public soon, in front of witnesses, and say the thirty names out loud. Every one. Captain Oduya. Little Bren, who was sixteen. Ilse's sister Marta, who married the rope-maker's brother, which is how I learned the bride's family's grief, though I never told her I was one of the reasons for it. All thirty. And when I do, whatever Meridian does to me afterward, I will finally be able to sleep on any mattress in the world, bought or unbought, because the knocking will have somewhere else to go. Into other ears. Ears that didn't sign."

Hazel was crying openly now, and she didn't wipe her face, which he respected enormously. Her mother's daughter. Straight-backed even bent.

"The festival," she said at last, voice rough. "Tomorrow night. If the road fills with lanterns—"

"If the road fills with lanterns," Sefa said, "then it fills with witnesses. Hundreds of them. Fishwives and farmers and children, all gathered in one place, all looking for something to see." Something kindled in his old chest, something that had been banked coals for forty years and was now, dangerously, gloriously, catching flame. "And I know a chandler's boy who owes me for a debt older than you, and he can get us a lantern big enough to hide a letter in, and a widow's veil for your mother, and—"

He stopped. Through the loft's small window, down on the dark street below, a lantern was swinging past the chandlery door. Company green glass. And behind it, walking with the particular unhurried stride of a man who is being paid by the hour to be thorough, a figure Sefa recognized, and had hoped never to see again this far south.

The clerk. Not the same clerk — the original would be dust by now — but the same *kind* of clerk, ledger under arm, going door to door along the festival route.

Checking, Sefa understood with a cold settling in his gut, every single lantern that would pass the gate tomorrow night.

"They're counting the lights," he murmured. "Girl — wake your mother. We need to change what we're carrying, and we need to change it tonight."
