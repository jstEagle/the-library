## 15. The Festival Cover

The smuggler's loft smelled of tar and dried fish and forty years of men deciding not to ask questions. Hazel had been awake since the gulls started, which was to say since well before the sun bothered to make an argument for the day. She lay on her back beneath a window no larger than a loaf of bread and watched the light come up over Tern Gate.

From here you could see the fort plainly — its squat stone shoulders, the flag of the Meridian Trading Company snapping above it like something alive and hungry, and below it all, the road. The only road. It ran along the causeway between the marsh and the sea, narrow as a sentence, and at its middle sat the gate: timber and iron, two lanterns burning even by daylight because company men liked their little fires of authority. Beyond the gate, three miles on, the magistrate's courthouse with its white columns pretending to be somewhere more civilized.

Three checkpoints. They had passed two. The third was a mouth, and everything they carried would have to walk straight into it.

"Hazel." Sefa's voice from the ladder, low and gravelled. "You've been staring at that gate so long I'm starting to worry it's staring back."

"It is," she said. "It's very good at it."

* * *

They gathered around the table that afternoon — Hazel, Sefa, and Maren, who had refused, after Grey Reach, to be sent anywhere ever again, thank you very much, and had made this position known with the particular firmness of a woman who had once been arrested on false charges and found she rather disliked it. A pot of weak tea steamed among them like a fourth conspirator.

"The contact at the fort is gone," Sefa said again, though everyone knew it. "Tomas Vey. Twenty years he held a post there, passing what needed passing. Now his bunk is empty and his name isn't on any roster, and when I asked after him at the fish market, a man who owed me money suddenly remembered he didn't." He turned his cup in circles. "So. No friend inside. One road. One gate. Guards who are looking — and now looking harder, thanks to my old face asking questions."

"Then we stop trying to be invisible," Hazel said.

She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way her father's compass swung — sudden, certain, pointing at something no one else could see yet. All three of them looked at her.

"There's a festival," she went on, and now the words came easier, the way water comes easier once you've broken the surface. "The Lantern Night. For the drowned of the whole coast — every village sends boats, every road fills up. Fishwives and divers and children carrying lights for their dead. Maren, your mother took you when you were small?"

"Every year until I was twelve," Maren said slowly. "The whole causeway glowed like the sky had fallen into it and couldn't get back up."

"The guards can't turn that away," Hazel said. "Hundreds of mourners, thousands of lanterns. If they search every hem and cuff they'll have a riot, and Meridian hates riots worse than it hates magistrates. They'll wave people through. They'll be watching for smugglers skulking in the reeds — not for a girl walking down the middle of the road in broad festival colors with everyone else."

"And the letter?" Sefa asked. His eyes had gone sharp, the way they did when he was measuring a current before trusting it.

Hazel reached beneath the floorboard and drew out the packet — the magistrate's letter and the Lorelei manifest, wrapped in oilcloth, worn soft now from being carried against her ribs through storm and checkpoint and betrayal. She set it on the table between the teacups, and it looked so small. It always did. Thirty drowned men, folded down to something you could lose in a pocket.

"Not in my coat," she said. "Not in my boot, not in my hair, nowhere they'd think to look on a person. In a lantern."

Maren's breath caught. Sefa said nothing, but his eyebrows performed a slow, skeptical migration upward.

"A festival lantern," Hazel pressed on, warming to it, feeling the strange bright thing in her chest that had been fear all week transmuting into something with edges and weight. "A big one, paper over cane frame, the kind you carry on a pole. Everyone will assume there's a candle inside. There will be — a real one, small, off to one side, because a dark lantern is suspicious and a lit one is invisible. And behind the flame, taped to the frame where no guard's hand will go near a live wick—" she tapped the oilcloth packet, "—this. Let them confiscate my bag. Let them pat my coat. I'll hand them my mother's sewing kit if they want it. The one place a careful man won't reach is into fire."

"That's either brilliant or insane," Sefa said.

"In our family those are usually the same thing," said Maren.

* * *

They spent the next two days building the lantern, and it was, unexpectedly, almost pleasant work — the first hours since Salt Quay that Hazel's hands had something to do besides shake.

The frame came from a fisherman in the loft below who traded it for Sefa's opinion on a disputed anchor. The paper was rice-paper, thick enough to hide what lay behind it but thin enough to glow, bought from a chandler who assumed they were mourning someone specific and offered condolences that none of them knew how to refuse. Maren sewed the oilcloth packet flat against the inner frame with waxed thread, stitches so fine they'd have shamed a surgeon, and then — this was her idea, and Hazel loved her fiercely for it — she sewed a second layer of plain paper over the packet itself, so that even if a guard's eye somehow caught the shape of it, he'd see only paper behind paper, a doubled wall like every lantern ever made.

"A candle sits here," Maren murmured, positioning a stub of beeswax in its tin cup, well forward on the base. "Flame nowhere near your letter. And if some fool tips the lantern, the candle gutters in its cup and the paper takes a scorch mark. Scorch marks are honest. Scorch marks say *festival*, not *contraband*."

"When did you become the smuggler?" Hazel asked.

"I raised a pearl diver and a liar," Maren said placidly, biting a thread. "I absorbed things."

Sefa, meanwhile, drilled them. That was the word for it — drill, like soldiers, except his soldiers were two women and a paper lantern, and the drills were questions fired across the tea table.

"Why are you traveling to the gate?"

"To carry our light for the drowned," Hazel answered. "Our family lost a man to the sea."

True, every word, and that was the trick of it — Sefa had insisted on this. "Never build a lie from scratch," he'd said. "Build it out of true things. Lies built from truth don't creak under pressure."

"What's in the lantern?"

"Our dead. What else goes in a lantern?"

"Who taught you to make it?"

"My mother. Her mother took her, when she was small."

Sefa nodded each time, then tried to break them — barked the questions in a guard's voice, in a bored voice, in a cruel voice, while Maren kept sewing and Hazel kept answering until the answers lived in her spine instead of her head.

On the second evening he stopped mid-drill and looked at her a long moment. "You know what you're really doing," he said. Not a question.

"Walking through the front door."

"You're carrying your father's name into a company fort with a candle for cover." His voice had gone rough. "Forty years I snuck past that gate in my own heart. You'll stroll through it holding a light."

"Somebody has to," Hazel said. "You taught me the route. Nobody taught you how to stop apologizing for surviving it. Maybe I can do both jobs."

Sefa turned away and pretended very hard to study the window, which was how that man cried.

* * *

The night before, Hazel couldn't sleep, and gave up trying around midnight. She climbed to the loft's little window with the compass.

Her father's compass. Brass worn to gold at the edges, glass clouded like an old man's eye. North meant nothing to it; it never had, not since the night she'd first noticed the needle straining east and down, toward the shoal where the Lorelei lay folded in the sand with thirty men inside her. Toward what was lost. Tonight the needle pulled that way steadily, patiently, a dog waiting at a door it knew would open.

"I'm coming," she told it quietly. "Not yet. Tomorrow we walk right past the ones who sank her, and then we finish it."

The needle trembled, or seemed to. Compasses don't answer. But she had stopped needing them to.

Below in the loft, Maren slept with her back to the wall and her boots on — she had done that every night since Grey Reach, and Hazel understood without asking. You don't take your boots off twice.

* * *

Festival day arrived smelling of woodsmoke and frying dough, and the road to Tern Gate filled like a wound fills with blood — slowly at first, then all at once, then everywhere.

Hazel had expected crowds. She had not expected joy. That was the part that undid her, standing in the staging yard by the ferry landing with the great lantern on its pole and hundreds of strangers pressing warm around her: the laughter. Children chasing each other between the legs of adults. Old men arguing cheerfully about whose village's lanterns were better constructed. A woman selling honey-cakes shouting prices over the heads of six customers at once. Everyone dressed in white and blue, the colors of the sea's mercy, and every single one of them carrying a light for someone the water had taken.

All these people, grieving openly, loudly, sweetly — and not one of them knowing that grief had a villain, that the water had been helped, that thirty men on the Lorelei had been murdered with ballast stones and lies. Their mourning was innocent. Hers was loaded.

"Stop making that face," Maren murmured beside her. She wore white too, and looked startlingly young in it. "What face?"

"The face of a girl about to commit a crime inside a memorial service."

"That's called a face, Mother. Everyone has one."

They joined the river of walkers where the marsh road bent toward the causeway, and the crowd closed around them like water closing over a diver — total, warm, anonymous. This, Hazel realized, was the whole genius of it, if genius it was: she had spent weeks learning to be hidden, and it turned out the best hiding place on the entire coast was other people. A smuggler alone is a target. A mourner among ten thousand mourners is weather.

The fort grew ahead of them, stone and iron and the company flag, and the gate with its two guards became real — became men, became rifles slung casually, became a sergeant with a ledger checking faces against some list Hazel tried not to imagine. Her heart began its old treacherous drumming. She felt for the pole in her hands, the smooth wood, the faint warmth of the packet through layers of paper and cane, and made herself breathe the way her father had taught her for diving: four counts in, hold, six counts out. Down deep, panic is just air mismanaged.

"Sergeant!" boomed a voice behind them, and Hazel's soul briefly left her body.

It was only a fat festival elder with a brass bell, haranguing the guards with the confidence of a man who had known the sergeant's mother. "You'll keep your men off the lantern-bearers! These are the drowned you're jostling, boy! Your own grandmother walked this road with a light, may she rest, and if you put one fingerprint on a widow's lantern I'll have the whole coast down on this gate by morning!"

The sergeant, visibly weighing his wages against his grandmother's memory, waved the column through with a jerk of his chin. "Lanterns lit at the gate arch! Keep moving, no stopping on the causeway!"

The line lurched forward. Ten paces. Five. The gate arch swallowed them, cool shadow, torch-smell, and a guard's hand — Hazel watched it come, watched it hover near her lantern, watched herself lift the pole slightly, offer it, smile the empty festival smile —

"Lantern's lit, miss," the guard said, squinting at the warm glow. "Mind the flame."

"Always do," said Hazel, and walked through.

Just like that. The most guarded gate on the coast, passed in nine words. On the far side the crowd re-formed around them, the fort fell astern, and the courthouse columns rose white and blind in the distance like a promise.

Maren's hand found hers and gripped it hard enough to mean several sentences at once.

They were through. Two hundred yards of open causeway left, the whole plan holding, the letter glowing gently inside its lie —

And then, at the roadside ahead, leaning against a milestone as though he'd been waiting all day, stood a young man in company grey with his arms crossed and a look on his face Hazel knew from another lifetime.

Tam Voss had found them anyway.
