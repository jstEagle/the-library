## 16. Tern Gate Burns Its Lanterns

Maren Ashgrove had buried a husband, outlived an indenture, and once pulled a living octopus off her own leg without screaming — so she considered herself a woman who did not frighten easily. But standing in the lantern-light of the festival crowd outside the fort at Tern Gate, watching her daughter's face do arithmetic with fear, she felt something new settle over her like wet wool.

It was not fear for herself. It was the particular dread of a mother who has spent nineteen years telling her child to be careful and has finally realized that careful was never going to be in Hazel's vocabulary.

"You're doing it again," Hazel said.

"Doing what?"

"That face. The one where you've already decided something terrible and you're deciding whether to tell me."

"I don't have such a face."

"You absolutely do. Sefa calls it your *storm face*. He says he can smell rain coming when you make it."

Maren adjusted the shawl at her shoulders and looked past her daughter's shoulder instead, which was easier. The road to Tern Gate had filled all afternoon with people walking in from the marsh villages: whole families carrying paper lanterns on bamboo poles, children with candles cupped in their hands like small captured moons, old women hauling baskets of sweet rice and fried dough as if grief were best attended on a full stomach. The lantern festival came but once a year, and this year it happened to fall within sight of the fort's walls, which meant that by nightfall there would be more witnesses pressed against the gate than Meridian could reasonably arrest.

That had been Hazel's reasoning, delivered in the smuggler's loft two nights ago with the fierce bright certainty that Maren recognized from Hazel's father, Orsen, who had also always believed that audacity was a kind of armor. It had gotten him drowned, in the end. But Maren kept this observation to herself, because she had learned something in the last weeks that had rearranged her understanding of her own daughter: Hazel was not being reckless. She was being brave, which is a different animal entirely, one that knows exactly how much it stands to lose.

The letter rode inside Hazel's coat. Maren knew this the way she knew where the salt jar sat in a dark kitchen — by long habit, without looking. Their whole journey since Grey Reach had been shaped around that folded square of paper, the two of them moving through festival crowds and marsh roads like women carrying embers in their skirts, careful of wind, careful of jostle, careful above all of Tam Voss, who had let them pass and then lied to his employers about it and was now, they could only assume, a man running out of lies.

"He'll search everyone," Hazel said quietly. "I can feel it. Look at the gate."

Maren looked. The great timber gates of Tern Fort stood open — they were open every festival year, because Meridian liked to appear generous one night out of three hundred and sixty-five — but flanking them stood six guards in company grey, and beside them a table, and behind the table a clerk with a ledger and the particular expression of a man who enjoys confiscations the way other men enjoy festivals.

"They're taking the lanterns," Maren said.

As she said it, a family near them reached the table. A father held up a lantern painted with a carp, wobbling on its pole, and the clerk took it from him almost gently, set it down behind the table among a growing pile of confiscated lights, and made a mark in his ledger. The children began to cry. The father argued, then stopped arguing, because a guard put one hand flat on the table and looked at him until he didn't.

"Why would they take lanterns?" Hazel whispered. "They're paper. They're nothing."

"Because they're not nothing," Maren said. "A lantern's a thing you carry. Anything a crowd carries can hide something." She had watched company clerks work for twenty years; she knew their grammar. "And because they can. That's half of any rule the company makes. They take what they can take, to remind everyone there are things they can take."

Hazel's hand had gone to her coat front without her seeming to notice she'd done it. Maren noticed. Maren noticed everything tonight, the way a drowning woman notices water.

* * *

They joined the slow river of people flowing toward the gate, and for a while neither spoke. Around them rose the ordinary music of a festival: someone selling sugared ginger, a fiddler tuning badly, two boys daring each other to touch the fort wall, which every child on the coast knew was forbidden and therefore irresistible. Overhead the first true dark was coming down, and the crowd's unlit lanterns hung pale as fish bellies.

"Sefa should have come with us," Hazel murmured.

"Sefa is seventy-one years old with lungs full of forty years of salt," Maren said. "He carried this far. We carry it the rest of the way." She paused. "Besides, if we're both taken, someone needs to know where the pearls went and how much we paid the ferryman, or we'll have been robbed twice."

Hazel laughed despite herself — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered. That was another thing Maren had learned about her daughter on this road: that humor lived in her like a spring under rock, and came up even when everything was worst, especially when everything was worst. Orsen had been the same. Grief and laughter sharing a well.

The queue moved forward. Ten yards from the table, Maren could see the clerk's hands clearly now — white, soft, ink-stained — turning back one traveler after another, lifting hems, probing baskets, sliding fingers along seams. Along seams. She saw his thumb find the stitching of a fishwife's bodice and press along it, methodical as a snail's foot, and something cold walked down Maren's spine.

Because she knew what was sewn into a seam ten feet ahead of her. She had sewn it herself, in the smuggler's loft, by candlelight, with Sefa's thread and her own steady hands, into the lining of her daughter's left coat pocket — a pocket now hanging empty and innocent, while the letter itself lay flat against Hazel's ribs beneath her shift, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her body.

No. Not the seam. Maren ran it back through her memory. The seam held only the false trail: three worthless river pearls and a scrap of cloth, bait they'd agreed upon at the last moment in case of exactly this. The letter was under the shift, against the skin. Which was worse. A seam a clerk might miss. Skin, never — a body search would find it in a breath, and a girl traveling alone who turned out to carry company documents would vanish so thoroughly that even Sefa's names would not find her.

The queue moved again. Eight yards.

"Hazel." Maren kept her voice low and level, the voice she'd used to call Hazel home from deep water when the tide was turning. "Listen to me, and don't argue, because I've had nineteen years of practice and you cannot win."

"Mama—"

"The clerk will search you. He's searching girls your age specifically. I've counted four. They think the courier will be young, so they're checking the young ones hardest." Six yards. "You cannot go through that table with it on your body."

"Then you take it and walk through as yourself, and I'll make noise on the steps and draw them—"

"And they'll take you, and I'll be holding the letter with no one left to read it aloud, which was the entire point of everything." Five yards. The clerk turned back a man with a wooden flute hidden in his sleeve, and the man went white as milk. "There's only one person at this gate they will not search thoroughly, and it is not you."

Hazel looked at her. And Maren watched her daughter arrive, visibly, at the answer — watched the protest rise and die in the same breath, because Hazel was quick, painfully quick, and she understood the shape of the thing before Maren finished drawing it.

"No," Hazel said anyway. Because she was also her mother's daughter.

"Yes," Maren said. "A grieving widow delivering memorial papers for the festival. Papers the guards won't want to touch, because touching a dead man's papers is bad luck, and these men are superstitious the way all bullies are superstitious. I'm the right age. Half the widows on this coast lost men to the water; nobody questions my face. And—" here she allowed herself the smallest smile, "—I have spent twenty years making company clerks believe whatever I needed them to believe about what a poor indentured woman could possibly matter. You learned boldness from your father, love. I learned this from them."

* * *

Four yards. The clerk's voice carried down the line: "Lanterns surrendered at the table. All lanterns. No exceptions. Next."

A guard began working backward along the queue collecting poles, and the crowd rippled with unhappiness — a low murmur, the sound of a festival being pickpocketed of its heart. Somewhere a woman said loudly that she'd carried that lantern three miles and would carry it home again unlit before she'd leave it, and the guard, remarkably, let her keep the unlit shell and took only the candle. Small mercies. Small enough to count.

"Now," Maren said. "Turn with me. Face me, laugh at something, as if we're only mother and daughter at a festival. Hands busy."

She took Hazel's hands in hers, and for a heartbeat the whole plan nearly failed, because Hazel's hands were shaking and Maren's suddenly wanted to. Nineteen years of handing things across this exact gap — a bowl of rice, a mended net, a feverish child's hot forehead — and none of those handings had ever felt like this one, like passing a coal between them, like trusting the other not to drop it.

But they were divers' wives and divers' daughters, and divers' hands learn steadiness young.

Under cover of an embrace that looked to the world like nothing more than a mother straightening her daughter's collar, Maren drew the oilcloth packet from beneath Hazel's shift — quick, practiced, the motion of a woman who had hidden contraband pearls from inventory clerks for two decades and had once, memorably, concealed a whole smoked ham in her petticoats during the famine winter, though that story had never been told and never would be — and slid it into the wide front fold of her widow's sash, where it settled flat against her stomach like a second heartbeat.

"There," she breathed. "Done. You're free."

"I'm not free," Hazel whispered fiercely. "We're both still—"

"Hazel." Maren caught her daughter's chin and made her look up. "For once in your life, let your mother carry the heavy thing."

Something moved across Hazel's face — resistance, terror, and underneath them both, unmistakable, relief, which Maren chose to interpret generously. Then Hazel's jaw set in the way Maren knew meant a decision had closed like a fist.

"When you get through," Hazel said, "you walk straight up the hill to the magistrate's house. Don't wait for me. Don't look back. If I'm taken, you deliver it alone. Promise me."

"I promise nothing of the kind. If you're taken, I will cause such a scene on those steps that they'll forget your name by morning, and I will still be standing there at dawn with the letter, and so will you, because you will not be taken. I forbid it."

"You can't forbid—"

"I'm your mother. I can forbid anything. It's the one law older than the company." Three yards. The clerk looked up. "Smile, love. We're only two women at a festival. Walk behind me and grieve beautifully."

* * *

The table arrived.

Up close, the clerk was younger than Maren had guessed, with a rash of pimples along his jaw and the anxious diligence of a man terrified of being blamed for whatever he failed to find. His eyes went to Hazel first — Maren could see him cataloguing her: young, coastal, alone-ish, suspicious — and Maren stepped smoothly into his line of sight like a rock shifting into a current.

"Papers for the magistrate," she said clearly, before he could speak. "Memorial papers. For the drowned. My husband is eleven years with the water, and I bring his name to be spoken at the fort shrine tonight, as the custom allows."

It was true, or true enough — there was such a custom, old as the coast, older than the company, and the clerk hesitated precisely as she'd known he would, because company men feared old customs the way cats fear vacuum cleaners: irrationally, and on principle.

"Memorial papers," he repeated.

"Names of the dead," Maren said, and let her voice roughen, which cost her less than it should have, because eleven years of grief was real and merely required borrowing. "Thirty-two names. My husband's among them. Will you make me unwrap them on this table? In front of everyone? Will you put your hands on a dead diver's name?"

Behind her, she heard the crowd go quiet in the way crowds do when someone speaks a truth too plain to interrupt. She heard Hazel breathing — controlled, shallow, a diver conserving air.

The clerk wavered. His eyes flicked to the nearest guard, an older sergeant with a grey mustache, and the sergeant shrugged with magnificent indifference and said, "Widow's papers go through, Fenn. Every year. You want the whole coast cursing your mother's house?"

"But regulations say—"

"Regulations say confiscate lanterns. Nobody said confiscate grandmothers' ghosts." He waved her forward with two fingers. "Through. Mind the threshold, it's raised."

Maren bowed her head — grief and gratitude braided seamlessly, and not entirely performed — and walked through the gate of Tern Fort with thirty-two names against her belly and her heart hammering hard enough to shake them.

She did not look back. Hazel had asked her not to, and besides, looking back was for people who believed the ones they loved might not follow. Maren had raised Hazel through storms, fevers, and one memorable encounter with a moray eel. Her daughter followed. Her daughter always followed.

Only — the thought arrived halfway up the fort road, in the sudden hush beyond the walls, where lanterns glowed in windows and the crowd thinned toward the magistrate's square — only, for the first time in nineteen years, Maren realized she was the one walking ahead, and Hazel was the one trusting her to lead.

* * *

She was fifty yards up the hill, close enough to see the magistrate's house lit gold against the dark, when the shouting started behind her.

She turned — breaking her own rule instantly, because rules bend for daughters — and saw torchlight boiling at the gate like disturbed ants. The crowd was surging. Guards were shoving. And above the noise cut one clear voice she knew better than her own, pitched high with fury:

"It's mine! It's my father's lantern — you can't burn it — *it's my father's!*"

They had found the decoy seam. Of course they had — Hazel had built the bait to be found. And they had found, tucked with the false pearls, the scrap of cloth Hazel had chosen with Sefa's help: a strip of Lorelei sail-canvas, forty years in the water. Evidence, planted deliberately, enough to make the guards believe they'd caught the courier.

Enough to make them keep her.

Maren stood on the darkening road with the true letter pressed against her heart, and watched torches begin to move up the hill after her, and understood that the plan had worked perfectly, and that perfect was going to cost everything she had left.
