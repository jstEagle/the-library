## 17. Betrayal at the Magistrate's Steps

Tam Voss had been given three orders in his life that mattered, and he had followed two of them.

The first had come from his mother, the winter she was dying: *Look after your sisters.* He had been eleven. He had looked after them the way an eleven-year-old does — badly, with great sincerity, and a good deal of stolen bread. The second had come from Meridian: *Find the Ashgrove girl and retrieve company property.* He had tried. He had even written his false report in his neatest hand, the one Mr. Quill at the village school had praised before Quill went to work for the company himself and stopped praising anything free of charge.

The third order had come this morning, delivered by a man named Brack with hands like boat cleats and breath like a bilge pump.

"Finish it," Brack had said, on the marsh road outside Tern Gate, while gulls argued over something dead in the reeds. "The girl. The papers. The old man if he's convenient." He had said it the way you'd discuss freight rates. Then he'd added, almost kindly, "They know about your report, Voss. The false sighting. They know."

And there it was — the thing Tam had been carrying since the marsh road, since Hazel Ashgrove had stood in front of him with her father's compass in her fist and told him about the Lorelei fleet, about thirty divers sent down into dark water by men who wanted their pearl beds, about how his own grandfather's name was on the manifest of the drowned. He had let her pass. He had lied in writing to the Meridian Trading Company, which was rather like lying in ink to the tide.

He had known, even as he wrote it, that the lie would find him out. Companies are patient that way. They forgive debt; they never forgive arithmetic.

So now he stood at the edge of the magistrate's square in Port Calloway, in a coat too fine for him — Brack's idea of a disguise, as though a coat could make a fisherman's son look like anything but a fisherman's son playing dress-up — and watched the crowd gather on the steps.

* * *

The square smelled of tar, frying fish, and the particular excitement of people who have heard something is going to happen and intend to witness it whether or not they approve. Word had traveled ahead of Hazel — word always did, moving through fishing towns faster than any official post, carried by fishwives and ferry men and one extremely talkative boy who sold candied ginger near the customs house. A crowd had assembled: divers with rope-scarred palms, net-menders, dockhands on their lunch hour, a scattering of merchants pretending to be merely passing through with excellent views of the steps.

Hazel stood near the bottom of the stairs with her mother, Maren, beside her. Maren held a bundle against her chest — memorial papers, or so she would say if asked, and Tam knew better than anyone alive that she would say it without blinking, because he had watched her lie to an auditor with the serene face of a woman discussing the weather. Beside them, leaning on a stick that seemed more ornamental than necessary, stood Old Sefa, looking like a man who had been waiting forty years for an appointment and intended to be early for it.

And above them, on the steps themselves, under the carved stone crest of the colonial administration, waited the magistrate — a small, dry man named Aldous Wren who had the complexion of someone who had spent his whole career indoors avoiding weather, conscience, and eye contact in roughly equal measure.

Tam's orders were simple. Get close. Take the papers. If the girl resisted — Brack had paused here, and the pause had contained everything — *handle it.*

He moved through the crowd. This was easy; crowds part for a man walking with purpose, especially a young man in a borrowed coat, especially when half the crowd assumes any purposeful person must be somebody's clerk. He got within ten feet of Hazel. She hadn't seen him yet. She was scanning the square, checking sightlines, doing whatever it was she'd learned to do between Salt Quay and here — and Tam thought, absurdly, of the girl she'd been two years ago, hauling nets at dawn, who used to trade him a share of her breakfast for stories about the mainland because she'd never been off the island.

She turned. Saw him.

He watched the recognition land, and then he watched something worse land behind it: understanding. Her hand went to the satchel at her side. Sefa's stick shifted, very slightly, to a more useful angle. Even Maren turned, calm as deep water, positioning herself between her daughter and him without appearing to move at all.

"I'm not—" Tam started, and found his voice had gone somewhere. He tried again. "I'm not here for that."

"You're wearing Brack's coat," Hazel said quietly. "Everyone knows Brack's coat. It has a stain shaped like a squid."

"It does," Tam admitted. "It's how I know whose coat it is."

A laugh rippled through the nearest listeners, quickly smothered — laughter being a currency the company taxed heavily. But it loosened something in the square, a small unclenching, and Tam understood suddenly that these people were not spectators. They were the audience of a story they already half-knew, waiting for someone to say the rest of it aloud.

* * *

Here is what Tam Voss knew about the Lorelei fleet, and had known since he was seven years old, sitting on his grandmother's knee:

He knew that his grandfather, Emeric Voss, had dived for the Lorelei line, and that Emeric had come home one season smelling of salt and secrets, and had said to his wife, in the low voice men use when children are meant to overhear nothing and overhear everything: *"If anything happens to me, it wasn't the water. Remember that. It wasn't the water."*

He knew that three weeks later, the Lorelei fleet went down in a storm that every old diver swore had no business sinking anything, and thirty men did not come home, and the company paid out settlements calculated to the penny and asked no questions, and neither did anyone else, because asking questions of the Meridian Trading Company was a pastime with a short life expectancy.

He knew that his grandmother had taken the settlement money and bought bread with it for eleven years, and had never once eaten a bite of it without looking, first, at the sea.

What Tam had not known — what Hazel had told him on the marsh road, standing in wind that smelled of coming rain — was that there were papers. That his grandfather's name was written in them. That the drowning had a signature, and the signature belonged to a founder of the Meridian Trading Company, a man whose portrait hung in the company hall smiling the smile of a man who had never once gotten salt water in his lungs.

*They paid us to forget,* Hazel had said. *Sefa took the money. Your family took the money. Everyone took the money, because the alternative was nothing to eat. That's not the same as agreeing.*

That was the sentence that had undone him. Not the accusation — the absolution. He had braced all his life for someone to blame his family for their silence, and instead this girl, this indentured diver with salt-cracked hands, had handed him back his own history and said: *this happened to you too.*

* * *

Brack was somewhere behind him. Tam could feel it, the way you feel weather coming — a pressure at the back of the neck. Two other company men flanked the edges of the square, trying to look casual, achieving the look of men who had been told to look casual by someone who hated them.

On the steps, the magistrate was speaking now, in the oiled voice of a man reading from notes prepared by someone else. Something about petitions. Something about proper channels. Something about the regrettable tendency of certain parties to circulate *unverified claims*, and how His Honor was confident such matters could be resolved *privately, and to everyone's satisfaction.*

Privately. To everyone's satisfaction. Tam had grown up around men who talked like that. It meant: *we will pay you, and then we will bury you, in whichever order is cheapest.*

Hazel climbed three steps. The crowd hushed. She reached into her coat — and Tam saw Brack shift forward at the edge of his vision, saw the big man's hand drop toward his belt, saw the two flankers begin to converge, unhurried, professional, the way sharks are unhurried.

And Tam Voss, who had followed two important orders in his life and botched both in opposite directions, discovered that he had a third order after all. It just hadn't come from anyone with the authority to give it.

He stepped past Hazel. He climbed the steps. He turned to face the square.

"Honorable Magistrate Wren," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, which was probably for the best, because a cracking voice sounds like honesty and his voice had never in its life sounded like anything else. "My name is Tam Voss. I'm a spotter for the Meridian Trading Company. I'm supposed to arrest this girl right now."

The square made a sound like the sea deciding something.

"And I'm not going to," he said.

* * *

Later — much later, in the retellings, which grew more polished with each telling until Tam barely recognized himself in them — it would sound decisive. It would sound like a speech. In the moment it was a disaster. He had planned exactly none of it. Words came out of him the way fish come out of a torn net: all at once, flopping, some of them still alive.

"They sank the Lorelei fleet," he said. "In 1879. It wasn't a storm. They cut the moorings and they weighted the catch lines and they let thirty men drown so they could take the beds. My grandfather was one of them. Emeric Voss. He knew something was wrong — he told my grandmother *it wasn't the water* — and they paid her off with eleven years of bread money and she died ashamed of taking it, and she had nothing to be ashamed of, because—"

His voice went. Fully, this time. He stood on the steps of the colonial magistrate's office in a coat with a squid-shaped stain, mouth open, eyes burning, unable to produce a single further syllable, and it was Old Sefa — of course it was Sefa, the old man moved faster than anyone believed possible for a man his age — who came up beside him and put one rope-scarred hand on his shoulder and said to the crowd, conversationally, as though continuing a chat over a fence:

"What the boy is trying to say is that shame was the company's best tool. Cheaper than guns. You don't need to silence a man who silences himself."

"I'm not finished," Tam managed.

"No one ever is," said Sefa. "Go on anyway."

"There are papers," Tam said. "Witness statements. A magistrate's seal on them — a real one, from before the company owned everything that moves. They name the men who gave the order. They've been hidden for thirty years because everyone who could have spoken was paid to forget, and I was sent to take them back, and I wrote a false report to cover for—" he gestured at Hazel, who was staring at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't dare try, "—for her. For them. So do what you like with me. Arrest me. I'd just like it noted that I'm a terrible employee and always have been. Ask anyone at the Salt Quay depot. I once filed a cargo manifest upside down and nobody noticed for four months, which tells you everything about the company's standards."

Someone in the crowd laughed — a real laugh this time, unsmothered — and the sound spread, and Tam felt the strangest sensation of his life, which was that the square was no longer watching him the way you watch a man about to be punished. They were watching him the way you watch a neighbor.

Brack reached the bottom of the steps.

* * *

There is a particular quality to the silence that falls when a large man stops moving in a crowd. Every instinct in every body present recalibrates at once. Brack stood at the foot of the stairs with his cleat hands open at his sides, and he looked up at Tam with an expression of genuine, almost paternal disappointment.

"Voss," he said. "Come down."

"No," said Tam.

"You'll be ruined."

"I'm a spotter on nine shillings a week living in a room above a chandlery that smells of pitch. Ruin will have to climb a fair way to reach me."

Brack sighed, the sigh of a man who had done this before, in other ports, with other boys. "Take him," he said, to the flankers.

What happened next entered local legend within the hour, and improved steadily thereafter. In truth it was brief and graceless. The nearer flanker grabbed Tam's arm; Tam, who had been in exactly one fight in his life (lost, aged fourteen, to a crab), yanked backward with the full strength of a man who hauled nets for a living, overbalanced them both, and brought the pair of them down onto the steps in a tangle of limbs and the borrowed coat, which tore along one seam with a sound like a small surrender. The crowd surged forward — not to fight, fishermen rarely fight when they can simply *be numerous and immovable* — but to stand, densely, in the way. By the time the guards from the magistrate's office pushed through, the flankers were pinned under six dockhands who kept apologizing profusely while refusing to budge an inch.

Tam was hauled upright by two uniformed guards, wrists bound, squid-stained coat hanging off one shoulder. Brack looked at him across the heads of the crowd, and there was something in the big man's face that Tam would think about for years afterward — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man seeing a version of himself that had taken a different turning somewhere, long ago, in another port.

"You'll hang for this," Brack said. Without heat. Almost gently.

"Probably not," said Sefa, from the steps. "But it's kind of you to worry."

* * *

They marched him up the steps to the magistrate's door, and here Tam expected the story to end — cell, trial, transport, the usual architecture of a life that had annoyed powerful men. But at the top of the stairs, Magistrate Wren was having what appeared to be a crisis conducted entirely in his eyebrows. He looked at Tam. He looked at the crowd, which had not dispersed, had in fact grown, word having gone out into the market streets the way word does. He looked at Hazel Ashgrove, standing below with her hand inside her coat, on the papers, on the proof, on thirty drowned men and one living boy in a ruined coat.

"The court," Wren began, in his oiled voice, "will hear testimony in due and proper—"

"He'll testify now," Hazel said. Not loudly. She didn't need loud. The square leaned in around her words like water finding its level. "In front of all of us. Or you can explain to London why the only witness to a company murder was arrested on your own steps before he could speak."

Wren's eyebrows performed a maneuver previously unrecorded among eyebrows.

And Tam, bound, coatless in spirit if not quite in fact, felt the strangest lightness. He had spent his whole life being *from* somewhere — the village, the chandlery loft, the company's payroll — and never once *of* somewhere. Standing on those steps, wrists tied, about to say his grandfather's name aloud in front of several hundred people who would carry it home to their kitchens, he understood that he was, at last, of somewhere. It cost everything he had. It was worth it.

"My grandfather's name," he began again, and this time his voice held, "was Emeric Voss. And what the Meridian Trading Company did to him—"

Below, at the edge of the square, a door opened. A tall man stepped out — silk hat, city boots, a face Tam knew from the portrait in the company hall, older now and considerably less smiling.

The founder had not fled yet. He had come to watch.

And behind Hazel, Old Sefa drew a breath that forty years deep, and began, very quietly, to count.
