## 18. The Reading

The magistrate's clerk had a voice like wet sand.

"Names," he said, "will be entered in order of arrival. Sworn testimony will be taken under seal. The magistrate will review all materials at his discretion."

His discretion. Hazel stood in the anteroom of the magistrate's hall with the letter pressed flat against her ribs where her coat lining had been cut open and resewn twice — once by her own hand at Salt Quay, once by Maren's last night, in the smuggler's loft, with thread pulled from a sailcloth scrap and a knot Sefa called "the kind that outlives its owner." Through the wall she could hear the square: hundreds of feet, lantern smoke, Tam Voss's voice still ringing somewhere in it like a bell that refused to be taken down.

Inside the hearing room, through doors of dark island mahogany, Meridian's money was already at work. She could hear it working. Not shouting — money never shouted — but murmuring, in the courteous tones of a company advocate who had arrived on the morning packet boat with two trunks of documents and a smile like an oiled hinge.

"The sinking of the *Lorelei* fleet," the advocate was saying, "was a navigational tragedy. A storm. A lamentable one. The Company grieved it publicly, as the records show—"

"The records the Company keeps," said another voice, drier.

"Which are, sir, the only records."

Hazel looked at Maren. Maren looked back, and for once neither of them needed to say anything, which was fortunate, because saying anything would have gotten them thrown into the street.

Old Sefa was not in the anteroom. He was outside, on the steps, doing what he had begun to do when the founder himself stepped out of his carriage to watch: counting. His voice carried through the open windows, thin as net-thread and just as hard to break.

"—and the fourth was Renna Kalis, who had a laugh you could hear across three boats. And the fifth was Odell Bray, who owed me four coppers and I forgive him. And the sixth—"

The clerk cleared his throat. "The Ashgrove deposition will be heard fourth. After the customs officer's statement."

Fourth. Behind three statements that would take an hour each and go nowhere, by which time the advocate would have found some flaw in every witness, and the light would be gone, and the crowd outside — crowds do not hold, Hazel knew; crowds are water, they find their level and drain away — the crowd would have thinned to idlers and children chasing lantern ash.

Maren's hand found hers and squeezed. Once. It meant: *whatever you're going to do, do it soon.*

Hazel had been doing arithmetic since before dawn, the way her father had taught her to reckon a dive: how long the air in your chest will last, how deep the bottom is, whether what you're reaching for is worth what it costs to come back up. She had imagined this moment a hundred times since Salt Quay. In most versions she handed the letter across the clerk's desk and sat very still while important men read it, and justice happened the way weather happens — somewhere above her, decided by forces she could not see.

But she had learned things between Salt Quay and these steps. She had learned that papers can be planted, and witnesses detained, and auditors can search a house because they know no one will stop them. She had learned that a letter under seal belongs to whoever holds the seal. She had learned, from Tam Voss standing on these steps an hour ago with his grandfather's name in his mouth, that a thing spoken aloud cannot be confiscated.

And she had learned — this was the newest lesson, still warm from the oven — that the founder had come to *watch*. He was down there in the square right now, silk hat and city boots, standing at the edge of the crowd like a man inspecting a fence he suspected of having holes. He had come because he believed the letter was a piece of paper. Papers can be burned, filed, buried, bought.

He had not yet understood it had stopped being paper somewhere around Grey Reach.

"Hazel," Maren breathed.

"I know."

"The Ashgrove deposition," the clerk announced, to no one in particular, "will follow the third statement."

Which meant never.

* * *

Here is what Hazel did not do. She did not burn the letter. She had stood at Tern Gate with the confiscation barrels smoking and thought about it — truly thought about it, flame against her fingertips, her mother free and the past safely dead — and she had discovered that some part of her would rather drown than do it, and that part had her father's stubbornness in it and her mother's temper too, and she had learned to listen to that part the way divers learn to listen to the sea: early, and completely.

She did not file it either. She did not walk up to the clerk's desk and surrender it into the tender care of a man paid by the year out of funds the Company helped administer, into a drawer in a building with a roof the Company's lawyers knew every beam of.

What she did was turn around and walk out the front doors.

The clerk made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. An usher reached for her arm and found Maren already there, stepping neatly between them with the smooth, terrible calm of a woman who had lied to an auditor and meant every word of it.

"Excuse us," Maren said pleasantly. "My daughter has forgotten something on the steps."

She had not. But it was beautifully done, and Hazel loved her for it, and then the doors were behind them and the light hit her face and the noise of the square rose up to meet her like warm water.

* * *

The steps were granite, wide, worn hollow in the middle by forty years of feet. Sefa stood at the top of them, still counting, his cap in his hands. Below him the square was full — fuller than Hazel had ever seen it, fishwives in their Sunday shawls, divers off the morning boats with salt still dried white on their forearms, children on shoulders, old men leaning on the customs-house rail. Lantern poles leaned everywhere, confiscated or surrendered, and their paper husks littered the cobbles like shed skins. Above it all, on the balcony of the Meridian factor's house across the square, the founder stood with his hands clasped behind him, watching, patient as a ledger.

Sefa saw her and stopped counting. His eyes went to the coat, to the place where the lining bulged, and then to her face, and whatever he read there made him go very still.

"No," he said quietly. It was not a warning. It was more like a man watching someone step to the edge of a wreck hole. "Girl. You don't have to carry it alone anymore. That's the whole point of what we've—"

"That's exactly why," Hazel said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Somewhere below, a fishwife — Berta Olem, she realized, who had mended nets beside her mother for fifteen years and once given her a boiled sweet shaped like a fish — went up on her toes to see.

"They'll take it," Sefa said. "A reading can be stopped. A paper can be seized. If it goes wrong, we lose everything in one afternoon."

"Yes," Hazel said. "That's the price of the dive." She unbuttoned her coat. Her fingers were shaking, so she let Maren's come over hers, and together, mother and daughter, they drew the letter out into the daylight — creased, wax-cracked, smelling faintly of brine and forty years of a dead man's chest.

The square noticed. Noise has a tide, and Hazel felt it turn: the murmur folding down and down until the whole square held its breath the way a diver holds air.

She climbed onto the lowest step above the crowd, turned, and faced them. The founder on his balcony. The magistrate's tall windows. Three hundred faces and the smoke of festival lanterns drifting through all of it like weather.

"My name is Hazel Ashgrove," she said. "My father was Tomas Ashgrove, a diver, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company. On the night of the ninth of October, thirty-one years ago, he watched the Lorelei fleet sink in a calm sea."

Silence. Even the children had stopped.

"This letter names the witnesses. It was written by a magistrate's own hand and sealed with his crest, and it has been hidden in my father's diving chest since the year I was born. It says the fleet was sunk deliberately. It says who paid for it." She lifted her chin toward the balcony, and her voice did not shake now at all. "It says why."

Down in the crowd, near the front, she saw the company men begin to move — three of them, big, unhurried, the way men move when they've been paid to be unhurried. Sefa shifted his weight like a man preparing to spend the last of something.

So Hazel opened the letter and began to read aloud.

*"'To the office of the colonial magistrate, this record being set down in confidence of law—'"*

Her voice was not made for squares. It was a diver's voice, quiet, used to speaking under the pressure of held breath. The first rows strained. Someone said, "Louder, girl," and then Berta Olem, magnificent, took up the words as Hazel spoke them, bellowing them row by row like a bosun calling line:

"*'In confidence of law!'*"

And the next row caught it, and the next, and then the whole square was doing it — not perfectly, not in unison at first, a ragged rolling echo, fishwives and divers passing the sentences outward the way they passed buckets on a fire line, until Hazel hardly needed her own voice at all. She read, and the square repeated, and the words left her mouth small and came back enormous.

*"'That the brig Lorelei and the schooners Kestrel and Annen did sink on the pearl beds of the eastern shoal—'"*

"*'—the eastern shoal—'*"

*"'—not by act of weather, but by act of men hired and paid—'"*

"*'—hired and PAID—'*"

On the balcony, the founder had stopped being patient. Hazel saw him lean forward and say something to the man beside him, saw the man hurry off, saw the first of the company men start up the steps toward her.

Maren met them. Hazel never learned everything her mother said — something low and quick, delivered with a look that had frozen better men than these — but it bought ten seconds, and ten seconds was a paragraph.

*"'The witnesses named herein, being Tomas Ashgrove, Emeric Voss, Sefa Tulane, and others, were each compensated to forget—'"*

At the word *forget*, the square made a sound Hazel had never heard a crowd make. Not a roar. Something lower and worse — a groan, deep and collective, the sound of three hundred people remembering at once. Old women put their hands over their mouths. A diver near the rail knelt down right there on the cobblestones.

And Sefa stepped forward, cap still in his hands, tears running freely down the channels of his old face, and shouted the sentence with them, louder than anyone, louder than he had shouted anything in forty years:

"*'Compensated to FORGET!'*"

* * *

They nearly reached her then. Hazel saw that plainly. Two of the company men got as far as the second step, and one actually caught her sleeve —

— and the crowd closed.

Not violently. That was the astonishing thing. They simply flowed forward, the way water fills a hole, fishwives and boatmen and boys, until the steps were full of bodies that had committed no crime and could not legally be moved and had absolutely nowhere else to be. Berta Olem planted herself like a mooring post. A boy Hazel didn't know wrapped both arms around a lamppost and grinned at the guards with the pure delight of youth discovering civil disobedience.

Hazel kept reading.

Her arms ached. The light was going gold and then grey. Somewhere in the middle pages the magistrate himself appeared at the tall window above the doors — she saw him, a grey, careful face, watching, calculating, his pen held ready and writing nothing — and she read straight through the sight of him, because looking at him was like looking at the sunken beds through bad water: you could see the shapes down there, but nothing moved until you went down yourself and touched them.

*"'Let it be recorded that thirty souls were lost, whose names are appended—'"*

She reached the list at dusk.

Thirty names. She read them slowly, giving each one its whole breath, and the square gave each one back, and by the twelfth name the repetition had stopped needing her at all — the names were moving through the crowd on their own, passed hand to hand like a flame down a line of lanterns, and people she had never met were speaking her father's crewmates' names into the evening air, and the evening air was keeping them.

The last name on the list was Tomas Ashgrove.

Hazel read it herself. No one echoed it — the square had gone quiet again, the whole held-breath silence of the beginning — and for a moment she stood there with the letter open in her hands and her father's name hanging in the lantern smoke, and she thought: *there. Now it's yours too. All of you. Try filing that.*

Then, from somewhere in the middle of the crowd, a single voice said, "Tomas Ashgrove," quietly, like an amen. Then another. Then dozens, soft and steady, a tide coming in over the list, until the square was murmuring all thirty names at once, overlapping, unhurried, a sound like the sea deciding to remember.

* * *

It was fully dark when the magistrate finally came out.

He came down the steps himself — no escort, no clerk, though the clerk scurried after him — and stood on the stone below Hazel with the letter's echo still settling around him, and looked out at a square that could not be dispersed, could not be arrested, and could not, as of this evening, plausibly be claimed not to know.

"You understand," he said, not unkindly, "that what you've done tonight may ruin you. It may ruin your mother. It may ruin half the people in this square. An inquiry is not a verdict, Miss Ashgrove. The Company's money reaches high."

Hazel folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way her mother folded nets.

"It reaches high," she agreed. "But it doesn't reach here." She nodded at the square — at Berta Olem, at the boy on the lamppost, at the kneeling divers, at Sefa standing straighter than she'd ever seen him. "Not anymore. Tonight it reached three hundred people, and tomorrow it'll reach nine hundred, and you can't buy back a memory once it's been said out loud. Sir." She held the letter out to him — formally, properly, everything in its legal order at last. "You can have it now. It's only paper. What matters is already ours."

The magistrate took it. His hands were not quite steady, and Hazel suspected it was not the letter that shook them.

Behind her, at the edge of the square, torches flared — a column of company men coming up from the harbor road, twenty strong at least, moving fast, and the founder's balcony stood suddenly empty.

Sefa's hand closed on her shoulder, gentle and iron. "Get your mother behind the customs rail," he said quietly. "Whatever happens next, the story doesn't stop here."

The first torch reached the corner of the square.

Hazel took her mother's hand and turned to face it.
