## 19. The Company Answers

Sefa had stood on many steps in his life. The steps of the Lorelei's boarding ladder, sixty years ago, when his knees were young and the sea owed him nothing. The steps of the customs house at Grey Reach, where he'd once been made to wait four hours while a clerk decided whether an old diver's bundle smelled of contraband or merely of old diver. And now these steps — the magistrate's steps, broad and pale and swept clean that morning by a boy who could not have known what they would hold by afternoon.

He stood on them because Hazel had asked him to.

Not in so many words. She had simply looked at him after she finished reading, her voice hoarse from shouting the letter into the wind, the pages trembling in her fists, and the crowd below — divers, fishwives, net-menders, children perched on their fathers' shoulders — had gone quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet, like the sea before it decides something.

"Someone should say their names," she said. Just that. Just to him, though half the square heard.

And Sefa, who had spent forty years perfecting the art of saying nothing, found that his mouth was already opening.

* * *

The first thing Meridian did was nothing, which was the most frightening thing of all.

Sefa knew how companies thought. He had worked for one before he worked against himself for forty years. A company hearing its name read aloud in a public square does not panic; it consults. Somewhere behind the magistrate's shutters, a man with a ledger was consulting. Somewhere down at the harbor, a boat was being loaded with men who carried truncheons the way other men carried umbrellas — casually, as if weather were expected.

"They'll come," Sefa murmured to Maren, who had taken up a position at the bottom of the steps like a woman guarding her own front gate. "Give them ten minutes. They'll want to look orderly."

"Orderly," Maren repeated. She had the letter now, folded into her bodice over her heart, and she kept touching the spot as though checking a wound hadn't reopened. "They drowned thirty men, Sefa. I don't think order is their problem."

"It's exactly their problem," Sefa said. "It's all they have left."

He was right about the timing. Ten minutes, nearly to the stroke of the harbor bell, and then the square began to fill from the seaward side — not a rush, not a charge, but a slow, deliberate pouring-in of company men. Sefa counted them out of habit: two dozen, maybe more, in Meridian grey, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no by a crowd. At their head walked a broad-shouldered fellow with a face like a closed ledger, whom Sefa recognized distantly as one of the fort's enforcers — Brannick, the fishermen called him, though never within earshot.

Brannick stopped at the edge of the square and surveyed it the way a man surveys a floor he intends to mop.

"By order of the Meridian Trading Company," he called, in a voice trained to carry across water, "this gathering is unlawful. Disperse to your homes. Anyone remaining in the square in five minutes will be detained under the terms of the company charter."

The charter. Sefa almost laughed. Forty years ago he had watched that same charter used to seize pearl beds, sink ships, drown men, and here it was again, held up like a lantern, illuminating nothing.

Hazel stepped forward on the steps beside him, chin up, hands empty — deliberately empty, he noticed, so everyone could see they were empty. "The reading is finished," she called back. "You're welcome to stay and listen."

"To what?" Brannick demanded. "There's nothing here but rumor."

"No," Hazel said. "There are names."

And that, Sefa thought, was the whole war in two sentences. He put his hand briefly on her shoulder — she flinched, then steadied — and turned to face the square.

His knees hurt. His voice, when it came, came out older than he wanted, cracked and salted, the voice of a man who had spent decades breathing compressed air and silence. But it carried. Old divers' voices carry; you learn it young, calling up through fathoms.

"You want names?" he said. "I have thirty-one of them. Thirty from the Lorelei fleet, sunk in the dark of a March night by men paid by the company whose man stands there telling you to go home. And one more name besides." He paused. "Mine. Sefa Toloa. Diver, second rank, Lorelei fleet. I am the name that got away."

The square had gone very still. Even the gulls seemed to be listening.

"The Lorelei fleet went down with thirty souls aboard and under," Sefa continued, and his voice found its footing now, grew surer, the way a needle finds north. "The company called it a storm. There was no storm. There were men with hammers and cold lamps and orders written in fine ink, and I know this because I clung to a piece of their hull for six hours and listened to my brothers die in the water around me."

He drew a breath. Beneath him, Hazel whispered, "Sefa—" and he squeezed her hand once: *let me finish the debt.*

"Tomas Vela," he said. "First name. Tomas Vela, who taught me to read the tide tables. Tomas Vela, who dove the deep beds off Serpent Rock until his ears bled and never complained, except about the food."

He said it plainly. No music, no performance. A man reciting a list, which is the most devastating thing a man can do, because a list cannot be argued with.

* * *

Brannick's five minutes expired somewhere around the fourth name.

"Enough!" he bellowed. "Detain them. Clear the square!"

But here is the thing Sefa had counted on, the thing he had learned in forty years of watching and saying nothing: a crowd is not a thing you clear. A crowd is water, and water does not obey truncheons. It moves around them. It closes behind them.

The first guards waded in toward the steps, and the crowd parted — and reformed behind them. Fishwives locked arms, an old trick from market disputes, immovable as coral. Two young divers knelt directly in the guards' path, not fighting, just kneeling, which is somehow worse to walk through than fighting. A grandmother set down her basket with great deliberation and sat inside the space it marked, daring anyone to move her.

And then a voice rose from the kneeling divers — a young one, Riko, who had been a child when Sefa last saw him — and said:

"Ilosa Fenn. My grandfather. Lorelei fleet, tender rank. He never came home and they told us it was the storm."

A woman near the fountain took it up: "Petra Ondu. Pearl-cutter. My mother's sister."

An old man Sefa didn't even know, some fisherman from up the coast who had come for the festival and stayed for the reading: "Davan Merr. Deckhand. May the sea hold him softer than the company did."

Name after name. Not Sefa's list anymore — the crowd was adding its own, names of kin lost to other accidents, other convenient storms, other years when the company needed something and people were the cheapest material at hand. The square filled with names the way a tide pool fills with light, and every guard who pushed into it found himself standing inside someone's grief, and grief, Sefa observed with a kind of fierce satisfaction, is very heavy to stand in.

Brannick grabbed a fishwife by the arm — big Maretta, who sold smoked eel and had forearms like anchor chain — and she simply looked down at his hand and then at his face, and said, "Ilosa Fenn," in a voice like a closing door, and he let go. Sefa would remember that look on Brannick's face for a long time afterward: the confusion of a man discovering that the rules had changed while he wasn't watching.

"Sir—" one of the younger guards said to Brannick, and there was something wrong with his voice, something uncertain. "Sir, this one's crying. We can't—she's just standing there crying, sir."

"Then arrest her," Brannick snapped.

"For what?"

It was a small question, quietly asked, but it landed in the square like a dropped anchor. For what? For crying? For a name? For standing in a square on a public day? The charter was a wall against smugglers and debtors; nobody had ever tested it against mourning. Companies are good at many things, Sefa thought, but they are helpless before a crowd that has stopped wanting anything the company knows how to withhold.

* * *

Hazel found him on the steps during a lull, when the names had slowed — not stopped, never stopped, but settled into a rhythm, a name every few breaths, like waves against a hull.

"You planned this," she said. Not accusing. Wondering.

"I planned nothing," Sefa said. "I planned for forty years to keep my mouth shut, and look how well that went. No. I only opened a door. The crowd walked through it on its own legs." He nodded toward the square, where a boy of maybe twelve was standing on an overturned crate, solemnly reciting the name of a great-uncle he had never met, learned that morning from the woman holding his hand. "That's the thing about truth, girl. It's like water. You don't have to carry it far. You just have to crack the dam."

"And now?"

"Now we find out whether the magistrate's spine is made of anything better than paper." He squinted at the shuttered windows above the steps. Behind them, movement — silhouettes crossing and recrossing, urgent. "He's been watching. He heard everything. Now he decides whether he's a magistrate or a company clerk who happens to sit in a magistrate's chair."

"And if he's a clerk?"

Sefa smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Then we have given him a problem no clerk can file. Look around you, Hazel. Every name spoken today was spoken aloud, in public, witnessed by hundreds. You can burn a letter. You can buy a magistrate. But you cannot un-say three hundred voices." He patted the step beside him. "Sit. Rest your voice. You'll need it."

She sat. Below, Maren had moved to stand near Tam Voss — who stood apart from everyone, having shouted himself hoarse on those same steps not an hour past, his testimony still ringing in the square's memory, guards having been dissuaded from seizing him by the simple arithmetic of the crowd around him. Maren was speaking to him quietly, and whatever she said made the young man bow his head and then, strangely, smile.

"What did she say to him?" Hazel asked.

"None of our business," Sefa said, "but knowing Maren, probably that he eats too little and thinks too much, and both habits need mending."

Hazel laughed — a short, startled laugh, the laugh of someone surprised to find the muscle still worked — and Sefa felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since March of a long time ago.

Below, the names continued. A tide of them. The guards had withdrawn to the square's edge, where they stood in their grey ranks looking less like enforcers and more like men waiting for instructions that no longer existed. Brannick had gone. Slipped away, Sefa noted, toward the fort — running, in the manner of important men, to consult.

Above, the magistrate's door opened.

* * *

He came out slowly, an old man in a good coat, blinking in the daylight like something kept too long below decks. The crowd's names faltered, then hushed — not out of fear, Sefa judged, but courtesy, the way divers go quiet when a shadow passes overhead.

The magistrate descended three steps and stopped. He looked out at the square — at the fishwives with linked arms, the kneeling divers, the grandmother by her basket, the boy still standing on his crate, the hundreds of faces turned up toward him — and Sefa watched him take it in, all of it, and watched something shift behind his eyes.

"I have received," the magistrate said at last, in a dry voice pitched carefully to carry, "a sworn statement. I have also received certain documents." His gaze found Hazel, then Maren, then Sefa. "I am further informed that a large number of persons present wish to be recorded as witnesses to events of the year of the Lorelei sinking."

A pause. The square held its breath.

"Inquiry," the magistrate said, "will be convened."

And then, before the cheer could fully rise, before Sefa could exhale the breath he'd been holding for forty years, the sound came — faint at first, then growing, rolling up from the harbor road: boots. Many boots. In rhythm. Coming fast, and wearing the cadence of men who had been sent not to disperse a crowd but to end a morning.

Beside him, Hazel stood up slowly, the letter's ash-and-memory weight somehow still in her hands, and said, very quietly:

"Sefa. That's not dispersal. That's the company answering."

The first column rounded the corner into the square just as the sun broke through the festival clouds, and every face — guards', divers', the magistrate's own — turned toward the harbor road at once.
