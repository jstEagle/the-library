## 22. What We Keep

The schoolhouse had been a salt shed before it was a schoolhouse, and it had not entirely given up its former life. The floor still smelled of brine on damp mornings, and the roof leaked in one particular corner that Hazel had named the Tide, because no matter what she did to patch it, the water always came back with the moon.

She didn't mind. The children didn't mind either. They had decided early on that a classroom with its own weather was far more interesting than one without, and they arranged their desks in a careful arc around the drip, the way fishermen arrange themselves around a hole in the ice.

"Again," said Pip Ferrow, who was seven and had opinions about everything, most of them loud.

Hazel held up the page. "From the top."

"*We, the undersigned witnesses of the loss of the Lorelei fleet*—"

"Slower. You're racing through the sad parts."

Pip slowed, dragging each word out like he was hauling an anchor. "*—do hereby declare before God and magistrate what was done at Tern Shoal in the year eighteen hundred and seventy-*" He frowned. "Seventy-one? Seventy-two?"

"Seventy-three," said Nesta Cobb from the back, who was eleven and never wrong and knew it.

"Seventy-three," Pip conceded, "in which thirty divers were drowned by the treachery of—" he squinted— "of *Meridian Trading Company*, whose founder did order the sinking so as to seize the pearl beds for himself." He looked up, flushed with triumph. "That's the whole first bit."

"That's the whole first bit," Hazel agreed.

It had taken her three months to copy the transcript out fair, letter by letter, working evenings at the kitchen table while her mother mended nets across from her — some things did not change, and Maren's hands were not built for idleness — and another month to talk the council into letting her teach from it. Old Brannic had objected. *History is for magistrates and priests,* he'd said, sucking his pipe. *What do children want with it?*

And Hazel had said, mildly, the way you say things when you have learned that mildness can be a kind of blade: *They're going to inherit it whether we give it to them or not. Better they get the true version.*

Brannic had smoked his pipe for a long moment. Then he'd said, *Well. Someone drowned for that paper. Be a shame to waste it.* And that was that. On this island, that counted as a rousing endorsement.

Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, silvering the mud, and somewhere down there — past the channel marker, past the kelp line, down where the light went green and then blue and then nothing — the Lorelei lay with a brass compass resting in her broken ribs.

* *

Maren came up from the water at noon, and Hazel met her at the shallows the way she had every day that week, carrying a towel and pretending not to count.

Her mother surfaced smooth and unhurried, pushed back her hood, and grinned like a girl.

"Well?"

"Two," Maren said. "Good ones. One's got a blush to it you won't believe." She held up the shell and tipped it, and there they were in the nacre: two pearls, small but clean, one of them shot through with a faint rose color, like dawn getting into it somehow.

"For the market or for us?"

"For us," Maren said, and laughed at Hazel's face. "For us, girl. I spent nine years diving for a ledger. I'm allowed one pretty pearl to keep in a box and look at of an evening."

This was the arrangement now, and it still struck Hazel as slightly scandalous, like seeing a judge dance. Maren dove when she pleased, sold what she pleased, kept what she pleased. No tally clerk came to the house. No company boat sat at anchor in the bay with its shadow lying over everything. Meridian's charter was suspended, its agents gone from the island, its name spoken in the village only as a cautionary tale — and even that, the children pronounced wrong half the time, *Meridium, Meridian, the Meridian*, until Hazel corrected them and felt, each time, a small fierce satisfaction she didn't bother to hide.

"You'll ruin your eyes reading all day," Maren said now, wringing out her hair. "Come eat."

They ate bread and smoked fish on the step, feet in the sun, and Maren told her about the wreck she'd found — nothing grand, just an old trading skiff, ribs green with weed, a school of silver bream living in it like tenants.

"There was a spoon," Maren said. "A whole spoon, just sitting there on the deck, bright as the day it was made. I nearly took it."

"Why didn't you?"

Maren considered this seriously, the way she considered everything. "Because it wasn't ours," she said finally. "Somebody ate with that spoon. Somebody washed it and put it away and thought they'd use it tomorrow. And then tomorrow never came for them." She shrugged. "Let them keep their spoon. Let the bream keep it too."

Hazel looked at her mother — sunburnt, salt-crusted, forty-six years old and freer than she had ever been in her life — and felt the old ache and the new gladness sitting side by side in her chest, the way they had learned to do.

* *

Afternoons, the older children came, and afternoons were for the harder lessons. Not the letters — those came easy enough once a child decided the alphabet was a puzzle rather than a punishment — but the other thing. The thing Hazel could not quite name and therefore taught without naming, the way her father must have taught her to read water.

Today's lesson was Tam.

He came twice a week now, officially as the ferryman's assistant delivering post, actually because Nesta Cobb had declared that if she had to learn sums she refused to learn them from anyone who couldn't also explain why the customs house at Grey Reach took its cut before the magistrate took his. Tam was twenty now, worked the Salt Quay crossing, saved his wages, sent half home to his mother, and carried the other half of his history around inside him like ballast.

He sat on the one desk built for adults, knees up near his ears, and helped Pip sound out words while Nesta checked his arithmetic and found it wanting, which she enjoyed immensely and he endured with the patience of a man doing penance.

"You're holding the pencil wrong," Nesta informed him.

"I'm holding it fine."

"It's a fist. You're strangling it."

"I'm writing, aren't I?"

"You're carving. There's a difference."

Hazel let it run. Some lessons you planned; others you simply stood back from and let happen. Nesta correcting a grown man's penmanship, Tam taking the correction because he owed the world corrections — none of that was in any primer, and all of it mattered more than the primer did.

When the little ones had been sent home — Pip leaving at a dead run, shouting the names of the thirty divers over his shoulder like a victory chant, which would have horrified Brannic and which Hazel chose to find moving — Tam lingered.

"The quay master wants me to ask you something," he said, not looking at her. "There's talk of a memorial. Proper one. Stone, down by the water. They want to know if you'd speak."

Hazel turned that over. A year ago, standing on the magistrate's steps with the letter shaking in her hands, her voice cracking on the third sentence and steadying on the fourth because Sefa's voice had come up beneath hers like a keel under a hull — a year ago she could not have imagined being asked to speak on purpose, in advance, in daylight.

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'd ask. That you don't much like being asked." He glanced at her. "Do you?"

"I mind less than I did." She smiled. "Tell him yes. But tell him I want the children to read part of it. If it's only me talking, it's my story. If they read it, it's theirs."

Tam nodded slowly, filing that away the way he filed everything, and then he said, awkwardly, scuffing his boot against the floorboards: "My mother came to the last class. Stood outside the window listening. She never learned her letters — nobody thought girls on our street needed them." He stopped. Started again. "Next term, could she—"

"Tell her the door doesn't lock," Hazel said. "Never has."

* *

Sefa died in the autumn, in his sleep, in his own bed, which by the standards of Lorelei men was a miracle so extravagant that the village treated it almost as a joke — *the sea couldn't catch him on land, the old eel* — and buried him above the tideline with a view of the shoal.

At the graveside, Hazel had not spoken. She had knelt instead, and begun the names, and one by one the village had taken them up, exactly as they had on the steps of the magistrate's hall, and it had occurred to her, kneeling there in the cold, that this was what Sefa had bought with his forty years of silence: not forgiveness, but this — a square full of voices, ready, practiced, waiting. He had spent four decades teaching himself the names by heart so that someday, somewhere, they could be said out loud by everyone at once. He had simply not lived to be surprised by how many somebodies there turned out to be.

His chair sat in the corner of the schoolhouse now. Nobody was allowed to sit in it. Everybody understood why, including Pip, who tested the rule exactly once and received such a look from the entire class, ages five to eleven, that he never tested it again.

* *

Winter came, and with it the long evenings, and with those the thing Hazel had begun calling, privately, the second lesson.

It started the night Nesta asked her — bluntly, the way eleven-year-olds ask everything — "Did your father know? About the letter? Did he know he was keeping it?"

Hazel had opened her mouth to say *yes, of course, he must have* — and then closed it again, because the honest answer was that she did not know, and would never know, and that the question had kept her awake more nights than she cared to admit.

"He knew something," she said finally. "You carry a thing that long, you know its weight even if you can't read its words. Maybe he hoped someone would come asking. Maybe he just couldn't bring himself to throw it away." She looked around the room, at the small faces lit by the lamp. "Here's what I've decided it means. Most people who keep history don't get to choose what happens next. They just keep it. Keeping is the whole job. The choosing comes later, if you're lucky, to someone else."

Nesta frowned. "That's not very exciting."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But it's true, and true things are usually quieter than exciting ones."

Pip raised his hand. "Was it scary? On the fort road? With the lantern?"

"Terrifying."

"More terrifying than the deep dive? When you found the manifest?"

"Much."

"Even though the compass pointed the way?"

"Even then." Hazel leaned forward. "Especially then. Because the compass only showed you what was lost. It couldn't show you what to do about it. That part you walk alone."

The children absorbed this in their various fashions — Nesta with a slow nod, Pip with visible disappointment that there hadn't been a sword fight — and Hazel sat back and realized she had just taught, without planning to, the entire sum of what she knew.

* *

On the first clear night of spring, she rowed out over the shoal.

She hadn't meant to make a ceremony of it. It was simply that the water was glass-still, the kind of evening her father would have called *a listening day*, and the boat seemed to steer itself. She anchored over the wreck — she knew the spot the way she knew her own doorstep now — and went down without the compass, without anything, just breath and habit.

The Lorelei had settled further into herself over the year. Weed dressed her rails. A conger had taken up residence in the hold and regarded Hazel with proprietary irritation. She swam the length of the deck, trailing her fingers along wood that had killed thirty men and, in a manner of speaking, given birth to everything else — the letter, the reading, the school, the stone memorial with its thirty-one carved names (the villagers had insisted on thirty-one; *one man lived*, Brannic had growled, *and he paid for it the rest of his life, that counts*).

Near the stern, wedged in the ribs, the compass waited where she had left it.

She'd expected the brass to tarnish. It hadn't — or barely. She tilted it, and the needle swung, lazy, and came to rest pointing not north but along the length of the wreck, bow to stern, as if it had simply decided that this was north now. As if the wreck were the whole world, and the center of it, and there was nowhere else worth indicating.

Hazel hung in the green water and laughed, bubbles streaming, because of course. Of course. It had never been a magic compass. It had been a faithful one. It pointed at what its owner loved and lost, and having delivered her father's daughter here, it had nowhere left to go, so it pointed at the loss itself, forever, patiently, the way grief does.

*I'm all right,* she told it, or told him, or told the water — the distinctions had gotten pleasantly blurred over the years. *We kept it. All of it.*

She let go and rose.

* *

The transcript hung framed on the schoolhouse wall now, behind glass, in Old Sefa's corner. Hazel had copied a second version for the magistrate's archive and a third for Tam's mother to learn from, but the original stayed here, where the Tide dripped in winter and the lamp burned in the evenings and the children read aloud, stumbling and correcting and starting over.

One gray afternoon late in the term, Hazel stood at the window watching Maren's boat come in — her mother standing tall at the oars, a string of fish and one rose-blush pearl in her pocket and no ledger anywhere in her life — and it came to her, plainly, the way the important things always arrived: not with thunder but with the tide, quiet, certain, covering everything.

History had never happened somewhere else. It had happened here, in these shallow waters, to these people, to her father, to her — and it was happening right now, this minute, to Pip and Nesta and the boy who strangled pencils and the woman learning her letters at fifty. It happened to everyone. The only question, the only question there had ever been, was who would remember it — and who would be taught to say it out loud.

Behind her, a small voice cleared its throat.

"Hazel?" It was a new one, a fisherman's daughter, four years old, first week, clutching a slate like it might bite. "Why do we have to learn the sad words first?"

Hazel looked at the framed pages, at the names marching down them in her careful hand, at the corner chair that no one sat in and everyone kept.

"Because the sad words are true," she said, "and somebody has to be able to read them when it matters. Come sit down. We'll start at the beginning."

The child climbed onto the bench beside Pip, and Hazel lifted the chalk, and outside the rain began again on the roof — soft, patient, coming back with the moon, the way water does, the way stories do, the way everything that is kept well always finds its way home.
