## 1. The Weight of Water

The barge came in on the morning tide, riding low with salted cod and iron ingots, and Wren Blackwood was already at the pier when its lines hit the cleats. He had learned long ago that the first man at the dock ate without pain and the last man ate after a beating. Today his ribs still remembered Tuesday.

He took the first barrel before the gangplank finished settling. The weight of it went through his shoulders and down into the old ache in his back, and he let it, because the ache was familiar and familiar things could be carried. Fourteen years old, and he knew the exact weight of everything the Meridian Trading Company owned: a barrel of brine-cured fish, forty pounds. A bolt of harbor canvas, sixty. A man's whole life, sold into paper for his father's debts—weightless until you tried to set it down.

"Blackwood!" Foreman Gretch's voice carried over the gulls and the winch-chains. "You're walking. Walk faster."

Wren walked faster. That was another thing he had learned: never answer, never look up, never give the foreman's fist a reason to remember your face. Visibility was death on the Meridian docks. The men who lasted were the men who moved like weather—present everywhere, remarkable nowhere. Wren had made himself fog. He kept his eyes on the planks, counted his steps without meaning to, and let the hours pass through him like water through a net.

The tally-keeper marked each barrel as it passed. Wren watched the chalk strokes out of the corner of his eye, because the tally was the only arithmetic that mattered. Six hundred and eleven days gone. Two hundred and ninety-four to go. He did the count every dawn and every dusk, subtracting one day like a coin from a purse that never seemed to lighten. On the docks they said indentures stretched—that the company found ways to add days for broken cargo, for missed bells, for looking wrong at the wrong moment. Wren's father's paper had been for seven years. It had been seven years since the *Kestrel* went down, and the ledger still showed four years owed.

He didn't think about that. Thinking about it was how men ended up screaming at Gretch and waking up in the company cell with three more days added. He thought about the count instead. Two hundred and ninety-three after tonight.

* * *

Midday brought the bell, and the bell brought bread and small beer and twenty minutes of sitting where you were told to sit. Wren took his portion to the end of Pier Three, past the tar-barrels, to the spot where a mooring post had rotted through and no one else bothered to sit because it smelled of bilge. It was the best place on the whole waterfront. From there he could see the harbor open to the horizon, gray-green and glittering, and he could pretend—for the length of half a heel of bread—that the water belonged to something other than the Company.

His father had loved that water. Had loved it the way other men loved wives or drink, helplessly, completely. Every morning of Wren's remembered childhood, Tam Blackwood had stood on this same shore with a pearl-diving knife at his belt and looked out at the tide like a man greeting a friend. *She gives what she gives,* he used to say, *and she keeps what she keeps.* Then the Company had bought up every diving license in the district, and Tam Blackwood dove for their pearls instead of his own boat, and then one autumn morning the *Kestrel* went down with nine divers aboard in water calm enough to float a leaf, and the Company's clerk had written *accident — weather* in a fine copperplate hand and closed the account.

Wren chewed slowly. He did not look at the water while he thought these things. He looked at his sleeve.

Left forearm, under the coarse wool of his shirt, sewn flat against the seam where even a searching foreman's fingers wouldn't linger: a coin. Not a company scrip chit, which was worthless outside Company walls, but an actual silver crown, worn thin at the edges, stitched into the fabric with waxed thread by his own hands over four patient nights. His father's coin. Found in the lining of Tam's old oilskin the week after the funeral, when the Company's men came to inventory everything the dead man had left behind—which was nothing, officially, because the debt consumed all—and Wren had palmed it before they saw, the way he'd seen his father palm a pearl from an oyster, smooth and invisible.

It was worth almost nothing against two hundred and ninety-four days. But it was *his*. It had never passed through a Company till, never been stamped with the meridian-and-anchor, never been weighed against a debt. Some nights, lying in the bunk-house with thirty other indentured men breathing around him, Wren would press his forearm against the plank wall and feel the coin's small hard shape and know that somewhere under all the paper and the chalk tallies there was still a boy who owned one single thing in the world.

That was dignity. Nobody on the docks would have called it that—they'd have called it foolishness, hiding contraband currency where a beating might crack a rib against it—but Wren knew better. Dignity wasn't grand. Dignity was a coin in a seam and a count in your head and getting up tomorrow to haul again.

"All right, ladies," Gretch bellowed. "Barge don't unload itself."

* * *

The afternoon load was Company freight—crates stenciled with the meridian-and-anchor, bound for the Company house on the hill. These got handled differently. These got handled by Wren specifically, because Wren was careful, and careful hands meant fewer cracked crates and fewer docked wages, and Gretch liked a worker whose mistakes cost nothing. So Wren hauled the sealed crates up the switchback road from the pier to the high town, sweating under the weight while the harbor fell away below him and the city opened up in tiers of slate roofs and chimney smoke.

The Company house sat at the top of everything, white-faced and columned, built of stone shipped in from the southern quarries on ships that had probably carried worse. At dusk its windows caught the low sun and burned gold—one window, two, a dozen—a whole facade of lit gold squares floating above the darkening streets. Wren had been inside once, three years ago, summoned to sign the papers that converted his father's debt into his own body. He remembered marble floors cold through his boots. He remembered a clerk with clean fingernaps writing in a ledger with a pen that whispered. He remembered thinking: *this is where the counting happens. This is where my life gets written down in someone else's book.*

Now he stood in the service yard with a crate on his shoulder and watched those golden windows come alight one by one, floor above floor, and behind each one somebody was dining, or reading, or signing something. Somewhere up there was a page with his name on it. *Blackwood, Wren. Indenture, remainder 294 days. Valuation: negligible.*

"Set it there." A yard steward pointed without looking at him. "Then back down. There's another run at eight."

Wren set it there. As he turned to go, a door on the ground floor swung open ahead of some departing gentleman, and light spilled out across the cobbles—warm lamplight, and with it the smell of roasted meat and wine and beeswax, rolling out like a tide of a different country. Through the doorway, just for a heartbeat, Wren saw a long table, a candelabra, men in dark coats laughing at something.

Then the door shut, and the smell cut off, and he was standing in the cold with his empty hands.

He walked back down toward the docks. Behind him the house glowed; below him the harbor lamps were being lit one by one along the piers, little orange seeds in the blue dusk. Between the two kinds of light lay the whole width of the city, and Wren descended through it like a man going underwater, deeper and deeper, back toward the pressure and the work and the dark.

* * *

The evening run went long. By the time the last crate was stacked in the bonded warehouse, his shoulders had stopped reporting anything useful and the lamp-oil smell of the docks had settled into his clothes for good. Gretch made his rounds with the tally-book, checking names off, and paused beside Wren a moment longer than usual—not menace tonight, just the dull arithmetic of a tired man counting his tired animals. Wren kept his eyes down and his breathing even and gave him nothing.

"Eight bells tomorrow," Gretch said finally. "Company's bringing in auditors for the centennial. Whole waterfront'll be crawling with clerks. Anyone steps wrong, anyone so much as spits crooked—" he tapped the tally-book, "—it goes in the book. Understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Centennial," Gretch muttered, moving off. "Hundred years of Meridian. They'll seal the accounts at New Year, I heard. Amnesty and all. Clean slate for the Company." He said it without opinion, the way a man says *rain coming*, and moved on down the line.

Wren stood very still among the crates.

Clean slate. Seal the accounts. He turned the words over the way he'd turn a strange shell found on the tideline, not knowing yet why it mattered, only that it was heavy. Somewhere in the Company house, in ledgers with whispering pens, were pages that recorded the *Kestrel*'s sinking as *accident — weather*. If the accounts were sealed—if the century turned and a pen came down and closed the books forever—then those pages would never again be opened by anyone who cared what was written on them. The water would keep what it kept, permanently, by law.

Two hundred and ninety-four days, and then freedom, and then a life spent walking around the shape of a drowned man whom the law itself had decided to forget.

He walked back to the bunk-house through the wet dark. The taverns along Dock Row were filling up, lamplight and noise spilling out onto the cobbles—the Broken Compass loudest of all, where Maren Collett poured beer and her son Tamlin, saved from the surf two winters past by a diver's boy who happened to be passing, sometimes carried barrels for extra coppers. Wren slipped past the noise and the light. Invisible. Fog.

In the bunk-house he lay on his bunk with his arm tucked against the wall and felt the coin in its seam, hard and certain, and did his count. Two hundred and ninety-three after tonight. And after that—after the amnesty, after the sealing—what exactly would he be free *into*?

He slept badly, and woke before the bell.

The docks at dawn were his favorite hour, before Gretch and the tally-men arrived, when the pilings stood black against a pewter sky and the water breathed fog. Wren came down early to sit on his rotted post at Pier Three and watch the tide turn. That was when he saw the stranger.

A man came along the sea-wall from the north road, walking like a man who had been walking for days—uneven, listing, one hand pressed to his side where his coat hung wrong. Even at fifty yards Wren could see the scar that dragged down the left side of his face, pale as a gull's feather, pulling his mouth into something between a sneer and a wound. He stopped at the head of the pier and looked out at the water for a long moment, and there was something in how he looked at it—not a sailor's look, not a diver's look, but the look of a man greeting an enemy he'd made peace with too late.

Then his legs went, and he sat down hard on the cobblestones, and the satchel he'd carried slung across his chest slid forward into his lap, and he stayed there, swaying, at the edge of the fog.

Wren should have looked away. Should have gotten up and walked to the far pier, invisible, fog, none of mine. That was the rule, and the rule had kept him alive for fourteen years.

But the man raised his head, and his eyes found Wren's across the empty dawn pier—found them, held them, with the terrible directness of someone who has run out of time to be polite—and he said, in a voice scraped raw:

"You work the Company docks? Good. Good." A breath that might have been a laugh. "I've been looking for someone the Company doesn't see."

And he held out the satchel.
