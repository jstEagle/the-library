## 2. A Stranger with Dead Ink

The Saltgull Tavern smelled of tar, spilled ale, and the particular despair of men who worked for a company that owned their futures down to the last copper. Wren took his usual place—end of the bench nearest the door, back to the wall, bowl of barley broth he could stretch across two hours if he ate slow enough. Invisible. That was the trick of it. Sit where the light didn't reach, keep your eyes on your food, and let the room forget you were in it.

Three days since Foreman Gedd's boot had found his ribs. He could still feel them when he breathed deep, a hot wire pulled tight beneath the skin. He breathed shallow and counted.

Two hundred and eleven days left on his indenture. Two hundred and eleven mornings of hauling Meridian cargo up gangplanks slick with fish blood, of bowing his head when company men passed, of sleeping in the barracks loft where the roof leaked salt water onto his blanket and no one cared enough to fix it because no one had to care about property that couldn't run away.

His father had owed them eleven years at the bottom of the harbor. Wren was paying it back with his spine.

The tavern door banged open and the cold came in with it, along with the wind off the water and a man who did not so much walk as fall forward, catching himself on the doorframe with one hand while the other clutched a leather satchel to his chest like a drowning man clutching driftwood.

Wren looked up before he could stop himself.

The stranger was tall, or had been once—now he moved bent over, favoring his left side, and there was something wrong with the way his coat hung, dark at the hem, stiff in places cloth shouldn't be stiff. His face was half-shadowed under a hood gone soft with rain, but what the lamplight caught made the room go quiet by degrees: a scar that started above his right eye and dragged down through the cheek to the jaw, old and white and brutal, like someone had tried to open him from temple to chin and failed.

He scanned the room the way a hunted thing scans—not for friends, but for exits. Then his eyes rolled back and he went down like rigging cut loose.

He hit the floorboards hard enough to rattle the bottles behind the bar. The satchel skidded from his grip and slid across the sawdust until it fetched up against the toe of Wren's boot.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then the room remembered how to breathe, and talk surged back in around the silence like water around a hull.

"Drunk," said the man at the next table, and returned to his cards.

"Dead, more like," said another, without looking up. Nobody wanted it to be dead. A dead man meant questions, and questions meant company men, and company men meant every soul in this room suddenly had somewhere else to be.

Old Tam the barkeep leaned over the bar and squinted. "Somebody roll him over."

Nobody volunteered. So Wren, because he was closest and because being invisible apparently only worked until it didn't, crouched and turned the stranger onto his back.

Up close the scar was worse, and so was everything else—the gray lips, the sweat standing on his brow in a room cold enough to see your breath, the tremor running through him like current through a wire. Fever. Bad one, by the look of it. Wren put two fingers to the man's throat and felt a pulse going fast and thin, a bird's heartbeat in a body too big for it.

"He's sick," Wren said. "Fever."

"Sick's his own business," Tam said, but he said it wearily, already reaching for the bottle of raw spirit he kept for exactly this kind of trouble, because Tam grumbled about everything and helped with most of it. "Get him off my floor, though. Corner table. And whatever he's got, don't you catch it."

Wren got an arm under the man's shoulders—and froze.

The satchel had come half-open when it fell. Papers had shifted inside, and one corner protruded into the lamplight: heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind the Meridian Trading Company used for correspondence worth keeping. And across its face, in ink gone brown with age, a hand Wren knew better than his own name.

*The quarterly assessments proceed as instructed. Halloway is pleased with discretion.*

Wren stopped breathing.

He had spent two years of his boyhood in the copying rooms of the company house, before they'd decided a diver's orphan was worth more on the docks than at a desk—a punishment dressed up as economy, though everyone knew the real reason: he'd asked one question too many about an entry he wasn't supposed to read. But two years was enough. Two years of tracing other men's words letter by letter, learning the architecture of hands. And Aldous Vane's hand had been the finest in the building—the auditor's script, precise as surveyor's lines, with that single telltale flourish on the capital *H*, the crossbar swept back like a gull's wing.

Wren had copied that *H* four hundred times. He'd know it blindfolded. He'd know it dead.

And Aldous Vane *was* dead. Everyone knew it. Three years ago the company house had worn black crepe for a week; there'd been a service at the Seamen's Chapel that the factor himself attended, dry-eyed and solemn, and afterward a sealed coffin rowed out past the breakwater and given to the sea. Drowned, they said. Lost overboard during an inspection voyage. The company even pensioned his household—until the household quietly vanished too, which nobody remarked upon, because nobody ever remarked upon anything the Meridian Trading Company preferred unremarked.

Wren looked at the paper. He looked at the unconscious, burning man bleeding through his coat onto Tam's floorboards. And he understood, with the cold certainty of deep water closing over his head, that the man in front of him was carrying letters written by a dead man—and that they were recent letters, because the ink on the visible page hadn't browned the way three-year-old ink browns. It had browned the way *months*-old ink browns.

He should have pushed the satchel away. He knew that later, looking back—he knew it the way you know which plank rots only after you've stepped on it. Push it toward the fire, let some drunk roll it under a table, go back to his broth and his counting and his invisibility. That was the whole art of survival at Meridian docks: see nothing, hold nothing, owe nothing.

Instead he tucked the papers back inside, closed the buckle with fingers that didn't feel like his own, and hauled the stranger to the corner table.

* * *

Tam brought the spirit and a threadbare blanket from the storeroom and charged Wren two coppers for both, which was robbery, and Wren paid it, which was foolishness. The tavern filled and emptied around them through the evening shift-change, dockworkers coming in steaming and leaving darker, and none of them looked twice at the corner where a diver's boy sat over a fevered stranger. Invisibility held. For now.

The stranger surfaced twice.

The first time, his eyes opened—dark, fever-bright, fixed on nothing—and he said, in a voice scraped down to gravel: "Is it midnight?" And then, before Wren could answer: "Don't let it reach the governor. Don't let it—" and sank again.

The second time was worse. His hand shot out and closed on Wren's wrist with a strength that shouldn't have lived in that body, and he pulled himself halfway upright, and his face was inches from Wren's face, and the scar stood out white as bone.

"You read?" he rasped. "You looked. I saw you look at the satchel."

"I didn't—"

"You're company." His grip tightened. "You've got the calluses of a copyist, boy. I know those hands. I've kissed those hands good morning for six years." A wet, broken laugh. "Whose desk did you sit at?"

"The copying rooms," Wren heard himself say. "Before they moved me to the docks."

Something shifted behind the fever-glass of the stranger's eyes. He studied Wren's face the way he'd studied the room earlier—for exits—but this time what he seemed to find was not an exit. His grip loosened, finger by finger.

"What's your name?"

"Wren."

"Wren." He said it like he was testing whether the word would hold weight. "My name is Orsen. Remember it or don't—it doesn't matter after tonight." He sagged back against the wall, breath whistling. "There are men coming for me. Company men. They've been one tide behind me since Corvane, and I bought myself a day at the toll bridge, and days run out." His eyes found Wren's and held them, and there was something in them worse than fear—something that had walked all the way through fear and come out the other side into a kind of terrible calm. "I can't carry it further. My legs won't take orders anymore. So I'm asking you to do the one thing I swore I'd never ask a company hand to do."

"I'm not—"

"You're indentured. There's a difference, and Vane would want me to say so." Orsen coughed, deep and rattling, and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth. When he lowered it, Wren saw the dark stain on his knuckles and pretended he hadn't. "The satchel. Take it."

"No." The word came out fast and flat. "Whatever it is—no. I keep my head down. That's how I'm still breathing. You want me to carry dead men's mail straight into the company house? They'll hang me from the customs crane by morning."

"They'll do worse than that," Orsen agreed mildly, "if they find it on me instead." He smiled, and the scar split the smile in half. "That's the arithmetic of it, Wren. Not whether you're in danger tonight. Only whose danger you'd rather stand in."

From outside came the sound that ended the argument: boots. Not the scattered shuffle of workmen, but the matched, unhurried tread of men who walked in step because discipline was cheaper than loyalty. Four of them, maybe five. Coming up Rope Walk toward the Saltgull, stopping now and then—Wren could picture it without seeing it—holding lanterns up to faces, checking doors.

A bounty. It had to be. Word traveled faster than ships these days.

Orsen heard it too. He moved then with a speed that frightened Wren more than the boots did—all his weakness converted at once into purpose. He shoved the satchel across the table and into Wren's chest, both hands flat against the leather, pinning it there, pinning Wren back against the wall.

"Aldous Vane wrote these," he said, low and fast and clear, each word driven home like a nail. "The company told you he drowned. The company lied. These letters tell you what else they've lied about, and how many, and for how long, and it will turn your stomach inside out, and you'll wish to God you'd never touched them. I'm sorry for that. Truly." The boots reached the street outside; lantern light swung yellow across the tavern's fogged windows. "But there is no one else. Do you understand? I ran out of everyone else three ports ago."

The door handle turned.

Orsen slumped sideways off the bench and onto the floor, boneless, breathing in great theatrical snores, a drunkard dead to the world—and his eyes, for one last instant before they shut, met Wren's with a message plainer than any spoken word: *Now you choose.*

The satchel was warm from the fever heat of Orsen's body. Wren had perhaps two seconds.

The door opened, letting in wind and lantern glare, and the room's noise died the way it always died for company colors—three men in Meridian blue-and-silver, and behind them, filling the frame, a broad shape Wren's body recognized before his mind did and hated itself for recognizing.

Foreman Gedd.

"Well," Gedd said pleasantly, to the whole room, in the voice he used just before beatings. "Cold night for drinking. Mind if we warm ourselves?"

Wren did the only thing left. He pulled the satchel under his jacket, against his ribs—against the bruised ones, so the pain would keep him honest—and picked up his broth spoon, and became invisible, staring into his bowl like a boy with nothing in the world to hide.

Gedd's boots crossed the floor toward the corner. Toward the unconscious man. Toward him.

Under the jacket, against Wren's hammering heart, the dead man's letters waited to be read.
