## 4. The Forger's Basement

Wren

Petra Quill lived beneath a chandler's shop in Saltrow, behind a door that smelled of tallow and lies. Maren had given them the direction in a voice pitched low enough to slip under the floorboards—*the woman who writes dead men's names*—and a warning to go after dark, when the chandler's apprentices went home to their suppers and the street above emptied of anyone who might remember faces.

Orsen could barely walk. Wren bore most of his weight down the narrow stair, the man's arm across his shoulders like a spar from a wrecked ship, fever still banked but burning somewhere underneath. Three days in Maren's loft had pulled him back from the edge, fed on fish broth and Maren's unsentimental care, but the scar tissue along his jaw had gone angry and red again, and his eyes had that glassy shine Wren remembered from men drowning.

The knock was three short, one long. Maren had drilled it into him twice. The door opened a hand's width on a chain, and an eye looked out—not at Orsen, but past him, cataloguing the stairwell, the street, the shadows.

"You're the diver's boy," said a woman's voice, dry as old vellum. "The widow wrote ahead. She has a talent for writing ahead."

"We need papers," Wren said.

"Everyone needs papers." But the chain rattled, and the door opened wide enough for them both.

The basement was nothing like he'd imagined. He'd pictured a villain's den, ink-stained and furtive; instead it was almost clerical, almost holy—a long workbench lit by three lamps trimmed to equal brightness, drawers labeled in a small precise hand, reams of paper sorted by weight and age. A press. A cabinet of pens, each nib wrapped in cloth like a relic. On the wall hung samples: customs manifests, letters of passage, bills of lading, each one perfect, each one false. Wren recognized Meridian stock among them and felt his stomach drop through the floor.

Petra Quill was smaller than her voice. Grey shot through dark hair pinned severely back, ink stains on her fingers so deep they looked like bruising, and eyes that did not blink so much as measure.

"Sit him there." She nodded at a stool by the warm flue of the chandler's ovens above. "And you—" she pointed at Wren "—stand where I can see your hands."

Orsen sank onto the stool with a groan he tried to swallow. Petra crouched before him, studied the satchel strapped to his chest—the satchel Wren had been guarding like his own heartbeat—and then looked up at Orsen's face.

"Aldous Vane's hand," she said quietly. "I'd know it anywhere. I taught half of it to him."

Wren went still. "You worked for the company."

"Twelve years. Senior copyist, second archive." She straightened. "Until I corrected a factor's arithmetic aloud, in front of his superiors, and discovered that competence is a greater crime than theft in that house. They didn't dismiss me. They erased me. My name struck from the rolls, my wages forfeit, my lodging revoked. As far as Meridian records show, I never existed—which is, I grant them, a very efficient form of forgery." Her mouth twisted. "So yes. I can replicate Vane's hand. I could do it in my sleep. Whether I *will* depends entirely on what you're carrying and who else wants it."

"The ledger," Orsen rasped. "Vane's ledger. And his letters."

Something moved behind Petra's measured face—too quick to name, gone before it settled.

"And you are?" she asked Orsen.

"His partner," Orsen said. "Formerly."

"Ah." She drew the word out like thread. "The famous former partners. Aldous wrote me once, two years ago, from a port he wasn't supposed to be alive in. He said if anything happened to him, someone might come down a stair smelling of fear and salt, and that I should help them." She turned to Wren. "He described you oddly. He said: *a boy who copies beautifully and thinks no one notices*. Do you know what that means, diver's boy?"

Wren said nothing.

"It means Aldous Vane noticed you years ago, and planned for you before you knew there was a plan." Petra crossed to her workbench. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part."

* * *

Orsen

The fever broke properly sometime during the second hour, and Orsen resented how clearly it let him see.

Petra had laid it out plainly: she would forge passage papers for all three of them, credentials good enough to pass harbor customs and perhaps even the company house—but first she wanted certainty, and she took her certainty the way she took everything, precisely and without mercy.

She put the knife question to Wren while the boy stood with lamplight on his face.

"Halloway's men will come here eventually," she said. "They watch Maren's tavern already—I have ears. When they come, they'll offer me my name back. My wages. A place in the second archive with a window. All I have to do is hand over a scarred man and a diver's boy." She tilted her head. "Convince me I shouldn't take it."

"That isn't a fair test," Orsen began.

"Nothing worth having is fair." She hadn't taken her eyes off Wren. "Well? You've been invisible your whole life, by the look of you. Invisible men survive. Speak."

Orsen watched the boy struggle with it—watched the war move across his face like weather. Wren Blackwood had the kind of silence that came from being beaten early and often, and Petra had just offered him every exit.

"I could give you up," Wren said finally. His voice was rough. "You're asking me to prove I won't. I can't prove it. Anyone can break—Halloway's men broke better people than me, I've seen it done. So I won't insult you with promises." He lifted his sleeve, the left one, and picked loose a stitch with his thumbnail. A coin fell into his palm—worn thin, hoarded, sewn against his skin for years. "This is everything I own that the company doesn't count. I've kept it hidden since I was twelve. If I give it to you, and we fail, you'll know I bet the only thing I had on you. That's not proof. It's just true."

He set the coin on the workbench between the lamps.

The basement was very quiet. Above them, faintly, the chandler's last apprentice locked the street door and clattered away.

Petra looked at the coin for a long moment. Then she laughed—a single dry note, surprised out of her.

"Aldous was right about you," she said. "I hate that." She swept the coin back across the bench toward him. "Keep it. Buy yourself something warm when this is over—if it ends well. If it ends badly, spend it on running." She was already pulling paper from the third drawer, already uncapping an ink bottle, her hands moving with the economy of long habit. "Passage papers take me a night. Credentials for the company house take two, and they'll cost you more than a coin. Now sit down, both of you, and tell me exactly what Halloway knows and when he knew it, because I don't forge for corpses."

Orsen eased himself onto a crate and felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since Vane died—since before Vane died, if he were honest, since the night on the quay three years ago when he'd held his friend's letter and understood that Aldous had chosen a grave as a hiding place and meant Orsen to be the shovel.

Three of them now. A forger, a widow, a diver's boy, and himself. It was not an army. It was barely a crew. But Aldous had always said the company's strength was also its weakness—that a thing so large only noticed what was large, and that the small moving together could pass through it like water through a net.

"Start with the harbor," Petra said, pen poised. "And speak quickly. We have less time than you think."

"Why?" Wren asked.

She glanced up, and for the first time her composure cracked around the edges.

"Because Halloway posted new notices an hour ago. Not for a scarred man this time." Her pen touched paper. "For a diver's boy. Alive, preferably. There's a price on your name now, Wren Blackwood—and prices make neighbors into hunters."

* * *

Wren

They worked through the night, and Wren learned what it was to be believed in.

Petra forged while Orsen dictated—dates, seals, the cadences of official language that he knew from the inside, from years of carrying Vane's correspondence through ports that no longer existed on any honest chart. Wren ran errands and held lamps and, when Petra barked for it, copied sample lines in Vane's hand until she grunted approval, which he learned was her highest register of praise.

It was near dawn, the lamps burned low, that Orsen spoke without being asked.

"You should know who I am," he said, not looking up from the papers. "Before you carry this any further. Before you spend more of yourself on it."

Wren set down the pen.

"I met Aldous twenty years ago, hauling cargo on these same docks. We came up together—he with his figures, me with my fists. We went into partnership, freight and insurance, small honest trade. Then Meridian bought our debts, the way they buy everything, and made us their instruments. I was the collector, Wren." His voice stayed level in the way that Wren recognized as its own kind of wound. "When a debtor couldn't pay, I was the man who went to the boat. I told myself I only frightened people. For years I told myself that."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the tide turned against the pilings.

"Your father's boat," Orsen said. "The *Gull's Mercy*. I wasn't on her that night—I'd begun refusing, by then, and Halloway sent others. But I signed the order that put her crew on the water. It passed through my hands. My seal is on it. It will be in the ledger, because Vane put it there deliberately, so that I would have to read my own name among the drowned." He finally raised his eyes. "Aldous didn't turn me with arguments. He turned me with arithmetic. He sat me down and showed me the column of names, and mine was in it, and I have been trying to climb out of that page ever since."

Wren thought of his father's hands, rope-scarred and gentle, teaching him to hold his breath in the shallows. Thought of the entry he'd read in Maren's loft, one line among dozens: *Gull's Mercy — lost with all hands — debt discharged.*

Discharged. As if a man were a sum.

"I'm not going to forgive you," Wren said at last. "Maybe I never will."

Orsen nodded slowly. "I'm not asking you to."

"But you're carrying his letters to the finish," Wren said. "That counts for something. I don't know what yet. But it counts."

Orsen's face did something complicated—grief and gratitude tangled past separating—and then Petra slammed a finished sheet onto the bench and shattered the moment on purpose, because that was clearly her nature.

"Done. Passage papers for three, clean as scripture." She held up a second document, cream-colored, heavy, sealed with wax she'd pressed from a stolen company matrix. "And this is the dangerous one. An auditor's credential, valid for the centennial audit, bearing a name that hasn't walked into the company house in three years." She looked at Orsen over the top of it. "Aldous Vane's ghost is going to walk into Meridian's front hall. I hope you're ready to be dead, Mr. Orsen, because by tomorrow noon, half the city will believe you are—and the other half will want to make it true."

Orsen took the credential in both hands, like a man accepting a commission or a sentence.

Outside, the first bell of morning rang across the harbor—and beneath it, faint and wrong, Wren heard boots on the chandler's stairs.
