## 5. Checkpoint One: Salt and Seals

The barrel smelled of three years of salt and rot, which was exactly the point. Nothing that reeked this badly would be opened twice.

Orsen crouched in the fish-cellar behind Maren's tavern, watching Wren seal the last staves with a mallet and a prayer of tar. The boy worked with the economy of someone who had learned early that wasted motion was a sin the body paid for. He'd wrapped Petra's copied letters in oilcloth, then in waxed linen, then wedged them into the hollow center of the cured herring like a pearl inside a very foul oyster.

"Third layer," Wren said, not looking up. "Petra's seals will hold. The tar's the weak point—heat softens it."

"Then we pray for cold weather," Orsen said.

"We're sailing south."

"Then we pray faster."

It earned him half a smile, gone as quickly as it came. The boy rationed his smiles like coin. Orsen understood that; he'd spent twenty years on the same allowance.

He flexed his hands against his knees. The scar tissue across his left forearm pulled tight in the cellar damp, the old burn aching the way it always did before he did something stupid. Vane used to say the arm remembered every mistake before the mind could. Aldous had been right about most things, in the end. It was why they'd had to kill him officially. A man who is right is dangerous; a man who is right and dead is only inconvenient.

"You'll take the front door," Wren said. "I take the barrel through the fish gate. Maren's cousin Tam meets me at the second pier with the *Kestrel*."

"I know the plan. I wrote half of it."

"You wrote the half where you stand in front of armed men and talk." Wren set down the mallet and looked at him properly, dark eyes level. "The half where you bleed isn't written anywhere."

"That's what makes it literature," Orsen said, and immediately regretted the flippancy—the boy's father had bled for less than a joke. But Wren only nodded once, gravely, as if literature were a real and heavy thing, and went back to the tar.

Above them, through the floorboards, they could hear Petra scratching away at her desk, still perfecting the manifest. She'd been at it since dawn: the *Kestrel*, out of Greyharbor, consigned to a salt merchant in Corrow Bay, fourteen barrels cured herring, one inspector's stamp she'd forged so precisely she'd wept over the serifs. "A forgery is only as good as its flaws," she'd said, blowing ink dry. "Too clean and they smell the pen. I've given this one a trembling hand and a bad temper." She'd signed it with a dead man's initials and called it a eulogy.

Maren came down the stairs with bread and hard cheese and no kind words, which from Maren was affection. She set the plate between them and stood with her arms crossed, a broad woman built like a mooring post, her gray hair tied back with netting twine.

"Tam says the tide turns at four," she said. "Customs runs the morning sweep at ten past. You want to be in the queue before the sweep, not after—they search the stragglers harder, out of boredom."

"Four o'clock," Orsen said. "Before the sweep."

"Three hours." She looked at the two of them—at Orsen's ruined face, at Wren's careful hands—and something in her jaw softened by a degree. "My son ate at your table once, diver's boy. Before you paid his debt. Whatever happens on that pier, you come back or you send word, because I don't bury people who owe me nothing."

"It's the ones who owe me everything I worry about," Orsen said.

"Then worry quietly." She turned and climbed the stairs, and the boards complained after her all the way up.

* * *

The harbor at dawn was a country unto itself, with its own laws and its own weather.

Orsen walked into it wearing a stevedore's coat two sizes too big and a hat pulled low, carrying the forged manifest in a leather tube under his arm like any merchant's clerk with somewhere to be. The disguise was simple and therefore good. Petra had wanted to give him a false beard; he'd refused. "They're looking for a scarred man," he'd told her. "A scarred man in a bad beard is a confession. A scarred man hauling rope is Tuesday."

The customs shed sat at the head of the third pier, a long timber building with a scale out front big enough to weigh a cannon. Two inspectors worked the queue in company-gray coats, moving barrel to barrel with a long steel probe—a spike they drove into each cask to sample the cargo. Orsen watched the probe go into a barrel of whale oil, then a barrel of pitch, then a barrel of nails, and felt the old cold settle into his stomach. He knew that probe. In another life, in another port, he had held one himself, and the men whose barrels he'd probed had watched him with the same flat animal patience he now saw on the faces around him.

The queue moved. Somewhere behind him, Wren was rolling their barrel toward the fish gate with Tam, keeping to the shadow side of the sheds, and Orsen made himself not look. Looking was how men died. He'd learned that in the company's service, back when he'd believed the ledgers were honest and the drownings were accidents.

"Manifest," said the inspector at the table. He was young, with a clerk's neat hands and a hangman's patience, and he did not look up.

Orsen passed the tube. The inspector drew out Petra's document and read it the way clerks read—backwards, hunting the lie in the flourishes.

"*Kestrel*. Greyharbor registry?"

"Cleared out of Greyharbor on the ninth," Orsen said. "Re-registered here for the southern run. Manifest's countersigned."

"By whom?"

"Aldous Renner, factor's clerk." A dead man's name, chosen by Petra because the company's own records listed Renner as transferred inland two years ago—present enough to be plausible, absent enough never to be checked.

The inspector grunted. His probe leaned against the table within reach of his hand, and Orsen watched the man's eyes do the arithmetic every inspector did: coat too fine under the stevedore's wool? Boots too good? Voice too educated?

"The consignee is a salt merchant," the inspector said. "Why is a salt merchant buying fourteen barrels of herring?"

"Because salt's cheap this year and herring isn't, and merchants are vultures like everyone else." Orsen let a note of bored grievance into his voice, the tone of a man who had answered this question forty times and would answer it forty more. "You want to inspect the barrels, inspect the barrels. You want to inspect my morning, buy me breakfast first."

Something flickered in the young inspector's face—not suspicion. Recognition, almost, of a species. Every port had these men, the ones who talked because silence looked like guilt.

"Barrel four," the inspector said, and reached for the probe.

That was the moment Orsen heard the boots.

Not the queue's shuffle—boots, paired, deliberate, coming down the pier behind him with the rhythm of men who were paid to walk toward trouble. He didn't turn. Turning was looking. He kept his eyes on the probe as it rose, as the inspector braced the fourth barrel—

"Wait." The voice from behind him was older, rougher, and it stopped the probe midair. "That face. Turn around, clerk."

The arithmetic of the next three seconds was the fastest of Orsen's life.

He knew the voice. Deputy Inspector Crale—he'd drunk with the man a decade ago, back when Orsen wore company colors himself, back when Crale had been a junior officer with ambitions and Orsen had been the company's own auditor, respectable and blind. Crale had aged into jowls and a drinking redness, but voices kept better than faces, and Orsen's voice had just spent a full minute being bored and educated six feet from the man's ear.

He could run. They would take him at the end of the pier, and worse, they would tear the pier apart looking for what a scarred fugitive might have brought aboard, and the barrel would be cracked open on a customs table with the letters inside it like a heart in a ribcage.

Or he could stay, and gamble everything on the distance between a memory and a certainty.

He turned around slowly, letting the hat brim fall as it fell, giving Crale a tired merchant's scowl. Behind Crale stood a younger officer, hand already resting on the truncheon at his belt, reading his superior's face.

"Do I know you?" Crale said.

"No," Orsen said. "But everyone says that to me. It's the hat."

Crale stepped closer. Close enough that Orsen could smell the gin beneath the official cologne, close enough to see the exact moment the man's eyes traveled the topography of Orsen's cheek—the burn, the split, the scar that ran from temple to jaw like a coastline on some drowned map. Close enough that recognition began, slow as tide, to fill the space between them.

"You were—" Crale said, and stopped, because saying it aloud would make it true, and true things required reports.

Orsen did the only thing left. He leaned in, close, and spoke low and fast, a confessor's whisper meant for one man alone.

"Deputy Inspector. Ten years ago you signed off on a shipment at Greyharbor that you never weighed. Four hundredweight of tin, short. Your signature bought a house in Corrow Bay—I've seen the deed. Now. You can shout, and I'll deny everything, and when they finally believe me, the tribunal that's coming for the company will come asking why you buried the tin." He smiled with as much warmth as his scars allowed. "Or you can finish weighing my herring and spend the rest of your career having never seen me."

The pier sounds went on around them—gulls, winches, the slap of water—while two men stood inside a silence the width of a gallows.

Crale's jaw worked. His eyes went to the younger officer, then back. Ten years of comfortable corruption performed their quiet arithmetic behind his face, and Orsen watched the sum come out in his favor, and hated every part of it—hated that blackmail was the currency, hated that he'd learned it in the company's school.

"Herring," Crale said loudly, stepping back. "Fourteen barrels, cured. Wave it through, Voss, the tide won't wait for your probe."

"But the manifest—"

"The manifest is countersigned." Crale took it from the table himself, and here Orsen's heart stopped, because the man's thumb passed directly over Petra's forged seal—and paused there, one heartbeat, two, feeling the wax, reading the tremor in the dead clerk's initials. Then he stamped it. The thump of the customs stamp sounded, to Orsen, like a bell.

"Next," Crale said, and did not look at Orsen again, and would not look at him for the rest of either of their lives.

* * *

The *Kestrel* took the tide at half past four, and Orsen watched the harbor fall away from her stern rail with the giddy, unclean joy of a man who has cheated at cards and gotten away with the whole deck.

Wren found him there. The boy's hands were still tar-stained, and he was breathing like a man who had run the length of the fish gate—which, from the speed of the *Kestrel*'s departure, he probably had.

"Well?" Wren said.

"Weighed, stamped, waved through." Orsen spread his arms. "Your letters are currently the most boring cargo in the southern sea."

For a moment the boy just looked at him, and then the smile broke through the rationing entirely, sudden and crooked, and he laughed—one short, disbelieving bark of it, the first Orsen had heard from him. Around them the crew coiled rope and shouted depths, and neither of them cared.

"We did it," Wren said.

"We did the first one," Orsen said. "There are three."

"There are three," Wren agreed, and didn't stop smiling, and Orsen thought: this is what Vane died holding onto. Not justice. This. The look on a drowning man's face when the rope arrives.

He should have known better than to feel it. Triumph was a debt the world collected promptly.

It collected at dusk.

They had put thirty miles behind them when the *Kestrel*'s captain came aft, walking oddly, and said without preamble: "Company cutter, stern. Running dark. Been following us since the point."

Orsen turned. Far back along the darkening water, a shape without lights was eating the distance between them with the calm of something that had never needed to hurry. And Orsen understood, with a cold that started in the burned arm and finished everywhere else, what Crale's pause over the seal had purchased. Not silence. A message. *He's aboard. Follow.*

"They can't know what we're carrying," Wren said beside him. His voice was steady. His knuckles, on the rail, were not. "They can't prove anything."

"They don't need proof," Orsen said. "They need a fire, and us in it."

The captain spat over the side. "Can't outrun her. Can't fight her. There's the shoals past Gullmark—water I wouldn't sail in daylight, and I'd wreck us sure as sunrise."

"Then put us ashore before the shoals," Orsen said. "Small boat, dark stretch, anywhere with rocks."

"That coast'll kill you."

"Everything kills you eventually," Orsen said, watching the dark cutter close. "The question is only what you get done first."

By full dark they were lowering away in the *Kestrel*'s dinghy with the satchels strapped across Wren's chest and the ledger's location burning in Orsen's memory, rowing hard for a black line of coast that had no harbor, no road, and no reason to welcome them. Behind them, the cutter's lantern finally bloomed—too late, catching only an empty ship and a crew of innocents.

Ahead lay the marshlands, and beyond them the drowned chapel, and between here and there, three days of country that belonged to the company's hunters.

Orsen shipped his oars at the shallows and stepped into water up to his knees, cold as a debt.

"Now," he said to Wren, "we learn what we're worth on foot."
