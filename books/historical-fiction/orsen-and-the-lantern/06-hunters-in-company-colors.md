## 6. Hunters in Company Colors

The notices went up on the morning tide.

Wren saw the first one before he saw anything else that day—a square of white paper pasted to the customs house wall, ink still sharp enough to smell. He had learned long ago to read a room in glances, to take in a street without letting his eyes rest anywhere long enough to be remembered, and so he took in the notice in a single flicker and kept walking, shoulders loose, face empty, a dockhand with nowhere to be and nothing worth knowing.

*A scarred man, forty years or more, left cheek marked by fire or blade. Travels with a boy of the harbor, dark-haired, known for silence.*

They hadn't gotten his name yet. That was the only mercy in it. But someone had described him well enough—*known for silence*, as if silence were a feature a man could wear like a coat—and beneath the description sat the number that made Wren's stomach go cold: fifty crowns for the pair, alive; thirty for proof of their deaths.

Fifty crowns was more than his father had earned in three years of diving.

He did not slow his pace. He did not look at the notice again. He walked on toward Maren's tavern the way water walks toward its level, unhurried, inevitable, and inside him something small and quiet lay down and died—the part of him that had spent nine years perfecting the art of not being seen.

* * *

Halloway's men had come to the docks at dawn.

Wren heard about it from Tam, the rope-boy, who told it the way boys tell things they're proud to have survived—breathless, embellished, delighted. The chief factor himself had stood on the quay in his gray company coat while his clerks pulled men out of the work lines one at a time. Not the beatings Wren knew from foremen with quotas to meet. Worse. Questions, asked softly, with a ledger open on a folding table and a clerk writing names into it as men answered.

"They wanted to know about strangers," Tam said, coiling rope with quick fingers. "Scarred man, fevered, come off a boat three nights back. Who'd seen him. Who'd fed him." The boy glanced up, and for a moment his eyes were older than his years. "They wrote down the ones who said nothing, too."

"Did they now."

"Maren's name came up." Tam's voice dropped. "Two of Halloway's men are sitting in her common room right now. Been there since the doors opened. Drinking small beer like it's holy wine and watching everyone who comes in."

Wren nodded as though this were gossip of no consequence, and walked away with his heart hammering against his ribs like a thing trying to escape a net.

Maren's loft above the tavern held Orsen, still feverish but mending, and the copied letters Petra had finished sealing in oilcloth two days ago. It held everything. And now it was being watched.

* * *

He watched the tavern himself for an hour from the fish market, standing behind a stall of iced herring with a basket he never filled.

Maren moved through her own common room like a woman wading a river she knew was full of stones. The two company men sat at the corner table nearest the door—one lean and young with ink-stained fingers, one broad and gray with the settled patience of a man who'd done this twenty years—and Maren served them, joked with them, refilled their beer, and every few minutes her eyes flicked to the stairs that led up to the loft, casual as a woman checking a kettle.

She was telling them, with her whole body, that the stairs led nowhere. That the loft was empty. That there was nothing above but salt-rotted beams and old nets.

Wren could have wept for her. Instead he counted the exits, counted the watchers, counted what he knew of Halloway's methods—because he had copied documents for the company once, and among them had been surveillance reports, and he knew the pattern. Two men visible meant four men unseen. The lean one would write everything down. The broad one would follow whoever left with purpose in his stride.

So when Wren finally crossed the street to the tavern, he made sure to leave the fish market with a full basket and the slouching gait of a man going home to supper, and he entered through the kitchen, where Maren's daughter's girl let him in without a word because Maren had trained even the children in the grammar of silence.

"You shouldn't be here," Maren whispered, finding him in the pantry between sacks of barley. Her hands smelled of onions and tallow. Up close he could see what the watching had cost her—new lines around her mouth, a bruise-colored tiredness under her eyes.

"And you should sleep," he said. "Neither of us is doing what we should."

"The letters are ready?"

"Petra sent word. Papers too. I'm to collect them tonight from the basement." He hesitated. "Orsen—"

"Fever broke this morning. He ate half a bowl of broth and asked twice if you'd been taken." Her mouth twisted. "I told him the truth. That you're too mean to get caught."

Despite everything, something in Wren's chest loosened. "That so."

"That's so." She gripped his wrist, hard, her calluses rough against his skin. "Wren Blackwood. Listen to me. Those men upstairs will be relieved at dusk—I've watched Halloway's rotations since my husband worked the pearl boats. One hour of dark streets with nobody on them. Whatever you're carrying tonight, carry it then, and don't come back here after. Not for a week. Not until I send the boy."

"And your son—"

"My son is alive because a diver's boy dove into black water after him when no grown man would." Her voice cracked and steadied itself. "We're past keeping score, you and I."

* * *

Petra's basement smelled of lamp oil and cut paper, and it was lit like a tomb—two candles hooded behind tin shades, just enough glow to see the work. She had the papers spread across her bench when Wren came down the stair: passage manifests, a customs seal pressed so faithfully into red wax that Wren had to touch it to believe it wasn't company wax, and a set of movement chits signed in a clerk's hand that didn't exist and never had.

"Your wanted notice is on every post from the fish gate to the north wall," Petra said without looking up. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained knuckles and eyes that missed nothing, and she spoke the way she forged—precisely, without ornament. "I passed three of them coming back from the chandler's. They've added a detail. Someone gave them your father's name."

Wren went still. "*What?*"

"Wren, son of Aldric Blackwood, pearl-diver, deceased, debt inherited under indenture number—" she recited it from memory, and it was his own number, the one branded into his memory the way it had been written into a book in a company office, "—indenture seven-four-one-one. They're not hunting an anonymous boy anymore. They know exactly whose son they're hunting, which means Halloway has been reading the debt rolls, which means he knows you copied documents for the company three years ago." She looked up then, candlelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Do you understand what that means? You're not a rumor to him. You're a line in his ledger. He's coming for you personally."

For a moment Wren couldn't speak. His father's name on a wanted notice. His indenture number, read aloud in Petra's dry voice like a sentence being passed. Nine years of being invisible, of moving through Meridian like smoke through a crack, and in two days the company had unraveled all of it—not because he'd failed, but because the ledger had made him matter. Because Vane's dead hand had reached out of the grave and dragged Wren's name into the light.

"I need to walk past my own face tonight," he said finally. "To deliver these."

Petra studied him for a long moment. "There's a copy of the notice on the chandler's post, halfway down Rope Street. If you take the delivery route you want, you'll pass it."

"I know. I chose the route."

"Why?"

"Because if I can't walk past my own name without flinching, I'm already caught." He picked up the oilcloth packet and felt its weight—letters, copies, the first thread of the rope that would hang the company. "They took invisibility from me, Petra. I'm not giving them fear on top of it."

Something moved behind the forger's eyes—approval, or its close cousin. "One more thing." She handed him a folded scrap. "A chit for the north warehouse district, valid till midnight. After that the watch chits change color and yours becomes a confession. Don't be late. And Wren—" she paused at the edge of the candlelight, "—when this is done, I'm forging myself a new name and a new city. You'll do the same. None of us gets to be who we were."

* * *

Rope Street at night was a throat of shadows between leaning warehouses, lamplit in pools, empty except for the sea-sound underneath everything. Wren walked it with the packet flat against his belly, bound under his jacket, and his breath steaming in the cold, and he kept his stride even and his eyes forward the way he'd practiced for nine years.

The notice hung on the chandler's post exactly where Petra said it would be, white as a wound in the dark.

He saw it from ten paces. He did not alter his speed. At five paces he could read the fresh addition in the clerk's neat hand: *Wren, son of Aldric Blackwood*. His father's name, printed in company ink, offered up to the harbor like bait. At two paces he could see his own description—*dark-haired, known for silence*—and beneath it, the reward, and beneath that, the company seal, stamped fat and certain as a thumbprint on a drowning man.

Someone had tacked the notice low. At a boy's height. So a boy would see it.

Wren stopped.

It was madness. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to keep walking, to give the notice nothing, not even the courtesy of a pause. But he stopped, because he understood suddenly what the notice was trying to do—it was trying to make him feel hunted. It was Halloway's hand reaching out of the company house, saying *I see you, diver's boy, I have always seen you.* And if Wren walked past with his eyes fixed ahead, pretending it wasn't there, he would be doing what he'd done for nine years—being invisible, being smoke, being nothing.

He was done being nothing.

He stepped close to the notice. Close enough to touch it. He read his own name, his father's name, the sum of his life priced at fifty crowns—and then he reached up, slow, deliberate, and tapped the paper twice with one finger, the way a man taps a debt ledger to confirm an entry. A greeting. An acknowledgment between professionals.

Then he walked on.

Behind him, from the doorway of the chandler's shop, where a watcher had been posted all along, he heard a chair scrape.

Wren did not run. Running was what guilty men did, and he was, in this moment, something stranger than guilty—he was a courier with legitimate papers in a company-sealed folder, a dockhand with a valid chit, a boy out after hours with an explanation ready. He walked, counting his heartbeat, listening to the footsteps behind him quicken into pursuit, and he turned the next corner into the lantern-light of the north warehouse checkpoint, where a watchman raised his lamp and asked to see papers.

Wren handed over Petra's chit with steady fingers. The watchman examined it, examined him, and waved him through into the warehouse district.

But the footsteps did not stop at the checkpoint. They followed him through. And when Wren glanced back, once, at the end of the lane, he saw the lean clerk from Maren's tavern standing under the last lamp, ink-stained fingers folded neatly, watching him go—with the patient expression of a man who had found exactly what he was looking for and needed only to report it.

By dawn, Wren knew, Halloway would know everything: the route, the packet, the basement, the widow, the forger, the fevered stranger in Maren's loft.

Unless Wren moved first. Unless the letters left the loft within the hour, carried by other hands, down other streets.

He turned toward the harbor, toward Maren's, walking fast now, faster, breaking at last into a run along the black water—and above him, in the high lit windows of the company house, he saw a light come on.
