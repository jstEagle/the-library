## 7. The Ledger's Resting Place

**Wren**

The final letter was written on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Wren read it by candlelight in Maren's loft while rain ticked against the shutters, and Orsen sat across from him with his scarred hands wrapped around a cup of tea he never drank.

*"If you are reading this,"* Vane had written, *"then I am beyond helping you, which will be no great change from when I lived. I hid the ledger where I hid my conscience—in a place the company considers already drowned, already paid for, already forgotten. The chapel of Saint Brannoc stood at the tide line before they built the new quay over it. The sea took it in the flood year. The sea keeps what the company gave away. At low water, the crypt door stands open to anyone desperate enough to kneel in the mud. Bring a lamp that burns wet. Count your minutes. The tide does not negotiate."*

Below the signature, in a smaller hand, almost shy: *"Tell Orsen the lantern was always meant for him."*

Wren lowered the page. Outside, the gulls were screaming over the harbor the way they did before a storm.

"Saint Brannoc's," he said. "I know where that is. Every dock boy does. We used to dare each other to swim down to the bell tower at slack tide."

"Can you find the crypt?"

"I can find it." He looked at the window, at the gray light bleeding through the seams of the shutters. "The question is whether we can leave it again."

* * *

**Orsen**

Low tide came at dawn, and dawn came gray and mean, the sky the color of dishwater wrung out over the city. Orsen stood at the edge of the exposed flats with his boots sinking into silt that smelled of rot and old iron, and felt something in his chest loosen and tighten all at once.

He had walked ground like this before. That was the thing Wren did not yet fully understand—could not understand until Orsen made himself say it—and every step toward the drowned chapel carried him closer to the saying of it.

The chapel emerged from the retreating water like a whale's carcass. Only the top of the bell tower broke the surface even now; the rest lay revealed in ribbons: walls furred with weed, arched doorways filled with sand, and there—the crypt entrance Vane had described—a squat stone door set into the seaward wall, its iron ring crusted orange, standing open by a hand's width because the flood of forty years ago had warped it off its hinges and the sea had never bothered to close it.

Wren crouched beside it first, testing the gap, his diver's hands sure in the muck. Maren held the lamp—a ship's lantern sealed against weather, borrowed from her son, who still didn't know what it was for. Petra hung back on higher ground with a spyglass trained on the quay road, watching for company men.

"The tide gives us four hours," Maren said. "Maybe three if the storm pushes the water back early. So whatever has to be said in that hole gets said fast."

Orsen knew she meant his confession. She'd heard enough fragments over the past two days to know one was owed. She wasn't going to let him walk into the dark with his debt unpaid.

They went in single file, bent double under the low arch, Wren leading with the lantern held high. Water dripped somewhere deeper in, slow and patient, counting time better than any clock.

* * *

**Wren**

The crypt was smaller than Wren expected—a chamber perhaps twelve feet square, its floor ankle-deep in cold brine, its niches stacked with stone shelves where coffins had once lain. Most were empty now, washed clean or rotted open by the decades. One, near the far wall, remained intact: lead-lined, its lid stamped with an anchor-and-scales seal that Wren recognized from every crate the Meridian Company ever shipped.

Vane hadn't hidden the ledger in a niche among the dead. He'd hidden it inside one of them.

"He chose this one," Wren said, running his fingers along the seal. The lead was cold enough to burn. "Why this one?"

Orsen had stopped moving. In the lamplight his face was gray, and not from the cold.

"Because I put him there," Orsen said.

The dripping water counted. Wren waited.

"Twenty-two years ago," Orsen went on, "the company cleared a fishing village called Harrow's Reach. Debt collection, they called it. The village owed more than the fish would ever pay, so Halloway's predecessor decided the debt should be collected in land instead, and the people—" His voice caught and steadied. "The people objected. There was a fire. Eleven died. And I was the man who helped load what was left of them into lead boxes and bring them here, into the dark, because the parish priest refused to bury them in consecrated ground and the company couldn't have a mass grave washing up on the flats. It was my first job for them. I was nineteen years old. I told myself it was mercy, carrying them gently, saying the words over them. I told myself that for twenty years."

Wren looked down at the coffin between them. The ledger of sanctioned drownings, sealed inside a box of sanctioned dead.

"And Vane?" he asked.

"Vane was there too. He kept the accounts of it. And for twenty years neither of us said anything, because saying anything meant admitting what we'd done." Orsen knelt in the brine, unbothered by the cold soaking through his trousers, and laid both scarred hands flat on the coffin lid. "Three years ago he started writing the letters. Three years ago I started reading them. That's the whole of my redemption, Wren. A man who helped bury eleven people spent the rest of his life digging them back out, and he only began because someone else picked up a pen."

The lantern flame bent in a draft from the crypt door. Somewhere above, thunder muttered across the flats.

"You want forgiveness," Wren said quietly. It wasn't cruelty. It was just the truth, spoken plainly, the way things had to be spoken in Maren's loft or not at all.

"I want forgiveness," Orsen agreed. "And I know better than to expect it. Forgiveness isn't a wage you earn. It's a gift someone decides to give you or doesn't. I'm not asking you to decide tonight. I'm asking you to know who's handing you this ledger. Know what my hands did before they carried it."

Wren thought of his father, thirty fathoms down with a debt chained to his ankle. Thought of eleven villagers in lead. Thought of how the company had taught them all to call these things accidents, losses, write-offs, entries.

"My father's name is inside that box," Wren said. "When I read it, I'm going to need to hit something. When I do, don't stop me."

"No," Orsen said. "I won't."

Together they worked the lead lid. It groaned, shed flakes of corrosion, and came free with a suck of trapped air that smelled of metal and silence. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a bundle the size of a hymnal—and beneath it, arranged with terrible care, a row of small objects: a child's wooden bird. A clay pipe. A woman's hair comb. A brass button stamped with a fish.

Grave goods. Vane had given them grave goods. Names for the nameless, gathered over twenty years of guilt.

Wren lifted the oilcloth bundle and held it against his chest, and it weighed nothing and everything, and he understood then that he would carry it the rest of the way himself—not because Orsen couldn't, but because some weights belong to the sons of the drowned.

* * *

**Orsen**

Petra's whistle cut across the flats like a knife—one short blast, then two. Company riders on the quay road. Not searching for them specifically, maybe; maybe just patrol, routine, the centennial tightening everything like a screw. But a patrol finding four people kneeling around an opened coffin at the drowned chapel would ask exactly the wrong questions.

They moved. Wren took the ledger inside his coat, bound tight against his ribs. Maren doused the lantern and led them by memory along a channel she'd fished since girlhood, water rising past their knees, past their thighs, the storm pushing the tide home early just as she'd predicted. Orsen brought up the rear and watched Wren's back, the boy's shoulders set like a man twice his age, the ledger's corner pressing visibly against his soaked shirt.

At the seawall ladder, gasping, salt-burned, Orsen caught Wren's arm.

"The letters say the lantern was mine," he said. "Vane's last line. But he was wrong. It was always yours. All of yours." He nodded at Maren, hauling herself onto the stones; at Petra, already scanning the road again; at Wren. "I'm just the one who carried it badly for twenty years."

Wren looked at him for a long moment, water streaming from his hair, and said nothing—which Orsen accepted, because nothing was more than he deserved, and because he had seen the boy tuck the ledger closer, deliberately, out of Orsen's reach.

Not forgiven. Not yet. Perhaps never. But trusted with the walking, if not with the holding.

That was more than enough to go on.

Then Petra grabbed Orsen's shoulder and turned him toward the quay road, and her voice dropped to a whisper that erased every other thought.

"That rider at the head of the column. Look at his colors." Her face was bloodless. "That's not a patrol. That's Halloway's personal guard—and they're riding straight for Maren's tavern."
