## 8. Checkpoint Two: The Company House

Petra's forgery smelled of vinegar and old fear.

She held the credential up to the lamplight in her basement, turning it so the seal caught the flame—Meridian Trading Company, Office of the Centennial Audit, and beneath it a name in faded iron-gall ink: *Orsen Vale, Special Auditor, Seconded from Port Ceres.*

"Dead men make the best auditors," she said. "Nobody argues with paperwork. They only argue with faces." Her eyes flicked up to Orsen's. "Which is your problem."

"My problem," Orsen said.

"Your face is on every bounty wall between here and the governor's gate. Scarred man, forty-odd years, walks like he's counting exits. I can forge the paper, but paper doesn't walk past Halloway's door wardens. Someone has to walk it in wearing you."

"That's why Wren goes with me."

Wren stood by the coal stove, arms crossed, watching the credential as though it might bite. He'd been quiet all evening—the particular quiet of a boy doing arithmetic he didn't want finished. Finally he spoke. "The centennial audit runs three days. Every clerk in the company house will be drowning in manifests. The door wardens on the harbor side check seals, not faces—too much traffic to look at anyone twice. But the inner stairs, the ones that go up to the record hall—" He paused. "Those are watched by men who know faces. Men who were there three years ago."

"You were there," Orsen said.

"I was there. I copied the audit summaries for two years before they found out I could read too well." Wren's jaw tightened. "Halloway's factor, Drossel, works the stair landing on audit days. He had my father sign his last contract. He'll know my face even if he doesn't know my name."

"Then we don't give him time to know either one," Orsen said.

Maren, who had come down with bread and stayed with the brandy, snorted from her chair by the stairs. "Listen to him. Three weeks ago this man couldn't lift his head off my loft floor without weeping into the fish barrels, and now he's planning to stroll through Meridian House like he owns the deed."

"I did own part of the deed once," Orsen said. "That's rather the point."

The room went still around that. Petra busied herself with sanding the credential's edges. Wren looked at the fire.

It was true, and they all knew it now—he had confessed it in the drowned chapel crypt at low tide, kneeling in black water with the ledger dripping in his hands: *I helped them bury the first ones. Before Vane's letters. I carried bodies; Vane carried numbers.* Aldous had turned first, and Orsen had followed slowly, the way a man follows a rope thrown from a ship he swore he'd never board again. Forgiveness had been asked. It had not been given—not by Wren, not fully, maybe not ever—and Orsen had learned to carry that the way he carried everything else: on the outside, where it could be seen, because hidden debts grow interest.

"We go tomorrow," Wren said at last. "Second day of the audit. First day everyone panics. Second day they're tired." He looked at Orsen, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there in Maren's loft when the letters were still sealed—a hard little pilot light of purpose. "I know the building's rhythms better than any man alive who isn't paid to. You'll be a dead auditor walking, and I'll be the clerk's runner nobody sees. That's how I survived six years in that place. Invisible."

"And if visibility finds us?"

"Then we run," Wren said simply. "And if we run, we're dead anyway. So we won't."

* * *

They dressed him in Petra's work that night: a company auditor's coat, good wool gone deliberately shabby at the cuffs, buttons mismatched just enough to suggest a man traveling on expense and resenting it. His scar was the thing no forgery could cover, so they didn't try. Petra powdered it faintly, aged the skin around it, made it look less like a wound and more like an old misfortune—the kind auditors collect.

"Auditors are supposed to look half-dead already," she said. "You're perfect."

In the grey hour before dawn, Orsen sat alone with Vane's final letter and read it again, though he knew it by heart. *If you are reading this, I am past apology. Take the ledger where I could not. The house eats its own; feed it its own history.* Below the signature, in fresher ink, Aldous had added something during their last meeting, the night before the fever took him on the road: *The record hall, third floor. The centennial registry is open on the audit table. Whatever passes into that registry before midnight of New Year's Eve becomes permanent. They built the amnesty to bury us. Make the burial a monument instead.*

One hour before midnight, Petra's trick would need the registry. But first the evidence itself—the copied letters, certified against the original ledger pages Wren now carried sewn into his coat lining—had to enter the building under official eyes, logged, stamped, witnessed. Evidence smuggled in could be dismissed as smuggled. Evidence *received* could not.

That was the whole architecture of the plan, and it rested on a dead man's face surviving one staircase.

Wren appeared at the basement door before light, dressed in his old dock clothes scrubbed clean, a runner's satchel over his shoulder. In it: the letters. On his hip: nothing. A boy carrying documents, invisible as ever.

"You don't have to do this part," Orsen said. "I can take the satchel."

"No." Wren said it flatly, no room in it. "You walk in as an auditor, they'll search you at the inner door. Runners aren't searched. Runners are furniture." He shouldered the satchel. "My father carried pearls for them until the water took him. I can carry this across a floor."

* * *

Meridian House at audit time was a beast mid-feeding.

Orsen felt it before he saw it—the crowd-noise spilling out of the harbor-side doors, clerks with armloads of manifests, factors shouting about tonnage and tariff schedules, porters hauling crates of records that stank of mildew and salt. Smoke stood over the building like a banner. Somewhere above, on the third floor, the centennial registry lay open on its table, waiting to swallow or sanctify whatever entered it.

He walked behind Wren, half a step back and to the left, exactly as a seconded auditor would follow the local man who knew the building. The credential hung around his neck on its ribbon; he kept his chin down, his gait deliberate, a man bored by his own importance. It was astonishing, he thought, how easy invisibility was once you stopped trying to hide and simply became dull.

The harbor doors went smoothly. A warden glanced at the credential, stamped Wren's runner's chit, waved them both through with eyes already moving to the next body in line. Orsen counted the exits as he passed—three doors, two windows barred, one corridor running east toward the factor's offices—and made himself stop counting. Counting was a hunted man's habit. Today he needed to be furniture.

The atrium opened above them, four stories of gallery and gilt, the Meridian arms carved into the marble: a ship over waves, and beneath the waves, if you looked long enough, the carver had put small drowned faces. Orsen had never noticed them as a young man. He wondered if the sculptor had known what the company would become, or if it was only stone being honest.

"The east stair," Wren murmured. "Drossel works the west landing today—I saw the roster posted at the gate. East is a boy named Quill, sixteen, more afraid of the clerks than of runners."

They crossed the floor. And that was when Orsen heard the voice.

"—no, I don't care what the manifest says, the *seal* says otherwise—"

Chief Factor Halloway stood at the base of the west stair, twenty feet away, directing traffic with the calm of a man rearranging furniture in a house he owned. He was smaller than Orsen remembered—or perhaps Orsen had simply been younger, and fear had inflated him. Grey now at the temples. Same cold eyes, same habit of scanning a room the way an auditor scans a column, looking for the figure that doesn't add.

Orsen did not freeze. Freezing was movement, and furniture didn't move. He turned his head slightly, studying the carved ship overhead as though it were fascinating, and let Wren's shoulder draw him forward toward the east stair.

"Hold." Halloway's voice again—but aimed elsewhere, at a clerk. Then, sharper: "You. Runner."

Orsen felt Wren stop beside him. Felt the boy's breath go shallow and controlled.

"Runner. The Ceres correspondence—has the seconded auditor's packet arrived from the harbor office?" Halloway was descending the last steps, coming toward them, and Orsen understood with terrible clarity that the question was routine, that hundreds of packets moved through this building daily, that the danger was not the question but Halloway's eyes, which never stayed on the mouth they were addressing.

"They're logging it now, sir," Wren said, pitched perfectly—bored, competent, slightly put-upon. "Harbor office said the Ceres man came in on the morning tide. He's upstairs expecting his escort."

Halloway's gaze slid from Wren to Orsen. One second. Two.

Orsen gave him the look he had perfected over thirty years of corporate survival: the flat, faintly insulted patience of a man from head office being delayed by provincial incompetence. He let his scar show. Let the powder age it. He was Aldous Vane's partner once; he knew exactly how these men saw strangers from Ceres—as weather, as tax, as nothing personal.

"Vale," Orsen said, before Halloway could speak. "Seconded audit. I'm told the registry table is ready." He produced the credential and held it steady, and his hand did not shake, and he would remember that steadiness later with a kind of disbelief.

Halloway took the credential. Turned it over. His thumb passed across the seal—and Orsen felt the whole plan balance on that thumbprint, on vinegar and old paper and Petra's genius, on whether a dead man's name could bear the weight of a living man's pulse.

"Ceres sent *you*," Halloway said.

"Ceres sends whoever Ceres sends," Orsen said. "Your centennial, your expense. I'd have preferred someone who knew the port."

Something flickered at the corner of Halloway's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ancestor of one. Provincial arrogance annoyed him. Good. Annoyance was a door, and Orsen walked through it while it was open.

"The third floor," Halloway said, handing the credential back. "Quill will log you. Don't touch the registry yourself—the archivists handle entry. And Auditor—" the cold eyes came up one last time, and held, and Orsen felt the temperature drop, "—your predecessor from Ceres, some years back. A man named Vane. You'd have known him."

The atrium roared softly around them. Somewhere a clerk dropped a crate. Orsen kept his face arranged in polite blankness while his heart performed its slow, enormous work.

"Everyone at Ceres knew Vane," he said. "Brilliant man. Terrible drinker. Left suddenly, I heard. Why?"

"He died," Halloway said. "Officially. Though the company has a long memory for its auditors." He stepped aside, gesturing toward the east stair with two fingers, a small courteous blade of a gesture. "Do thorough work, Auditor Vale. We pride ourselves on transparency this century."

"Transparency," Orsen agreed, and climbed.

* * *

The east stair was narrow and smelled of lamp oil. Wren walked ahead of him, and only when they reached the second-floor landing did the boy exhale—a long, controlled release, like a diver surfacing.

"He looked right at you," Wren whispered.

"He looked at the credential. Men like Halloway believe in paper more than faces. It's how they sleep." Orsen's voice was steady, but his hand, when he finally allowed it, trembled against the rail. Terror sharpened into precision—that was the discipline of it, the whole discipline: you let the fear exist and then you gave it a job.

At the third-floor landing, a boy with a freckled face and an oversized uniform looked up from a ledger. "Name and office?"

"Orsen Vale. Seconded, Port Ceres. Here for the registry certification."

Quill consulted his book, found the line Petra had caused to exist—an hour of her finest work, a forged entry in the genuine log, a ghost given a bed in the company's own memory—and nodded. "Archivist's table, end of the hall. You'll wait for certification. Runner can't stay past the rope."

Wren set the satchel on the archivist's table, and Orsen watched the boy perform the small miracle of unremarkability: signing the delivery chit, answering the archivist's questions in the flat tones of a thousand identical deliveries, standing unnoticed in a room where the fate of dozens of drowned men lay in a canvas bag. The letters went into the intake tray. The archivist stamped the chit. *Received, 11:40, centennial audit, pending certification.*

Pending. Not yet permanent. Everything still hung between the tray and midnight.

They descended together, past the rope, past Quill, down the east stair with its smell of oil, and it was on the second-floor landing that Orsen saw Drossel coming up the west steps—too late to turn, too many eyes around, and Drossel's gaze snagging on Wren with the slow recognition of a man matching a face to a debt. The factor's hand went to the boy's shoulder.

"Wait. I know you. Harbor side—Blackwood's boy—"

Orsen stepped between them, smooth as a man adjusting his coat. "The runner is with me, factor. Certification waits for no one." And to Wren, crisp, dismissive, head-office to furniture: "Back to the harbor office. Tell them Vale requires his copies within the hour."

Wren bowed—actually bowed, a runner's reflex—and went, and Drossel's suspicion had nowhere to land except on Orsen's credential, which he examined for a moment too long before the press of audit traffic carried him away up the stairs.

Orsen walked out through the harbor doors into the cold, into the noise, into the ordinary daylight, and did not let himself breathe fully until he was three streets gone and could see Maren's chimney smoke.

By dark, Wren had returned to the basement, alive, the satchel empty, his hands finally shaking now that it was safe to shake. Petra poured brandy all around. Maren laughed the ragged laugh of a woman who has been holding her breath for ten hours.

"It's in the tray," Wren said. "Stamped. Pending."

"Pending until midnight," Orsen said, and looked at the letter in his pocket, at Vane's handwriting, at the words *before midnight of New Year's Eve becomes permanent.* Outside, the city was already hanging lanterns for the century's turning. "Three days. One checkpoint left."

Petra set down her glass. "Then we'd best talk about the governor's gate," she said, "and what happens when Halloway remembers the face he almost recognized."
