## 9. Checkpoint Three: The Century Turns

The governor's gate wore its finest for the end of the world.

Not the world, of course—only a hundred years of it—but you would never have known the difference from the way the city had dressed itself. Lanterns hung from every balcony along Governor's Row in strings like caught fish, gold and white and the deep company blue that had been ordered by the thousand and paid for out of accounts that should have gone to widows. Musicians tuned somewhere behind the hedges. Servants hurried with trays. And above it all, past the wrought-iron gate with its meridian line worked in silver, the governor's house blazed with light from a hundred windows, warm as a hearth, cold as a ledger.

Wren stood across the square with his hood up and his hands in his pockets, feeling the folded credential against his ribs where Petra had sewn it into his coat lining, and thought: *this is what they spend while we drown.*

"You're staring," Maren murmured beside him. She had come in her good shawl, gray hair pinned up, looking exactly like what she was—a dockside widow come to gawk at the rich—and no one gave them a second glance. Invisibility was still a skill, even now. Even tonight.

"Counting windows," Wren said. "Sixty-one lit. That's sixty-one rooms I've never been allowed to sweep."

"Petra says the record room is on the second floor, east wing. Behind the clerk's gallery." Maren's eyes never stopped moving—the guards at the gate, the carriages, the crowd thickening as midnight approached. "She also says if this fails, she'll haunt us all personally, and she'll be insufferable about it."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. His gaze found the far side of the square instead, where a man in a fine dark coat stood beneath a lantern, talking to two others whose posture he knew too well—company men out of livery, Halloway's shadows. The chief factor himself had not yet appeared, but he would. Amnesty papers were signed at midnight. By twelve o'clock and one bell, everything Aldous Vane had bled to write down would be legally worthless, sealed under a century's clean slate, and the Meridian Trading Company would enter the new era with clean hands and full pockets.

Unless the evidence was already inside the record before the ink dried.

That was Petra's plan. It had seemed, in the basement three days ago, like a beautiful thing: forge Orsen an auditor's credential, walk him through the company house during the centennial audit, log the letters and the copied pages into the official record as routine audit material—an hour before midnight, when amnesty could not touch what was already filed. Wren had walked those halls since he was eleven years old. He knew which stairs groaned, which clerks took their supper at half past eight, which corridors emptied when the bells rang the hour.

It had seemed beautiful. Now, standing in the cold with the bells less than two hours away, it seemed mostly like a door made of paper between them and the sea.

* * *

They were twenty minutes from the gate when Wren saw Halloway.

He came up the row in a carriage with the company crest on the door, stepped down into the lantern-light like a man stepping onto his own property—which, Wren thought bitterly, it might as well have been—and paused. Just paused, there on the flagstones, turning his head slowly, the way a man turns toward a sound only he can hear.

Halloway was broad and gray-templed and ordinary-looking until you met his eyes, and then you understood why dock workers went quiet when his name came up. He had beaten men for losing cargo. He had beaten Wren once, two years ago, for a miscounted crate that turned out not to be miscounted at all. He had never apologized. He had simply added the debt back onto Wren's indenture, four months' service, entered neat as scripture.

And now his gaze swept the crowd and caught, for one terrible instant, on Wren's face.

Wren turned away too fast. He knew it was too fast. He felt the recognition land on his back like a hand.

"Walk," Maren breathed. "Don't run. Walk."

They walked. Past the musicians' cart, past a knot of drunk clerks singing the centennial anthem badly, past the hedge and around the corner into the narrower street where the servants' entrance waited—Petra's forged credential would get Orsen through the front, but Wren knew the servant's door off the kitchen court, and the stair behind the laundry that let out on the second floor not thirty feet from the record room.

Behind them, boots on stone. Not running. Walking. Deliberate as a tide.

"Halloway," said a voice, smooth and carrying. "Stop walking, boy. You know I'll find you either way. Make it easier on your friend the scarred man."

Maren grabbed Wren's sleeve. He shook her off gently.

"Maren. Go to Petra. Tell her it's moved early." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Tell her—tell her the diver's boy sends his regards, and she'd better be right about the timing."

"Wren—"

"He has Orsen's description. If he takes me, he learns nothing he doesn't already know. But if he finds Orsen first—" Wren swallowed. "Go."

She went, after a look that promised she'd haunt him personally either way.

Wren turned and walked back around the hedge with his hands open at his sides, the way you walked toward foremen and customs officers and anyone else who owned the ground you stood on. Halloway stood waiting in the lantern spill, flanked by his two shadows, and beyond him—at the edge of the square, being held by the collar by a third man Wren hadn't seen approach—

Orsen.

They had him by both arms, and he wasn't struggling, which frightened Wren more than struggle would have. Orsen's scarred face was calm, his pale eyes finding Wren's over the distance, and he gave the smallest shake of his head: *don't.*

"Well," Halloway said. "The diver's boy. I wondered which grave you'd crawl out of." He smiled without warmth. "You've been busy. Papers moving through my docks. A dead auditor's handwriting in my audit queue—yes, we found the first copies, boy, did you think we wouldn't? And here you are on the governor's doorstep with the rest of whatever Vane left rotting behind him."

Wren kept his voice flat. "I don't know what you mean, sir."

"No?" Halloway studied him the way he'd once studied a miscounted manifest. Then he nodded to the man holding Orsen, and Orsen cried out—a short, sharp sound, quickly bitten off—as the man wrenched his arm up behind his back. "Let's try again. Where is the ledger?"

The word *ledger* hit Wren like cold water. They didn't know where it was. They had the copies—Petra's copies, the ones meant for the record—but the original, Vane's own book, pulled from the drowned chapel crypt two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped beneath Wren's coat—heavy against his heart all night—that they did not have.

If they searched him, they'd have it. If he ran, they'd take Orsen apart in a cellar somewhere and the century would turn with everything sealed and drowned.

"I have it," Wren heard himself say.

Orsen went rigid. "*Boy*—"

"Quietly," Halloway said, and the man tightened his grip, and Orsen's protest became a strangled breath.

"It's hidden," Wren went on, and was surprised to find his voice didn't shake, because some part of him had been preparing for this moment his whole life without knowing it—every day of keeping his head down, every coin sewn into a sleeve, every hour spent learning how invisible people move through visible places. "Only I know where. You need me. So you'll let him go."

"And why would I do that?"

"Because if anything happens to me—if I stop walking, if I go silent—you'll never see it. It goes to the harbor authority the moment I'm taken. It's arranged." A lie, most of a lie, but delivered in the tone of a man reciting terms of trade, and Halloway was a merchant before he was a brute; merchants respected leverage even when they hated it.

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the square, the musicians began the first slow piece of the evening program. An hour to midnight. Maybe less.

"Search him," Halloway said at last. "Then bring the scarred man. We'll keep them both comfortable in the coach."

Hands patted Wren down—ribs, sleeves, boots—and found the credential in his lining, and Halloway read it by lantern-light and laughed, low and genuinely amused. "An auditor's pass. Vane's own signature forged. Petra Kell's work—I'd know that hand anywhere; she learned it copying mine." He tucked the credential into his own coat. "You'll hang for this, boy. All of you. But first—"

"The ledger's location," Wren said. "For his freedom. Those are the terms."

Halloway looked at him for a long moment, and something almost like respect flickered behind the contempt—or perhaps only accounting. A trade was a trade.

"Done," he said. "Where?"

"Drowned chapel. Old Saint Brin's, below the tideline. Crypt under the altar slab. Low tide's at one o'clock; you'll want lamps." Every word true, every word useless—by one o'clock it wouldn't matter what Halloway found there or didn't. Wren watched the chief factor nod to his men, watched them release Orsen's arms, watched Orsen stumble free and stand there breathing hard, staring at Wren with an expression Wren had no time to read.

"Get in the coach," Halloway said. "Both of you."

* * *

The company coach smelled of leather and Halloway's pomade. They sat opposite each other, guards on the bench beside each of them, and through the window the lanterns of Governor's Row slid past like drowning stars.

Orsen leaned forward, chains of tension still in his shoulders, and his voice came out barely above breath. "You gave up the location."

"I gave up a location," Wren whispered back. "You told me yourself—Vane hid it where he hid his conscience. There are things buried under that chapel nobody will ever dig up, and none of them are the ledger. The real one hasn't left my coat since the crypt."

Orsen stared. Then, slowly, something moved through his ruined face—not quite a smile, but its foundation. "You lied to Halloway's face."

"You taught me the handwriting. Someone had to teach me the rest."

The coach turned. Through the window Wren saw the governor's house again, closer now, and the clock tower rising above it, and the great illuminated face of the clock showing a quarter to twelve. Forty-five minutes. The record room was upstairs in the very building they were being driven toward, and Halloway had taken Petra's credential out of Wren's coat and put it in his own, and none of it mattered because—

Because the plan had never depended on the credential reaching the record room.

Wren closed his eyes and let himself remember Petra's voice, three days ago, chalk in hand, tapping the diagram of the audit queue: *"Here's the trick nobody checks, because nobody thinks backward. Credentials don't just open doors—they leave marks. When Orsen's pass was logged at the company house checkpoint this morning, the entry clerk recorded it against the audit register. Standard procedure. What they don't realize is that the register entry references the materials attached to the credential—copies, letters, the whole packet we submitted for 'inspection.' It's in the official record, loves. It's been in the official record since ten minutes past eleven this morning. Midnight can't amnesty what's already filed. We're not smuggling the truth past the gate. We did that hours ago. Tonight we just have to survive long enough for someone to read it."*

Eleven-ten. Nearly two hours ago. While Wren had been walking past his own wanted notice, while Orsen had been decoying inspectors, the evidence had quietly become law.

All they had to do was live until someone looked.

The coach stopped. Doors opened. Halloway stood in the courtyard of the governor's residence with the lanterns blazing above him, and he gestured them out with a courteous cruelty that was worse than shouting, and Wren climbed down into the light with the ledger heavy against his ribs and the clock face burning overhead.

Eleven-fifty. Ten minutes.

And then, from the direction of the house—running feet. A clerk in audit green burst through the doors waving a paper, shouting a name, and the name he shouted was *Halloway*, and the words that followed it, half-lost across the courtyard, included *register*, and *eleven o'clock*, and *governor wants you now*.

Halloway's head turned. For one suspended heartbeat, the whole night balanced on it—the lanterns, the music, the clock ticking toward the century, and the gray-templed man realizing, visibly, cell by cell, that the trap had sprung hours ago and he had been standing inside it the entire time.

His eyes came back to Wren. To the ledger-shaped bulge under the boy's coat.

"Take it from him," Halloway snarled. "Now. Burn it in the—"

The first bell rang.

Midnight had not come—it was the quarter-bell, the old custom, rung fifteen minutes before the year turned so the city could ready itself. But the sound rolled across the courtyard like judgment, and with it came more feet, more voices, the governor's steward, a magistrate in ceremonial white arriving early for the signing, asking questions in a voice that carried, questions about registers and filings and *who authorized the suppression of audit materials*—

Wren ran.

He didn't decide to. His body simply remembered every corridor and courtyard it had ever cleaned or carried through, and chose the gap between the coach and the hedge while every eye was on Halloway and the magistrate. Orsen ran too, beside him, breath ragged, and together they came out through the servants' court as the great clock began to gather itself for the hour.

They reached the iron fence at the row's edge and turned, both of them, chests heaving, to watch.

The eleventh stroke fell. The twelfth.

And then the bells broke loose—all of them, every tower in the city at once, a hundred years rolling over in bronze and thunder—and beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, rose a different sound: voices in the governor's courtyard, many voices, angry, astonished, awake.

Orsen put a scarred hand on Wren's shoulder. "It's done," he said, and his voice cracked on it. "Whatever happens next—it's done. It's in the record."

Wren watched the lights of the house, the figures moving behind the windows, the century arriving over all of it, and pressed his palm flat against the ledger through his coat, over his heart, where his father's name slept in ink.

"Not done," he said. "Just finally begun."
