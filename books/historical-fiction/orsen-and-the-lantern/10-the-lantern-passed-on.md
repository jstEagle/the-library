## 10. The Lantern Passed On

The trials ran through the winter like a fever through a body—hot at first, then sweating, then breaking.

Wren read the accounts in the broadsheets Maren brought home from the tavern, her boots crusted with harbor slush, her voice carrying that new note he was still learning to recognize as pride. *Meridian Factor Convicted of Conspiracy to Drown Debtors.* The headline writers had found their poetry at last. They printed Halloway's name in black letters thick enough to blot out the man himself, and beneath it they printed the number: forty-one. Forty-one drownings sanctioned by ledger entry between the company's founding year and Vane's death. Forty-one boats sent out heavy and rowed back light, or not rowed back at all.

Forty-one names. Wren had read them all now, in the courtroom gallery where the public was admitted on trial days, his hands folded so tightly his knuckles went white. His father's name sat eleventh from the top. *Tomas Blackwood, diver, debt of 340 crowns, vessel recovered intact.* Recovered intact. As if the boat were what mattered. As if the boat hadn't come home with his father's oilskin still coiled in the stern, still smelling of him, and Wren—a boy of nine—had pressed his face into it until his mother pulled him away because she couldn't bear the sound he made.

He had thought, walking into that courtroom for the first time, that seeing Halloway in chains would feel like something. Justice was supposed to have a taste, a weight. Instead it felt like hauling cargo: necessary, exhausting, and over too slowly. Day after day, the factor's counsel argued procedure. Day after day, the ledger was read aloud in a dry clerk's voice, entry after entry after entry, until the numbers stopped being numbers and became water again, rising in the room. On the ninth day Halloway shouted that Aldous Vane had been mad, that the letters were forgery, that no civilized house of trade could be held accountable for the losses inherent in maritime venture—and Petra, sitting beside Wren with her ink-stained fingers laced together like a woman at prayer, leaned close and whispered:

"Listen to him. He thinks we forged it. He still can't believe anyone kept honest books."

She smiled then, small and crooked, and Wren understood that this was the closest thing to joy the trials would give any of them: watching powerful men discover that paper remembers.

* * *

The restitution came in February, on a morning so cold the harbor had gone glassy and gray, gulls standing on the ice like punctuation marks.

Wren was summoned to the governor's annex—not arrested, summoned, which took some getting used to—and led past three doors into a room where an official with a face like a folded napkin counted coins onto a table. Not coins, exactly. The first minting of the new era, they called them: copper stamped with the century's date instead of the company's seal. Meridian's face was coming off the money itself. Someone had decided that was symbolic. Wren decided it was the funniest thing he'd heard in years.

"Restitution for dependents of record," the official said. "Sign here."

Wren looked at the sum. It was more money than his father had owed when he drowned. More than his whole indenture had been priced at. It was, the arithmetic of it told him plainly, the exact value the company had once placed on ending Tomas Blackwood's life rather than waiting for him to work off 340 crowns.

They had paid more to kill him than to keep him.

Wren signed anyway. His hand didn't shake until afterward, standing outside in the bright cruel cold, holding the purse against his chest like something wounded. Then it shook, and he let it, right there on the steps, in view of everyone, because he was done being invisible in ways that cost him.

Maren found him there. She didn't ask what was wrong. She just stood next to him, a widow built like a dock piling, and watched the frozen harbor with him until his hands went quiet.

"They'll want to give you a medal next," she said. "Governor's people came by the tavern asking after 'the young man instrumental in the recovery of documents material to the public interest.' I told them you'd died."

"You told them I *died*?"

"I told them what they wanted to hear." She shrugged. "You don't want a medal, Wren. Medals are how they turn men back into entries. Come inside. There's stew."

There was stew. There always was, now, in Maren's kitchen, and there was Petra hunched over the good table doing legal transcription—the real kind, the honest kind, work that had found her the way work finds the best forgers, through reputation—and there was a fourth chair that nobody said anything about but everybody left empty at supper, because Orsen was still at the governor's offices most days, giving testimony, answering questions in his low scarred voice while clerks wrote down everything Vane had spent three dead years trying to tell them.

The chair wasn't empty tonight. Orsen came in just after dark, shaking snow from his coat, and Maren put a bowl in front of him without asking, and he ate the way he did everything now—slowly, deliberately, like a man learning that meals could be unhurried and no one would come through the door.

"The last hearing is Friday," he said. "Then it's done. Sentences pronounced, company charter dissolved, assets divided. The governor wants me to stay. Wants to make me some kind of commissioner." He turned his spoon over. "Vane would have hated that."

"And you?" Petra asked, not looking up from her pen.

"I'm leaving Saturday."

The spoon went quiet. So did the room. Wren felt the words land somewhere under his ribs, expected and unwelcome all at once, the way a storm you've been watching build finally makes landfall.

"There are other ports," Orsen went on. "Other companies with the same habits. Vane copied letters for six houses before he died. Six sets of ledgers' worth of sins, and I'm the only one who knows where the copies sleep. If the lantern stops here—" he looked around the table, at each of them—"then it only lit one room."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about Orsen; he'd earned the right to be believed. But Wren heard Maren set her bowl down harder than necessary, and saw Petra's pen stop moving, and understood that the family they'd built in cellars and smuggled barrels and stolen stairwells was about to lose its strangest member, and none of them knew yet how to say so.

* * *

Saturday came gray and still, the kind of winter morning where the smoke rises straight and the whole city holds its breath.

Orsen traveled light. One bag. Vane's letters, the originals, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest where they'd lived since the chapel crypt. He shook Maren's hand and she pulled him into a hug instead and he allowed it, stiffly, like a man accepting surrender. He kissed Petra on both cheeks and she told him his forgery of a farewell speech was derivative and he laughed—that rare, rusted-hinge laugh—and then he turned to Wren.

For a moment neither spoke. What do you say to the man who handed you your father's murderer in a satchel? To the man whose fever you nursed, who taught you to read ledgers like battle maps, who stood in the company house doorway and said, quietly, *you walk in front, boy, you know the rhythms better than I ever will*?

"You were invisible when I found you," Orsen said at last. "Best hiding place I ever used." He reached into his coat. "I brought you something. It's on your doorstep. Don't open it till I'm gone—I never could abide goodbyes with an audience."

And then he walked down Maren's steps and along the frozen street, and none of them followed, because he'd asked, and because following a man like Orsen was the one thing he'd never needed from anyone.

Wren waited until the figure disappeared past the fish market. Then he opened the door.

On the doorstep sat a lantern. Old brass, salt-scoured, the glass clouded with years—but clean, freshly trimmed, the wick cut square, the reservoir filled with oil. It was the kind of lamp a night watchman carries. The kind a signalman hangs. The kind, Wren realized, remembering a story Orsen had told once, half-delirious, in Maren's loft, that Aldous Vane used to hang in his window during the bad years—a light left burning so that anyone rowing home late would know where the safe landing was.

Beneath it, a note in a hand Wren almost recognized—almost Vane's, but steadier, more alive:

*It isn't the light that matters. It's who keeps it burning. — O.*

* * *

They bought out the indenture on Monday.

Not with restitution money—Wren had offered, and Maren had looked at him the way she looked at dock thieves, and Petra had actually laughed—but with a leather pouch of coins counted out three ways on Maren's kitchen table. First wages of the new era, Maren said. Petra's transcription fees. Maren's tavern profits, the honest ones. Orsen's share, left behind in an envelope marked *for the boy's freedom, spend the rest on decent lamps.*

The clerk at the indenture office read the papers twice, suspicious of anything that easy, and then stamped them, and that was all. Twenty-three years old and free since birth, legally speaking, though the law had taken eleven years to notice.

Wren walked out of that office into the February light and stood very still on the steps, waiting to feel different.

He didn't, particularly. His shoulders ached the same. The harbor smelled the same. But when a foreman shouted at a hauler down the street—an ordinary shout, an impatient one—Wren found he didn't flinch, and hadn't flinched in weeks, and understood that this was what had changed. Not the paper. The body. Somewhere between the crypt and the courtroom, his body had stopped bracing for the blow.

He hung the lantern in his window that night. His own room above Maren's tavern now—he'd signed a lease, his name on a line, his hand steady—and from the window he could see the harbor, the channel markers, the dark water where boats came home late.

He lit it before full dark and left it burning.

Petra knocked around ten, ink on her knuckles, a client's contract under her arm. "You're visible from the mole," she said. "Fishermen will start using it. You know that, don't you? Old habit. They steer by whatever light's kept."

"Good," Wren said.

She studied him a moment, then nodded once, satisfied, and went downstairs to where Maren was keeping the fire up and a bowl warm for whoever came in from the cold.

Wren stayed at the window a while longer. Out past the breakwater, far off, he thought he caught it—a single point of light moving down the dark coast, northbound, steady as a heartbeat. A ship's lantern, or maybe just a star low over the water. He chose to believe it was Orsen, already carrying the letters onward, and he raised one hand briefly, foolishly, to the glass.

Then he trimmed the wick, topped up the oil, and settled in to keep the watch—because someone had to, and because the light was his now, and because somewhere out on that black water, a boat was always coming home late.
