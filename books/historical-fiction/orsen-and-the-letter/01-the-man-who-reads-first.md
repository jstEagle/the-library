## 1. The Man Who Reads First

The kettle was the first thing Isandro had ever owned, and it was the only thing in Marlowe House that knew what they did at night.

It was a small copper thing with a dented lid and a whistle that had been broken since before the war, so it never sang — it only sighed, a thin thread of steam escaping where the solder had given way. Isandro liked that about it. A kettle that kept its own counsel. They set it on the ring in the scullery at half past nine each evening, after the household had gone up to bed, after Edith Marlowe's footsteps had crossed the landing twice — once for the lamp she carried, once for the lamp she'd forgotten — and gone still. Then Isandro sat down at the scrubbed table with the day's letters fanned out before them like a hand of cards, and waited for the water.

Three letters tonight. Isandro counted them the way other men counted coins: not from greed but from habit, because a thing you count is a thing you can't lose track of. Three envelopes, three wax seals, three names written in Edith's slanted, urgent hand. One to Mrs. Aldous in Netherby. One to the schoolmistress at Cranmere, a Miss Voss whose handwriting Isandro had learned to read as easily as their own thoughts. And one addressed simply to *The Hon. Friend, Ashcott*, which meant nothing to anyone who didn't already know, and everything to someone who did.

The water sighed. The steam rose.

Isandro lifted the first envelope over the spout with a pair of tongs they'd bought secondhand from the ironmonger, holding it at the angle that worked best — too close and the paper warped, too far and nothing happened. The seal softened. The flap lifted on its own, slow as something waking, and Isandro set the envelope down and slid the letter out with two fingers, careful of the ink, careful of everything.

They had been doing this for fourteen months.

It had started, as most ruinous things start, innocently. A letter left on the hall table instead of the post tray. A corner of paper showing through an envelope not quite sealed. Isandro had driven Edith Marlowe six days a week that first winter — to the station, to the county meetings, to the houses of women who received her at side doors and spoke in lowered voices — and they had noticed things, because noticing was all a driver was for. You noticed which houses had two exits. You noticed when Madam stopped speaking mid-sentence as you turned into a street. You noticed that her letters went out heavier than her correspondence came in, and that some of them were posted in towns she hadn't visited.

The letter on the hall table had been one of those. Addressed to a name Isandro didn't know, sealed, waiting for Thursday's run. And Isandro — alone in the house for twenty minutes while Madam finished a meeting that ran long — had picked it up, held it to the window light, and put it down again without reading a word.

That was the last time they ever put one down unread.

Not because curiosity won. Isandro would spend years refusing to call it curiosity. It was simpler than that, and worse. It was the discovery that a person could hold another person's entire secret war in their hands, could feel its weight through the envelope, and that no one — no one in the world — thought to wonder whether the driver could read. Edith Marlowe was a woman who had spent thirty years fighting for a vote, who could recite the franchise clauses of four failed bills, who had been imprisoned and force-fed and released and followed, and who took more precautions with her letters than any woman in the county. She checked the wax. She varied her routes. She used coded names and false dates and a system of underlinings that Isandro had mapped within a month.

And every week she handed those letters to her driver, and watched them go into the post, and slept soundly.

Because drivers couldn't read. Or because this driver was nobody — an orphan off the workhouse register, taken on by word of mouth through the vicar's sister, no surname anyone had ever needed, no references beyond a nod. A person who existed in the margins of other people's ledgers. *The Galloway boy*, people said, though Galloway wasn't even right; it was the name the matron had pulled from thin air when a form demanded one, the name of a farmer who'd died forty years before Isandro was born. Isandro Galloway. A borrowed coat of a name. It fit loosely enough that nobody looked twice.

Nobody looked twice at Isandro. That was the whole architecture of their life, and for twenty-six years it had served them well.

Isandro read the first letter now, standing, because sitting made it feel too much like theft. Steam still rising off the page, ink smelling faintly of the vinegar Edith mixed into hers to keep it from feathering.

*Dear Ruthie — The frost has come early to our garden, as you predicted, and I find the late beans have proved hardier than expected. Mr. C— has been buying at both windows again. I enclose figures that will interest your committee; note especially the September entries, where his arithmetic improves remarkably whenever inspectors are due. Tell H. that the mill accounts are copied and safe. We must be patient until the ice breaks.*

There it was. *Mr. C—*. Magistrate Corven, though Isandro had never once seen the initials expanded, had learned the shape of the man entirely through abbreviation. *Buying at both windows* — the requisition office and the relief office, buying grain cheap from farmers compelled to sell, selling dear to families compelled to eat. Isandro had pieced it together across months of letters the way you piece together a landscape in fog: one detail, then another, then suddenly the whole valley.

They read it twice, closed their eyes, and said it back to themselves. *Frost. Beans. Both windows. September entries. Arithmetic improving before inspectors. Mill accounts copied and safe. Patient until the ice breaks.* Every word filed in the place behind their eyes where nothing decayed, a memory so reliable it frightened them sometimes — the one gift the workhouse had given them, drilling scripture and sums into children until recitation became breathing.

Then the letter went back into its envelope, the flap pressed down, the tongs fetching a fresh stick of sealing wax from the drawer Isandro had fitted out beneath the sink. Red wax — Edith used red for the network and black for her sister in Scarborough, and Isandro had matched the shade within a fortnight, melting test drops onto the cold range until the color was indistinguishable. A thumbprint of pressure, a breath of cooling air, and the letter was whole again, innocent again, exactly as it had been.

Except that now it also lived inside a person nobody believed could carry it.

* * *

The shame came later. It always came later — in the mornings, mostly, when Isandro was buttoning Edith Marlowe's motor car against the cold and she appeared on the step wrapped in grey wool, letters in hand, trusting as a woman handing bread to a stranger.

"Two today," she said on the third morning of November, breath fogging. "Netherby and Ashcott. The usual care."

"The usual care," Isandro agreed.

She paused on the step and looked at them — really looked, which she did perhaps once a month, the look of a commander taking stock of a soldier rather than a person. Her face was sixty years of weather and unbending principle, handsome the way a cliff is handsome. "You've never asked," she said, "what I write."

Isandro kept their eyes on the crank handle. "No, madam."

"Most men would ask."

"Most men would be answered badly, I expect."

Something moved behind her eyes — approval, or amusement, or the particular loneliness of a conspirator surrounded by people she can't conspire with. "You're a discreet creature, Galloway. I've often wondered where you learned it." She handed the letters over. "The workhouse, I suppose. Discretion is the one liberty they grant freely, because it costs nothing and serves them."

Isandro took the envelopes. Their hands were steady. Their hands were always steady; it was their insides that shook.

"Yes, madam," they said.

And that was the whole conversation, and it was unbearable, and Isandro drove her into town at a sedate twenty miles an hour with two sealed secrets riding in the door pocket beside their hip like live coals.

That was the arrangement of Isandro's conscience, and they had made their peace with it the way you make peace with a limp — by never letting yourself notice how far it slowed you. They told themselves a story, and the story was this: *I am not a spy. I am a witness.* Spies steal. Witnesses only see. Spies act; witnesses remember. If the house burned down tomorrow, if Madam's network collapsed overnight, if every letter were seized and every ally arrested — then, and only then, would what Isandro carried matter. Until such a day, they were merely a vessel, a walking archive, a man-shaped deed box that happened to roll along behind a horseless carriage. They weren't betraying anyone. They were keeping a copy. There was, Isandro insisted to themselves in the small hours, a moral difference, and someday — someday — the difference might even matter to somebody.

It was a good story. Isandro had told it so long they almost believed it, the way you almost believe a room is warm if you stand in it long enough with your coat on.

The quiet part — the part they didn't say even to themselves, except on nights when the kettle sighed and the seals cracked and the words went in — was that Isandro *liked* it. Liked being the only one who knew. Twenty-six years of being nobody, of being *the boy* and *the driver* and *that Galloway creature*, of watching history happen to other people through windscreen glass — the recruiting parades, the farewell crowds, the women in white sashes marching past the car while gentlemen shouted from the pavement — and here, finally, was a place where Isandro stood at the very center of events and no one knew it. Every evening, the true story of the town poured itself across a kitchen table, and there was exactly one soul alive who heard it. Not Corven. Not the constable. Not even Edith Marlowe herself, who wrote the letters and yet, Isandro sometimes suspected, understood them less completely than the person who read them in secret — because Edith wrote in code, and codes blur, and only Isandro held the full decoded weight of what *buying at both windows* meant in sacks and shillings and children's hollowed faces.

Knowledge was the only property Isandro had ever owned outright, freehold and unencumbered. Of course they hoarded it. Of course it shamed them.

* * *

Wednesday was the post run. Isandro drove to Ashcott first, because the Ashcott letter was the dangerous one.

Magistrate Corven lived in a red-brick pile on the square that everyone called the Courthouse even though it was just his house, because he conducted so much of his business from its front room that the distinction had eroded. Isandro drew up at the gate at ten minutes past eleven, as always, and the clerk — a narrow, damp-eyed man called Hobb who smelled permanently of ink and cold toast — was waiting on the step, as always, with the outgoing tray.

"Morning, Galloway." Hobb never got the name wrong. Isandro had never decided whether that was courtesy or surveillance. "Cold enough for you?"

"Cold enough."

"Madam Marlowe well?"

"Very well."

"Busy woman." Hobb sorted the outgoing post with long pale fingers, not looking at Isandro while doing it, which Isandro had learned long ago was a technique — the illusion that a man absorbed in papers cannot see anything else. "Keeps her carriage running all winter, does she? Most would lay it up till spring."

"Madam doesn't lay up."

"No," Hobb said softly. "She doesn't, does she."

He handed the tray across. Two letters from the magistrate's office, franked, official. Isandro added Edith's envelope to the collection with the same unhurried motion they used for everything, the motion of a man with no interior life whatsoever, and tipped their cap, and drove away at twenty miles an hour while the back of their neck prickled like sunburn.

In the mirror, Hobb stood on the step longer than usual. Watching the car. Not the road — the car.

Isandro filed that too.

* * *

That night there was only one letter to steam, and it was short, and it undid Isandro more thoroughly than any long one ever had.

*Dear Miss Voss — The child at the mill gate is yours to place; her mother's pension has come short again, third quarter running, and the girl was seen picking coal by the sidings. Do what we discussed. As for the rest: patience. When the guns stop, the accounts will be tidied faster than you can blink. Whatever we mean to do, we do it before the armistice, or not at all. — E.M.*

Isandro read it three times. *Pension come short.* Somewhere in the fog-landscape of the scheme, a widow's allowance was being shaved — a few shillings a quarter, invisible in the totals, fatal at the mill gate — and a little girl was picking coal by the sidings because of it, and somewhere in London men were deciding the date the guns would stop, and when they stopped, the arithmetic would improve one final time and the ledger would close clean forever.

*Before the armistice, or not at all.*

Isandro sat in the darkening scullery with the letter cooling in their hands and felt the strange vertigo they had felt only once before — the day they'd realized, aged eleven, that the workhouse bell that ordered their days would ring whether they obeyed it or not, that the world was a machine and they were not even a cog in it, only the grease.

History was happening here. Not in Flanders, not in London, not in the newspapers — here, in this kitchen, in these envelopes, in the gap between a magistrate's two windows. It had been happening the whole time Isandro drove through it at twenty miles an hour, invisible, telling themselves they were only a witness.

But a witness, Isandro thought, setting the kettle back on the ring for the resealing, is someone who might be asked to speak.

And no one — not Edith, not Corven, not God himself — knew that the only complete witness in the town was a person with no legal existence, no birth record, no surname of their own, and no one to vouch for them at all.

A witness who could not testify was just a ghost with a good memory.

The wax dripped red onto the flap. Isandro pressed their thumb down, held it, and wondered — for the first time in fourteen months — what it would cost to become visible.
