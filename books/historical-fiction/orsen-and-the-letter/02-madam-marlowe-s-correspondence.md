## 2. Madam Marlowe's Correspondence

Edith Marlowe wrote her letters the way other women said their prayers: every morning, at the same hour, in the same chair by the east window where the light came up grey over the river.

The routine had hardened into ritual over eleven years. Blotter first. Then two sheets — one for the letter itself, one for the draft that would be burned. She wrote the draft faster, without stopping to think, because thinking was where caution crept in and caution, in her experience, curdled prose into mush. The fair copy she made slowly. Deliberately. Each sentence weighed twice before it earned its ink.

To a stranger reading over her shoulder — and she assumed there was always someone reading over her shoulder, an assumption that had kept her free and out of Corven's court for a decade — the letters would have seemed like nothing. Garden talk. Recipe requests. Complaints about her knees and the price of coal.

*My dear Vera, the frost has taken the last of the beans. I find I have more preserves than shelves this year; do tell me whether your cellar runs warm or cold, as I should hate to lose another batch the way we lost them in '14.*

Vera Ashcombe, three counties east, would read *beans* and understand requisition orders. *Preserves* were the pension rolls she'd copied before leaving her post at the county office. A *warm cellar* meant a safe house; *cold*, one watched. And *the way we lost them in '14* — that was the year Corven's court had taken her sister's case, twisted it, and buried it so thoroughly that when Margaret died of the fever in the workhouse infirmary, no record of her complaint survived longer than a season.

Edith dipped the pen. Her hand did not shake. It had stopped shaking years ago, somewhere between grief and purpose, and she had come to think of the steadiness as the truest monument she could raise to Margaret: a hand that never trembled, not even now, writing to forty women across four counties who each believed they were corresponding with a lonely widow with too many preserves and opinions about beans.

She believed — she needed to believe — that none of it could be traced. Not through the letters themselves. Not through the network, which was built like a root system, each woman knowing only her own thread. And not through delivery, because delivery was handled by Isandro Galloway.

* * *

Isandro had come to her in the spring of 1915, walking up the drive in a driver's coat two sizes too large and asking for work in a voice like a held breath. No references. No surname history worth mentioning — an orphan raised at the parish edge, work wherever work could be found, no papers beyond a work card issued by a parish clerk whose name Isandro couldn't quite recall.

Any sensible woman would have turned them away. Edith had hired them on the spot.

Not because she trusted them — trust was a currency she kept locked away — but because Isandro was exactly what she required: a person with no records, no family, no ties, no history that anyone could pull on and unravel back to her door. In the eyes of every clerk and constable in the county, Isandro Galloway did not properly exist. You cannot subpoena a shadow. You cannot interrogate what no ledger bothers to list.

It was, Edith reflected sometimes with a private wince, the same logic Corven used when he dismissed the testimony of women and the poor. An unrecorded life is an invisible life. She had simply learned to weaponize the erasure that had been used against her sister.

And Isandro drove well. Quiet, punctual, incurious — or seeming so. Never asked about the letters. Never lingered near the writing desk. Took the sealed envelopes to the post or carried them personally when the matter was delicate, and waited outside doors with the patience of furniture.

Furniture, Edith thought, dipping her pen again, does not read.

She had settled this question in her own mind years ago, the way one settles any uncomfortable question: by never examining it closely enough to disturb the answer. Isandro could not read. This was common among the parish-raised; the workhouse schools taught obedience and little else. She had never tested it. Testing felt like an accusation, and besides — what would be the harm if some stray word were glimpsed? The code was hers alone. The cipher of beans and cellars was meaningless without the key, and the key lived nowhere but in her head and in forty heads scattered safely across the countryside.

Still. There were moments.

There was the time, two winters past, when she'd mentioned in a letter to Vera that the *cellar ran cold this season* — meaning the safe house in Millbrook was being watched — and Isandro, loading the carriage, had suggested without prompting that they take the long road through Fenwick rather than past the mill. Avoiding the mill road entirely. Where the watchers stood.

Coincidence, surely. The Fenwick road was smoother in ice. Any driver would prefer it.

She had written to Vera anyway, that same night, changing half the code words by prearranged rotation, and told herself the unease in her chest was only the weather coming on.

* * *

Today's letter was different. Today's letter was not going to the post at all.

Edith sanded it, folded it, sealed it with plain wax — deliberately plain, deliberately unremarkable — and set it on the hall table beside her gloves. Then she rang for Isandro.

"Madam." Isandro appeared in the doorway with that particular stillness, hands loose at their sides, eyes level and dark and giving back nothing. Eleven years, and Edith had never once seen those eyes dart. It was unnerving, sometimes, how completely still a person could be.

"I need you to take this yourself. Not the post. To the courthouse — to Magistrate Corven's chambers, and into his own hand or his clerk's, nowhere else."

Something moved behind Isandro's eyes then. Not surprise exactly. Edith had learned to read the small tectonics of that face the way sailors read water — and what moved there now was less surprise than *recalibration*. As though some internal sum had just been checked and found correct.

"Yes, madam. Shall I wait for a reply?"

"No reply expected. Leave it. Take the river road — the bridge is still open, and I want you home before the light goes. They're saying early ice this year."

"The ferryman says the ice will come a fortnight early," Isandro said. "He says the river's already smoking at the bends."

"The ferryman says many things." Edith allowed herself a thin smile. "Go on, then. Straight there, straight back."

Isandro took the letter. And here was the thing Edith noticed afterward, turning it over in her mind like a stone with something underneath it: Isandro took the letter by its edges. Carefully. The way one handles a thing one understands to be fragile — or dangerous — rather than the careless pinch of fingers a person gives to an envelope that is only paper.

She told herself it meant nothing. Drivers learned care with their employers' property; it was bred into good service like polish into silver.

She told herself this while watching from the window as the carriage pulled away down the drive, and the telling did not sit easily.

* * *

The contents of that letter would have made strange reading for anyone who intercepted it — which was, of course, precisely the point of its strangeness.

*Dear Magistrate,* it began, in Edith's roundest, most guileless widow's hand.

*I write to thank you for your courtesy at the harvest festival, and to say that my late husband's accounts have lately come into my hands, and I find among them certain figures concerning the county grain requisitions that I confess I do not understand. The tonnage recorded seems to me far greater than what our farms delivered, and yet I am assured the shortfall was paid for at full rate. Perhaps an old woman's arithmetic is simply failing her. I would be grateful if you might look over the enclosed figures and tell me where my error lies.*

*Yours most respectfully,*

*Edith Marlowe*

There were no enclosed figures. There was nothing enclosed at all. But the letter was a message all the same, and its recipient would read it perfectly.

It said: *I know the requisition ledgers are false. I know grain was recorded as collected that never entered the stores — sold instead, at famine prices, to the very mouths it was meant to feed. I know, and I am telling you that I know, politely, in writing, in a letter that will go into your files and stay there, dated, signed, and impossible to deny.*

It said: *I am not afraid of you anymore.*

And it said, in its gentlest line — *perhaps an old woman's arithmetic is simply failing her* — the thing Edith wanted him to lie awake with: *I have allies who keep their arithmetic very sharp indeed, and they are copying everything.*

Corven would read it standing at his window, she imagined, with his neat gray face going neater. He would summon his clerk. He would begin pulling threads — looking for Vera, looking for the network, looking for the source of the leak in his requisition office. Let him pull. Every thread ended in a dead woman's garden talk and a widow's bad arithmetic.

That was the theory, at least. Theories, Edith knew better than most, were the things that broke first.

* * *

She spent the afternoon of Isandro's absence doing what she always did with an afternoon: nothing that looked like anything. She reorganized the linen cupboard. She wrote three harmless letters to three harmless correspondents — actual recipes, actually requested. She walked the garden path to the cold frames and cut the last kale with scissors worn bright at the hinge, Margaret's scissors, the only thing of her sister's that Corven's men hadn't inventoried and burned.

At four o'clock she found herself at the east window again, watching the river road.

The light was going coppery. Frost smoke stood up off the water downstream, where the river bent and deepened, and the bridge — that old iron-and-timber span, the town's single artery to the coach road and the world beyond — wore its first rime of white along the rails. The ferry sat moored and empty at its landing. Another week, perhaps two, and both would close for the season. The river would become a wall.

She thought about walls. She thought about how convenient it was, for a man like Corven, to live in a town that sealed itself off every winter — where letters slowed, where strangers thinned to nothing, where a magistrate's word became, for six frozen months, the closest thing to law the law itself could reach. Margaret had died in a February. Everything Corven had ever buried, he had buried under snow.

The carriage came up the drive at half past five, right on schedule, lamps already lit against the dusk.

Edith went to the hall. When Isandro handed her back the gloves and reported — "Delivered, madam. Into Clerk Hobb's hand; the Magistrate was in session" — the report was precise. Too precise? Or merely professional?

"And how did it look, the courthouse?" she asked, lightly. Making conversation. Only making conversation.

"Cold," Isandro said. "There were men in the corridor waiting about pensions. One of them had been waiting since morning. The clerk made him show his discharge papers twice."

"Twice."

"He said the first copy wasn't legible." Isandro paused, and something flickered across the stillness of their face — gone so fast Edith could almost believe she'd invented it. "The paper looked legible enough to me."

Then Isandro bowed slightly, said good evening, and went out to the stables, leaving Edith standing in her own hallway with the chill of the words settling around her shoulders like a coat.

*Looked legible enough to me.*

A person who could not read, she reasoned, might say such a thing. Might repeat a complaint they'd heard. Might notice that a clerk was being cruel and remark upon it, the way anyone might.

Or.

She climbed the stairs to her study, lit the lamp, and sat for a long while without opening any of her drawers. Below, faintly, she heard the stable door, the soft industry of evening chores. The house ticked and settled around her.

Eleven years she had run this network on a single wager: that the enemy watched everyone who mattered and no one who didn't. That invisibility was armor. She had chosen a driver with no name of record precisely because the world refused to see such people — and she had never once considered that the world's blindness might not be shared by the driver. That Isandro Galloway might see everything, and let the world go on believing otherwise.

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, the front door latch clicked — Isandro, bringing in the last of the coal perhaps. Ordinary sounds. An ordinary household.

Edith opened her drawer at last, took out tomorrow's unfinished letter to Vera, and read the first line over twice.

Then, in the margin, in a hand so small it might have been a stray mark of the pen, she added a single new notation — one that belonged to no code either woman had ever agreed upon:

*Ask after the driver's schooling.*

Outside, the first flakes of the season came down through the dark, fine as sifted flour, settling without sound on the garden, the road, the black smoking water — beginning, quietly, to close the world.
