## 3. The Bridge Closes

The ice came early that year, and it came like a thief — quietly, in the dark, so that the town woke to find itself already robbed.

Isandro heard it first as a change in the river's voice. For weeks the water below Marlowe House had been talking to itself, that low mutter of a current dragging at its own edges, and then, on the morning of the fourth of November, it simply stopped. Silence has a texture. Isandro lay in the loft above the carriage house and listened to the absence and understood, before looking, what had happened.

By seven the boy who swept the post office steps was standing on the bridge road with his broom forgotten, staring down at a skin of grey-white glass that had crept out from both banks overnight and met itself in the middle, seam imperfect, like two pages badly joined. By eight there were men on the span itself, stamping their boots, spitting over the rail to test the wind's opinion. By nine, Magistrate Corven's notice was nailed to the bridge posts on both sides, the ink still sharp:

**BY ORDER OF THE MAGISTRACY — THE RIVER CROSSING IS CLOSED TO ALL TRAFFIC UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. THE FERRY IS SUSPENDED. THIS FOR THE SAFETY OF THE PUBLIC.**

The ferryman, old Tewson, stood under the notice with his cap in his hands, reading it three times through, though he could have recited it from memory by the second pass. Isandro, bringing the motorcar around from the livery for Mrs. Marlowe's morning errand, slowed near him.

"They closed it fast," Isandro said.

"Fast as anything." Tewson rubbed his jaw. "Ice is thin as window glass yet. I've carried heavier loads on thinner water than that. But there's no arguing with a posted notice, is there." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on the paper a moment too long, the way a man looks at a door that has just been locked from the outside.

"No," Isandro said. "There isn't."

The word *further* sat oddly in Isandro's chest all the way back up the hill. *Until further notice.* Not until spring. Not until the ice bears. *Further.* A word that could mean anything, which meant it could be made to mean whatever the man holding the pen wanted it to mean.

Mrs. Marlowe was waiting at the door when the car stopped, wrapped to the chin in grey wool, her breath standing in the cold air like small ghosts leaving her one at a time.

"So," she said. "They've sealed us in."

"It's weather, madam. It happens every year."

"It happens every year," she agreed, climbing in with the particular care she used when she wished to seem merely old. "And every year I take the ferry to Harwick twice in November and once in December, and post my letters from the larger office where they are less read. And this year I shall post them here, from our own little office, where everyone knows everyone's business and Mr. Hobb counts the letters like a miser counts coins." She settled her skirts. "Well. Nothing to be done about it. The mill yard, if you please. And we shall see what we shall see."

What they saw, at the mill yard, was Magistrate Corven's clerk.

Hobb was not a large man, but he had the gift of occupying more space than his body required — a narrow chest, a long neck, spectacles that caught the light and returned nothing. He was standing outside the mill office with a ledger under his arm, ostensibly checking the grain tallies against the requisition chits, and when the motorcar drew level with him he did not look away quickly, as a polite man would. He looked exactly as long as was proper and then a half-second longer, and Isandro felt that half-second land somewhere between the shoulder blades like a hand testing a lock.

"Mrs. Marlowe." Hobb touched his hat brim. "Cold work, driving, this time of year."

"There are colder trades, Mr. Hobb."

"There are indeed." His gaze slid sideways, unhurried, to Isandro at the wheel. "Your driver keeps regular hours. I've noticed. In at the post office most mornings, regular as the collection."

"I pay them to keep regular hours," Mrs. Marlowe said pleasantly. "It's a rare quality in servants. One hopes it isn't being punished for."

Something moved behind the spectacles — not quite a flinch; Hobb was too well-made for flinching — but a recalibration, a small internal shifting of weights on a scale. "No punishment intended, madam. Only, with the bridge shut and the roads what they are, the magistracy likes to know who's coming and going. Wartime. Everyone understands wartime."

"Everyone understands wartime," Mrs. Marlowe agreed, and Isandro put the car in gear, and the conversation ended the way conversations with Hobb always ended: with him having said nothing and having taken something anyway.

*

The post office occupied the ground floor of the old wool exchange, a room of dark wood and darker varnish, smelling of ink, dust, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by many worried hands. There were four windows; only two were ever open, and one of those belonged to Hobb himself when the mood took him, which lately it did more often. The letter rack ran along the wall behind the counter, cubbyholes labeled in fading gilt — the town's entire interior life sorted into alphabetical boxes anyone could glance at while pretending to wait.

Isandro had been coming here three mornings a week for two years. Three mornings a week, Mrs. Marlowe's letters went into the slot, addressed to names in towns Isandro had never seen: a Mrs. Aldous in Harwick, a Miss Verity Crane in Ashden, a Colonel's widow named Prewitt further north. Ordinary letters, ordinary hands. Isandro steamed each one open the night before delivery — not from curiosity, Isandro told the ceiling of the loft, only from habit, the way some men chewed — memorized the contents, resealed the flap with a warm knife, and forgot everything except the route.

That was the arrangement. That was the story. Isandro was a driver. Drivers drove. What was in the letters was other people's history, and history, properly conducted, did not require a witness.

Except that witnesses, Isandro was beginning to understand, do not get to choose when the thing witnessed turns and looks back.

The morning after the bridge closed, the queue at the counter was longer than usual — the closure backed up a fortnight's correspondence, farmers wanting money orders, wives writing to sons at the front with the new hope that maybe, this winter, the guns would stop. Isandro stood in line with five of Mrs. Marlowe's envelopes in an inside pocket, each one carrying its small cargo of coded civility. *I trust your garden is keeping well through the frost. The yield here has surprised even me.* Yield. Corven's yield. The grain that left the county by night trains and came back sold at famine prices to the very farms that grew it.

Isandro knew that now. Had known it since October, since the letters had stopped being pleasantries and started being arithmetic, and Isandro had done the unwelcome arithmetic of complicity: two years of carrying the evidence without knowing it was evidence, and then two months of knowing, and still carrying it, because the alternative was to become visible.

Ahead in the queue, Hobb was serving at the second window. He served slowly. He asked each farmer about his sons, each wife about her ration book, and wrote things in a small notebook between customers — not official notes; the official ledger sat open beside him untouched. Private notes. Isandro watched the notebook close over a thought the way a hand closes over an insect.

When Isandro's turn came, Hobb was at the window.

"Morning," Hobb said.

"Morning."

"The usual weight?" Hobb weighed the envelopes without waiting for an answer. Five letters. His fingers paused on the third — the one to Miss Crane, the thickest, the one with the arithmetic in it — and he turned it over once, examining the seal, the address, the return corner, which was blank because Mrs. Marlowe never used a return corner. "Heavy letter."

"People write more in winter."

"Do they?" Hobb set it on the scale with exaggerated gentleness, as though it might bruise. "I find people write more when they're frightened. Or angry. Winter brings both on." He looked up over the spectacles, and for the first time he looked directly at Isandro — not past, not through, not at the uniform cap or the gloved hands, but at the face, holding it, the way Corven himself held a face when he wanted a man to know he was being counted. "You've been driving for Mrs. Marlowe how long now?"

"Two years."

"Two years." He nodded slowly, as if the number confirmed something he'd suspected at one. "Long time. You must know her habits better than her own family would. If she had family." He stamped the letters, each stamp falling like a small verdict. "Where was it you were before? I don't recall you on any register."

"I've driven in the north."

"The north." Hobb smiled. It was a courteous smile, entirely empty, a smile with nothing behind it but more questions. "Funny thing about registers. A man can live his whole life careful, and the paper trail behind him is thin as thread, and nobody thinks anything of it — right up until the day somebody wants to pull the thread." He pushed the letters across. "Two shillings. No receipt necessary."

Isandro paid. The coins made a small bright sound in the till, absurdly loud in the quiet room, and Isandro understood that the sound had been heard by everyone in the queue, and that Hobb had counted on that too.

At the door, Isandro paused. Habit demanded a parting pleasantry; instinct screamed against giving him one more syllable of voice to file away. Habit won, barely.

"Cold enough to freeze the river solid by Christmas," Isandro said.

"Mm," said Hobb, already writing in his private notebook. "Or it might thaw early. Weather's a funny thing. Full of surprises." He didn't look up. "Mind the ice, driver."

*

The trap, Isandro thought, driving home with the empty pocket where the letters had been, was not being sprung. It was already sprung. It had been sprung quietly, months ago, and what had changed today was only that Isandro had finally heard the click.

Because consider it plainly, the way one considers a locked room from inside it: the bridge was shut. The ferry was suspended. The last train south had run in October and would not run again until the thaw, and the thaw, everyone said, was late coming this year. Which meant that every letter Mrs. Marlowe posted from here to the end of winter passed across Hobb's counter, under Hobb's spectacles, into Hobb's notebook. Which meant the network — Mrs. Aldous, Miss Crane, Widow Prewitt, all the patient correspondents stitching their evidence together town by town — was now reachable only through the one pair of eyes that should never see it.

And Corven knew it. Of course he knew it. Men like Corven did not fight wars; they arranged weather. An early freeze, a suspended ferry, a posted notice — and suddenly the magistrate did not need to burn any files or silence any widows. He needed only to wait. Winter would do the sieging for him. The town would seal itself like a jar, and everything inside it would keep until spring, and in spring there would be peace treaties and victors' histories and amnesties, and the grain figures and the pension skims would dissolve into the general fog of what everyone had suffered and nobody had caused.

Unless something crossed the ice that wasn't supposed to.

Isandro parked the car in the livery, rubbed it down, fed and watered the horses though the motor had made half of them ornaments, and climbed to the loft, and sat on the edge of the cot in the blue dusk with the day turning over and over like a page that wouldn't lie flat.

There was a way, of course. There was always a way; that was the curse of a mind like Isandro's, which could not stop finding exits in rooms that other people hadn't yet noticed were rooms. The ford below the weir, where the water ran fast and never froze true. The quarry road east, two days' walk to a junction with a main line. The letters themselves, copied, hidden, carried in a coat lining. Any of these could be done. All of these could be done.

But doing them meant becoming someone who did them. It meant stepping off the pavement of invisibility onto open ground, where footprints showed, and Hobb was already crouched at the edge of that ground with his notebook open, counting footprints, waiting for one to appear that had no name attached.

Because that was the other thing Isandro had understood today, standing at Hobb's window while the stamps fell. Hobb hadn't been asking about the letters at all. The letters were scenery. What Hobb had been weighing, gently, on his little brass scale, was Isandro. *I don't recall you on any register.* Two years in the town and no record, no surname anyone had ever needed, no birth certificate, no vote, no legal existence whatsoever — a person made entirely of useful habits, employable precisely because untraceable. Mrs. Marlowe trusted a driver who couldn't read. Corven feared a witness who didn't exist. Both of them, in their different ways, had built their arrangements on the same convenient absence, and Isandro had lived comfortably inside that absence for thirty-one years the way one lives inside a coat.

But coats, Isandro thought, pulling the blanket up as the cold came down through the roof slates, can be searched.

Below, the river lay silent under its new glass, holding the whole town's reflections perfectly still, the way a held breath holds a secret — badly, briefly, and only until something breaks the surface.
