## 5. A Driver's Place

Aldous Corven believed in the discipline of small observations. A man who watched only grand things — elections, wars, the price of wheat on the exchange — missed the grain of truth that actually moved the world. It was in the small things. The way a clerk licked his thumb before turning a ledger page when he was nervous and didn't when he wasn't. The way a widow's grief could be measured in the number of black buttons she wore, and how the number always dropped by spring. The way a man's hands betrayed him before his mouth ever learned to lie.

Corven had built a career — and a considerable private fortune — on such grains.

So it was with some professional irritation that he found himself unable to classify the Marlowe woman's driver.

The fellow had been at the magistrate's office three times that November: once to deliver a parcel of legal documents from Thistlewaite's solicitor, once to collect a certified copy of a land transfer, and once — this last one still bothered him — simply waiting outside in the cold while Edith Marlowe spent forty minutes inside on some matter of parish relief records. Forty minutes. In weather that would crack a teacup. And when Corven had passed him in the corridor, the driver had inclined his head exactly as far as courtesy required, no more, no less.

That was the first grain that wouldn't sit right.

Servants performed deference. That was what servants *did* — they made a small theater of their smallness, a bobbing and scraping that said, *I know my place, I am not a threat.* Corven had watched deference for thirty years the way a jeweler watches stones, and he could grade it like coal. Most people overplayed it out of fear. Some underplayed it out of resentment, which was its own confession.

This one did neither. He gave you precisely the amount of respect the law required and not a hair more, as if deference were a tariff he'd calculated down to the farthing and intended to pay in full but never overpay. His eyes came up level when he spoke. Not insolent — never insolent — just *level*. Steady. Like a man looking across a table at an equal rather than up at a superior.

Corven had put it out of his mind the first time. The second time, he'd noted it. By the third time, he'd sent for the parish register.

* * *

"You'll take tea?" Corven asked.

They stood in the magistrate's private room behind the courtroom, where Corven conducted the business that didn't belong on any public docket. A fire burned low in the grate. On the desk, arranged with deliberate carelessness, lay a sheaf of papers Corven had assembled that morning: requisition abstracts, pension schedules, a copy of the bridge closure order. Bait, laid out with the artlessness of a man who had never been artless in his life.

Isandro Galloway — or whatever name the fellow truly answered to; Corven suspected even that was provisional — stood with his cap in both hands and looked at the papers without appearing to look at them.

"No thank you, sir."

"Sit anyway. I dislike conducting conversation with a man hovering."

Galloway sat. Perched on the edge of the chair, back straight, cap on his knees. Corven poured two cups himself, which was a performance in its own right — magistrates did not pour tea for drivers — and pushed one across the desk. The driver took it, murmured thanks, and drank nothing. Just held it. Warmth in the palms. Sensible. Also the behavior of a man conserving his attention for something other than tea.

"I'll be brief," Corven said. "I find myself short-staffed since young Petch went into uniform, and the winter roads being what they are, I've need of a reliable man to carry documents between here and Harrowgate. Court business. Confidential, naturally." He smiled the smile he used on widows applying for relief — warm, regretful, immovable. "Mrs. Marlowe tells me you're steady. I thought perhaps you might divide your time."

"That's kind of you, sir. But Madam Marlowe has me engaged through the winter."

"Engagements can be adjusted."

"Her engagements come first, sir. She pays me regular and I give her regular. That's the arrangement."

Corven studied him over the rim of his cup. No fear in the face. No greed, either, which was rarer and more useful to know about. When Corven offered men things, they generally did one of three things: grabbed, flinched, or calculated. This one simply... declined. As though the offer were weather. As though refusing a magistrate were something a man could do before breakfast without indigestion.

"May I ask," Corven said pleasantly, "where you were engaged before?"

"Harrowgate, sir. Livery work. Before that, various."

"Various." Corven let the word sit. "You have family in the district?"

"No, sir."

"Parents living?"

"No, sir."

"The workhouse, then? Harrowgate took in a good many during the flood year."

"I was raised about, sir. Here and there."

*About. Here and there.* Corven set down his cup and opened the folder he'd had prepared — the parish register copy, the poor law union rolls, the school admission books going back twenty years. He did not shove them forward dramatically; he merely rested his hand on them, letting the driver understand what they were.

"I make it a practice," Corven said, "to know the people who move through this town. Names, histories, references. It protects everyone. You'd be surprised how much trouble a magistrate can prevent simply by knowing who a man is." He paused. "And yet I find no record of you anywhere. No baptism. No school entry. No workhouse intake. Your letters of reference —" he lifted one, the livery master's note, all four lines of it — "are thin as winter ice. Isandro Galloway. A Christian name and a borrowed surname. Do you know what we call that, in my trade?"

"A convenient arrangement, sir."

Corven laughed — genuinely, briefly. The answer had wit in it, and nerve.

"What we call it," Corven continued, "is a man without standing. A man who cannot testify in court because no court can establish who he is. A man who cannot vote, cannot hold property, cannot sign anything binding — because legally speaking, there is nothing to bind." He leaned back. "You exist, Mr. Galloway, exactly as much as the paperwork says you exist. Which is to say: hardly at all. Now, most men in your position would find that frightening."

"And you find it useful, sir."

There it was again. The level eyes. Corven felt something move in his chest that he recognized, after a moment's search, as interest — the collector's itch, the sensation of finding a coin that doesn't match any catalogued mint. Men like this were rare. Men without records were shadows, and shadows could be made to do anything, given the right pressure — but only if they feared the light. This one didn't seem to fear the light. Which meant either he was a fool, or he had already decided the light couldn't hurt him.

Fools didn't hold their teacups like that.

"I find everything useful," Corven said mildly. "It's a failing of mine. Tell me — does Mrs. Marlowe know about your situation? Your, ah, documentary situation?"

"She knows I drive well and keep quiet. That's what she hired."

"Keeping quiet is a profession, Mr. Galloway. Most men practice it badly." Corven turned a paper on his desk a quarter-inch, aligning it with the blotter edge. "I wonder if you've considered what happens to men with no records, should questions arise. Questions of loyalty, say. Wartime questions. There's a tribunal in Harrowgate now dealing with deserters and draft-dodgers and foreign elements. A man with no papers, no history, no one to vouch —" he spread his hands, regretful — "well. Such a man could be *made* to be anything. An deserter. A spy. The tribunal isn't fussy. They need convictions the way a stove needs coal."

The driver looked at him then — fully, directly, and Corven saw something flicker behind the steadiness, quick as a fish under ice. Fear? No. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a mechanism described that he'd already deduced.

"I keep clear of trouble, sir."

"I'm sure you do. Trouble keeps clearer of some than others, of course. It's a question of whose attention you attract." Corven rose, signaling the interview's end, and came around the desk. Close enough now to see that the driver's eyes were grey-green, unremarkable, and utterly unrevealing. "I'll make the same offer again in the new year. Think on it. A man in my employ has standing. Papers. A history, if he wants one — I can arrange baptisms, references, the whole biography, as easily as ordering boots. I can make you *real*, Galloway. Few men can offer that."

He extended his hand. The driver took it — firm, brief, correct — and Corven felt the calluses of rein-work and the absence of any tremor whatsoever.

"Thank you for the tea, sir."

At the door, the driver paused. Half-turned. And said, without any change of expression at all:

"There's a page missing from the November requisition abstract, sir. The grain figures. Folio forty-one. Thought you'd want to know, in case anyone asks you about it."

Then he was gone, cap in hand, boots quiet on the corridor flags, leaving Corven standing alone with his baited papers and his cooling tea.

For a long moment the magistrate did not move. Then he crossed to the desk, thumbed rapidly through the sheaf — folio thirty-nine, forty, forty-two — and found the gap where forty-one should have been. He had assembled those papers himself, that very morning, from the locked cabinet in Hobb's office. Every folio present. He was certain of it.

Almost certain.

Corven sat down slowly and poured himself a fresh cup of tea, though he no longer wanted it. Outside, the wind drove fine snow against the window in long white sighs, and somewhere below, the river ice groaned like a ship settling.

*A man without standing,* he had told the driver. *Legally speaking, nothing to bind.*

He had said it as a threat. He realized now — with the particular cold clarity that had served him thirty years and had never once arrived too late until tonight — that the driver might have heard it as something else entirely.

As a description of power.

As instructions.

* * *

That evening Corven sent for Clerk Hobb.

Hobb came in damp from the walk, spectacles fogged, clutching his notebook against his chest like a hymnal. A faithful creature, Hobb. Timid, methodical, devoted to procedure the way other men were devoted to God. Corven had chosen him years ago precisely because a timid man's silence could be purchased with small kindnesses and maintained with smaller fears.

"Folio forty-one," Corven said. "November abstracts. Grain figures."

Hobb's throat worked. "Sir?"

"The requisition ledger. The cabinet was accessed recently — the lock has been picked or the key copied, I haven't determined which. I want to know when, and by whom, and what was taken. Or copied." He watched Hobb's face with the patience of a man who had watched ten thousand faces. "You keep the keys, don't you, Hobb?"

"Yes, sir. Always on me, sir."

"Then someone copied them. Or you lent them." Corven smiled gently. "Or you used them yourself. One of those three things happened, and you're going to tell me which."

"I never — sir, I would never —"

"Of course not. Of course." Corven poured a measure of whisky and slid it across the desk, watching Hobb's trembling hand close around it. "Drink that. Then think carefully, because here is what will happen otherwise. Tomorrow morning I telegraph Harrowgate and have a tribunal examiner appointed to audit this office — every ledger, every folio, every figure going back to 1914. And when the audit finds irregularities, Hobb — and it will, because I will *make* it find them — the clerk who kept the keys will hang beside the man who signed the orders. Tribunals aren't fussy, remember. They need convictions the way a stove needs coal. I told someone that today, as it happens. Perhaps you'd like to hear the rest of the conversation."

Hobb stared at him. The whisky sat untouched in his shaking fist.

"Now," Corven said, leaning back, folding his hands over his waistcoat, "you will go home, and tomorrow you will bring me a complete accounting: who entered this office besides yourself in the past month, who has handled the November abstracts, and whether Mrs. Edith Marlowe's driver — a man calling himself Galloway, no record, no history, nobody's son — has been seen near these premises at any hour when the building ought to have been empty."

Something crossed Hobb's face then, so quickly and so deeply buried that a less patient man would have missed it. Not guilt. Not quite. Something older and sadder — the look of a man who has just been shown the trap he has lived inside for years and told, courteously, that he may continue to live in it.

"Very good, sir," Hobb said.

He left. Corven listened to his footsteps diminish down the corridor — hesitant, uneven, the footsteps of a man carrying something heavier than a notebook — and Corven allowed himself, at last, the luxury of full calculation.

The driver had known about folio forty-one. Had known it was missing — or had made it missing — and had chosen to *tell* him. That was the part Corven kept turning over, the stone that wouldn't sit flat in the wall. A thief conceals. A spy conceals. Only one sort of man announces a theft: a man who wants the theft discovered. Who wants the loss investigated. Who wants the magistrate's own machinery — audits, tribunals, examinations — pointed inward at the very ledgers that must never be examined.

The boy wasn't threatening him. The boy was *baiting* him.

Corven rose and went to the window. Below, the town lay blue-white under the moon, chimneys smoking, streets emptying, the river a black seam stitched shut with ice. Somewhere in that grid of lamplit windows, Edith Marlowe was writing letters — he had no proof, only the certainty of thirty years' instinct, the certainty that had never yet failed him and that he suddenly, uneasily, understood might be aimed at *him*.

He had offered the driver a name. Papers. Standing. Everything a shadow could want.

And the driver had answered with a warning shot across his desk — courteous, precise, deniable — that said: *I know where your records are thin. I know what your pages hide. And I am telling you that I read.*

Corven pressed his palm flat against the cold glass.

"Nobody's son," he said quietly to the darkened street. "We shall see whose son you turn out to be, Mr. Galloway. We shall see whose son you are."

Behind him, the fire settled. In the locked cabinet across town, folio forty-one was either in its place or it was not, and either answer was worse than the other, and the winter had only begun to close its fist around the town.

Tomorrow he would set Hobb to watch the post office. Tonight, he would begin a file of his own.

On the driver. On the woman. On every letter that left Marlowe House bound for anywhere at all.

Let them write, Corven thought, pulling the curtain against the snow. Let them write everything down.

Paper burns beautifully in winter.
