## 6. The Unsent Drawer

The letters Edith Marlowe did not send lived in the bottom drawer of her writing desk, beneath a false bottom of cedar slats her father had built before the war that mattered, back when drawers only needed to hide embroidery and a woman's private debts.

There were forty-one of them now. She counted on the first Tuesday of every month, by candlelight, after Isandro had taken the outgoing post down to the office and she could hear the motor coughing away toward the bridge road. Forty-one letters, each one sealed, addressed, stamped — and never mailed. They were addressed to people who would never receive them: her sister most of all, and then, in careful rotation, the others. A sister is only the first name you learn to write to. The rest are practice.

Tonight she counted thirty-nine, then stopped, because she was lying to herself and counting was how she caught it. There were forty-one. There had always been forty-one since October. She made herself say the number plainly, alone in the cold study with her coat still on: *Forty-one.* The first lie of grief is arithmetic. You miscount on purpose so the loss stays slightly wrong, slightly open, like a door left unlatched for someone who isn't coming home.

She had been doing it for eleven years.

* * *

Her sister's name had been Verity, which their mother had chosen because it was a virtue and therefore safe to say out loud in church, and which had turned out, in the end, to be the cruelest thing about her — because Verity told the truth once, in open court, in front of Magistrate Corven, and it cost her everything there was left to cost.

That had been 1907. Edith had been twenty-six and already dangerous, though she didn't know the word for what she was yet. She'd been organizing mill girls in the next county over, teaching them arithmetic under cover of hymn practice, and Verity — younger, braver in the way of people who have not yet learned that courage is billed monthly — had gone to the magistrate's court to swear an affidavit against a foreman who had docked wages from women who couldn't read their own pay slips. It was supposed to be simple. Sworn testimony, a hearing, a fine levied, a lesson taught gently enough that no one would be ruined.

Corven was not yet Sir anything then. He was a rising man with a lawyer's hands and a magistrate's patience, and he listened to Verity's affidavit the way a man listens to water finding a crack in his cellar wall. Then he asked her, courteously, whether she could produce the pay slips themselves as evidence, and when she said they had been destroyed — burned, the foreman said, as waste paper — he looked at her with something almost like tenderness and explained, in language any schoolmistress would applaud, that testimony without evidence was not testimony at all but merely opinion, and that the court could not act on opinion, especially opinion offered by a young woman evidently agitated beyond her understanding.

He fined her five shillings for wasting the court's time. He recommended — again courteously, again with that terrible gentleness — that she be examined by a physician, since a woman who made such claims without documents might be suffering from nerves, or worse, from ideas.

The foreman kept his position. Two of the mill girls lost theirs anyway, quietly, within the month. And Verity went home and sat in the kitchen and told Edith, very calmly, that the world was held together with paper, and that whoever held the paper held everything, and that she intended to learn exactly how it was held.

She didn't live long enough to finish learning. Pneumonia, the winter after, the kind that takes the poor and the furious first because they burn hotter. But she left behind a notebook — a cheap one, penny-ruled, its cover soft from handling — in which she had written out the whole architecture of Corven's little kingdom as she understood it: who owed him, who feared him, which clerk took which bribe, which requisition forms went into his office and came out heavier. She had gotten perhaps a tenth of it right. Edith had spent eleven years correcting the other ninety percent.

* * *

The method was Verity's, really. That was the joke Edith never told anyone — that the great Marlowe correspondence network, the one certain gentlemen in this county spoke of with such careful contempt, *the suffragist and her pen-pals*, was built on a dead girl's penny notebook. Verity had understood that you cannot fight paper with fire; fire leaves ash, and ash can be denied. You fight paper with paper. You write it all down, and you write it to someone, because a letter has an address, and an address means a witness, and witnesses multiply.

So Edith wrote. Every week, sometimes twice, to women in four counties: a schoolteacher in Ashford who owed Edith her position and her silence; a widow in Harrowgate whose son's pension arrived eleven shillings light every quarter, regular as church; a printer's wife who could set type faster than any man in the district and had opinions about margins. None of them received plain letters. They received letters full of weather, and garden talk, and inquiries after health, and threaded through them, in phrasings agreed upon years ago, the actual cargo.

*My hydrangeas have failed again this year — I believe the soil itself is poor where they stand,* meant: the requisition figures are falsified at the source. *Mrs. Pemberton's boy writes that his allowance arrives short, though his uncle swears the sum was sent entire,* meant: pension disbursements skimmed at the county office. *I hear Mr. Corven's new barn roof cost a pretty sum; one wonders who supplied the slate at such a friendly price,* meant: follow the grain contracts.

And every week Isandro Galloway carried those letters down through the town, through weather, past the courthouse windows, past the post office where Corven's clerk Hobb sorted the mail with his long careful fingers, and delivered them to the network, and brought back replies, and never — Edith believed this with the settled faith other people reserved for scripture — never read a word of them.

Why she believed it: because Isandro was an orphan with no surname of record, hired out of the livery stable six years ago for seven shillings a week and a room over the coach house, and because in six years Isandro had never asked a question, never lingered, never shown the smallest curiosity about the cargo. Because the letters were sealed and Isandro was, by all appearance, exactly what the town believed: the quiet driver, the one who kept the motor running and their eyes on the road. Because Edith had decided — and here was the vanity in it, the flaw she could examine and re-examine and still keep — that a person with no history would want none of hers either. That invisibility was a temperament, not a discipline.

She knew better, of course. She had spent her life among women who had been told they couldn't understand things, and she had watched every one of them understand perfectly. But there was a difference between knowing a thing generally and applying it to the specific young person who drove your motor and stoked your furnace and asked for nothing. Affection makes fools of careful women. She had trusted Isandro the way you trust a lock you've never tested.

Tonight she decided to test it.

* * *

It was the clerk who prompted her. Hobb had begun watching the house — she'd seen him twice from the upstairs window, standing across the lane in the grey afternoon, ostensibly examining the drain grate, a man who had never in his life examined a drain grate. And the bridge had closed early, weeks ahead of schedule, which meant the season's last letters had gone out on the final ferry crossing and now everything moved through Hobb's hands at the post office or not at all. The net was drawing in. She could feel it the way farmers feel frost: not seeing it, just knowing the air had changed weight.

Which meant it was time to know, precisely, what lived inside the head of the person who slept twelve feet below her study floor.

Isandro came up at seven to bank the study fire, as always. Edith sat at the desk with a letter half-written, and did not look up, and let the silence do its work. Isandro fed the stove, set the poker down with that particular economy of motion that Edith had catalogued over six years — everything done once, nothing wasted, a person who had learned early that being noticed costs money — and turned to go.

"Stay a moment," Edith said. "I've a letter I can't finish, and you've a good ear. Read me the last paragraph aloud while I think."

It was a small thing to ask. A natural thing. Employers asked such things constantly of literate employees, and Edith watched — not staring, watching the way she'd learned to watch, peripherally, the way you watch weather — as Isandro crossed the room, took the page, and held it.

For three full seconds, nothing happened. Then Isandro smoothed the page against the desk edge, tilted it toward the lamplight, and began to read in a flat, careful voice, stumbling once on "correspondence," and Edith felt something in her chest go loose with relief and tight with shame in the same instant, because the paragraph was harmless — pure garden talk, hydrangeas and soil — and because she had just watched a person perform illiteracy the way an actress performs a limp: competently, but with a hesitation exactly one beat too studied.

Or she was imagining it. Six years of trust, and one winter of fear, and a woman alone will begin to see spies in the coal scuttle. She honestly could not tell which it was. That was the worst of it — not certainty, but this suspended state, this weighing of a person she had come to regard as, if not a friend, then the nearest thing her circumstances allowed.

So she gave it more rope.

"The soil analysis," she said, taking the page back. "You'd have no notion, but there's a man at the agricultural station who tests samples. Mrs. Quill in Ashford sent hers in September — did I mention? — and they found the nitrogen entirely depleted. Not poor soil, you understand. Stripped." She said it lightly, looking at the letter, watching the mirror on the far wall instead of the person beside the desk. In the mirror she could see Isandro's face in profile, and it was perfectly composed, and that was the trouble with composed faces; they told you nothing except that composition was occurring.

In the letter she had actually posted to Mrs. Quill — via Isandro's own hands, three weeks ago — she had written *nitrogen depleted*. That much was true.

What she said now was: "Stripped of lime, of course. It's always lime in this valley."

It was not always lime. It was a lie, a small deliberate one, a canary in a mine shaft. If Isandro had read the letter, the error would snag somewhere behind those steady eyes, the way a wrong note snags even in listeners who claim not to know music. People who have memorized a document cannot help flinching at its corruption. She had used the trick twice before in her life, on a servant who was selling her household lists to a rival society lady, and both times it had worked within the hour — the guilty mind reaches to correct what it knows to be wrong.

Isandro said, "I wouldn't know about lime, madam," and gathered the fire irons, and wished her good night in a voice as level as a frozen pond, and went down the stairs.

Edith sat alone with the lamp and the unfinished letter and the drawer beneath her.

* * *

Afterward she couldn't sleep. She got up past midnight and knelt at the desk and slid the false bottom out, and took the letters out one by one, and read them — not the ones she'd sent, but the unsent ones, the ones addressed to Verity at an address that had been a grave for eleven years.

*Dear V. The boy at the ferry says the ice is forming early. I have counted my papers twice and they are all accounted for.*

*Dear V. He has bought slate again, at a friendly price. I have the number of the wagon now. I am keeping the number.*

*Dear V. Sometimes I think you were the only honest witness this county ever produced, and they fined you five shillings for it. I am building the fine back with interest. You would laugh at how slow the work goes. Eleven years, and still I write to you as though the case were tomorrow.*

She read until the candle guttered, and then she sat with the last unsent letter, the one she wrote every year on the anniversary, and thought about the driver downstairs, asleep or awake, innocent or the best actor she had ever encountered, and about the question underneath the question, the one she had not dared ask directly in six years because the answer would change the shape of her whole house:

*Do you read my letters?*

And beneath that, older and colder: *If you do — and you have said nothing, all this time, while the net drew tighter around us both — then what else have you seen that you have never mentioned? What else do you know that I don't?*

Because here was the thing she had not admitted to anyone, not even the drawer: some part of her had always hoped. Some part of her had watched Isandro carry those letters through the town like a nun carrying relics, sealed and untouched, and had thought, with a loneliness she was ashamed of: *what if someone were reading them? What if every word I wrote to save this county had been received, truly received, by at least one soul besides the ones who needed convincing?* A letter unread is a prayer spoken into a well. She had been speaking into wells for eleven years.

She put the letters back. She slid the cedar bottom home. And as she pinched out the candle, she heard — or thought she heard — a floorboard shift in the room below, the small settling weight of a person turning over in bed, awake at the same hour she was, thinking whatever Isandro Galloway thought about at midnight in this house full of secrets that were not theirs.

Two watchers, she thought. One house. Tomorrow she would test again, and harder, and if the canary died — if the lie went uncorrected a second time — then she would know that she had been sharing her life's work, unknowing, with the one person positioned to destroy it.

Or to finish it.

She lay in the dark and could not decide which possibility frightened her more.
