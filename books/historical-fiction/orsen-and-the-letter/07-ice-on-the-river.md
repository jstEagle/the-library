## 7. Ice on the River

The cold came for the river first.

I noticed it on my morning rounds, driving Edith's letters to the post office in the blue hour before the town woke — the water moving slower than it had a week ago, the current dragging at its edges like an old man pulling blankets up to his chin. Ice feathered out from the banks in white lace. By Thursday it had reached the pilings of the ferry landing. By Friday, Ferryman Aldous stood on his deck with his arms crossed and told anyone who asked that he'd not be crossing again until the thaw, and God help whoever disagreed with him.

That was how a town sealed itself. Not with walls or gates. With weather.

I want to say I understood what that meant for me, that week. I didn't. I understood only that the letters kept coming, and so did I.

* * *

The boy's name was Tam Weller, though I wouldn't learn that until after. He was eleven or twelve, one of the postmaster's runner boys who carried telegrams through the streets for pennies, all elbows and chapped ears and the particular hunger of children who'd grown tall faster than they'd grown fed. The war had thinned them out — half the runners were girls now, and the ones who remained wore their brothers' coats like borrowed authority.

He was on the river path the morning of the accident because everyone was on the river path that morning. Word had gone round that a mail packet had come as far as the south bank before the ferry stopped running, and Aldous had rigged a line across the narrows to haul the last of it over by basket. Half the town stood along the rail watching the basket swing over the black water, and there is no sight more irresistible to a crowd than something arriving from elsewhere.

I was there because Edith had a letter in that packet. She always had a letter in every packet. She'd given it to me with her usual instruction — *the usual care, Mr. Galloway* — which meant: watch it, guard it, ask nothing. And I gave her my usual answer, which meant: I will do both, and you'll never know why the second part costs me.

The basket made three trips. Coats, parcels, a crate of medicine from the county dispensary. On the fourth trip, the wind took it.

It wasn't dramatic. That's what I remember most — how undramatic it was. A gust came down the valley, the line shuddered, and a single envelope slipped from the top of the basket load and went into the river between one heartbeat and the next. White paper on black water. It floated for perhaps four seconds.

Tam Weller went over the rail before anyone could shout.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching a child fall through ice: time does not slow. Time simply stops pretending to be anything other than what it is, which is a series of decisions made too fast to be called decisions.

The ice near the bank held him for two strides. Then it didn't.

The sound it made — I have heard rifle fire described as a whip-crack, and this was nothing like that. It was softer. A sound like a door closing in another room. And then the boy was in the water up to his chest, gasping, clawing at the edge of the hole, and the letter he'd been reaching for was still floating six feet beyond him, riding the current toward the narrows where the water ran fast and deep and would take both of them under the shelf ice and out the far side, if it took them at all.

The crowd screamed. That is nearly all a crowd can do. Aldous was shouting for rope; someone was shouting for the constable; Mrs. Petch, the baker's widow, was shouting the boy's name — *Tam, Tam, hold on, Tam* — in a voice that told me she knew his mother, and probably his mother's mother.

I was already moving.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the rail under my hands, and then the ice under my boots, and the specific, obscene lightness of frozen river surface bearing a man's weight for exactly as long as it chose to. I dropped flat when it began to go — some instinct from a childhood spent on thin ice behind the workhouse, though I hadn't thought of that childhood in twenty years — and crawled, distributing myself across the surface the way you distribute yourself across a lie: carefully, everywhere at once, committing to nothing.

The boy had stopped struggling, which is the worst sign. They tell you drowning men fight; what they don't tell you is that after the fighting comes the quiet, and the quiet is the water winning. His mittened hands were on the ice lip and his face was gray and his eyes had gone somewhere else entirely.

"Look at me." My voice came out lower than I intended, steadier than I felt. "Not the water. Me."

His eyes found mine. Eleven years old, and trusting a stranger on a frozen river because there was no one else.

"Belt," I said, without turning my head. "Somebody give me a belt or a rope or a rein, now."

A strap hit the ice beside me — someone's coat belt, thrown the way you throw things when your hands are shaking. I looped it twice around my wrist, slid forward until the ice groaned beneath me, and took the boy's mitten in my fist.

The ice let us both go.

For one long moment we were in the river together, and the cold was not cold but a kind of violence, a full-body seizure, the water pulling at my coat like hands. But the belt held. The belt and whatever arm was hauling it — Aldous himself, I learned later, the old ferryman lying flat on the bank with the strap wrapped around his shoulders, cursing steadily in a language I suspect he invented. Between his pull and my push we got the boy onto solid ice, and then I got myself out, and then I lay on the frozen river with the breath knocked out of me and the sky wheeling overhead, and the first thing I saw clearly, when the world reassembled itself, was the letter.

It had caught against a snag of reeds at the river's edge. Sodden, half-sunk, but there.

I got it before anyone else did. I tell myself that was instinct. It was also, I knew even then, something older than instinct.

* * *

They carried Tam Weller up the bank wrapped in half the coats of the assembled town, and he was crying and coughing and alive, and Mrs. Petch had him by the collar as if she meant to shake the river out of him personally. His mother arrived at a dead run with her apron still on, and the sight of her son upright undid her so completely that she sat down in the snow and laughed and sobbed in equal measure while the boy protested, mortified, that he was fine, he was *fine*, Mother, honestly.

And then the crowd turned to me.

I want to describe this accurately, because it matters. For six years I had driven through this town as a function — the Marlowe driver, the quiet one, the one whose name nobody used because nobody needed to. People nodded at drivers the way they nod at lampposts. I had counted on that. I had built my whole life inside that nod.

Now forty people were looking at me as if I were a person, and I found I did not know where to put my face.

"That's Marlowe's driver," someone said, wonderingly, as if identifying a species previously unrecorded.

"He went straight out on the ice," said another. "Didn't hesitate. Straight out."

"The letter too." This from the postmaster's clerk, Hobb — Corven's Hobb, the one with the close-set eyes who watched me too carefully at the post office counter. He was looking at the sodden envelope in my hand with an expression I couldn't read, and didn't like. "Went back for the mail as well. Very thorough."

There was nothing in the words. There was everything in the tone.

"It's addressed to Madam Marlowe," I said, level. "It's my job to see her post delivered dry if I can manage it. Today I couldn't quite manage it." I held the envelope up so the address showed, and let the crowd see me being exactly what I appeared to be: a driver, doing his duty, damp and unremarkable.

Hobb smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Duty," he said. "Yes. I expect it is."

* * *

Edith met me at the door of Marlowe House before I'd finished climbing down from the cart. She had heard, of course — news travels through a sealed town faster than through an open one, because there is less of the town to travel through and more time to talk.

"You went onto the ice." She said it the way she said most things: as a fact requiring explanation, not praise.

"The boy required it."

"And the letter?"

I handed it to her. Water-stained, the ink at the corner bloomed soft, but legible. Her eyes went over the address once, then lifted to mine, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw her look at me the way Hobb had looked at me. As a question rather than a fixture.

Then it passed, and she was only Edith Marlowe again, brisk and armored. "You'll catch your death. There's a fire in the kitchen and Mrs. Quill has soup whether you want it or not. Go."

I went. In the kitchen, drying my boots by the stove, I finally allowed myself to open the letter's memory — because of course I had already read it, days ago, in the steam over the kettle, before resealing it and carrying it as though it were only paper. It was a short one. A list of figures. Grain tonnage, requisitioned at fixed prices, sold onward at others. A pension roll with names missing from it. The numbers had a shape I now recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd you've been avoiding.

And folded into the figures, one sentence in Edith's careful coded plainness: *The frost closes our roads, but I am told spring opens all of them.*

Spring. The bridge. The network beyond. Everything hinged on getting past this winter, past Corven, past the closed river — and today the whole town had watched me crawl across the ice to keep one page of it dry.

I sat by the stove and felt something I couldn't immediately name. It took me until the soup was cold to name it, and when I did, it frightened me more than the river had.

It wasn't fear of being seen. It was the terrible, unfamiliar warmth of it.

* * *

By evening the story had grown its customary third arm. I had gone out on the ice *without hesitation*, people said, though I remembered hesitation well enough. I had pulled the boy out *with one hand*, though both of mine had shaken for an hour after. Mrs. Petch sent a loaf to Marlowe House with my compliments requested. Tam Weller's mother sent a knitted scarf, army-gray and lopsided, with a note in pencil: *You saved my boy. We don't forget.*

We don't forget. As if I were someone one could forget or remember. As if I existed.

I hung the scarf on the peg by my cot in the carriage house and told myself it meant nothing. Then I sat on the edge of the cot for a long time, listening to the wind worry the eaves, and understood that something had cracked — not in the ice, in me. Six years of invisibility, and one morning on a frozen river had put a hairline fracture through the whole structure, and I could feel it spreading.

Because here was the thing I could not stop thinking: Hobb had seen me reach for the letter. Hobb had weighed it. And Hobb reported to Magistrate Corven, who had begun, lately, to look at me a moment too long at the market, to ask the ostler casual questions about the Marlowe household, to be courteous in the particular way of men preparing a trap.

A driver who reads. A driver who dives for documents. If Corven ever suspected what I was, invisibility would not protect me — it would condemn me. A man with no record, no surname of note, no kin to vouch for him, can disappear very easily in a small town in winter. I had always known that. I had built my life on knowing it.

But tonight, for the first time, another thought arrived alongside it, unwelcome and warm:

*A man with no record,* the thought said, *has nothing to lose by writing one.*

I lay down. I did not sleep. Somewhere across the dark town, in a house with good locks and bad intentions, a clerk was probably writing my name in a ledger — *Galloway, driver to Marlowe, rescued the Weller boy, recovered the mail* — and for the first time in my life, I wondered what it would mean to write it there myself.

In the morning, there was a knock at the carriage house door before dawn.

Magistrate Corven stood on the step, hat in hand, smiling his courteous smile, with the cold behind him and the whole sealed town at his back.

"Mr. Galloway," he said. "Might I have a word about your remarkable morning?"
