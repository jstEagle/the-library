## 8. The Clerk's Ledger

"Mr. Galloway," he said again, when I didn't answer fast enough to suit him. "You saved a boy yesterday. The town is talking."

I came down the carriage house steps and stood where the lamplight wouldn't quite reach me. Old habit. "The boy fell through the ice. I pulled him out. There isn't more to tell."

"There is always more to tell." Corven turned his hat in his hands, a slow, thoughtful rotation, like a man shuffling cards he already knew the order of. "That's what people forget. Every act has its ledger entry. A rescue. A letter recovered. A name attached to both." He smiled. "Yours is an interesting case, you know. No birth record in this county. No parish register that claims you. A man who appears out of nowhere at twenty and drives for Madam Marlowe as if he'd always been there." He let the silence do some work for him. "Where were you before, I wonder?"

"On the road," I said.

"Yes. That's what the road people say." He put his hat on. "Well. It's nothing to me. A man without papers can't be prosecuted, can't be conscripted, can't be sued. In a way you're the freest creature in this town." He said it pleasantly, the way a man might praise a horse's teeth while checking them. "But freedom is fragile, Mr. Galloway. Ice breaks. Bridges close. And when spring comes, every account gets settled. Do drive carefully."

He wished me good morning and went up the lane toward the courthouse, his breath trailing behind him like smoke off a doused fire.

I stood in the cold until my hands stopped shaking, and then I went inside and looked at the floorboards under my cot, where the notebook lay sewn into the lining of my spare coat, and understood that something had changed. For seven years I had been invisible by choice. Now I was visible by decision — his decision — and invisibility, once surrendered, does not come back.

The ledger was kept in the requisition office behind the courthouse. I knew this the way I knew most things about this town: by carrying other people's words past it, twice a week, for years. Clerk Hobb worked the daylight hours and locked the outer door at six. The inner door — the one to the records room — had a lock older than the war, older than Hobb, possibly older than Corven, and I had watched Hobb fight it every evening with a key that fit badly, jiggling it left, then right, swearing softly under his breath like a man arguing with a stubborn mule he couldn't afford to replace.

A lock like that doesn't want picking. It wants patience.

* * *

I waited three nights.

Not out of caution — or not only. Out of craft. On the first night there was a card game at the Ferry Inn that ran late, and Hobb was at it, losing, because Hobb always lost; I'd seen him pay out his coins with the resigned face of a man donating to a cause he didn't believe in. On the second night the moon was too high and too white over the square, and I made myself stay in the carriage house with my coat on and my boots laced, listening to the wind worry the loose shingle, telling myself that wanting to go and needing to go were different animals and only one of them got you killed.

On the third night it snowed.

Soft, dry, windless snow, the kind that comes down like flour through a sieve, filling footprints behind you as fast as you make them, muffling the town until your own breathing sounds loud. I told Edith I had an errand in Millbrook — which was true in a way, since everything now was errands toward Millbrook — and she looked at me across the lamp for one long second with those gray eyes that missed nothing and said, "Take the scarf, Isandro. It's colder than it looks."

She thought I couldn't read. She thought the letters passed through my hands as blank paper, sealed cargo, inert. Somewhere in her mind I was still the silent driver, the furniture with a heartbeat. And here I was going to break into the magistrate's office at midnight to steal, with my eyes, the proof she had spent three years trying to gather by post.

I took the scarf.

The square was empty and blue-white under the snow. The courthouse stood black against it, its windows blind, its clock above the door frozen at twenty past eleven — it had been frozen at twenty past eleven since before the war, and nobody repaired it because a town that could read time in its sleep had no use for a clock that lied. The requisition office was around the side, down the passage between the courthouse and the cooper's shed, a narrow throat of dark where the snow drifted knee-deep against the door.

Hobb had gone to bed. I had watched the light in his window above the chandler's shop go out two hours before, and I had counted, the way I count everything now, four hundred heartbeats after dark meant asleep, six hundred meant asleep and dreaming, eight hundred meant gone for the night.

The outer lock gave to the flat strip of iron I keep in my boot sole — it wasn't even locked properly, just dropped shut — and then the inner door, the old mule of a lock. I knelt in the dark and fed the strip in and listened. Old locks talk if you listen. This one talked about a ward worn smooth on the right side, about a spring tired of holding, about forty years of clerks who locked up at six and never once wondered whether the lock deserved their trust. It took four minutes. My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that. Outside, somewhere, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Then I was inside, and I closed the door behind me, and I did not strike a match.

There are two kinds of darkness. The kind in a closed room, which is total and friendly, like being unborn; and the kind outdoors, where shapes move and eyes might be. I stood in the first kind and breathed and let my eyes do their slow work. The window over the cooper's shed leaked a gray suggestion of light, enough to raise the room out of pure black into a world of grays: shelves, boxes, a tall cabinet with drawers, and on the desk by the window, exactly where a careful clerk would leave it for morning, the requisition ledger.

I had planned the next part for days, and it went mostly as planned, which frightened me more than failure would have. I took off my coat and folded it over the desk edge so the notebook was at hand. I took out the pencil stub and the scrap of tallow candle I'd brought, lit it, cupped my hand around the flame, and opened the ledger.

Grain. Page after page of grain, dated back through the harvest of '16, '17, and the autumn just gone. *Requisitioned from Hollis farm, twelve sacks. Requisitioned from the Penhallow acreage, nine sacks. Received at county store, value assessed per government schedule.* Government prices, written small and neat in Hobb's careful hand. And then, in the same hand, same ink, same dates, a second column — and this was the thing, this was the rotten heart of it, the thing Edith's letters had been circling for years like a bird over carrion it couldn't land on — a column headed *disbursed*, with figures two and three times the first. Grain taken from farmers at the fixed price and sold back to them, through middlemen whose names I memorized on sight, at famine prices, the difference flowing into accounts marked with initials instead of names. C.M. C.M. C.M. Sometimes R.T., whoever R.T. was, but mostly C.M., patient as frost, month after month, year after year, while men from these very farms froze in trenches outside Ypres and Amiens and their families back home paid Corven's markup for the bread made from their own wheat.

And pensions. Farther back in the book, interleaved with the grain, the pension rolls. *Widow Tarn, monthly disbursement.* *Widow Oakes.* *Mother of Pte. Weller, deceased.* Each entry carried a gross figure and a net figure, and the space between them had a heading too, small and official-looking: *administrative fee.* Two shillings here, half a crown there, skimmed off the grief of every widow in the parish, and the initials beside each deduction were the same. C.M.

I copied figures. I wrote names, dates, amounts, initials, the numbers of the pages themselves — because page numbers matter, page numbers prove a document existed whole, prove nothing was added later. My pencil moved in the dark in short strokes, and I memorized as I wrote, the way I had memorized seven years of other people's letters, line by line, because memory is the one archive no constable can search. *Hollis, twelve sacks, October '17, disbursed at 2.6 times schedule, C.M.* I said it over in my head like a prayer, moved down the page, said the next one. Terror and purpose, I found, feel almost identical in the body — the same quick blood, the same sharp hearing — and differ only in what you decide they mean.

The candle burned low. I pinched it out to save it and worked by touch and memory, leafing pages with wet fingertips.

Which is why I heard the key.

Not a sound in the street — a sound in the building. Above me, faintly, the outer door's hinge, the one I'd oiled with attention but not with oil, and then boots on boards, and then the scrape-and-argument of the inner lock beginning to turn.

Hobb.

My mind did something strange and fast. It did not panic; it calculated, cool as a bank clerk itself: the window over the cooper's shed was too narrow for a grown man and I'd known that coming in; the coal chute in the corner was barred; the front door was ten feet away and opening. There was nowhere to go. So the calculation skipped past escape entirely and arrived, without stopping anywhere in between, at: *then don't be caught doing anything.*

I folded the notebook into my shirt, dropped the coat over my arm, stepped behind the tall drawer cabinet, and became furniture.

The door opened. Lamplight swung in and laid long bars across the floor, and Hobb came in — I saw his shadow first, hunched, hatless, his collar up — and stopped dead.

He stood in the doorway a long time. Long enough for me to count his breaths, eight of them, shallow. He was looking at the room the way a man looks at a room he knows is empty but doesn't feel empty. The lamp in his hand trembled slightly, and the bars of light trembled with it, sliding across the desk, across the ledger lying open — I heard him notice that, a small intake of breath — because I had left it open, hadn't I, I'd been reading it when the key scraped, and a ledger doesn't open itself.

"Hello?" Hobb said, to the room, quietly, the way people speak when they hope the answer is no.

I stood behind the cabinet with my heart going like a telegraph key and my face arranged into nothing. Through the gap between cabinet and wall I could see him sideways: a thin man, younger than I'd thought, with ink stains on his fingers and his mother's hunger somewhere in his face — though I didn't know about his mother yet, that knowledge was weeks away, and all I knew now was that he looked less like Corven's creature than like Corven's hostage.

He crossed to the desk. He set the lamp down. He looked at the ledger, touched its edge, and frowned — and here was the moment, here was the hinge of my whole life swinging, because if he checked the pages, if he counted entries, if he was the kind of clerk who reconciled figures at midnight out of guilt or fear, then everything ended here in a coal-dark office and the story would belong to someone else.

He didn't check. He closed the ledger gently, squared it with the desk edge, and stood looking at it with an expression I filed away with everything else: not suspicion. Shame. He looked ashamed of the book, as if the book were a body he'd been keeping.

Then he raised the lamp and swept the room with it, slow, methodical, and the light came around toward the cabinet, toward the gap, toward my shoe —

— and the dog barked.

Out in the square, the same dog from earlier, suddenly and furiously, at nothing, at the snow, at God. Hobb spun toward the window, lamp held high, and the light jumped away from my shoe and splashed against the glass, and in that second I did the only thing left: I coughed. Once. Low. From the far side of the room, or near enough, near enough that direction was impossible to fix in echo and dark.

"Who's there?" His voice cracked upward. "I've a pistol!"

He didn't have a pistol. I had driven him to the gunsmith's in September to collect a repaired fowling piece, and I knew to the day how long ago it had gone back to its owner. But he believed himself armed, and belief straightened him, and he came around the cabinet with the lamp thrust out before him like a lantern at a séance —

and the room was empty. Because while he stared at the window I had done the last thing a hiding man does, the thing no hiding man dares: I had come out. Two steps, silent, and folded myself into the blackest corner behind the door itself, the one place a searcher never looks because a searcher looks *into* rooms, not at the edges of them, and certainly not at the door he entered through, which his mind has already classified as *safe.*

Hobb searched. Badly, quickly, fearfully — under the desk, behind the crates, his own breath sawing. Then he stopped in the middle of the floor, and I watched his shoulders drop, and heard him say, half aloud, to himself, to the ledger, to the shame in the room:

"I'm going mad. Sitting alone with these numbers till all hours. Hearing things." A pause. "It's the numbers. Any man would hear things."

He gathered the ledger under his arm — taking it with him, I noted, taking it home, or to Corven, either way it would be elsewhere tomorrow and I had gotten what there was to get — blew out his own nerve along with nothing else, picked up the lamp, and let himself out. The inner lock argued with his key. The outer door thumped. His boots faded toward the chandler's, and the snow began filling his prints, and I stood in the corner behind the door and discovered that my legs had opinions about standing.

I gave them a minute. Then another. Then I went out the way I'd come, into the falling quiet, and walked home through flour-sifted streets with the notebook against my ribs, warm as a second heart, and every figure in my head repeating itself in Hobb's neat hand: *C.M. C.M. C.M.*

* * *

At Marlowe House the kitchen embers were still alive. I sat by them without lighting anything and transcribed the memorized pages onto paper — not the notebook, plain paper, a fair copy, because a copy in fair handwriting can be read by anyone, and I was beginning to understand that someday soon it would need to be. Names. Dates. Sacks. Shillings. Widows' names with two shillings struck from their grief. Pte. Weller's mother, paying an administrative fee on her son's death for three years running.

When I finished, dawn was graying the windows and my hands finally shook — late, the way soldiers say it happens.

Edith came down before the fire was rebuilt, wrapped in a shawl, and stopped when she saw me sitting there among papers. She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion yet, not quite, but its cousin.

"You were out late," she said.

"The roads were bad."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman filing something away. She poured herself tea, and then, casually, terribly, she said: "I've had a letter from my cousin in Millbrook. She writes that there's talk in London of peace before Christmas. Real peace. An armistice within weeks, some say days."

The word landed in the kitchen like a coin dropped in a church.

"When peace comes," Edith went on, stirring tea she wouldn't drink, "there will be such a hunger for forgetting in this country. Men like Magistrate Corven are counting on it. Files burn very quickly in a victory winter, Mr. Galloway. Whatever anyone means to do — it must be done before the bells ring."

She looked at me over the rim of the cup, gray eyes steady, and I sat very still with my stolen numbers drying on the table between us, and wondered which of us she was warning.
