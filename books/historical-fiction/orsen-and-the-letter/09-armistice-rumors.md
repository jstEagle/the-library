## 9. Armistice Rumors

The letter came on a Tuesday, in a hand I did not know, addressed to Mrs. E. Marlowe, Marlowe House, and franked through three post offices before it reached me — which meant it had traveled the long way around, the way letters do when someone wants them to look like nothing.

I was in the morning room with the window cracked despite the cold. Isandro had gone to the depot for coal. The house had that particular silence of a place where one person is always listening for another person's footsteps.

I broke the seal and read it twice before my hands stopped shaking.

*Peace feelers resumed at Spa. Talk now is not if but when — some say within six weeks. Some say less.*

Six weeks. Possibly less.

I sat with it a long while, watching my breath fog the glass and fade, fog and fade, as though the window itself were practicing for an ending. For four years the war had been the fixed star by which every other thing moved. Corven's requisitions were justified by it. The grain levied from our valley and sold back to us at famine prices — wartime necessity, he called it. The pensions that arrived thin as winter broth — the country's sacrifice, he called it. Every theft wore the uniform of the emergency, and the emergency excused him, and would excuse him forever so long as the emergency lasted.

But wars end. And when they end, they are written down.

That was the thought that turned my blood to ice water. Not the peace itself — God knew I wanted the peace more than I had wanted anything since Thomas fell at the Somme. It was what peace would mean in a county like ours, in a province where Corven's friends sat in the regional offices and the provincial archives and the ministry back rooms where the records lived.

Victors write history; everyone says it as though it were a clever observation rather than a warning. But there is a second clause nobody says aloud: *and the victors' friends burn the evidence.* When the guns stopped, the machinery of justification would be dismantled — and with it, every ledger entry that had been justified by the war. Corven would have six weeks, perhaps less, to feed the requisition books into a stove and call it tidying. The pension skim would vanish into "administrative reorganization." The grain figures would be "lost in the transition." And in fifty years some historian would write a tidy paragraph about how our county endured the hunger years with remarkable fortitude, and no one would ever learn why the fortitude was required.

Unless the record already existed somewhere the fire could not reach.

I rose and went to the desk. My knees made a small complaint, which I ignored. There is a kind of arithmetic that fear does better than calm, and I did it standing, because sitting down felt too much like settling in for grief.

The network: eleven correspondents I could vouch for, four more whose discretion was probable but unproven. Of those fifteen, perhaps nine held fragments — a copied requisition total here, a pension roll discrepancy there. No one but me had seen all the pieces. That had been deliberate. A chain can be cut at any link; a scattered handful cannot be gathered once the hands holding it are arrested. Four years of careful fragmentation, and now fragmentation was exactly what could kill us, because scattered pages burn faster than bound ones.

And the full account — the whole shape of it, the connections only I had traced from Corven's court to the quartermaster's office to the two brothers-in-law who owned the mill that bought the cheap grain — existed nowhere but in my head and in one letter, drafted and redrafted, that I had never dared send.

I unlocked the bottom drawer.

The unsent drawer, I called it, though never aloud. Inside lay the confession of four years: the sister who was not spoken of, the hearing that lasted eleven minutes, the magistrate who smiled while he did it. I will not describe that document here. It is described elsewhere in this history, by someone braver than I am about describing things. Suffice to say that everything I had built — every coded phrase about garden seeds and church bazaars, every innocuous inquiry into the price of flour that was actually an inquiry into the price of flour — grew out of that drawer like a plant out of a grave.

I took out the draft and read it again, and this time I did not weep, because weeping takes time and time had just become a currency with a hard deadline.

*Six weeks. Perhaps less.*

*

Isandro returned at noon with the coal and a face that told me something before any words did. I have watched that face for three years across the seat of a motorcar. Most people see nothing in it — that is Isandro's genius, or armor, or both. But I had learned to read the weather in the stillness. Today there was weather.

"You've heard," I said, before the coal scuttle was even set down.

"Heard what, madam?" Perfectly level. Perfectly polite.

"The armistice rumors." I let the word *rumors* carry its own weight. "From the telegraph office, I'd imagine. Or the railway men."

A pause of precisely one beat too long. "There's talk at the depot," Isandro said. "Talk's cheap this year."

"Talk is never cheap. It's merely denominated in different currencies." I gestured to the chair opposite me, and after a moment Isandro sat, coat still dusted with coal, hands folded like a schoolchild summoned before the headmistress. "Sit down properly. We need to discuss the correspondence schedule."

"The schedule, madam?"

"I'll be writing heavily these next weeks. More letters than usual. Some of them long." I watched the eyes — steady, dark, giving away nothing, giving away everything. "Some of them will need to go out quickly. If the bridge situation worsens—"

"It won't reopen before spring either way. Ice is thickening downstream of the pylons."

"Then we may lose the post entirely, and I need to plan around that." I leaned forward. "Tell me honestly. Do you believe the war ends soon?"

Another pause. This one longer. And then Isandro said, in a voice carefully stripped of opinion, "Everyone believes something different, madam. The optimists say Christmas. The pessimists say spring."

"And you?"

"I drive the car, madam. Wars end when they end."

It was such an Isandro answer that I nearly laughed, and nearly laughed again at myself for expecting otherwise. Three years I had employed this driver on the strength of a reference from a woman who trusted another woman who trusted another, the whole chain resting on the assurance that Isandro Galloway could not read. An illiterate driver was a safe driver. Illiteracy was a locked room. I had put my entire conspiracy in a motorcar driven by a locked room and told myself the lock was sound.

Looking at those steady eyes, I found myself — not for the first time, but for the first time seriously — wondering who had told me the lock was sound, and whether they had ever actually tested it.

No matter. Not yet. There would be time — there had to be time — for that accounting later.

"The schedule," I said, and pulled paper toward me. "Here is what we're going to do."

*

What we were going to do, I explained over the next hour while the fire ticked and the afternoon light went copper on the snow, was finish it.

Not the campaign — the campaign against Corven could run for years, suffrage work runs forever, reform is the religion of people who expect to live a long time. I meant the case. The evidence. The record. Everything I had spent four years assembling in fragments across fifteen desks needed to become, within six weeks, a single coherent account — dated, sourced, signed by enough witnesses that burning any one copy would be like trying to burn a river.

"A single account," Isandro repeated.

"A complete account. Every figure, every connection, every name — including his." I had never said Corven's crimes aloud in this house. Saying them now felt like opening a window in January. "The grain. The pensions. The mill. The court cases used to silence anyone who asked questions." My voice did something I did not authorize it to do. Isandro pretended not to notice, which was its own strange kindness. "When the armistice comes, his friends in the provincial office will begin destroying files within days. I've seen it happen before, in smaller matters. A scandal surfaces, and suddenly there's a fire in the records room, a flood, a rat infestation. Paper is very fragile, Isandro. Men make sure of it."

"So the account must exist before the fires start."

"Yes. And it must exist in many places at once, in hands that don't know each other, so no single search recovers it all." I tapped the page. "Which means the network must stop being a network and become something else. Witnesses. Each correspondent signs their own testimony to the parts they can personally verify — what they saw, what they counted, what they were told. Not my claims. Their observations. A hundred small affidavits instead of one large accusation."

"Why?" asked Isandro, and it was the first question all hour that sounded genuinely curious rather than carefully neutral. "Wouldn't one strong document be safer? Fewer copies to intercept."

"One strong document has one author, and one author can be discredited, arrested, or dead. Fifteen signatures are fifteen separate problems for him." I allowed myself the smallest smile. "Corven's whole method is isolation. He ruins one person at a time, quietly, so no one else connects their ruin to his name. He can do that to me. He cannot do it to a town."

Something crossed Isandro's face then — quick as a fish under ice — and was gone. I logged it without naming it. There had been a great deal of that lately: expressions arriving and departing too fast for me to read, as though the face behind them were doing sums it didn't want observed.

"You'll want the letters to go out immediately," Isandro said. "Before any freeze stops the ferry riders."

"Tomorrow. And I'll need to travel — Millbrook, Harrow Cross, at least twice more to Ashcombe before the roads close. Can the car manage it?"

"The car manages. It's the roads that object." Isandro stood, brushed at the coal dust, hesitated. "Madam. If the war truly is ending — the papers say there'll be celebrations. Crowds."

"There will be."

"Crowds are loud," Isandro said. "Loud towns forget things quickly. Everyone will be looking at the sky and the bells." A pause. "If a man wanted to burn papers, a celebration night would be the night to choose."

I looked up sharply. It was precisely my own fear, phrased in my own logic, delivered back to me by a driver who was not supposed to understand the logic at all.

"That," I said slowly, "is exactly what I intend to guard against. Which is why the account goes out early. Before the bells. Before anyone has an excuse to be looking elsewhere."

"Yes, madam," said Isandro, and went out to tend the car, and I sat alone with my draft and my deadline and the growing, unwelcome suspicion that I had just held a strategy meeting with someone who had not needed my explanations at all.

*

That night I wrote until the lamp oil ran low.

Fourteen letters. To the schoolmistress at Harrow Cross, asking after her *garden catalogues* — her pension-roll copies. To the ferryman's wife, whose brother kept the mill books, requesting *seed prices for spring comparison* — the grain ledgers. To old Dr. Penhale, who had treated the families the famine prices had starved, asking him to *record his patient notes formally*, which he would understand meant dates, names, causes of death, in his own hand, witnessed by his own nurse. Each letter innocent to any censor. Each letter a lit fuse.

Between letters, I reread the unsent draft, and for the first time in four years I did not put it away.

Instead I laid it flat on the desk and began, in a fresh ink, to add the final section — the part I had always withheld, the part that named names outright instead of circling them in seed-catalogue code. If the deadline was real, the era of circling was over. Peace would come like a landslide, and whatever was not already downhill of it would be buried.

I was three lines in when I heard it: the front door latch, soft as a breath, and footsteps in the hall that did not continue to the kitchen stairs.

Isandro stood in the study doorway, coat still on, hat in hand — a posture of a messenger, though no message had been sent for.

"Forgive the hour, madam. There's a man watching the house."

The pen stopped moving. "Watching it how?"

"From the lane. By the elm. He's been there since I got back with the coal. He thinks the hedge hides him." Isandro's voice stayed perfectly flat, which was somehow worse than alarm. "He's young. New boots. I don't know his face, but I know the boots — the clerk Hobb wears the same pair. Corven keeps his office boys shod from the same cobbler."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft, righted itself. I thought: *he knows.* I thought: *of course he knows; four years is a long time to dig and he has had nothing to do all war but dig.* I thought of the drawer beneath my hand, and the fourteen letters in their envelopes upstairs, and the six weeks that might be five, that might be three.

"Did he see you notice him?" I asked.

"No."

"Then we change nothing tonight. Nothing visible." I closed the draft, slid it beneath the blotter, and met Isandro's eyes across the lamplight. "But tomorrow we move faster than he can report. The letters go out at first light — all fourteen — before he can wake his master's suspicions into orders."

"At first light," Isandro agreed, and paused at the door, half-turned. For a moment something almost surfaced in that careful face — a question, or the shadow of one.

"Madam," Isandro said. "When you say *the account* — the complete one. Who writes the last copy? The one you keep here?"

"I do," I said. "As I've written every letter that matters in this house for four years."

"Of course, madam," said Isandro, and the door clicked shut behind them, and I sat very still in the ticking silence, hearing the sentence hang in the warm air like smoke:

*Every letter that matters in this house.*

I had said it to reassure us both. Watching the door, I understood that it had reassured neither of us.

Outside, beyond the hedge and the elm, the new boots crunched once in the snow — then went quiet, and stayed quiet, which was its own kind of message. Six weeks, the letter had said. Perhaps less.

I dipped the pen and finished the section before the oil gave out.
