## 10. A Name of One's Own

The census man came to Marlowe House on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Isandro cleaned the lamps.

He was a small official with a larger clipboard, sent down from the county office in Corven's district, and he stood on the front step breathing steam and asking questions of Mrs. Pell, who kept the door the way a fortress keeps its gate.

"Names and ages of all persons resident," he said. "For the register."

"You have my name," Mrs. Pell said. "You have Miss Marlowe's name. You have the names of every soul under this roof, because you had them last year, and the year before."

"Persons change," the clerk said.

Isandro, halfway up the ladder outside the drawing-room window with a lamp glass in one hand, heard that and went very still. Persons change. It was true enough. Persons died, were born, married, vanished into France and did not come back. The war had made the whole country fluid, names washing downstream like autumn leaves. But there was another kind of person who never appeared on any register at all, and Isandro was one of them, and they had known it so long they had stopped thinking of it as anything but the shape of their life — the way you stop noticing a limp.

"Who's your driver, then?" the census man said, craning his neck. "The one on the ladder."

"That's Galloway," Mrs. Pell said, without hesitation, without looking, without any flicker of the thing she must have known she was doing.

"Galloway." The pen scratched. "Christian name?"

Mrs. Pell paused just long enough. "Isandro," she said. "Now are we done? The wind's coming off the river and I've a kettle on."

He wrote something. He would write whatever he liked; no one could prove him wrong. That was the whole architecture of it. Isandro finished the lamp glass, climbed down, and stood in the grey light holding a rag, watching the census man trudge back toward the high street, and felt the old familiar vertigo open beneath them like a crack in ice.

*Galloway.* Even the surname was borrowed. There had been a groom named Galloway at the Harrowgate stables where Isandro had been taken at six years old, or perhaps seven — nobody had asked, and children too young to be asked simply arrived, the way cats arrive, the way weather arrives. Old Galloway had taught them to hold reins and read harness leather and keep a horse's feet sound on frozen ground, and when he died in Isandro's fourteenth winter, the stablemaster had looked around at the orphaned half-person mucking his stalls and said, *Well. Galloway's still got hands, doesn't he,* and that was that. A name conferred like an old coat. Warm, and not yours.

They had never been christened. Never registered. There was no paper anywhere in England that said *here is this person, born on such a day, of such parents, and therefore entitled.* Entitled to what? To a ration card that matched a real name. To sign for a parcel at the post office. To vote, if votes were ever to come — and Edith Marlowe had given nine years of her life, her health, her sister's memory, to the idea that votes should come, and here was Isandro, who would not appear on any list even if the lists opened wide as church doors.

They put the rag down and stood in the yard a long moment, until their fingers ached from the cold, and then they went inside, because a driver's place is indoors by the fire after the lamps are done, and a driver's place was the only place Isandro had ever been sure of.

Except now the driver's place was exactly what Magistrate Corven was circling.

* * *

It started, as these things did, with tea.

Corven came to call on Edith Marlowe in early November, ostensibly about the road levy — a fiction so thin Isandro could see through it from the stable, and Corven surely knew that, and did not care, because the visit was never about the road. He sat in the drawing room with his cup balanced on his knee and his voice pitched at the frequency of reasonableness, and Edith received him with the glacial courtesy of a woman who has buried a sister on this man's watch and intends him to know it in her spine if nowhere else.

"The county is compiling its rolls," Corven was saying as Isandro carried in the second pot. "War makes such work necessary. Men return changed. Records must be kept current, or how does a magistrate protect his people?"

"Protect them from what?" Edith said.

"From confusion. From persons of uncertain status." He turned then, unhurried, and looked directly at Isandro for the first time since entering the house — a full look, deliberate, the kind of look a man gives a horse's teeth before buying. "Your driver. I understand there's some question about the name."

"There's no question," Edith said. "Galloway has driven for me two years. Ask anyone in town."

"Oh, everyone speaks well of Galloway." Corven smiled. He had a good smile; Isandro had watched it work on farmers and aldermen and once, memorably, on a deputation of soldiers' widows whom he was robbing by post. "Steady. Quiet. Keeps their eyes on the road. Rare qualities." He set down his cup. "But steady people with no history make a magistrate uneasy, Miss Marlowe. A man with papers can be vouched for. A man without papers—" he spread his hands, apologetic, reasonable, "—is a gap. And gaps get filled. Sometimes wrongly."

"I'll thank you to fill your gaps elsewhere," Edith said, "and take your cup with you."

He laughed — genuinely, Isandro thought, amused rather than offended — and rose, and paused at the door with his hat in his hand.

"Galloway," he said. "Do give it some thought. The county office is friendly to those who regularize their situation. Papers can be arranged. For the right cooperation." His eyes moved once around the room, cataloguing the writing desk, the letter tray, the locked drawer Isandro had seen Edith open only twice. "Everyone should exist somewhere, after all. Don't you find?"

Then he was gone, and the cold came in behind him, and Edith sat very straight with her hands folded, staring at nothing, and Isandro stood with the teapot going cold in their grip and understood that they had just been offered the one thing in the world they wanted most, priced in the one currency they could not pay.

* * *

That night they took out the tin box.

It lived under the floorboard beneath the cot in the room over the carriage house, and it held everything Isandro owned that could not run: a photograph of Old Galloway gone soft with thumbing, a horse brass shaped like a sun, three letters from a girl named Tilly at the Harrowgate dairy who had written twice and then married and then stopped, and a folded page torn from a parish calendar in 1904 that someone had used to note a birth — not Isandro's, a stranger's, a child named Ada, born March the ninth, weighed eight pounds — kept purely because it was the only document Isandro had ever held that said *a person began here.*

They had stolen it. They had been eleven, sweeping the vestry, and the page had fallen from the vicar's pile, and Isandro had picked it up and read it — read it fluently, though no one knew they could — and something in the plain miracle of it had closed their hand around the paper like a trap. *Ada. Born March the ninth.* Proof that being written down was possible. Proof that other people simply were, on paper, everywhere, always.

No one had ever written *Isandro began here.* No one knew when. No one knew where — Isandro themselves didn't, not the town, not the year, not whether the woman who left them at the Harrowgate gate was mother or aunt or a stranger paid by the mile. There was no birthday. They had chosen midwinter, arbitrarily, at fifteen, because the stable boys celebrated theirs and it seemed a small theft to commit one.

And here was the thought that had been circling them for weeks now, patient as a hawk, and tonight it finally landed:

Corven did not need to destroy Isandro. He needed only to offer them existence.

Because that was the engine of the whole machine — Isandro saw it now, saw it whole, the way you finally see the pattern in wallpaper after years of sleeping beside it. Every scheme in the ledger Hobb guarded, every line of requisitioned grain sold back to starving villages, every pension shorn of its shillings before it reached a widow — all of it ran on the same principle. It ran on people who could not appear. The dead soldier whose allotment was "adjusted" had no advocate because the paperwork said one thing and the widow couldn't read it. The grain moved through ledgers written in figures only clerks could audit. The whole edifice of Corven's comfort was built on the gap between what happened and what was recorded, and he tended that gap like a gardener. He made gaps. He looked for the unlettered, the unregistered, the invisible, and he built his profits in the space where they lived.

Isandro had spent thirty-one years believing invisibility was shelter. It was shelter. It had kept them fed and employed and unremarkable through two coronations and a war. But it was also the exact shape of the hole Corven needed them to stay in. Their erasure was not neutral. It was load-bearing. Somewhere in the dark structure of the county, their missing name was holding up part of a roof, and men walked comfortably overhead.

They sat on the edge of the cot with the tin box open and the candle guttering, and for the first time in their life they felt anger — clean, specific, directional anger, aimed outward instead of the diffuse shame they had carried since childhood like a stooped shoulder. Not anger at being poor; poverty was weather. Anger at the arithmetic. Anger at a system in which Edith Marlowe could spend nine years building a case out of letters and the case itself could be voided by one question: *and who, precisely, attests to this? Who are you? What is your standing? Show me your standing.* Anger at a world where the truth needed a signature to be true, and signatures were sold, and the people with the most truth to tell were precisely the people whose hands were never allowed near a pen that counted.

Tilly's letters lay in the box. Isandro lifted the top one. She had written: *You always were one for listening, Iso. You'll go far, listening.* Everyone had always said that — quiet, steady, keeps their eyes on the road. Compliments, all their life, for the very quality that made them usable.

Well. The war was ending. Armistice rumors ran through the town like fire through stubble — weeks now, maybe days, the whole rotten century's great convulsion grinding to a stop, and when it stopped, the historians would start, and the victors' clerks would file the peace the way Corven filed his grain. If the record was going to be written anyway — if names were going to decide what happened next — then Isandro wanted, for once, with a hunger that frightened them, to be one of the names.

Not Corven's name, bought with papers and passage. Their own. Earned. Signed.

They put the tin box away and lay in the dark listening to the river grind its ice against the pilings, and they thought: I will need to tell her. Sooner than I planned. Because Edith was building toward something — Isandro had watched the letters thicken, watched the network tighten, watched her count the weeks to the armistice the way a general counts the hours to an offensive — and when the moment came, the testimony would need witnesses, real ones, witnesses who existed, and Isandro could either stand there as a ghost or stand there as a person, and ghosts could not swear oaths.

There was a knock at the carriage-house door. Two raps, brisk, familiar.

Isandro opened it on Mrs. Pell, lamplit, breath steaming, face unreadable in the way of women carrying messages they'd been told not to comment on.

"Miss Marlowe wants you," she said. "In the study. Tonight." A pause, and then, grudgingly, humanly: "She's been writing for two hours, and she burned two pages, and then she said fetch Galloway, and I'd say that's a summons, wouldn't you?"

Down the garden path, through the cold, Isandro followed the lamplight to the study window and saw Edith Marlowe sitting upright at her desk, a single sheet before her, her pen capped, her hands flat on the wood — waiting, it seemed, the way one waits before opening a letter one has written but not yet dared to send.

Isandro's heart began, quietly, to pound.
