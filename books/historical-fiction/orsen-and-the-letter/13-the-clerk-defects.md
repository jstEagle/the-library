## 13. The Clerk Defects

The ledger lay open on my desk the way it had lain open every working day for eleven years, and for eleven years I had looked at it the way a man looks at a scar he has stopped believing is his own.

Column one: pensioner's name. Column two: amount assessed. Column three: amount disbursed. The difference between two and three went nowhere, officially. It went into a column that did not exist, in a hand that was not mine — Corven wrote those figures himself, in his own office, with his own pen, and then he locked the book away overnight as if the lock were what kept it honest.

I copied the numbers out. That was my whole employment. I sat in the cold half of the requisition office with chilblains on my knuckles and I made the theft legible.

My mother's name was in column one. Row forty-one. *Widow Hobb, née Ashworth.* She had been dead four years by then, but her name stayed on the rolls because a dead woman's pension, reduced by the quiet arithmetic of column three, still yielded something to whoever held the difference. Corven had never met her. He had never seen her hands, which were ruined before she was fifty from the laundry work she took when Father drowned. He had never stood in her kitchen in the last winter of her life while she boiled one potato and split it, and told me she wasn't hungry, and watched me eat her half because I believed her.

She wasn't hungry. She couldn't afford to be.

I was twenty-two when I first understood what column three meant. I am thirty-four now. For twelve years I have told myself the same story every man in this town tells himself about Corven: that he is how things are done; that the war made everyone complicit; that a clerk who objects will simply be replaced by a clerk who doesn't, and nothing will change except the size of my mother's ghost in the room.

Then Isandro Galloway drove past the window at half past nine, and I realized I had stopped telling myself the story sometime in the last month without noticing, the way you stop hearing a clock.

* * *

It started, if anything can be said to start, with the boy on the ice. Everyone in town talked about it for a week — Tam Weller going through the river after a dropped letter, and the Marlowe driver going in after him. But what nobody talked about, because nobody knew to look, was what happened afterward at the post office, where Tam's letter was dried out and resealed and sent on its way, and where I signed the register beneath the driver's name the way I sign beneath everyone's name.

Isandro Galloway. No parish. No next of kin. No record.

I have spent twelve years keeping records. A man without one is either nothing or everything, and I had learned enough by then to know which.

And there was something else. Something small, which small things usually are not. On the fourth of November, a letter had come through the office addressed to Magistrate Corven in a hand I did not know, franked from outside the county, and Corven had read it standing up, twice, and then burned it in the grate rather than file it — which he does perhaps three times a year, and each of those three times, in my experience, someone ends up poorer.

I did not know then what the letter said. I know now. Everyone will know soon. But on that morning all I had was the ash, and the driver's name in my register, and my mother's name in row forty-one, and a feeling in my chest like ice shifting under weight — that sound the river makes at night, the crack that means the surface has decided something.

* * *

Corven summoned me on the twelfth. Not to the requisition office — to his house, which he never did unless he wanted a thing off the record.

He received me in the study with a fire lit, though it was barely past noon, and sherry poured, though he knew I didn't drink. Menace delivered as courtesy. I had watched him do it to farmers and widows for a decade and thought myself immune, being too unimportant to bother with. Then he bothered.

"Hobb." He said my name the way he entered it in ledgers, testing the weight of it. "You've been with me a long time."

"Eleven years, sir."

"Eleven years. And in all that time you've never once asked what becomes of the difference."

The fire ticked. I heard my own pulse in my ears, loud and stupid.

"I enter what I'm given, sir."

"Yes. That's why I keep you." He smiled, and it reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window — all over the glass, touching nothing behind it. "There's a matter arising. The Marlowe woman. You handle the post; you'll have seen her correspondence volume."

"Mrs. Marlowe writes a great many letters," I said carefully.

"She writes a great many letters." He let the sentence sit between us like something he'd set down and expected me to pick up. "Letters to persons in three counties. Letters that go out sealed and come back answered, sealed again, week upon week, year upon year. A remarkable correspondence for a widow of no trade." He turned to the window. "A magistrate's duty is to notice patterns, Hobb. Sedition doesn't announce itself. It arrives in envelopes."

"And you suspect Mrs. Marlowe of sedition, sir?"

"I suspect her of *correspondence*." He said the word as if it were a disease. "Which during wartime is nearly the same thing, and after the war will be exactly the same thing, once the proper instruments exist. There will be an inquiry. Quietly. After the armistice, when attention elsewhere has slackened." He came back from the window and looked at me directly, and this was the moment, this was the hinge, though I didn't know it yet. "I'll want the office records assembled. Everything touching Marlowe House. Deliveries, addressees, frequencies. You'll compile it."

"When, sir?"

"Now would suit."

I felt the shape of it close around me even as I nodded. Compile the record, and I became the instrument. Refuse, and I became the obstacle, and Corven had a way with obstacles that left no marks and no witnesses. Twelve years of stories about how things are done pressed down on me, and I heard my own voice say, "Yes, Magistrate," and hated it with a completeness I hadn't managed since the potato winter.

At the door he stopped me.

"One more thing. The Galloway person. Your driver register shows no antecedents."

"No, sir. No record."

"No record." He repeated it slowly, tasting it. "A man with no history can be given any history we like. Remember that, Hobb. It may prove useful to him, if he proves useful to us. And if he doesn't—" The smile again, frost on glass. "Well. No record cuts both ways."

I walked home through the early dark with my breath smoking and the compilation order folded in my pocket like a coal I couldn't drop and couldn't hold.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in my room above the chandler's shop with a single candle and my mother's photograph, taken when I was nine and she still smiled with her teeth, and I did the thing I had trained myself never to do: I added the columns honestly.

Not the money. The years. Eleven years of entering figures I knew were stolen into books I knew were false, for a wage that depended on my not knowing. Eleven years of telling myself that a clerk's place was to copy, that history happened to other people, that my mother had died of her heart and her heart had died of age and age had nothing to do with famine prices and skimmed pensions and a magistrate's second carriage.

Row forty-one. *Widow Hobb.* Disbursed: four shillings six. Assessed: seven shillings. Difference, over the five years of her roll: some eleven pounds, gone into the column that did not exist.

Eleven pounds. She had starved politely on less than that per annum, splitting potatoes, lying about hunger, and the sum of all her lying had fit inside a line item a decent man might have missed.

I put the candle out so the dark could hold what my face was doing, and when I had finished, I found that the fear was still there — the fear of Corven, of the watch he'd set, of what happens to men without protectors in towns like this — but it had changed position. It was no longer between me and the thing I must do. It was beside it, walking with it, the way fear walks with any soldier going anywhere worth going.

Fear choosing a side. That's what it felt like. Not courage — I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole point now. Courage is a story soldiers tell afterward. What I felt was fear, fully intact, that had simply picked its side and refused to be moved to the other one.

* * *

The missing page came to me the way the important things always do: through the ordinary machinery, misfiled by accident, rescued by habit.

On the fifteenth I was assembling Corven's compilation — the surveillance of Marlowe House, envelope by envelope, my own handwriting turning my own conscience into evidence against me — and I needed a folio from the back cabinet, the 1916 grain requisitions, to confirm a delivery frequency. The cabinet is Corven's alone. He keeps the key on his watch chain. But he had been called to the Harrow assessment that morning, and the key hung on its nail in his inner office while he was away, because Corven's habits are the habits of a man who believes no one in this town has a spine.

I took the key off the nail the way you take a hot kettle — fast, so the burning doesn't get time to argue.

The 1916 folio was thick. Requisition orders, transport manifests, price schedules. And tucked at the back, loose, unentered, in Corven's own hand — the hand that writes column three — a single page headed *Adjustments, Michaelmas 1916*, listing eleven farms by name and the sums by which their requisition compensation had been *adjusted*, downward, with the aggregate carried forward to no account at all. Eleven farms. The Fenner brothers among them, who sold their plough horses that winter. The Ashcroft widow, whose boy came home from France to find the land sold. Every name a family I had grown up queueing behind at the relief office while the grain they'd grown was sold back to them at prices set by the man who'd taken it.

One page. Loose. Unmissed, because Corven did not know it existed — he had drafted it, used it, and forgotten it in the folio the way powerful men forget things, because forgetting is a luxury their systems purchase for them.

I stood in his office holding one page of truth, and I understood with total clarity that this was the moment the rest of my life would be measured against.

I could put it back. I could go home and sleep, and wake tomorrow as Clerk Hobb, eleven further years stretching ahead like a corridor with no doors.

Or I could fold it into my waistcoat, walk out of the magistrate's own office with his signature against him in my breast pocket, and become — what? A witness. The word sounded absurd applied to me. Witnesses were people in stories. Clerks copied things down for witnesses.

But then, I thought, buttoning my coat over it, feeling it lie against my chest like a second heartbeat: so far as I know, witnesses are only clerks who decide the record matters.

I locked the cabinet. I hung the key on its nail. My hands shook the entire time, and I noted, distantly, that they shook no worse than they did any ordinary morning entering figures, and that this fact told me something damning about what my ordinary mornings had cost.

* * *

I went to Marlowe House at dusk, which was stupid and I knew it, because dusk is when watchers are set and dusk is when watchers expect watchers to move. There was a man across the lane from the gates — I marked him by his stillness, the way a heron is marked by stillness among reeds. One of Corven's from the Harrow side, a nephew of somebody. And another, further down, by the bridge road, smoking with a patience that wasn't natural to him.

So Corven already suspected. Already watching. Whatever he suspected me of, he had hedged the house itself, which meant the house mattered, which meant whatever was happening inside it had frightened him — and Corven was not a man who frightened easily, and I had just made myself one of the few people alive who knew why.

I almost turned back. I want that recorded too. I stood in the lane with the page against my chest and the watcher's cigarette smoke drifting gray in the cold, and I very nearly went home to my candle and my photograph and my corridor with no doors.

Then the kitchen door of Marlowe House opened, spilling lamplight, and Edith Marlowe herself stood in it with a bucket — the mistress of the house carrying her own coal, which told me the household had pared itself down to essentials — and she looked straight down the lane at me. Not at the watchers. At me. As if she had known for some time precisely which clerk would come and on which evening, and had merely been waiting for my fear to finish choosing.

"Mr. Hobb," she said, when I reached the door. "You look like a man carrying something heavy."

"It's only a page, ma'am."

"In my experience," she said, stepping aside to let me in, "it's always only a page. Until it isn't."

Inside, in the kitchen, the driver was mending a harness strap by the stove, and looked up when I came in, and I saw the same thing in those steady eyes I had seen in the post office register — the look of a person deciding, at their own pace, whether I was weather or a wall.

I took the page from my waistcoat and laid it on the table between us, and smoothed it flat with both hands, and my hands had finally stopped shaking.

"Michaelmas 1916," I said. "In his own hand. Eleven farms. I can testify to the ledger entries it corresponds to, and to the practice, and to the man who ordered it. I've kept his records for eleven years." I heard my own voice do something it had never done in that house or any house: state a fact about myself as though I expected it to count. "Whatever you're building here — I want my name in it."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still in the draftless room. Edith Marlowe looked at the page, then at me, then — significantly, I thought — at the driver, who had set down the harness strap.

"You understand," she said quietly, "what it costs a man to bring a thing like this through a watched street."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you brought it anyway."

"The alternative, ma'am," I said, "is row forty-one."

She didn't ask what row forty-one was. Perhaps she saw it in my face. She put her hand flat on the page — Corven's own hand beneath hers, pinned to a kitchen table at last — and turned to the driver.

"Light the good lamp," she said. "We've testimony to discuss, and I think we haven't much time. Mr. Corven's watchers won't report a visit tonight. Tomorrow they'll report everything."

That was how it was settled, then — not with speeches, but with a page on a table and a lamp being lit. I had been inside the machine for twelve years, and in the space of one evening I had walked out of it carrying a piece of it, and the strangest part, the part I am still learning to live with, is how light the page was. All that weight, and it weighed no more than a leaf.

Outside, the heron across the lane shifted at last, and began — I would learn later, from the boy Tam, who sees everything on this street — to walk toward the telegraph office, moving quick through the dark, on some errand of Corven's that none of us yet knew the shape of.
