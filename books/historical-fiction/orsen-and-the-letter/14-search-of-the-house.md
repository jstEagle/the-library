## 14. Search of the House

They came at nine in the morning, when the frost was still on the windows and the town was still pretending to sleep.

I heard the wagon first — the particular creak of a hired conveyance driven by someone who doesn't care about the horse — and then boots on the frozen gravel, four pairs at least, arranged in the loose rhythm of men who had been told where to stand before they were told why. I was in the kitchen with a kettle going. Edith Marlowe was upstairs with her correspondence, which meant the confession letter was in her writing desk, which meant it was forty feet from me and might as well have been in my own chest.

I put down the kettle.

"Madam," I called up the stairwell, not loudly. Just enough. She would hear it in my tone if not my words; after three years of driving her she could read my voice like a telegraph clerk reads tape. There was a pause above me — no footsteps, which was right, no answer either — and I understood that she was already moving, already folding whatever needed folding, and that the only thing I could do for her now was go to the door and be exactly what Corven believed me to be: nobody.

The knock came anyway. They always knock, these men, once, as a formality, the way a flood knocks before it takes the door off its hinges.

I opened it.

There were five of them. Two constables from the borough force, faces I half knew from market days, both looking anywhere but at me. One man I didn't know, big through the shoulders, with the flat hands of someone who'd loaded crates for the army and never forgotten how. And behind them, on the gravel, stood Clerk Hobb with his ledger under his arm and his collar buttoned wrong, and behind *him*, unhurried, breathing steam into the cold like a man arriving for Sunday dinner, Magistrate Corven.

"Missus Marlowe at home?" he asked. Not me. Through me. He did this deliberately; I had watched him do it with servants and shopmen all winter, this trick of addressing the air just past a person's face until the person felt themselves dissolve. Three years ago it would have worked on me. This morning I felt the old reflex — the drop of the eyes, the step aside, the whole trained choreography of being invisible — rise up in me like a tide, and I let it. Let him think the tide still ran his way.

"She's dressing," I said. "You'll wait."

Something flickered across Hobb's face. The big man shifted his weight. Corven smiled the way frost forms on glass — slowly, evenly, everywhere at once.

"Routine matter," he said. "Requisition audit. Wartime powers." He produced a paper without looking at it, held it out toward me and then, seeing me not take it, let it fall to my hand anyway. "We'll want the house open. All rooms."

"Madam Marlowe will want to see the warrant herself."

"Then she may read it while we work."

* * *

I have thought, since, about what a house looks like while it is being searched. Not the furniture dragged or the drawers upended — anyone can imagine that, everyone in that town had imagined it since the first rumors of Corven's men turning over the ferryman's boatshed — but the smaller thing, the way the light changes. How a room that has kept your secrets for years suddenly shows you every place a secret could hide, and every one of them is wrong. Under the floorboards: too obvious. Inside the mattress: the first place hands go. Behind the books: done before breakfast by any schoolboy hiding a penny dreadful. A house, I learned that morning, is a machine for betraying its owner, and all its cleverness belongs to whoever brings the crowbar.

Edith came down at half past nine dressed as though for church, gray wool, high collar, hair pinned like a fortress. She took Corven's warrant from my hand, read it twice — once fast, once slow — and said, "This authorizes an inspection of stored goods against requisition records. It does not authorize a search of private correspondence."

"Stored goods," Corven agreed pleasantly, "are often wrapped in paper."

"Then you may inspect wrapping. You may not read letters."

"Of course, madam." He gestured, and the big man went past us both into the parlor, and the reading of letters began.

That was the arithmetic of the morning, and I did it standing in the hall with my hands folded like a good servant while the house came apart around me: there was one letter that mattered, and it was written in Edith's own hand, addressed to no one, unsent, and it contained everything — the grain sold back at famine prices, the pension skimming, the names, the dates, the whole architecture of Corven's theft — laid out plainly because Edith had finally decided that coded language had cost the network too many months. It was the truest document in the county and it sat in the third drawer of her writing desk beneath a false bottom she'd had the carpenter install in November, and false bottoms are the second place hands go, after mattresses, because everyone who builds one believes themselves the first person ever to think of it.

Corven knew about the desk. I saw it in the way he didn't look at it.

That was the worst part, worse than the crowbar prying the pantry boards, worse than the constable shaking out the curtains in a gray cloud of dust and moths. Corven walked through the wreckage like a host at his own party, touching nothing, directing everything with small tilts of his head, and his eyes went everywhere except where he wanted them to go. He looked at the bookshelves so long that I understood he wasn't searching them, he was waiting. He wanted the letter found by their hands, not his. A magistrate's deniability is a garment stitched from other people's labor.

* * *

Midmorning the kettle situation resolved itself into tea, because Edith insisted, pouring for the men who were dismantling her home with the same composure she used for parish committees, and I carried the tray because carrying trays was what I existed for, in Corven's cosmology, and I have rarely been so grateful for a cosmology. It gave me movement. Movement gave me the hall. The hall gave me the stairs, and the stairs gave me thirty seconds alone outside her study door while downstairs a constable found Edith's suffrage banners folded in a trunk and held one up like a trophy fish, and Corven said something dry about women and flags, and every eye in the house was on that banner.

Thirty seconds. The study smelled of ink and cold ash. Third drawer, false bottom. I had watched her open it a dozen times — press the rear left corner, lift — and I did it now with hands that surprised me by being steady, and there it was, four pages, her handwriting marching across them like troops, and no time to think, only time to know one thing absolutely: it could not be found here, and it could not be found on me, and it could not go back in the drawer, because the drawer was next.

Downstairs, the big man's crowbar sang against a floorboard.

Where? My mind ran the house like a driver runs a road at night, every turn remembered. Kitchen. Coal scuttle. I had filled it that morning before the knock — blacked the range, banked the fire — and it sat by the stove, iron-hooped, heavy, ordinary beyond all suspicion, because coal scuttles are the furniture of drudgery and no magistrate on earth inventories drudgery. But paper in a coal scuttle gets blackened, and blackened paper is ruined paper, and Edith needed those pages legible. So: wrap. I snatched the oilcloth from the bread crock — bread could fend for itself — rolled the letter tight, three turns, tucked the ends, and dropped it into the coal at the bottom of the scuttle, then shoveled fresh lumps over it until the oilcloth vanished, then pressed the whole mass flat with the shovel the way I'd pressed it flat ten thousand mornings of my working life, and set the lid ajar exactly as far ajar as I always set it, and picked up the scuttle, and carried it.

Carried it past Corven in the hall.

He was examining Edith's portrait of her sister — the one she never spoke of, the one who had gone into his court twenty years ago and never come out — and he glanced at me, at the scuttle, at the servant-shaped fact of me, and dismissed the entire transaction in half a heartbeat. That dismissal was the finest gift anyone gave me that winter. I walked the scuttle out the back door, across the frozen yard, past the woodpile, and set it down beside the coal shed among six identical scuttles belonging to neighbors, because poverty makes every street uniform, and then I stood in the cold shaking, not from fear, I told myself, from the wind, and listened to the house being taken apart behind me.

* * *

They found things. Of course they found things. That was the design — give them findings, feed the machine — and Edith fed it with the precision of a woman who had rehearsed this morning in her head for two years. The trunk of banners: seditious, fine, take them. The shelf of pamphlets: take those. A locked box of correspondence that turned out, when forced, to hold fifteen years of entirely innocent letters about committee minutes and subscription dues and one very dull dispute over the placement of a memorial bench — take that too, Hobb recorded it all in his ledger, his pen scratching like a mouse in a wall, and I watched him record it and wondered what was happening behind that careful face. His mother, I'd heard somewhere in the letters, lived on a pension. I filed the thought away the way I filed everything, and hated myself a little for how naturally the filing came now.

By noon the warrant's pretense collapsed. Corven stood in the middle of the ruined parlor and made it official without making anything official at all: "Nothing relevant to requisitions, I see. Though material of a political nature has come to light." He said it to Hobb, for the ledger, for the record he was building brick by brick — Marlowe as agitator, Marlowe's house as a nest of pamphlets, and someday soon, when the armistice came and the files burned, only his version would remain.

"You'll want to note," Edith said, standing amid her scattered life with her spine straight as a flagpole, "that you found no letters. No evidence of correspondence with anyone."

"I found a great deal of paper, madam."

"You found banners. Banners are sewn, not written." Her eyes crossed the room and touched mine for a quarter of a second, no longer, and in that quarter-second passed the whole of our unspoken treaty: whatever happens, the pages survive. "You may tell your magistrate's court whatever you like about flags."

Corven smiled again, that frost-smile. "Oh, I intend to tell them a great deal." He settled his hat. At the door he paused, and turned, and looked at me directly for the first time all day — fully, squarely, the way he had begun looking at me lately, the way that made the skin of my neck prickle, the look of a man doing sums.

"Galloway," he said. My name. My borrowed name, the one I'd worn like a coat for years, in his mouth it sounded like an accusation of theft. "You were here the whole time?"

"From nine o'clock, sir."

"Mm. Faithful." He drew on his gloves, finger by finger. "Faithfulness is a rare quality. One likes to know what it's attached to." And then he was gone, down the steps, into the wagon, and Hobb followed with the ledger clutched to his chest like a child, and the constables, and the big man with his crowbar, and the wagons crunched away down the frozen street leaving the town's curtains twitching behind them.

* * *

We put the house back together in silence, which is a different labor from taking it apart and takes three times as long, because everything must be returned not merely to its place but to its angle, its dust-shadow, its history of sitting there. Edith worked beside me. Neither of us spoke until the parlor was square and the curtains rehung and the kettle, faithful object, had been boiled twice and poured.

Then she sat down at the wreck of her writing desk, opened the empty third drawer, and looked at the space where four pages had been.

"The coal," she said.

"The coal," I agreed.

She nodded slowly, and for a moment I thought that would be all — the code of servants and mistresses, the whole conversation conducted in single nouns — but then her hands flattened on the desk and she said, quietly, "I wrote those pages knowing they might burn. I did not write them knowing they'd sit in a coal scuttle overnight. If the range draws badly tomorrow—"

"They won't stay in the coal tomorrow."

"No," she said. "They won't." She looked up at me, and the lamplight caught the lines around her eyes, twenty years of them, all of them earned in rooms like this one. "Isandro. When Corven looked at you at the door — I saw it. He has stopped seeing a servant."

I stood very still. Outside, the light was failing early the way it did in December, the ice on the river groaning somewhere below the garden, the whole town sealing itself in for another night.

"He asked my name," I said. "He's never asked my name before."

"Names are how he hunts." Edith rose, straightened her collar with two precise tugs. "And tonight, while he's deciding what yours means, we're moving those pages out of coal and into the only safe place left in this county — which is to say, into more heads than one. Get your coat. We're going to see the ferryman, and you're going to learn a page of that letter by heart before we reach his door."

I got my coat. In the pocket, against my wrist, the hidden notebook I'd copied from the clerk's ledger pressed its thin secret weight, and I thought: shrinking room, shrinking room, and yet somehow every wall we lose teaches us another way through. Then Edith blew out the lamp, and we went down together into the dark, toward the river, while somewhere across the frozen town a lantern swung in Corven's window, and behind it a man was writing my name in a margin.
