## 15. The Frozen Crossing

The river spoke to me that night.

I had heard it all my life without listening — the mutter under the bridge pilings, the gurgle at the weir, the long dark silence of the deep channel where the ferry crossed in summer. But on the fourth night of hard frost it made a new sound, a high thin groaning that carried up from the water like a woman singing behind a closed door. Ice forming. Ice thickening. The river locking itself shut one more time before spring.

I lay in my cot above the carriage house and listened, and I thought: *it will hold.*

That was the whole of my plan, laid out in the dark between one groan of the river and the next. It would hold for one crossing. One man — I had stopped pretending to myself I was anything else; Edith's question had seen to that — carrying papers that weighed almost nothing and everything. Across the ice, up the far bank, three miles along the mill road to Harrowgate Junction, where the telegraph office never slept and the morning train south left at seven. The regional paper was printed there. Mr. Calloway had told me once, over a pint he'd bought me because I drove him home too drunk to walk, that the *Harrowgate Courier* would print anything you paid the set-rate for, and more than that if you brought them something true enough to be dangerous. He'd laughed when he said it. I hadn't.

* * *

I had copied the pages twice.

The notebook went into its old place, sewn flat against my ribs inside the coat lining, where it had lived since the night in Corven's office with the candle guttering and Hobb's footsteps in the hall. But paper could burn, could sink, could rot in a river — so I wrote the testimony out again, fair copy, in a hand I kept small and even, the hand I used for Edith's envelopes. Eleven pages. Hobb's missing requisition sheet, transcribed whole. The pension figures. The grain contracts, dated and priced, the same grain sold twice, once to the Army board and once back to the very families whose sons the Army was feeding on it. And at the bottom, in letters larger than the rest, the account of what we knew and how we knew it, ending with a line I had argued with myself about for two nights:

*Witnessed and attested by Isandro Galloway, driver, and by the undersigned townsfolk of Marlowe's Ford.*

Forty-one signatures. Hobb's first, his pen shaking so badly the ink had spattered. Then Mrs. Tewson, whose boy I'd pulled from the river in November and who had looked at me afterward as though she were seeing a man she'd walked past every day of her life. The ferryman Aldous. The butcher. Two of the churchwardens. Edith herself, her signature bold as a headline, *Edith C. Marlowe*, no apology in it anywhere.

Forty-one people who had decided, in the space of one December week, that they would rather stand in Corven's lantern light than keep their heads down through one more winter. I had not asked any of them. That was the part I still couldn't get straight in my chest. I had spent years believing that invisibility was a kind of armor, mine alone, worn tight against the skin — and then the town had gone and shown me that everyone else had been wearing it too, all this time, and that some of them were tired of the weight.

Eleven pages, folded into an oilcloth packet, the packet sewn into the breast of my coat beside the notebook. Redundant. Edith had taught me that word, laughing at my careful driving, *redundant, Isandro, like having two horses — if one throws a shoe you still arrive.* If the river took one copy, the other stayed against my heart. If the river took both—

It wouldn't take both. It wasn't going to take either. It was going to hold, because I needed it to hold, and in four days of frost-hard nights I had learned nothing whatsoever from the river except how badly I wanted things.

* * *

Edith caught me at the kitchen door at half past five in the morning, dressed, booted, the coat already buttoned to my throat.

She stood in the doorway with a shawl around her shoulders and the lamp turned low behind her, so I saw her mostly as a shape, but I knew her posture the way I knew the creak of the third stair. She'd been waiting. Of course she'd been waiting. She had known me for six years and read me for four days, which apparently amounted to the same education.

"The ice won't hold," she said.

"It held in '12," I said. "Aldous walked it with a sack of mail."

"Aldous weighs eleven stone and has been drinking gin since Queen Victoria died. You're carrying a man's weight and a fool's courage." She came out onto the step into the cold, refusing to let the door close between us. "Wait for thaw. Wait for the ferry. Three weeks—"

"Three weeks is after the armistice." My voice came out lower than I meant it, rough with the hour. "You said it yourself. Peace comes, and Corven's friends in the county offices start burning files before the church bells stop ringing. Every clerk who ever took his shilling gets a quiet order and a match. We have until the guns stop. Not a day past."

"And you'll be dead a day early, and the file burns anyway, and I'll have traded a driver I trusted for a corpse and a packet at the bottom of a hole in the ice."

"You'll have forty-one signatures," I said. "And a memory. Mine."

"That's not the same thing and you know it isn't. Paper can be printed a thousand times. A memory dies once." Her breath hung white between us. In the lamplight her face was older than I had ever let myself see it, and I understood suddenly that she had spent these same four nights lying awake doing this arithmetic, weighing my life against the record the way she weighed every letter, every phrase, every coded sentence — and finding no answer that didn't cost.

"Then give me a reason that isn't three weeks," I said.

She was silent a long moment. Down in the town a dog barked twice and gave up. When she spoke again her voice had changed, gone quiet and level, the voice she used for dictation.

"If you go," she said, "you go at first light, not now, when the ice has had the coldest hours to thicken. You go straight across at the narrows below the weir, where the current runs fastest and the ice sets hardest. You do not stop, you do not turn around, you do not go back for anything you drop. And Isandro—" her hand came out and gripped my sleeve, hard, an old woman's grip with surprising iron in it, "—if the ice speaks to you, if it groans or cracks or shifts under your feet, you lie down flat and you crawl, and you crawl toward the far bank and not toward me. Do you understand? Not toward me."

"I understand."

"Liar," she said softly. "But go, then. Go, since you will." She pressed something into my hand — a flask, brandy, heavy glass, warm from her pocket. "For after. Not before. I'll know."

* * *

The narrows below the weir were gray-white in the false dawn, the ice stretched bank to bank like a road nobody maintained. Snow lay on it in patches, wind-scoured to bare glass in others, and through the bare places the water showed beneath, black-green, moving, alive. I stood on the near bank with my boot toes at the edge and felt my heart going like a boy's.

Below me the weir muttered. Above, the deep pool held its silence. The far bank was a smudge of alder and snow, three hundred feet of nothing between me and being a man with a destination.

I thought of Edith's arithmetic and refused it. I thought of Corven's offer — *papers, a legal name, passage out* — aimed like a surgeon at exactly the wound he'd found, and I thought how strange it was that a man who wanted to erase me had been the first person in my life to admit I existed at all. I thought of my own name, written large at the bottom of eleven pages. *Isandro Galloway.* A name I had given myself at fifteen, borrowed off a gravestone in the parish yard, and it was no less mine for that. Names taken are stolen. Names given away are bought. But a name written down by your own hand, witnessed by forty-one people — that was the only kind there was.

I stepped onto the ice.

The first hundred feet were easy. The ice near shore is always thickest, frozen down to the mud, and my boots rang on it like a floor. The cold came up through the leather and bit at my ankles. Breath tore at my throat. I walked fast, arms out a little for balance, the way you walk a beam, and I did not look down through the clear places, and then I did look down, once, and saw the current sliding beneath my feet — the whole weight of the river, moving, patient, indifferent, and me a dark shape reflected on its ceiling.

At the middle third the character of the ice changed. It went from white to gray to a bruised dark color, thinner where the current slowed the freezing, and the sound of my steps dropped from a ring to a dull knock, and then to no sound at all, which is the worst sound ice can make.

*Lie flat,* Edith had said, *if it speaks.*

It spoke.

Not a crack, exactly — a sigh, a long settling groan that came up through my boots and out ahead of me across the whole width of the river, traveling, I swear it, faster than I could think. Somewhere off to my left the ice answered itself, a sharp report like a pistol shot, and a seam opened in a line of white frost-flowers thirty yards away, a black zipper unzipping.

I went down flat. Spread-eagled, cheek to the ice, the way Aldous had described it once in the Ferry Inn, laughing, demonstrating on the table with his forearms. Distribute the weight. Crawl. Forward, always forward — not back, never back, back was the bank I'd come from and forward was the reason I'd come at all.

I crawled. The oilcloth packet rode stiff against my chest. Under my palms the ice was slick and scalloped and terribly thin-looking where the frost flowers grew, and through the black seams I could see the water sliding, unhurried, going about its business. Twenty yards. Fifteen. The far bank alders resolved out of the murk, individual trees, individual branches. Ten yards of gray ice and then white again, thick ice, shore ice, safety shaped like a strip of gravel and dead reeds.

My knee went through.

No warning. No groan. One moment the ice was holding and the next my right leg was in the river to the thigh, and the cold hit like a fist — not pain at first, worse than pain, a great white shock that emptied my mind of everything except the fact of it. I heard myself cry out, one syllable, swallowed by the flat gray morning.

I did not panic. That is the thing I want written down somewhere, whatever else becomes of me: I did not panic. I spread my arms wider, took my weight onto my chest and elbows the way the whole crawl had trained my body to do, and began dragging the leg backward out of the hole, slow, slow, angling my hips flat, feeling the broken edge grind against my boot. The water pulled at the leg gently, almost kindly, the way it pulls at everything, wanting only to take me down into the dark and keep me.

The boot came free. I got the leg out. I lay gasping on the wet ice, soaked to the thigh, my trouser leg already stiffening, and I remember thinking with absurd clarity: *the dry half of me can still make the bank.*

I crawled the last ten yards on that thought. Shore ice rang under my palms, honest ice, a foot thick. Gravel. Reeds. Alder roots. I hauled myself up the bank on my arms because the right leg barely answered, and I knelt there in the snow among the trees with the river talking behind me, and I laughed — actually laughed, one bark of it, alone, at the gray sky — because I had done it. Slipped through, survived the slip, made the crossing. The packet was dry against my chest. The notebook was dry. I was half-frozen and the sun wasn't up yet and the mill road was three miles long, but I had done it.

I got up. The leg folded.

I caught myself on an alder trunk and stood again, leaning, and started down the mill road in a lurching half-drag, telling myself the stiffness was cold, only cold, that walking would warm it. The brandy flask knocked against my ribs. *For after,* Edith had said. *I'll know.* I saved it. God help me, I saved it, rationing myself against a need I hadn't met yet, husbanding warmth I no longer had.

By the second milestone the leg had stopped hurting altogether, which I knew, distantly, from a horse doctor's lecture once, was the wrong direction for hurt to travel. By the third milestone I was falling every few hundred yards and getting up slower each time, and the sky had lightened to the color of dishwater, and somewhere behind me, muffled by miles and alders and the river's mutter, the church bell of Marlowe's Ford began ringing the hour — seven o'clock, the train leaving Harrowgate Junction, the presses starting their morning run without me.

I sat down against a fence post to rest, meaning to rest only a moment. The snow was soft. The cold had moved past my skin into the meat of me, a deep humming numbness, almost comfortable, and I thought drowsily that this was how it must feel to be erased after all — not torn away but gently dissolved, the record closing over you like water. *Isandro Galloway, driver, drowned within sight of the far bank.* Even the river would keep no ledger of me.

The oilcloth corner showed at my collar where I'd worked it loose to check it. Dry. Dry as bone.

I put my hand flat over it, over the notebook beneath, over the name written in my own hand, and I made myself stand up. I fell. I stood again. Somewhere ahead, past the whitened fields, the Junction smoked its morning smoke, trains moving, wires singing, a world of records and printing presses and names in type — a world one mile from a man crawling on his hands and one knee down a frozen lane, dragging a leg that no longer belonged to him.

The last thing I saw clearly, before the dishwater sky tilted and the snow came up soft against my face, was the telegraph wire above the mill road, running arrow-straight toward the Junction, humming faintly in the wind like a struck string that no one had gotten around to playing.

Then the field tilted the other way, and I heard — distant, impossible, coming from the direction of the town I had just escaped — a bell begin to ring. Not the hour. Faster. Frantic. The alarm bell.

Someone had found my tracks on the ice.
