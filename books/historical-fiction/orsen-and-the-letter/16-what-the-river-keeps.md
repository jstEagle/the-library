## 16. What the River Keeps

Edith Marlowe had not slept. She sat in the front room of Marlowe House with the confession letter open on her knee, rereading the same paragraph about requisitioned grain, and every time she reached the end of it she started over, because the alternative was to think about Isandro Galloway standing on the far bank of a frozen river at midnight with her whole war sewn into their coat.

The lamp was turned low to save oil. The fire had burned down to a red eye in the grate. Somewhere past two o'clock, the cold came up through the floorboards in a way it hadn't come before, and Edith understood — the way you understand a change in wind, without evidence — that the river had finished freezing. Hard. All the way across.

She was on her feet before she had decided to stand.

* * *

She took the lantern and went down through the yard, past the coal shed where a confession letter had hidden from Corven's men three days ago, past the bare apple tree, to the rise above the water. The river lay white under a half moon, a road of ice running bank to bank, smooth as poured tallow. It looked solid. It looked like a thing God had built for exactly this purpose.

Nobody sane would walk on it.

Edith stood at the top of the rise and told herself that Isandro would not try. Isandro, who read everything and said nothing; who had spent years perfecting the art of being furniture; who understood better than anyone alive that the safest place in any room is the one nobody looks at. That person did not cross rivers at night. That person waited for spring.

But that person had climbed a telegraph pole's worth of courage into their chest somewhere between the search of the house and tonight, and Edith knew — because she had done the same arithmetic herself, twenty years ago at her sister's trial — what happens when an invisible person decides to be seen. They do not do it halfway. They cannot afford to.

She heard the ice before she saw anything wrong with it: a sound like a door groaning on its hinges, carried flat across the water. Then a second sound, sharper. Then nothing, which was worse.

Then a cry — thin, torn off by the wind, more animal than human — and Edith was running down the rise onto the bank with no plan except the direction of the sound.

* * *

The hole was thirty yards out. She could see it as a black seam in the white, and beside it — or near it, or gone from it — nothing. The lantern threw a small yellow circle that stopped four feet from her boots and lied about everything beyond.

"Isandro!" Her voice cracked across the river and came back to her smaller.

Nothing.

She went out onto the ice. She knew better. Every child on this river knew better. She went anyway, low, weight on her palms, sliding more than walking, the lantern abandoned behind her because a lantern does not help a drowning woman and its light only shows you the exact spot where your help ends.

Ten yards out the ice spoke again beneath her, a long cracking whisper, and she froze, and in the frozen silence she heard it: a scraping. Rhythmic. Faint. Someone dragging themselves over ice.

Toward shore.

She crawled toward the sound. Twenty yards out, close enough now to see the black seam gaping wider than she'd guessed — wide enough that a body could go under and stay under — she saw a hand. Just a hand, hooked over the lip of intact ice like a claw in a story, and then the shoulder, and then the dark head, streaming, and Isandro hauled themselves out of the water the way a drowned thing hauls itself anywhere, with no dignity and no thought, pure animal refusal.

They didn't stand. They couldn't have. They rolled away from the hole, flat, spreading their weight the way the ferryman's son once taught Edith's own brother to do, and began dragging themselves toward her across the ice, leaving a wet black trail that steamed.

"Don't get up," Edith called, crawling to meet them. "Don't you dare get up."

Isandro's face was gray. Their lips were the color of the moon. When they tried to speak, what came out was not words but a kind of clicking, teeth against teeth, and their hands — their hands were the worst, blue and bloodied, the nails torn where they'd clawed at the ice, and Edith thought with a strange, distant clarity: *those are the hands that steamed open my letters for three years, and I will not let them freeze off.*

She reached them. She got an arm around them, and together, crawling, sliding, swearing — Edith swore, she heard herself do it, words her sister would have boxed her ears for — they crossed twenty yards of river ice that could have swallowed them both, and the bank rose up to meet them like mercy.

* * *

She got them to the house by half carrying, half dragging them through the yard, and then there was the terrible practical business: the wet clothes cut or torn away, the blankets, the bath that wasn't too hot because too hot stops a chilled heart, Mrs. Prewitt summoned from next door in her nightcap and taking command of the kitchen as though she'd been waiting her whole life for a casualty. Isandro shivered so violently the table shook, and then, terrifyingly, stopped shivering, and Mrs. Prewitt slapped their cheeks and forced hot sweetened milk between their lips until the shivering started again, and Edith learned in the space of ten minutes that the return of shivering is a gift you weep over.

It was nearly four when Isandro lay wrapped in blankets before the rebuilt fire, conscious, gray-faced, alive. Edith sent Mrs. Prewitt home and sat down across from them, and the first thing she asked was not *are you well* or *what were you thinking.*

"The copies," she said. "Where are they?"

"Gone." Isandro's voice was a ruin, scraped raw by river water and cold. "Satchel went under with me. I felt it go. It filled and it pulled and I let it." A breath. "I let it go. I had to, to get a hand on the ice."

Edith closed her eyes. Three weeks of copying. Hobb's page. The dates, the tonnages, the prices — the whole architecture of Corven's theft, inked out fair and true, lost to the bottom of the river along with the satchel, the oilskin, the rope harness, all of it.

"I'm sorry," Isandro whispered.

And Edith opened her eyes and found that she was not grieving. She searched herself for the grief and it wasn't there, and what was there instead made her lean forward.

"You memorized them," she said. "Didn't you. Before you ever wrote a word down. You memorized the ledger pages the same night you copied them."

Isandro's eyes came up to hers. In the firelight they were steady — those steady eyes Corven had noticed, the ones Edith had been looking at for years without seeing.

"All of them," Isandro said. "Dates. Tonnage. Prices paid and prices charged. Names on the requisition orders. Hobb's missing page, twice over. I can write them out tomorrow. I can write them out ten times if you want ten copies."

Edith sat back. Outside, the wind moved along the eaves. In the fire, a log settled and threw up a fan of sparks.

"You went onto that ice," she said slowly, "carrying a satchel you already knew was worthless. You risked your life for paper whose contents live behind your eyes."

Isandro looked into the fire. For a long moment Edith thought they wouldn't answer. Then:

"It wasn't worthless," they said. "If I'd gone under, and they'd dragged the river and found it — found my handwriting, my notebook, the pages in my coat—" They stopped. Started again. "The copies were never for me. They were for whoever found me. So the truth wouldn't drown just because I did."

The words hung in the warm room. Edith heard what lived underneath them, and it undid her more than the hole in the ice had.

"And the notebook?" she asked quietly. "The one in your coat lining?"

Isandro's hand went to the blanket, to where the coat would have been, and something crossed their face — panic, quick and total — and Edith was already moving, already lifting the sodden coat off the drying rack by the stove, feeling along the inside seam with her fingers until they found the ridge of it. Oilcloth-wrapped. Stitched flat. Bone dry.

She set it in Isandro's lap. They held it the way people hold things they'd been told were buried.

"Mrs. Prewitt will need to wash that coat," Edith said, "and she'll find the stitching, and she'll ask no questions, because Mrs. Prewitt has been asking no questions about this house since 1913. But Isandro." She waited until their eyes came up. "Why didn't you tell me? About the notebook. About the memory. You sat in this room three nights ago and confessed to reading my letters, and you never said you'd been building your own archive the whole time."

The fire ticked. Isandro drew a breath that shook at the edges.

"Because telling you made it real," they said. "As long as it was just mine — just a habit, just ink in a seam — it was still the old life. Still the driver who reads and says nothing. Witness to other people's history." A pause. "I think part of me believed if I never named what I was doing, I'd never have to be the person doing it. And tonight I walked out onto that ice, and I understood, halfway across, that I've been walking on thin things my whole life pretending they were roads."

Edith said nothing. There was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said better.

* * *

Dawn came up pale and hard-edged. Isandro slept in fits by the fire, waking each time with a jolt, hands grasping at nothing, and Edith sat watching them and did her own accounting.

She had spent six years building a network of letters, and she had trusted its delivery to a person she believed could not read, and she had been wrong in a way that should have ended everything and had instead — she could see it clearly now, in the gray morning light — saved everything. Because Corven hunted visible enemies. He hunted Edith, hunted her network, hunted names on paper he could seize and burn. He had never once looked properly at the quiet person holding the reins, and the quiet person had spent three years assembling, page by memorized page, the complete account of his crimes.

The invisible self had drowned in the river last night. Edith grieved for it — genuinely, unexpectedly grieved, the way you grieve for a stranger whose funeral you attend by accident. Whoever Isandro Galloway had been before the ice — the watcher, the witness, the person who insisted they were only furniture — that person had stayed under. What had crawled out of the hole and across the ice was someone else. Someone who had walked onto a frozen river at midnight knowing exactly what they were risking, and why.

Someone with a name they were finally prepared to use.

Isandro woke for the fourth time as the sun cleared the rooftops, and Edith poured tea and set it in their ruined hands, and watched them drink.

"When you write it out," she said, "you won't write it alone. We'll do it here, at this table, in daylight, with Mrs. Prewitt at the window. Ten copies. Twelve. And they go out separately, to twelve addresses, so that no single burning destroys it — the way we always planned, except now the hand writing it is yours."

Isandro set down the cup. Something moved through their face — fear, and under the fear, unmistakably, relief.

"There's one problem," Isandro said. "Corven's watch on the house. They saw me leave last night?"

"They saw a figure on the river," Edith said. "Hobb tells me the clerk reported it at dawn — a person on the ice, fell through, made the far bank or didn't. Corven doesn't know it was you. Yet. Which means he thinks the copies are either drowned or delivered, and either way he has perhaps two days before he decides to stop guessing." She leaned forward. "Which brings us to the question I've been sitting with since four o'clock this morning, Isandro, and I'll put it plainly, because we're past plainness now."

She waited until she was certain she had their whole attention.

"Corven offered you papers. A name. Passage out. He aimed it at the exact wound, and I know you refused — Hobb heard him order you held until after the armistice. But held where, Isandro? Because his men will come for you today, and this time they won't be searching for letters."

Outside, far off down the high street, a church bell began to ring — not the hour, not the quarter, but fast and continuous, the alarm peal, the one rung only for fire or flood.

Or for news.

Isandro was on their feet before the third stroke, blankets falling, swaying, catching the table, and through the window they both looked down at the town where figures were spilling into the street below, running toward the telegraph office, and Edith felt the word arrive in her chest before any runner could bring it, the word the whole world had been holding in its mouth for four years:

Armistice.
