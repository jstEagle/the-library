## 17. Corven's Offer

The house on Wren Lane had been a school once, before the war emptied it of children, and Corven had bought it for less than half its worth from a widow who signed where he told her to sign. He liked the building. It smelled of chalk dust still, faintly, under the coal smoke, and chalk was the smell of erasure — of clean slates, of names wiped away and written anew. He had done his best thinking here for eleven years.

He sat now in the old schoolroom with the ledger open before him and did not read it. He knew what was in it. What he needed to know was who else did.

Hobb had brought him his tea at four o'clock as always, and Hobb's hands had shaken. Not much. A tremor, no more than a man might have from too little sleep or too much cheap tobacco. But Corven had spent thirty years reading hands — witnesses' hands, petitioners' hands, the trembling hands of men signing away their land — and a clerk's shaking cup told him everything a confession would have. Hobb had seen the missing page. Hobb knew what Corven knew: that page forty-one of the requisition ledger, the one recording the second shipment of grain that had never reached the relief stores at Harrow Bend, existed in only two places in the world. The ledger itself, which could burn. And whatever copy someone had already made.

Someone had copied pages out of his office. Someone had been inside his locked drawers while he slept twenty feet away, and had left no mark but absence — a wrongness in the way the papers lay that only he, who had filed them with his own meticulous hands, would ever feel. Like a tooth missing. Like a wife's chair pushed back an inch too far.

He had three suspects. The clerk, whose hands shook. The woman Marlowe, who could not have entered without being seen by every servant in the district. And the driver.

Corven turned his fountain pen over in his fingers and considered Isandro Galloway — or whoever Galloway was, because there was no Galloway in any registry Corven had consulted, and Corven had consulted them all. No birth record. No parish entry. No conscription card, no ration book issued before 1916 when the emergency rolls had swept up everyone regardless of provenance. A man — or something between a man and a shadow; Corven had noticed the smooth jaw, the careful voice pitched neither high nor low — who had walked out of nowhere sometime around 1912 and taken up driving, first for a timber merchant, then for the mill, then for Edith Marlowe.

A person with no papers was a person who could not testify. That was the beauty of the law as it stood: testimony required standing, and standing required a name, and a name required a record, and a record required someone, somewhere, at some moment, to have bothered to write you down. Corven had ruined men simply by refusing to write them down. He had made whole grievances vanish by declining to enter them into the minute books. The system did not need his corruption. The system *was* his corruption, elegant as arithmetic.

And yet.

He thought about the eyes. He kept coming back to the eyes. Most people, meeting a magistrate's gaze, performed something — deference, resentment, blankness, fear. The driver gave him nothing. Steady, level, patient, like a well you dropped a stone into and heard no bottom. Twice now, in conversation about the roads or the weather or the closure of the bridge, Corven had deliberately let fall a crumb — a false date, an invented shipment — and watched to see whether it would travel back to him through some listening ear. Both times the crumb had gone nowhere. Which meant either that the driver repeated nothing, or that the driver weighed everything before passing it on, and had weighed those crumbs and found them counterfeit.

Only a reader could weigh a lie against its weight. Only a reader who knew what true paper looked like.

Corven set down the pen. Outside, the river ice boomed and settled in the dark, great tectonic groans rolling up the valley, the sound of the world contracting. In three weeks, perhaps four, the armistice would come — everyone felt it now the way animals feel weather — and when it came, the files in this room would have a shelf life measured in days. His friends in the county administration were already talking about "consolidation," a bureaucratic word that meant bonfires. He had until the thaw. Possibly until Christmas. After that, if the pages surfaced, he would be a magistrate no longer but a case file himself, and the tribunal would not be held in this town where he owned the courtroom, but in the county seat, where he owned nothing.

He rang the small brass bell on the desk.

His man Wilkes appeared — a former soldier with a game leg who asked no questions, which was why Corven paid him triple what questions were worth.

"Bring the motor round at eight tomorrow," Corven said. "Not mine. The grey one, from Tull's garage. And tell Tull I want no record of the hire."

Wilkes nodded and withdrew. Then Corven did a thing he had not done in years: he took out a fresh sheet of paper and began composing by hand, slowly, a document that was not a threat, not a summons, not a requisition. It was an offer. He wrote it twice, crossed things out, wrote it again. Precision mattered. With people like the driver, precision was the entire language.

* * *

At nine the next morning, Isandro Galloway stood in the doorway of the Wren Lane schoolhouse, cap in hand, having been summoned by a note that read, in Corven's own copperplate: *Regarding the winter routes. Come at your convenience.* Convenience. As though the magistrate of the district concerned himself with a chauffeur's convenience. They both understood the courtesy for what it was: a leash dressed as an invitation.

"Sit," Corven said pleasantly, gesturing to the schoolchild's desk he'd had converted into a visitor's chair — deliberately smaller than dignity required. The driver sat without visible discomfort, which annoyed Corven faintly. Humiliation usually landed better than that.

"You'll have heard the armistice talk," Corven began. "Everyone has. Whether it comes next week or next month, it will come, and when it comes there will be a great settling of accounts across the country. Medals for some, prosecutions for others." He laced his fingers. "I intend to be among the former. Naturally."

Isandro said nothing. The silence was expertly done — respectful, attentive, the silence of a good servant. But Corven had rehearsed this scene all night and refused to be deflected by pantomime.

"I'm going to speak plainly," he said, "because I believe plainness will serve us both. You are not who your papers say you are." He paused to enjoy it. "Chiefly because you have no papers. No birth entry. No school record. No service record — you were called up under the emergency roll in '16, served fourteen months in the transport corps, discharged with a lacerated wrist, and the army's clerks spelled your name three different ways in three different documents, which means legally you are three different people, none of whom exists." He smiled thinly. "I've checked. I check everything. It's my profession."

Still nothing moved in that face except the eyes, which came up and met his — steady, patient, bottomless. Corven pressed on.

"Now. A person in that position is invisible. Invisible persons can do invisible work, and no doubt it has suited you. But invisibility has a cost, and you are about to discover it. Because when accounts are settled, invisible persons cannot defend themselves. They cannot testify. They cannot swear an oath that binds anyone to anything. If a charge were brought against you — theft, say, or forgery, or conspiracy — there would be no character references, no history, no *you*, legally speaking, to bring forward in your own defense. You would be convicted of being nobody, and nobody is always guilty."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a door closed — Wilkes, moving about, or perhaps nothing at all.

"So," Corven said, and slid the folded paper across the desk. "Here is what I am prepared to do."

Isandro did not reach for it. Corven unfolded it anyway, smoothing it flat, turning it to face them, and read aloud in the tone of a man reading terms of purchase.

"A legal identity. Fully documented, backdated, unimpeachable — birth certificate, baptismal record, schooling, a work history with verifiable employers, two of whom owe me favors and will confirm anything I ask. A name of your choosing, or mine, whichever pleases you. Papers that would survive any scrutiny in any court in this country." He tapped the page. "Along with it, a bank draft sufficient to establish yourself anywhere you like. Canada, if you want distance. Australia, if you want more. Passage booked, references provided, employment arranged through contacts of mine in the shipping offices. Within a month, you would be a person. A real one, with a past and a future and a signature that meant something on any line in any office in the Empire."

He leaned back. "All I require in return is a notebook — a small one, sewn, I'm told, into a coat lining — and your continued silence regarding matters that don't concern you. That's all. Not even silence, really. Just absence. Go somewhere far from here and be somebody, and let this town settle its own accounts."

He had expected many reactions. Anger, he could crush. Tears, he could ignore. Bargaining, he could grind down. What he had not prepared for — what arrived in the schoolroom like cold air through a cracked window — was the expression that crossed Isandro Galloway's face. Not temptation exactly. Something older and sadder. Recognition, almost, as though Corven had described a wound the driver had carried so long it had become part of the body, and now a stranger was pressing a finger directly into it.

Because it *was* the wound. Corven saw that with the sudden clarity of a man who has aimed well without meaning to. Everything he offered was everything this person had never had. A name. A legal self. A door marked EXIT. He had spent thirty years taking such things from people, and now he stood offering them, and the offer was not cruelty — it was worse, it was accurate.

"You've thought about this," Isandro said quietly. First words spoken since entering. The voice was low and even.

"Every detail."

"No." The driver shook their head slightly. "You've thought about what it costs *you*. You haven't thought at all about what it costs me to take it."

Corven frowned. "Costs? It costs nothing. It's a gift—"

"It's a purchase." For the first time something sharpened in the level voice. "You're buying the same thing you've always bought, Magistrate. You're just calling it a name instead of a bribe. And the name you'd give me—" they paused, and Corven watched something settle in their face, some decision arriving like a stone reaching the bottom of a well, "—the name you'd give me wouldn't be mine. It would be yours. Another thing in this world with your handwriting on it."

Silence. The ice boomed on the river, long and distant, like artillery from a war that hadn't ended yet.

"I'll consider your terms," Isandro said, rising, replacing the cap. "I'll give you an answer before the thaw."

"There may not *be* a thaw," Corven said, letting the steel show through at last, "before it matters. And I should tell you — the alternative to my generosity is not nothing. A person who doesn't exist can be made to stop existing entirely. Accidents happen on icy roads. Documents go astray. People go astray. I would hate for you to become one of the disappeared, when becoming *appeared* is so easily arranged."

Isandro stopped at the door. Turned halfway. And gave him the look again — the well-look, the no-bottom look — and said, in a voice so quiet Corven had to lean forward to catch it:

"You keep saying I don't exist. But you've spent three weeks trying to find out who I am, and you can't. Whereas I know exactly who you are. Every page. Every figure. Every widow on Wren Lane." A beat. "Think about which of us the river keeps."

The door closed softly. Footsteps faded down Wren Lane toward the Marlowe house.

Corven stood a long while in the empty schoolroom, the offer lying on the desk between the inkwell and the ledger, unsigned, unsignable. He had offered the exact wound and the wound had looked back at him. Slowly, deliberately, he opened the ledger to page forty-one and ran his finger down the figures, and then he rang for Wilkes and gave two instructions: double the watch on Marlowe House, day and night — and send word to the telegraph office that no messages leaving this district were to pass uncensored until further notice.

If the truth wanted out of this valley, it would have to fly. And nothing flew in December.

He was wrong about that. But he would not learn it for two more nights.
