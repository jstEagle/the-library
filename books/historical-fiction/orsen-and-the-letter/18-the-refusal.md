## 18. The Refusal

The papers sat on the desk between us like a small country waiting to be claimed.

Corven had laid them out with the care of a man dealing cards he knew would win. A birth certificate. An identity card stamped by the county registrar. A railway warrant to a city far enough away that no one would ask questions. And beneath them, folded once, a passport — an actual passport, cream-colored and official, with a blank where the name should go.

"Any name you like," Corven said.

I looked at the blank space for a long time.

Twenty-four years I had lived without one. Born in a workhouse infirmary that burned its records in a stove before I could walk, passed hand to hand through a county that never bothered to write me down, hired by Edith Marlowe under a name I had invented myself at sixteen because a man needs something to say when people ask. *Galloway*, I had chosen it from a sign over a shuttered tailor's shop in a town I only passed through. It had fit me the way borrowed coats fit — loosely, apologetically, ready to be given back at any moment.

And here was a magistrate offering to make it real. To write me into the ledgers of England. To give me what no one else ever had: proof that I existed.

It was the cruelest thing anyone had ever done to me, and he did it gently, the way he did everything.

"You'd be a free man," Corven said. "Papers in order. History behind you. The war's ending — there will be work for men who can drive, who can keep their mouths shut. You could be anywhere by Christmas."

The fire ticked in his study grate. Through the window behind him I could see the river, white now from bank to bank, holding its breath under ice. Somewhere past that river was the regional paper, the telegraph office, the world where truth became record. Somewhere behind me, sewn into the lining of my coat, was the notebook with every figure I had copied from his requisition ledger — the grain sold back at famine prices, the pensions skimmed from men who were dying in France to pay for his carpets.

He thought the notebook was still in Marlowe House. He had torn that house apart two nights ago looking for it. He did not know I wore his ruin against my ribs.

"A name," I said. "You're offering me a name."

"I'm offering you a life." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost kind. "You've never had one on paper. I'm told that weighs on a man. Never being able to vote, to own, to inherit. Never existing in the eyes of the law." He smiled thinly. "I can fix that. I have that power."

*I know,* I thought. *That is precisely why you cannot.*

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing in that warm room while the river froze outside: a name is not the same as a self.

I had learned this slowly, the way you learn the shape of a room in the dark, by touching everything in it. For years I had believed my problem was paperwork — that if some clerk somewhere wrote *Isandro Galloway* into a register, I would suddenly become solid, real, entitled to my own breath. That the shame I carried was a clerical error someone could correct.

But Edith had taught me better without meaning to. She had a name, a house, a vote denied but a history undeniable — generations of Marlows in parish registers going back to the time of the second George. And they had erased her anyway. They had erased her sister, tried her in Corven's court on a charge manufactured out of nothing, and buried her so thoroughly that Edith had spent twenty years rebuilding her out of letters. A name had not saved her sister. A name had merely told the executioner where to point.

Names are addresses. They tell power where you live so it can come for you. What they do not give you — what no document gives you — is the right to your own account of yourself. That has to be taken. Written. Witnessed.

And Corven was offering me an address.

"Say the name," he said softly. "Whatever name you want. I'll have it registered by Friday."

"What happens to the files?" I asked.

His smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a shutter drawn, a lamp turned down. "What files?"

"The requisition ledgers. Hobb's pages. The pension rolls." I kept my voice level, driver-flat, though my heart was going like a telegraph key. "The ones you'll burn after the armistice. Once the guns stop, once everyone's celebrating, once no one wants to hear about profiteers — that's when the fires start. Isn't it. That's the schedule."

For a moment we simply looked at each other, and I watched him revise his estimate of me. It was almost worth what it cost. All those years of being furniture — the man who drives, the man who waits with the motor running — and now I could see the exact moment I stopped being furniture in Magistrate Corven's eyes and became a problem. Problems got solved. Furniture just got used.

"You've been reading," he said. "Her letters."

"I've been driving," I said. "What's in the letters is none of my business."

"But you made it your business."

"No," I said, and heard the truth of it come up through me like water finding its level. "It became my business the day I read the first one. There's a difference. Reading is a choice. Knowing is not. Once I knew what you'd done — the grain, the pensions, the boys' mothers counting coppers while you bought Turkish carpets — I couldn't unknow it. Nobody offered me that choice. Not you, not Madam Marlowe, not God. Knowledge doesn't ask permission. It just moves in."

"And now it wants rent," Corven said.

"Now it wants the truth on the record. That's all. That's the whole price. Cheaper than what you charged everyone else."

He stood. He was not a large man, but he had spent thirty years making rooms feel too small for other people, and he did it now, walking slowly around the desk until he stood near enough that I could smell his pomade and the coal smoke on his jacket.

"Consider what you are," he said, gently, horribly. "No birth record. No surname of record. No next of kin. If you disappeared tomorrow, how long would anyone look? A week? Who would even file the report?" He let that sit. "Madam Marlowe's protection ends the moment I decide it ends. Your position in that house is a courtesy extended by my tolerance. You have nothing, Mr. Galloway — or whatever you are — except what I am presently offering to give you. Papers. A name. A future. In exchange for silence about matters that don't concern you and never did."

And there it was. The whole architecture of him, laid open as plainly as his ledger. He did not fear the truth; he feared the witness. He had built his life on people who had nothing on paper, because people with nothing on paper could be erased without ceremony. My invisibility wasn't an accident of my birth. It was the foundation stone of his entire world. Every man he had cheated, every woman his court had broken, every orphan and drifter and unregistered soul in this valley — we were all of us the soft ground his house stood on.

He was offering me a place in the walls.

* * *

I thought about the tailor's shop. Galloway & Sons, the sign said, gold letters flaking off black wood, in a town whose name I've honestly forgotten. I was sixteen, walking roads because there was nowhere to arrive, and a constable asked me my name, and I looked up and read it off the window and said *Galloway* as though it had been mine since the christening font. The constable wrote it down. It was the first time anyone had written me down anywhere. I walked out of that town feeling like a coin that had been minted.

But coins get minted by whoever owns the press.

If I took Corven's papers — if I let him fill in that blank — then every good thing the name might ever do would belong to him. Every signature, every lease, every vote cast under that name would carry his fingerprint inside it. I would walk around for the rest of my life as a document he had issued, and documents can be revoked. He wouldn't even need to burn me. He'd just need to report the forgery. One afternoon's work and Isandro Galloway would cease to exist again — this time officially, this time with his own consent in the file.

No. If I was going to exist, I would exist the hard way. On the record. In my own hand.

"I don't want your papers," I said.

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted off the hour with the patience of a thing that had outlasted better men than Corven.

"They'd be yours," he said. "Legally. Untraceably."

"They'd be yours," I said. "A name given by you is just another way of being owned by you. I've been invisible my whole life, Magistrate. Invisible means nobody sees you — but it also means nobody writes you down, nobody files you, nobody gets to decide what you are. You want to cure my invisibility by putting me in your ledger. But your ledger is where the grain went missing. Your ledger is where the pensions thinned out. Everything in your keeping comes out lighter than it went in." I picked up the passport, held it a moment — the weight of it, the promise of it, the trap of it — and set it back on the desk. "I'll take my chances with the truth instead."

Something moved behind his face. Not anger. Anger would have been easier. This was recalculation — a man discovering that his lever had been pushed into empty air.

"You understand," he said, "that I can't let you leave here."

"I assumed as much."

"You'll stay as a guest. Officially, you came to discuss the winter coal allotment for Marlowe House, and the discussion ran long. There's weather coming. Roads are unsafe." He was already moving to the bell-pull, already arranging the furniture of the lie with the smoothness of long practice. "You'll be comfortable. And after the armistice — which cannot be more than days now, the whole continent can smell it — after things settle, we'll continue our conversation. Perhaps you'll think differently with a hot meal and no audience."

"After the armistice," I repeated. "When the files burn."

"When *nothing* burns," he said pleasantly, "because there will be nothing left to burn."

A footman appeared — or what I took for a footman, broad across the shoulders, standing the way men stand when their real job isn't serving sherry. Corven gave quiet instructions. Guest quarters. See that he's comfortable. See that he stays.

I did not resist. Resistance was for moments that could be won, and this one couldn't. I let him walk me to the door, and at the threshold I stopped and turned, because there was one more thing I needed him to understand, not for strategy but for myself — because a man who has just refused everything should at least know why.

"You asked what I'd call myself," I said. "If I took the papers. So you'd know what to write in the blank."

"I did."

"My name is Isandro Galloway. I chose it myself, off a dead tailor's window, seventeen years ago, and it's the only thing I've ever owned outright. It's not on any register, and that used to shame me. It doesn't anymore. When it goes into a record — and it will go into a record, Magistrate, soon — it'll go in because I put it there. As a witness. With witnesses of my own. Not as a gift from you." I held his eyes. "You can keep the papers. Burn them, if you like. Burning seems to be the family trade."

The footman's hand settled on my arm, not rough, not gentle — professional. Corven watched me with the expression of a man doing arithmetic and disliking the total.

"Hold him comfortably," he said again, to the footman, but his eyes stayed on mine. "And send word to the telegraph office. Remind Mr. Petch that the line requires maintenance tomorrow. Extensive maintenance."

* * *

They put me in a room at the top of the east wing, warm, well-lit, with a view of the frozen river and a lock on the outside of the door. A guest room shaped like a cell. I sat on the bed and listened to the house settle and thought about the armistice bells, which could not be more than days away now — maybe hours. Every hour that passed was an hour closer to the fires.

Through the window, far off, I could see the roofs of the town, and among them the thin chimney smoke of Marlowe House, and I thought of Edith waking to find her driver gone and knowing exactly what it meant, because she had spent twenty years learning to read this man's silences the way I had learned to read his ledgers.

They had taken my coat at the door. Of course they had — standard practice, and I had counted on it. The coat hung somewhere below with its lining intact, and the notebook sewn inside it, and I could only hope it would hang forgotten in some closet until the world changed. But hope was a poor strategy. Hope was what you had when you had nothing else planned.

So I planned.

I had memorized every page of that ledger — that had always been the true archive, the one between my ears, and no search of any house would ever find it. I knew the figures cold: 412 tons requisitioned, 290 accounted for, the difference sold back through three intermediaries at famine prices. I knew the pension roll, 61 names, 9 of them dead men kept alive on paper so the payments could flow elsewhere. I knew Hobb's missing page and where Hobb's courage lived. And I knew that Corven had just made one mistake, small but fatal, in a career built on never making any.

He had locked me in a room with a window overlooking the telegraph office.

I crossed to the glass. Below, past the frozen gardens, the telegraph office sat dark under its single lamp, and beside it rose the pole, and strung along the pole the line that ran south toward the regional paper, toward the wire, toward the world. Corven would shut that line down tomorrow — Petch, maintenance, extensive. Which meant tonight was the last night the wire would carry anything true out of this valley.

I pressed my palm flat against the cold pane and did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since the door closed: the drop from the window, the distance across the yard, the sentry's rounds, the climb.

Somewhere out in the dark, a church clock began striking ten.
