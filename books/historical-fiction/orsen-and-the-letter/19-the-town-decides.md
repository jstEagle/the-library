## 19. The Town Decides

### Hobb

Clerk Hobb had spent eleven years learning how to make paper disappear.

It was a craft, like any other. A page entered in the wrong ledger, a folio misnumbered, a receipt filed under a date that had never existed — done well, a document did not burn so much as evaporate. It was never there at all. The truth of it was that Corven had never needed to destroy anything. He only needed Hobb to arrange the world so that certain facts had no address.

Hobb stood in his cold office above the courthouse with the requisition ledger open on his desk and his mother's face in his mind, which was not a face anymore but a arithmetic: four ounces of bread, winter of 1916, and the pension that should have fed her arriving short by two shillings and sixpence every month for three years while her son sat upstairs copying figures that proved it and said nothing.

Two shillings and sixpence. He had told himself it was small. He had told himself a great many things were small.

The missing page lay under his hand now — the one Corven had ordered pulled from the file after the Galloway driver was seen too long at the post office window. *Requisitioned: 340 sacks wheat, Millbrook granary. Disbursed: 112 sacks, army contract.* The other 228 sacks existed nowhere except in this page and in the bellies of townsfolk who had bought their own grain back at famine price from a dealer who paid Corven in silence.

Hobb took out a sheet of plain paper. His pen hovered.

He was not a brave man. He wanted that understood, even if only by himself. Brave men walked into magistrate's offices and said what they thought. Hobb had spent his whole life being the man who stayed after brave men left, tidying up.

But he wrote it anyway. *I, Alfred Hobb, clerk of the court of this district, do attest that I copied the following entries from the requisition ledger of 1917 at the direction of Magistrate E. Corven, and that the originals were removed at his instruction...* And beneath it he copied every figure, every date, every false entry, in his beautiful clerk's hand, the one skill he had that could not be taken from him.

Then he did the thing that frightened him more than Corven ever had. He signed it. Full name. Date. Witness line left blank, because he knew where he was going, and he knew they would want more than one name on it.

He folded the page into his coat, put on his hat, and went down the stairs past the portrait of the magistrate that had watched him for eleven years.

For the first time, Hobb did not look at it.

### Marta

Marta Voss had been telling anyone who would listen that her Tam nearly drowned for a dropped letter, and that the letter had been worth drowning for, though she'd boxed his ears black when she got him home.

She had not read the letter. She could read, but Tam had been half-frozen and secretive and she'd chosen warming him over interrogating him, and by morning the letter was gone wherever such letters went. But she knew this much: her boy had risked the river ice for a piece of paper belonging to Madam Marlowe, and the quiet driver had gone in after them both without a moment's thought, and something about the shape of those two facts had been working on Marta ever since like water working on stone.

So when the knock came at her door on the last Thursday of October — old Mrs. Petchy from the mill, who everyone knew couldn't thread a needle straight but could count a ledger to ruin faster than any man in the county — Marta listened to what she was asked, and then she sat down at her kitchen table and did it.

*I, Marta Voss, widow, do swear that in the winter of 1917 I purchased wheat flour from Denner's store at ninepence the pound, having grown that same wheat myself the summer before on my own strip, and sold it to the requisition at tuppence.*

Her hand shook writing it. Not from fear. From the strangeness of putting it down. For three years the whole town had known and the whole town had said nothing, each of them thinking themselves alone in knowing, each of them thinking the others had agreed to be silent. Writing it down broke something open. It was like the first crack going across the river — nothing, then everything.

Mrs. Petchy witnessed. Then Marta took the page herself and walked it through the freezing streets to the house of the ferryman, because Mrs. Petchy said the ferryman was collecting them, and Marta thought: *the ferryman.* Old Bram, who hadn't run a ferry since the bridge closed, sitting in his hut by the frozen landing like a man waiting for a river to change its mind.

Well. Perhaps the river was changing its mind.

### Bram

Bram Kettle had ferried this crossing for thirty-one years and he knew ice the way a priest knows scripture, and what he knew was that the river did not decide things all at once. It decided them in inches. One night the ice held a boy; another night it wouldn't hold a sparrow. You learned to read the gray of it, the sound of it under your boot — the high singing note that meant thin, the dull thud that meant honest.

He sat in his hut with a stove going and a growing stack of papers weighted under an iron kettle, and he thought about how a town decides.

Because that was what was happening, whatever Edith Marlowe called her network of letters. A town deciding. It had decided nothing for three years, and now, in the space of a fortnight, it was deciding everything, and Bram understood why: the armistice rumors had come down the telegraph wire like weather, and every soul in the valley knew what weather meant. When peace came, the men in the capital would sort the heroes from the villains, and the files here would be ash within a week, and Corven would stand up in some new commission with his medals and his mournful face and be counted among the men who served.

Unless the record didn't live in the files. Unless it lived in forty hands instead of one drawer.

That was the girl's idea — Isandro's. Bram had heard it secondhand through Mrs. Petchy, who heard it through the schoolmistress, and by the time it reached the ferry landing it had the weight of scripture: *no single burning destroys it.* Every witness keeps a copy. Every copy is signed. Forty papers in forty houses, and let Corven burn his way through the whole town if he liked, lantern in hand, door to door.

Bram picked up his pen. His writing was a ferryman's writing, big and blunt, letters like pilings driven into mud.

*I, Bram Kettle, ferryman of this crossing thirty-one years, do swear that on four occasions between March and September of 1918 I carried grain wagons across at night at the magistrate's private order, unrecorded, bound for Denner's store, and that I was paid for my silence in coin I am prepared to describe.*

He stopped. Looked at that last part. Paid for his silence. There it was, written in his own hand, the worst thing about himself, and the strange thing — the thing he would tell his grandchildren if he lived to have any — was that writing it down made it smaller. Confessed, it was a sin. Hidden, it had been becoming a life.

He signed. He blotted it. He added it to the stack under the kettle, and outside the wind came down the valley carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere upriver the ice boomed once, deep, like a drum.

### Edith

By Friday evening there were nineteen pages in the cedar chest, and Edith Marlowe sat at her desk reading them by lamplight the way other women read love letters.

Nineteen. She had built networks for ten years — suffrage circles, relief committees, the great patient web of women passing words hand to hand across counties — and she had never seen anything assemble itself this fast. But then, she thought, she had never before asked people to testify to their own lives. Votes were an abstraction. Ninepence-a-pound flour was a memory in the body. She had spent years writing coded letters about grain weights and pension disbursements, dressing the truth in petticoats so it could travel, and it turned out the truth had only been waiting for permission to walk in daylight.

Isandro came in with coal dust on their sleeves and stood in the doorway, and Edith looked up.

"Nineteen," she said.

"The schoolmistress makes twenty tonight. And Hobb."

"Hobb." Edith set down the page she was holding. "You're certain?"

"He came to the post office at closing. Asked me — asked me, of all people — whether a letter posted to a newspaper office would be opened by the censor in wartime." Isandro almost smiled. "I told him the war has eleven days left, by the latest rumor, and censors have short memories after."

"Eleven days." Edith rose and went to the window. Frost ferned the glass; beyond it the town lay under early dark, lamplit windows scattered like embers. "Eleven days, and Corven knows what's building. He'll have watched Hobb leave the courthouse today. He watches everything."

"Then we should send the copies out now. Tonight. Before—"

"No." She turned back. "Listen to me, because this is the part I got wrong for ten years. If we scatter the pages tonight, they're evidence — evidence needs a court, a commission, someone in London willing to look. Corven's friends will be the ones appointed to look. No." She tapped the cedar chest. "These stay here, in forty houses, until the war ends. Then, the hour the bells ring, they go out — every road, every rail, every hand we trust. Not evidence. Testimony. Signed by the whole town, so that burying it means burying us."

"And if he comes for them first?"

Edith met Isandro's eyes across the lamplight. Outside, far off toward the courthouse square, a lantern moved where no lantern had business moving at that hour.

"Then," she said quietly, "he'll find out what a town looks like when it has already decided."

### Hobb again

Alfred Hobb climbed the hill to Marlowe House after midnight, and the strange thing was how light he felt, as though he had been carrying the ledger on his back for eleven years and had finally, finally set it down.

At the gate he paused. Behind him, down in the town, a dog barked twice and quit. Ahead, the house windows glowed steady and warm. In his coat, against his heart, the page rustled with every breath — one page, one signature, one man's name put down in ink where the whole machinery of the court could see it.

They will call it betrayal, he thought. Let them. He had betrayed his mother for eleven years, two and sixpence at a time. This was the first loyal act of his life.

He lifted the latch, and behind him, far down the frozen street, a second lantern kindled, and a third, and began, unhurried, to climb the hill after him.
