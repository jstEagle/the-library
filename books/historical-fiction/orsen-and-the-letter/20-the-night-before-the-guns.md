## 20. The Night Before the Guns

I heard the horses before I saw the lanterns.

It was past ten. Edith had gone up an hour ago, and I was in the kitchen banking the fire for the night, my hands still smelling of coal dust from the scuttle — our scuttle, which had held a confession letter through a search and now held nothing but coal, because the confession lived in forty houses now instead of one.

That was the whole point of what we'd done. That was why I should have slept easy.

The horses came slow up Marlowe Hill, iron shoes striking sparks off the frozen ruts, and beneath them another sound: boots. Many boots. Not marching — Corven never marched, that was the thing about him, he *ambled*, he made menace look like a man out for his health — but the particular rhythm of men who had been told where to go and how many there would be when they arrived.

I went to the window and pulled the curtain back a finger's width.

Four lanterns on the drive, swinging with the walk of the men who carried them. Behind the lanterns, shapes: the clerk Hobb's replacement — a narrow young man called Pell whose eyes never settled on anything longer than a second — and two constables I knew by their coats, and behind them, at the gate, dismounting with the unhurried care of a man stepping into church, Magistrate Ambrose Corven himself.

He wore his good coat. Of course he did. He had dressed for this the way other men dressed for a funeral, and I suppose that is what it was.

"Isandro." Edith's voice behind me, low and awake. She had not gone to bed at all; she'd been sitting in the dark of her study with the lamp unlit, the way she did when she was thinking rather than reading, and the moment the hooves hit the hill she had come down without a candle, without a sound, as if she'd been waiting years for exactly these footsteps. Perhaps she had. "How many?"

"Five or six. Him."

She stood beside me at the window. In the blue dark her face looked carved, older than it was, and very calm.

"He'll want the letter," she said. "The original. He thinks there is always an original. Men like him believe everything has a master copy, kept somewhere, guarded, so that burning one paper can end a war." Her breath fogged the glass. "He cannot conceive that the master copy is Mrs. Tarn at the bakery and the ferryman's wife and a boy named Willet who nearly drowned for one of my letters."

"He'll burn the house if he doesn't find it."

"He'll *try*," Edith said. And then, quieter, in the voice she used only when something mattered more than fear: "Whatever happens at that door tonight, Isandro — you are a witness, not a shield. If they take me, you walk out the back and down to the river road and you keep walking until you're at the telegraph office in the morning."

"The office will be locked."

"Then unlock it."

We went to the door together. She would not let me open it alone, and I would not let her stand in front, and so we opened it side by side, which felt right, though neither of us said so.

* * *

Corven stood at the bottom of the steps with his lantern raised, and the light came up under his chin and made his face look like something left on a shelf too long.

"Mrs. Marlowe." A small bow, precise as arithmetic. "Galloway." He pronounced my name carefully, the way he always did — Galloway, the name I had chosen for myself at fourteen out of a newspaper serial, the name no register in any parish confirmed — and I understood suddenly that he pronounced it carefully *because* it was not real. He had checked. He had been checking since summer. "A cold night for callers."

"We weren't expecting any," Edith said.

"No. But I'm told you've been writing letters again." He climbed one step, no more. The constables fanned slightly, and Pell drifted toward the side of the house with his lantern held high, reading the windows like a man pricing curtains. "Letters containing certain statements about requisitions, pensions, and the conduct of this court. Statements which, if true, would be sedition, and which being false, are libel. Either way, Mrs. Marlowe, they are papers, and papers in wartime may be seized. You know the statute."

"I know the statute," Edith said. "I also know the armistice is expected within days. Possibly hours. Your authority expires with the war, Magistrate, and you know that too."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Arithmetic. I had watched him do it across a courtroom for years: the tallying of what a man could be made to lose against what he could be made to gain.

"There is no expiry," he said mildly, "on treason committed during wartime. But let us be reasonable. I have not come with a warrant for your person. I have come for paper. Give me the correspondence — the drafts, the originals, whatever you keep — and tomorrow morning this remains a private matter between neighbors. Refuse, and I must search, and a search of a widow's house in winter..." He spread one gloved hand, regretful. "Things get broken. Reputations especially."

And there it was, laid out plain: the offer he had already made me once in his study, the same coin, priced for her. Papers, silence, passage. Everything for the one thing he could not survive losing, which was not the letters themselves but the *fact* of them — witnesses, signatures, names.

Edith didn't answer him. She stepped back from the doorway, which I thought at first was retreat, until I saw her face, and understood it was invitation.

"You may search," she said. "You have the legal right, I won't dispute it. Search every drawer. But I warn you, Magistrate, and I say it loud so your men hear it—" her voice carried down the hill, clear as a bell over ice— "*whatever you carry out of this house tonight, there are a hundred more copies of it in this town, each one signed, each one witnessed, and each one of those signatories knows your face.*"

The lanterns wavered. One of the constables glanced at another.

Corven smiled the smile he used on juries. "Empty talk."

"Then search," Edith said, "and we shall see how empty."

He waved Pell forward, and Pell went up the steps past us into the hall with his lantern, and the constables followed, and for a long while the house filled with the sounds I had learned to hate in November: drawers, cupboards, the scrape of furniture, boots overhead. Edith and I stood in the parlor with our hands folded like people at a graveside, and she watched the ceiling as if she could see through it, and I watched the front door, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left to hide from each other and nothing left to say that hadn't been written down and copied four times.

They found the empty desk drawers. They found Edith's ordinary correspondence — invitations, a subscription notice, a condolence card — which Pell read aloud in a flat voice as though hoping scandal might be hiding in a funeral. They found nothing else, because there was nothing else. Every page that mattered had walked out of this house on foot, in aprons and coat linings and hymnals, over three weeks, to a hundred hearths.

It took them forty minutes. When Pell came down shaking his head, Corven was standing in the parlor doorway looking at Edith with an expression I had never seen on him before — not defeat. Recalculation.

"A hundred copies," he said softly.

"At least," Edith said. "I stopped counting at Christmas."

"And every signature a name I'd have to arrest. A hundred arrests, Mrs. Marlowe, in a town of six hundred, three days before peace breaks out and London sends men with questions." He shook his head slowly, almost admiring. "You've built yourself a fortress out of stationery."

"I built it out of witnesses," Edith said. "Stationery is only how you tell them apart."

For a moment — I saw it, I was standing right there — Corven looked past her, at me. Just for a second. And in that second there was no magistrate in his face at all, only a man doing sums that wouldn't close, a man discovering that the driver he had offered papers and passage and a new name had been worth ten times what he'd priced, and had known it, and had refused anyway.

"Galloway," he said. "You could still—"

"No," I said.

Just that. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. He studied me a moment longer, the way a man studies a bridge he's decided not to cross tonight, and then he put on his gloves.

"Ladies. Sir." A bow to Edith, a nod to me, mockery folded inside courtesy. "Merry Christmas, when it comes. If it comes."

He turned down the steps. And that was when I saw what I had half hoped for and never dared believe: lights. Down the hill, along the frozen street, lanterns — dozens of them, moving, converging on Marlowe House from the lanes and the yards and the bakery and the ferry cottages, drawn up the hill through the cold like moths that had finally agreed on a flame. Mrs. Tarn first, in her shawl, flour still on her sleeves. The ferryman and his wife. Old Cobb with his bad leg propped on a stick. Willet the boy, holding a lantern up as tall as his arm would reach, and beside him his mother with her hand on his shoulder. And behind them more, and behind those more, faces I knew and faces I only half knew, sixty of them, eighty, a hundred, standing silent in Corven's lantern light with the breath rising off them like steam off cattle, and not one of them speaking, because none of them needed to. They were the record. They had come to be seen counting themselves.

Corven stopped on the last step. He looked down the hill at all of it for a long moment — the lanterns, the faces, the town he had skimmed and starved and assumed was asleep — and I watched him understand, in the slow way powerful men understand, that the ledger had turned, that he was the entry now, and the column read *witnessed.*

"Good evening, Magistrate," Edith called down, pleasantly. "Mind the ice."

He gathered his reins without a word. The constables fell in. Pell scurried. The lanterns of the law withdrew down the drive, and the lanterns of the town closed behind them like water closing over a stone, escorting them — that was the word I'd use, escorting — all the way to the bottom of the hill before dispersing to their homes, unhurried, visible, deliberate.

Edith let out a breath that shook, just slightly, at the end.

"Well," she said. "He came for a bonfire and found a census."

* * *

But it wasn't over, and we knew it before the last lantern reached the square.

Because Corven had paused, at his gate, and spoken to Pell — we couldn't hear the words, but we saw the gesture, the sharp downward jerk of the chin toward the far end of the high street — and Pell had peeled away from the others and gone at a half-run through the cold, and a few minutes later we saw it from the upstairs window: a light kindling in the telegraph office. Then going out. Then a figure crossing in front of the glass, and the shutter bars going home, one after another, loud enough to hear across the frozen square.

"They've locked the office," I said.

"Of course they have." Edith's voice was flat and tired and absolutely unsentimental. "He can't burn the testimony, so he'll freeze it. No wire out of this town means no account reaches London before the files burn — and the files burn the week the war ends, you can bet the pension ledgers on it. He doesn't need to win anymore, Isandro. He needs twenty-four hours. Maybe less."

I stood at the window with my forehead nearly touching the glass, looking down at the telegraph office squatting black and barred at the corner of the square, and I felt the shape of the next hours settle onto me like a coat cut exactly to my size.

There was a pole behind the office. There was always a pole — I'd driven past it twice a day all autumn, the wire humming in wind, the maintenance ladder spikes rusting up its flank where the line-men climbed in October to tighten the insulators. I knew the height of it. I knew the frost on the rungs. I knew, in the way drivers know things without deciding to, that a man could go up that pole in the dark, and that the line itself ran south all night regardless of who held the keys to the office below, and that a key was only a lock, and a lock was only a suggestion to someone who had spent his whole life being invisible.

"Isandro." Edith's hand on my sleeve. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"It's armistice eve," I said. "Or near enough. The bells could come tonight, or tomorrow noon. Either way, the guns stop soon, and when they stop, his friends in the county start feeding ledgers into stoves by lunchtime." I turned and looked at her, and my heart was going hard but my voice stayed level, and I remember thinking, distantly, that this was the first time in my life I had ever sounded like a person rather than a function. "So it has to go out tonight. On the wire. Under my name."

"Under your name," she repeated.

"Isandro Galloway. Witness." I almost smiled. "It's the only name I've got, Edith. It isn't registered anywhere, it isn't legal anywhere, Corven says it isn't even real. So let's make it real the only way a name ever gets made real." I picked up my coat from the chair, and from the lining I drew the notebook — sewn there since November, warm from my body, full of requisition figures and pension sums and dates and initials, every page of Corven's ledger that I'd carried out of his own records in my memory and my ink. "Let's send it."

Down in the square, the telegraph office sat dark and barred. Behind it, invisible in the night, the pole rose up into the freezing air, and the southbound wire sang faintly above the town, running all the way to London, waiting.

I wound my scarf to my chin and took up the lantern, unlit.

The bells hadn't started yet.
