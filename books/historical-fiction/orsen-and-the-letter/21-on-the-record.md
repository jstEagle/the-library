## 21. On the Record

The telegraph office was locked, but the wire itself ran along a pole at the corner of Mill Street, and the key that mattered most in the world hung inside the operator's coat, and the operator was asleep.

I had counted on the sleep. Hobb had told me the schedule — young Petch took the midnight shift only when there was military traffic to move, and with the armistice rumored for days, the line had gone quiet as held breath. Everyone waiting. Everyone listening. The whole country leaning its ear against the wire like I was about to do.

Edith walked me to the corner and stopped where the lamplight ended.

"You don't have to be the one," she said. Not for the first time. "Any of us could learn the code by morning."

"None of you could climb it by morning." I looked up. The pole rose black against the blacker sky, crossarms silhouetted like the ribs of something washed ashore. "And it isn't about who could. It's about who should."

She was quiet a moment. Snow ticked down through the dark, fine as flour.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my sister wanted to sign her name to a petition. Just her name, nothing else. They told her a woman's name on a document was like a footprint on water." Her breath clouded. "I have spent forty years proving them wrong, Isandro, and I never once had to risk my neck on a pole in November to do it."

"That's because they watched you." I pulled the notebook from my coat — the real one, the one sewn into the lining since the river, since the night I came out of the shallows half-dead and left my old self under the ice. "They've never once thought to watch me. That's the whole of my life's advantage, Edith. Tonight is the last night I get to spend it."

She put her hand on my arm. Her gloves were thin; I felt the grip all the way through my sleeve.

"Isandro Galloway," she said.

It was the first time she'd said the whole of it out loud. My name. The one I'd chosen in the orphanage ledger's margins when no one gave me one, the one Corven had offered to make legal for the price of my silence, the one I'd refused because a name handed down by a corrupt man is just a leash with better stitching.

"Yes," I said. "That's me. Say it again after the bells."

* * *

The climbing was the easy part. Ten years of harness work, of swinging up onto cart beds and carriage roofs in all weather — my hands knew poles the way priests know psalters. I went up past the first crossarm, past the second, until I reached the insulator where the town's line tapped into the trunk route south. My boots found their purchase on the spikes. The wind up there had teeth. Below me, Marlowe Street lay empty and blue-white, every window dark except the church, where a single candle burned in the vestry — old Reverend Ainsley keeping his vigil, though he didn't yet know what he was keeping it for.

The test set hung from my shoulder on a strap Hobb had smuggled out of the office three nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth, looking for all the world like a lunch tin. I clipped the leads to the line. My fingers were already stiffening. I flexed them, blew on them, and listened.

The wire was alive. Somewhere far off, traffic murmured — a station calling a station, routine business, the nervous heartbeat of an empire waiting for peace. I waited for a gap. When it came, I pressed the key.

I had rehearsed this. In the coal cellar, tapping the rhythm against my knee while Edith read the testimony aloud. Four hundred words, distilled from two years of letters, one stolen ledger, one defected clerk, and the signed pages of forty-one townspeople. Grain requisitioned at famine rates and resold at famine prices. Pension funds skimmed while widows queued at Corven's own window for handouts from money he'd taken from them. Dates. Figures. Names.

But rehearsal is not the same as cold fingers on a brass key thirty feet above frozen ground with the man you're accusing asleep four streets away and his men watching the roads.

I started anyway.

*WITNESS ACCOUNT FOLLOW STOP*

My hand shook so badly on the first sentence that I garbled it and had to send it twice. Then something happened that I hadn't planned for: the sending steadied me. Each character was a small act, a small choice, tap by tap by tap, and there was no room in the rhythm for fear. This was what reading had always given me, hidden away all those years — the sense that marks on a surface could carry a whole human being across distance. Except now the marks were mine. Now I was the letter.

Halfway through the grain figures, the wire woke up.

*WHO IS SENDING STOP IDENTIFY YOURSELF STOP*

A dispatcher, somewhere south. Probably bored. Possibly alarmed. I kept my hand moving.

*CIVILIAN TESTIMONY RE MAGISTRATE CORVEN ASHFORD DISTRICT STOP FULL ACCOUNT FOLLOWS STOP*

*THIS LINE IS RESTRICTED WAR TRAFFIC ONLY STOP CEASE TRANSMISSION STOP*

I ceased nothing. The pension figures went out into the dark — sums I could recite faster than my own birthday, sums Hobb had wept over when he brought me the missing page. Somewhere in France, if the rumors were true, men were counting down hours to silence. Here I was counting down characters. Both of us, in our different ways, trying to get the truth said before something stopped us.

*YOU ARE IN VIOLATION OF POSTAL AND TELEGRAPH REGULATIONS STOP GIVE YOUR NAME STOP*

And there it was. The question underneath every question of my life. Who are you. What are you called. Whose permission do you carry.

My hands were numb past feeling. My feet were blocks of wood in wet boots. Below me Edith stood in the snow like a sentinel, and up the street, faintly, I could see other shapes gathering — Hobb with his collar turned up, Mrs. Tann with her boy beside her, the ferryman Dawes holding a lantern he made no attempt to hide. Forty-one signatures standing in the cold because I'd asked them to be visible tonight, all of us together, so that no single arrest, no single fire, no single burning drawer could ever hold all of us again.

I answered the wire.

*ISANDRO GALLOWAY STOP WITNESS STOP*

I stared at what my own hand had sent. It hung in the air between the insulators, humming faintly in the current, and I swear the word *witness* rang longer than the rest.

I had never signed anything before. Not once in thirty-one years. Every document of my existence was either blank or falsified or written by someone else about someone they pretended I wasn't. And here it was, going down the wire at the speed of light toward London, toward newspapers, toward investigators, toward history — my name, attached, accountable, unable to be taken back.

Corven had offered me a name in exchange for silence. I had gotten a better deal. I'd traded silence for a name, and nobody owned this one but me.

I finished the testimony. The last figure went out, then the closing lines:

*SIGNED BY 41 RESIDENTS OF ASHFORD STOP COPIES HELD SEPARATELY BY EACH STOP THIS ACCOUNT ENTERED INTO PUBLIC RECORD NIGHT OF ARMISTICE EVE STOP END STOP*

The wire went quiet. The dispatcher did not answer. Perhaps he was fetching a supervisor. Perhaps he was copying everything down himself, because dispatchers were people too, and people had mothers and brothers in uniform and long memories about men like magistrates.

Then, far to the south, faint as a pulse, the wire spoke one more time.

*RECEIVED STOP RELAYING LONDON NOW STOP GOD SPEED STOP*

I sat down hard on the crossarm. The wind pushed at me and I let it, gripping the pole with my knees, and I laughed — a ragged, freezing, disbelieving laugh that tore steam out of my mouth and scattered it. Down below, Edith's face tipped up toward me, pale smudge and darker eyes, and I raised one hand, and she raised hers, and neither of us needed the code to say what passed between us.

* * *

The bells began while I was still coming down.

I heard them first as a tremor in the wire itself — some sympathetic shiver running the length of the line from whatever distant exchange had first received the news — and then, a moment later, the real thing. St. Brannoc's, single bell at first, then joined by a second, then the full ring, wild and unpatterned and glorious, the sound of a ringer who has abandoned the method books entirely and is simply making noise because noise is all joy can manage at short notice.

Doors opened along Marlowe Street. Lamplight spilled out in yellow rectangles. Someone shouted from a window — *"It's over! The war is over!"* — and the shout was caught and thrown from house to house like a bucket at a fire. Down the hill, toward the square, I could hear the sound building, voices multiplying, and somewhere in it a fiddle starting up badly and being forgiven instantly.

Hobb climbed halfway up the pole to meet me, which was unnecessary and typical of him.

"It's done?" he shouted over the bells.

"It's done. London has it. Signed."

He gripped my forearm, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there are things bells say better than men.

When I reached the ground, the crowd had come up the hill. Mrs. Tann was crying and laughing at once, her boy — the boy from the river, the one whose life I'd bought with a soaked coat and a cracked rib — was dancing around with another child, both of them beating a tin kettle like a drum. Dawes raised his lantern to me. Old Reverend Ainsley had abandoned his candle and stood in the churchyard gate with his hat off, letting the snow land in his hair.

Edith came through all of it straight to me. She took both my hands in hers, and her gloves were gone — bare fingers, freezing cold, gripping mine like she'd never let go.

"Well, Isandro Galloway," she said. "Witness."

"Witness," I said.

"The record will show your name."

"The record will show all of ours. That was rather the point."

Behind her, up the hill, a light had come on in the telegraph office. Petch, woken by the bells, stumbling to his key, finding the logbook already annotated in a strange hand. He'd find Hobb's explanation in the morning, along with forty-one names and a story he'd tell for the rest of his life. Let him. The wire was ours for exactly as long as it needed to be.

Edith squeezed my hands once, hard, and turned to look down at the celebrating town, and her voice, when it came, was quiet enough that only I could hear it under the bells.

"He'll know by dawn," she said. "Corven. The dispatcher's report, the logbook, Petch's account — it'll reach him before his tea is poured. And a man like Corven doesn't hear a confession like that. He hears a declaration of war."

"I know."

"He'll come for the witnesses. Starting with the ones easiest to reach."

"I know that too."

"And yet you're smiling."

I looked at my hands — chapped, frozen, trembling, mine. I looked at the town spilling its light into the street, forty-one signatures breathing and cheering and crying in the snow. I looked at Edith, who had built a network out of envelopes and trust, and Hobb, who had crossed the floor at the cost of everything, and the boy with the kettle drum, who owed me nothing and cheered me anyway.

"I'm smiling," I said, "because for the first time in my life, when he comes, I'll be home when he does. Named. Present. On the record. There's no version of his revenge that can make me invisible again — and that means there's no version of it I can't survive."

The bells crashed on. The snow fell. And down in the dark at the edge of the square, where the lanterns didn't reach, I saw — or thought I saw — a still shape in a good coat, watching our celebration with the patience of a man checking a ledger.

By morning, Corven would know everything.

By morning, so would everyone else.
