## 22. The Bridge Opens

### Isandro

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday in March, all at once, the way winter things go — not gradually but in a single groaning surrender that woke half the town at four in the morning. I stood at my window above the livery and watched the plates of it grind downstream under a moon going grey, and I thought: *there it goes.* The old life, floating off toward whatever sea receives such things.

I had not slept much that winter. Nobody had. But the nights since the armistice had been different — not sleepless with fear but sleepless with a strange, humming alertness, like a wire after current passes through it. I had signed my name. It was down somewhere now, in an office in the regional capital, in print that could not be steamed open or burned: *Isandro Galloway, witness.* Galloway because Edith said every man needs a surname and the ferryman's grandmother had been one, and because when she asked me if it suited, I found that it did, the way a coat suits once you stop flinching from the weight of it.

The bridge was another matter. The county engineer came out in the second week of April with two assistants and a notebook of his own, and they spent three days walking its length, tapping the iron trusses with small hammers, listening. On the third day he pronounced it sound, which surprised no one more than it should have, since the bridge had been closing every winter for thirty years out of habit as much as danger.

"It opens Saturday," he told the crowd that had gathered at the near abutment, most of them out of nothing better to do. "First ferry traffic Monday, if the ferryman's got her patched."

The ferryman, old Tam Breck, spat thoughtfully into the current. "She's patched," he said. "Question is whether I am."

There was laughter. And I stood at the back of it, hands in my pockets, and understood that I was standing there as one of them — not beside the crowd, not behind it, not invisible within it, but *of* it. A man people nodded to. Mrs. Alder, whose boy I'd pulled half-drowned off the ice in January, nodded to me now with something in her face that was not gratitude exactly; it was more durable than gratitude. It was the look of someone who had decided where I belonged and would hear no argument.

Corven's house stood dark on the hill. It had stood dark since the morning the investigators came.

* * *

They arrived by the road, not the river — three men and a woman from the Ministry of Pensions Inquiry, in a motorcar that had to be pushed the last mile through the mud, so that the whole town turned out to watch them push it, which I suspect did more for the town's morale than anything the inquiry itself accomplished. Edith had telegraphed ahead, of course. She had been telegraphing for weeks: copies of testimony, Hobb's ledger pages, the account we had tapped out over the wire on armistice eve, already printed in the capital papers under headlines I kept folded in my coat pocket like letters from home.

*LOCAL MAGISTRATE HELD — WARTIME REQUISITIONS PROBED.*

*PENSION FUNDS: WHERE DID THE DIFFERENCE GO?*

Corven met them on his own steps. I will say this for him: he did not run. He stood in his good coat with his hands clasped behind him and received their warrant the way he had received petitions for twenty years, with a thin courtesy that had always been the sharpest thing about him. I watched from the bottom of the drive, because Edith was there and because some part of me needed to see it — needed it the way a man who has carried a stone needs to set it down and look at it.

He saw me too. Of course he did. His eyes found me across forty yards of gravel and held for a moment, and what passed between us was not hatred. It was accounting. He had offered me everything I had never had — a name, papers, passage — and I had refused, and he had never understood why, and I think that failure of understanding hurt him more than the warrant did. Men like Corven believe everyone has a price. It is how they know the world is real.

Then they took him down the drive past me, and up close I saw that his collar was not as crisp as usual, and that his hands, clasped before him now instead of behind, were trembling very slightly, and I felt — this is the truth, and I have made my peace with it — I felt almost nothing. Not triumph. Not pity. Just the wind off the river, and the smell of mud, and the sense of a chapter ending that had been ending for months.

"Driver," he said as he passed, because to the last he preferred to address people as their functions.

"Magistrate," I said.

And then the motorcar took him away, and Mrs. Alder's boy threw a clod of mud at it and missed, and nobody cheered, because cheering is for victories and this felt more like weather finally changing.

### Edith

I had imagined this morning for eleven years. That is not an exaggeration; I can date it precisely, to the day they ruled against my sister, when I stood in the back of Corven's courtroom with my hat in my hands like any obedient woman and understood, with a clarity that has never left me, that the law in this valley was a door with Corven's hand on the latch. For eleven years I had built the case letter by letter, ally by ally, in phrasing so careful that even I sometimes forgot what it concealed. I had imagined the arrest a hundred ways: dramatic, humiliating, violent. I had imagined myself shouting something across the square.

What I had never imagined was how quiet it would be. How ordinary. A man gets into a car. A door closes. The great machinery of consequence turns out to be mostly paperwork.

Isandro drove me home from the square. We passed Corven's dark house on the way, windows blank as a skull's sockets, and Isandro looked at it without slowing.

"You're not gloating," I observed.

"Gloating costs something," they said. They still used *they*, in their own head — I knew this because they had told me, in the terrible conversation of December, that they had never been able to afford a self at all, let alone decide what kind. "I spent six years being nothing, Madam Marlowe. Gloating feels like spending money I haven't earned."

"And what have you earned?"

They considered that, hands easy on the wheel, the horse stepping along at a pace that said spring as clearly as any calendar. "A name," they said finally. "A vote. A debt to about forty people in this town." A pause. "And a job, if it's still wanted."

We pulled up before Marlowe House and sat a moment in the harness-creaking quiet. I had known this question was coming. I had dreaded it, honestly, more than the inquiry, more than Corven's men at the door with their lanterns. Because the honest answer was that I wanted Isandro to stay, and wanting things had been a dangerous business for me since my sister died, and because the other honest answer was that I would not ask. I had spent years asking people to risk themselves. I would not do it again, not even gently, not even for the best driver in the county.

"The bridge opens tomorrow," I said instead.

"So I heard."

"After that, you could go anywhere. There are positions in the city. References — mine carry weight now, oddly enough." I looked straight ahead at my own front door, at the brass knocker Hobb had polished the week before, unprompted, in a fit of something he refused to call feeling. "You could be anyone. That's rather the point of what we did."

Isandro was quiet long enough that I finally turned to look at them. Their face in the spring light had changed over the winter — not older exactly, though the gray at the temple said otherwise; more *assembled*. Like something that had been kept in pieces and was now, for the first time, all in one place.

"I spent my whole life being anyone," they said. "It wasn't much of a promotion."

* * *

That evening the town gathered in the schoolhouse — not by proclamation but by gravity, the way it had begun gathering all winter, ever since the night we stood in Corven's lantern light with our signed pages and dared him to burn us. Hobb was there with his ledger, which the inquiry had copied and returned to him like a relic. Mrs. Alder was there with her boy, who had fully recovered and acquired, in the process, a permanent attachment to me that I confess I encouraged shamelessly. Tam Breck was there, arguing about ferry schedules with anyone who'd listen. Forty people, fifty, the room warm with wet wool and lamp oil.

Hobb stood up. This was remarkable in itself; in November, Hobb could not have stood up in a room of five people. He smoothed the ledger with both hands the way a priest smooths cloth.

"I've been asked," he said, in the voice of a man reciting something rehearsed at a mirror, "to read the names into the record. All the ones who signed testimony. So it's said out loud, here, where Corven's court used to sit in judgment — well, not here, but you take my meaning."

He read them. Name after name, farm after street, and each one answered — *here* — the way congregations answer, until the room sounded like a roll call of the living, which is what it was. When he reached the end of the list he paused, and looked around, and found Isandro sitting near the stove.

"One name more," Hobb said. "Read into the record first, actually, over the wire, the night before the guns stopped. Signed by hand." He cleared his throat. "Isandro Galloway. Witness."

"*Here,*" Isandro said.

And the room answered back — not the formal echo of the roll call but something rougher, warmer, half laughter, half the sound people make when a bell they've been waiting for finally rings. Mrs. Alder's boy shouted it loudest of all. Isandro sat very still through it, looking at the floor, and I saw their jaw work once, and I understood that being seen could hurt nearly as much as being erased, and that they were learning to bear it, the same way I had learned to bear losing, one loud ordinary day at a time.

### Isandro

Saturday morning the bridge opened, and I drove Edith across it, because she insisted on being among the first and because someone had to hold the horse's head while Tam Breck pretended the whole ceremony was beneath him. The planks rang under the wheels. Below, the river ran high and brown and quick with snowmelt, carrying the last of the winter off to sea, and on the far bank the road went on south toward the city, toward the inquiries and the newspapers and the wide world where a person named Isandro Galloway could, in theory, vanish into it entirely and start again.

Edith stood at the middle of the span and looked down at the water a long time. I knew what she was thinking. Her sister had crossed this bridge a hundred times; her sister's ruin had been sealed in the courthouse on the hill; every plank of it was memory. But when she turned to me her face was dry and level and quite decided, which is the only way I have ever seen it at moments that mattered.

"Well, Galloway," she said. "You've a vote to cast come June. First election of your life. Any thoughts on how you'll use it?"

"I thought I might vote for that Marlowe woman," I said. "I hear she's trouble."

"She is," Edith said, "a great deal of it," and for a moment she laughed — really laughed, the sound startling both of us, ringing off the iron trusses like the planks ringing under the wheels — and then she composed herself and looked at me with the expression she used when a matter required settling beyond appeal.

"You didn't answer my question from yesterday," she said. "About staying. You deflected into jokes about voting, which is a habit of yours I'm cataloguing."

The river went on below us. The town lay behind, smoke rising straight from the chimneys in the still air, and ahead the road ran open and empty and free, and I looked at it honestly for the last time — looked at the leaving the way you look at a door you could still walk through — and found that it had quietly become just a road.

"I'm staying," I said. "Not because I have nowhere else. Because I choose here. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I'd thank you to remember it when you start working me dawn to midnight come campaign season."

"Dawn to midnight," Edith agreed gravely, "with Sundays off for correspondence."

We drove on across. The planks rang. Behind us Tam Breck was already arguing with the engineer about load limits, and ahead of us the road unrolled, muddy and rutted and utterly ordinary, going everywhere and nowhere, and I sat up straight on the box with my name in my mouth like a coin I had earned fair, and drove into the spring.
