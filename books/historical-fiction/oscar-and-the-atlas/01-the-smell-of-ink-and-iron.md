## 1. The Smell of Ink and Iron

The trouble with ink, Coral Dravenmoor's father used to say, is that it never forgets what you've made it say.

He said this the way other men said things about weather or the price of tallow — a small wisdom offered up like a coin to a beggar who hadn't asked for alms. Coral had been eleven at the time, standing on a stool so she could reach the composing stick, her fingers black to the second knuckle, and she had thought: *Good. Let it remember. Somebody in this city ought to.*

Fourteen years later, she was no longer sure she agreed.

She woke before dawn on the first of December, as she always did, because the shop did not heat itself and neither did the kettle, and because Master Quillon paid her by the page and not by the hour, which meant every minute spent shivering was a minute spent poor. The garret above the print shop had one window, and through it Vellmark lay gray and still under its first real frost — the river steaming faintly where the mill races ran warm, the rooftops furred white, the bell tower of Saint Aldric's cutting the sky like a knife someone had forgotten to put away.

From somewhere below, already, came the sound of Master Quillon coughing.

Coral dressed in the dark, which was not difficult, since everything she owned was dark, and went down the stairs that smelled of lamp oil and cold iron. The shop occupied the ground floor of the house on Tanner's Lane — though *shop* was a generous word for it. It was a long room with low beams that Quillon struck his head on daily, an old Albion press that groaned like a penitent when worked too fast, and along the back wall, the type cases: row upon row of shallow wooden drawers, each divided into dozens of little compartments, each compartment holding its small metal congregation of letters.

Half of them had been her father's.

That was the thing about Vellmark. It was a city built on printing the way some cities were built on fishing or wool — the river brought the paper barges up from the south, the university brought readers, and the governor brought decrees, and all three needed setting into type. There were nine presses in the city proper and four more across the water in Saltmarsh, and every one of them knew the particular problem of Governor Atlas Corvane, which was that he required a great deal of printing and permitted very little of it to be true.

Coral banked the stove, filled the kettle, and let herself into the composing room while the coals caught. Her breath hung in the air. She lit two candles, then thought better of it and blew one out. Wax cost money. Everything cost money. Money was the reason she set type for whoever paid, and asked no questions, and kept her head down in a city where keeping your head down had lately become something of a civic virtue.

The morning's job sat waiting on the stone bench: a broadside for the Customs House, announcing revised tariffs on river goods. Four hundred words of bureaucratic porridge. She'd set half of it yesterday evening before her eyes gave out.

She pulled the case toward her — the upper case, capitals and figures, oak worn smooth at the edges by forty years of hands, most of them her father's — and began.

*By order of the Honorable the Governor of Vellmark and its Dependencies...*

Her fingers moved without her mind's participation, which was the great mercy of the trade. Pick, place; pick, place; the little brass-bodied letters slotting into the composing stick in reverse, mirror-writing, so that they'd come out right when pressed. After fourteen years she could read mirrored type faster than plain, which her mother had once said was a sign of a misspent youth, and which her mother had said while mending shirts for the garrison laundry, so Coral felt entitled to weigh the opinion accordingly.

*...tariffs henceforth levied upon all grain, salt, and dyestuffs passing the Hallow Bridge toll...*

Hallow Bridge. Her stick paused, just for a moment, over the word.

There had been something in the market talk last week. Something about Hallow Bridge, upriver, where the toll had tripled since summer and the farmers had begun going around it by the ford, and then there had been soldiers at the ford, and then — well. Then the talk had stopped, which in Vellmark was how you knew the talk had gotten interesting. Talk in this city behaved like a stream in hard frost: it didn't vanish, it just went underground, and you could hear it running if you knelt and put your ear to the cobbles.

Coral did not kneel. Coral set tariffs.

She was twenty-five years old, and she had learned the trade at her father's elbow until the winter she was eleven, when the fever came through the Tanner's Quarter and took him in four days along with the cooper's son and the midwife's sister and roughly one soul in ten besides. He had left her the type cases, a knowledge of ink mixing that bordered on the alchemical, and a single piece of advice delivered in the intervals between coughing: *Keep your hands busy and your mouth shut, little wren, and they'll leave you alone.*

They had left her alone. Mostly. Insofar as anyone in Vellmark was left alone, which was to say: watched, taxed, and occasionally conscripted to shovel snow, but not actively persecuted, which was more than could be said for the Kessinger family, or the pamphleteer who'd been flogged on the Corn Exchange steps in October, or — according to rumor, and rumor in Vellmark wore a long coat and many pockets — rather a lot of people upriver near a certain bridge.

The kettle sang. She poured the water over the grounds, wrapped her hands around the chipped brown pot, and allowed herself the luxury of standing still for exactly as long as it took to drink half of it.

"Dravenmoor." Quillon's voice from the doorway, preceded by his cough. "You're here."

"You're observant," she said.

"I'm dying," he said, with the satisfaction of a man reporting excellent news about himself. He lowered himself onto the stool by the press, unfolding his long frame joint by joint like a carpenter's rule. Quillon was sixty if he was a day, thin as a lath, with a face like a disappointed horse and a cough that had been killing him for six years with no success whatsoever. He owned the shop, the press, the lease, and — nominally — Coral's labor, though in practice their arrangement had drifted over the years into something neither of them had ever named: she ran the composing room, he handled the customers and the accounts, and between them they produced whatever the city required of them, which was usually dull and occasionally sinister and always paid.

"What have we today?" he asked.

"Tariffs. Then the funeral notices for the Aldric's register, then the playbills for the Theater Royal if the theater remembers it owes us."

"The theater always remembers. Remembering costs nothing. Paying is another matter." He peered at the case she'd pulled. "Whose type is that?"

"Father's."

"Mm." He looked at the old oak case a moment longer than the remark required. There was something in his face she couldn't read — or rather, something she could read and chose not to, the way you choose not to look directly at a dog that's been growling at the empty corner of a room. "Set the tariffs first. The Customs man comes at ten and he counts commas."

"He counts commas?"

"He counts everything. That's the whole of his personality. God grant him a long life and a distant posting."

She smiled despite herself and went back to work. This was the shape of mornings: the stove ticking, the candles — candle — burning, Quillon wheezing over his ledger, the letters marching into the stick. Outside, the city woke in its careful way: cart wheels on frozen mud, the fishwives' cries starting up down by the quay, a drum somewhere in the direction of the Citadel, beating the garrison's morning muster. All of it filtered through the window glass as though the world were happening to somebody else, in a story she'd been reading and might put down.

It was, she would think later, the last ordinary morning she would ever have. But ordinary mornings never announce themselves. They sit quietly among the others, wearing the same clothes, and only afterward do you pick them out of the crowd and realize what they were.

* * *

The Customs man came at ten and counted commas, finding one missing, which Coral restored with the expression of a woman being robbed. He left. A boy arrived with copy for the funeral notices — old Mistress Vane, aged eighty-one, beloved of all — and Coral set it in the smaller pica while Quillon argued with the theater's man about payment terms, an argument both parties had conducted monthly for three years and enjoyed immensely.

At noon she ate bread and cheese standing at the stone sink. At one o'clock the light through the south window turned the dust in the composing room to gold, and she pulled the tariff broadside — two hundred impressions, clean, the ink biting properly into the cheap rag paper — and stacked them for delivery.

And it was in the middle of the afternoon, with the second job half-set and the stove needing wood, that the knock came.

Not a customer's knock. Customers knocked twice, briskly, the knock of people with money and opinions. This was three slow raps, spaced out, the knock of someone who wanted to be heard by the person inside and no one else.

Quillon went to the door himself, which he rarely bothered with. Coral kept her head over her case, but she listened, because listening was free and everything else in Vellmark had a tax on it.

A murmur of voices. Quillon's, low and — she frowned at the type — *careful*, in a way she'd never heard it. The visitor's voice she couldn't catch at all except once, a single word that carried:

"—Hallow—"

Then the door closed, and boots receded down Tanner's Lane, and Quillon stood in the middle of his own shop looking like a man who has just been handed a package and told not to look inside it.

"Quillon?" she said. "Who was that?"

"A customer." He crossed to the press and stood beside it with his hand on the frame, the way a nervous man stands beside furniture. "For next week. Nothing for you tonight — go home early, the light's failing anyway."

"It's barely past three."

"Then go home early and enjoy it. Wren—" He stopped. Started again. "Lock your door tonight."

She looked at him. He looked at the press. Somewhere outside, the garrison drum was beating again, though there was no muster scheduled at this hour, and the sound of it seemed to make the frost on the windows tremble.

"Master Quillon," Coral said slowly, "in fourteen years you have never once told me to lock my door. You've told me to water the ink, and to stop reading over your shoulder, and that my father's cases are worth more than I am — which we've discussed — but never that."

"And I'm not telling you now," he said. "I'm suggesting. There's a difference. Suggestions are free." He attempted a smile, which on Quillon's face was less an expression than a structural failure. "Go home, Dravenmoor. Set nothing else today."

She went. She took her coat and her half-pot of tea money and climbed the stairs to the lane, and paused at the corner where the lamplighter was making his rounds, touching flame to the wicks one by one so that Tanner's Lane bloomed with little orange eyes against the blue dusk.

Behind her, through the shop window, she could see Quillon still standing by the press, motionless, his hand flat on the frame as though taking its pulse.

Ahead of her, the city lay down toward the river, chimney smoke rising straight in the windless cold, the Citadel on its hill with its lamps already lit, watchful, patient. Somewhere upriver, past the dark bend where the water narrowed, stood Hallow Bridge, and whatever had happened there, and whoever had been silenced for talking about it.

*Keep your hands busy and your mouth shut,* her father had said, *and they'll leave you alone.*

Coral walked home through the freezing dusk with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, and told herself, as she had told herself for fourteen years, that the advice still held. That she was a printer's daughter and a compositor by trade, and that type was type, and orders were orders, and the safest place in a city full of secrets was behind a composing stick, setting other people's words in mirror-image, where nothing was ever quite what it looked like and nobody expected it to be.

She locked her door. She banked her fire. And she lay awake in the dark listening to the frost creak on the window glass, telling herself that tomorrow would be tariffs and funeral notices and playbills, same as every day —

— which was, of course, precisely the sort of lie that ink never forgets.
