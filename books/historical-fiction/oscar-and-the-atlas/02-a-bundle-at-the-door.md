## 2. A Bundle at the Door

The knock came at half past eleven, and it was not a knock at all.

Coral Dravenmoor heard it from the little room above the shop where she slept — a soft, wet sound against the door downstairs, like a glove thrown by someone without the strength to throw it properly. Not the fist of a customer. Customers knocked twice, briskly, the way money knocks.

She lay still and counted to thirty, as her father had taught her to count when strange sounds came in the night. *One for fear, two for sense, three to see if it comes again.* The frost ticked in the window glass. A cart rattled somewhere on Tanner's Bridge, far off, its driver drunk enough to be singing — badly, and in the old language, which meant he was either very poor or very brave, and in Vellmark those were lately the same thing.

Nothing came again. But something had been left.

Coral dressed in the dark, which took no time, because she had learned to sleep half-dressed the way sailors sleep and soldiers sleep and printers' daughters sleep in cities that change governments the way other cities change their minds. She took the candle and the poker — the poker first, actually; she was her father's daughter down to the order of things — and went down the stairs that smelled of ink and tallow and forty years of her family's stubbornness.

The shop was black as a closed eye. She did not light the lamps. Light through the front windows was an invitation now, and invitations in Vellmark were being answered by men in grey coats with white armbands who wrote everything down.

She unbolted the door and pulled it open six inches, poker raised, candle held high.

There was nobody. There was snow falling straight down in that windless way that makes a city feel held in a cupped hand. And there, on the step, in the shallow drift the door's overhang had spared —

— there was a bundle.

Her first thought, God forgive her, was that it was paper. A delivery of stock paper left in the night, which happened sometimes when the paper merchants wanted to avoid the customs men at the morning gate. Her second thought was that paper does not breathe.

The bundle breathed. Small. Quick. Like a bird in a box.

Coral stood in the doorway for the length of one long breath of her own, and she would remember afterward — all her life, she would remember — the exact quality of that pause: the cold on her ankles, the candle guttering, the poker suddenly absurd in her hand, and beneath it all the small insistent sound of a living thing that could not wait out in the cold while a sensible woman decided what to do.

Sensible women, her father used to say, are just cowards with good posture.

She set down the poker. She picked up the bundle.

It was heavier than paper and lighter than bread and warmer than anything had any right to be on a December night in Vellmark. It was wrapped in a wool blanket, good wool, the kind that costs more than a week of setting type — and even through the blanket she felt the wrongness of its shape, too stiff in places, crackling faintly, as though the blanket had been lined with something.

The baby — and it was a baby, she could feel that now, the unmistakable architecture of it — made a sound like a kitten arguing with its own conscience, and Coral stepped inside and kicked the door shut behind her with her heel, because her hands were full and her heart had already made decisions her head hadn't approved.

* * *

She lit the lamp over the stone sink in the back room, the room where they washed the rollers and where the drain still ran faintly grey with ink, and she laid the bundle on the cleared end of the workbench, and she unwrapped it the way you unwrap anything you're afraid of: slowly, and braced, and telling yourself the whole time that you can always wrap it back up.

The blanket fell open.

Inside, along both sides of the child, folded flat and sewn into the lining with stitches so neat they must have been done in a hurry by somebody whose hands were trained to better, were papers. Pamphlets. Dozens of them, maybe more, packed around the baby like insulation, like ballast, like the stuffing of something meant not to be found until it was far past finding.

And in the middle of them, red-faced and furious at the cold, at the world, at everything except the arms holding him, was an infant boy of perhaps three months, wearing a christening gown worth more than the press.

"Oh," said Coral, to nobody, in the voice of a woman whose careful life has just developed a crack straight down the middle. "Oh, no. No, no, no."

The baby opened his eyes — dark eyes, startlingly dark in so pale a face — and looked directly at her with the frank, unblinking assessment of the very young, and screamed.

"I know," Coral told him, already unpinning the gown, checking his fingers and toes with the automatic competence of a woman who had helped raise two younger brothers before the fever took them. "I know. I'm not pleased about it either."

He was whole. He was cold but not dangerously so — the pamphlets, whatever else they were, had kept him warm, and she would think about the irony of that later, when she had leisure for irony, which was to say never. His cry was strong, which was good, and relentless, which was going to be a problem, because the walls of the Dravenmoor print shop were old and thin, and the lamplighter's boy slept next door, and the lamplighter's boy had a mother who reported unusual things to unusual people.

Coral worked quickly. Warm water, a cloth, the last of the goat's milk Sera Ondel had pressed on her weeks ago "in case of emergencies," and hadn't this turned out to be the emergency. The baby drank from a spoon, then from the corner of a folded cloth, glaring at her between swallows as if she were doing it wrong, which she was, but doing it wrong at speed.

It was only when he quieted — fed, burped, wrapped in her own spare blanket, installed in the type drawer lined with clean rags, because the shop had cradles of exactly one kind and it was full of lead — that she let herself look at the pamphlets.

She didn't read them. That was important, and she knew it was important, and she told herself firmly that she was not reading them, merely counting them. Counting is not reading. Counting is inventory, and inventory is business, and business was the wall she had built her whole life behind.

Forty-one pamphlets. Printed on a press better than hers, inked by hands steadier than most. Eight pages each. The topmost sheet bore a woodcut of a bridge — and Coral stopped counting, because she knew that bridge. Everyone in Vellmark knew Hallow Bridge. You crossed it to reach the grain market on the far shore. You crossed it every market day of your life.

The woodcut showed the bridge crowded with small black figures, and above them, larger than life, figures with muskets, and the caption underneath, in type set tight and angry:

*THE TRUTH OF HALLOW BRIDGE — WHAT THE GOVERNOR'S GUNS DID ON THE FOURTH OF NOVEMBER.*

The fourth of November. Six weeks ago. Six weeks of official silence, of the bridge closed for "repairs," of the grain market moved, of the governor's broadsheets explaining that the crowd had dispersed peacefully and the unfortunate accidents were under investigation by a commission whose findings would be published in due course, meaning never, meaning everyone knew it meant never.

Coral put the pamphlet down face-first, the way you turn over a dead thing.

Then she turned it back up, because she was her father's daughter, and her father had taught her that a printer who flinches from a page might as well sell the press.

* * *

That was when she saw the note.

It had been pinned to the outside of the blanket with a plain wooden peg — the kind used for hanging hams — and it was written in a fine, educated hand, quick but controlled, the writing of a person who had learned penmanship from a tutor and urgency from somewhere else entirely.

Three words.

**forget the face**

No name. No direction. No request, even — nothing asking her to feed the child or shelter him or carry him anywhere. Just that single instruction, in ink gone slightly feathered at the edges where a wet finger or a wet cheek — no. She refused to think about which.

"Forget the face," Coral read aloud, and then laughed, one short bark with no humor in it whatsoever, because the baby chose that exact moment to fix her again with those dark, dark eyes, and there was not a chance in the nine hells of Vellmark that she was ever going to forget the face. That was the trouble with instructions from strangers: they assumed you were someone else.

She checked the gown for marks. Nothing — except, stitched inside the collar in thread a shade darker than the linen, so small she needed the loupe her father had used for inspecting type, a single letter.

**A.**

An initial. Or a name. Or a message to whoever was meant to find him. She sat back on the stool and looked at the letter **A** for a long time, and something cold walked up her spine and settled between her shoulders like a cat that intended to stay.

Because there was an **A** in Vellmark that everyone knew. There was an **A** stamped on the customs seals and carved over the granary doors and printed in fat gothic letters at the head of every official broadsheet: **ATLAS CORVANE, BY THE GRACE OF THE CROWN, GOVERNOR OF THE RIVER PROVINCES.**

Which was ridiculous. Which was a mad thought, the kind of thought that got your shop burned with you in it. Babies abandoned at print shops were the children of desperate washerwomen and foolish apprentices, and their gowns were bought secondhand, and their initials were initials of nobody.

But the blanket was new. And the gown was new. And the pamphlets were real — she had held them, felt the weight of the paper, seen the woodcut — and pamphlets like that did not come from nowhere. They came from a press. Someone had risked a press to make them. Someone had sewn them around a baby like armor and carried him through a city crawling with Herron Vail's grey coats and left him on *her* doorstep.

Not Quillon's. Hers.

Master Quillon owned the shop. Master Quillon signed the leases and the guild papers and collected the tariffs from their clients and decided, with his soft voice and his soft hands, what got printed and what got declined. But the night work — the late corrections, the emergency funeral notices, the deliveries after dark — those came to Coral, because Coral asked no questions and remembered no faces and charged fairly and locked up tight. Everyone knew that. Apparently even the kind of people who smuggled infants through military checkpoints knew that.

"Someone has made a mistake," she informed the baby.

The baby, occupied with his own fist, disagreed.

She should wake Quillon. That was the sensible thing. Quillon lived four streets over in the Printers' Row house his grandfather had built, and he would know what to do, because Quillon always knew what to do, that was the entire architecture of their arrangement — she worked, he decided. She stood, took the candle, got as far as the stairs.

And then she stood on the stairs and thought about Herron Vail.

Everyone in Vellmark had thought about Herron Vail since the autumn, the way you think about ice on a bridge — constantly, involuntarily, while pretending not to. Vail's men had come to Ondel's chandlery in October over a song. A *song*, sung in a tavern, and the chandler had been fined, ruined, and his son had gone to answer questions at the Citadel and come back three weeks later not answering any questions at all, not really, sitting in the doorway of his father's shop watching the river with eyes that had been emptied and refilled with something else.

If she woke Quillon, there would be witnesses on Printers' Row. Lamplighters. Watchmen. Grey coats walking their rounds in pairs, breath steaming, boots ringing on frozen cobbles, bored and cold and grateful for anything at all to write in their books.

Coral went back upstairs. She banked the fire higher, because the baby needed the warmth, and told herself that was the only reason.

* * *

Master Quillon arrived at seven o'clock precisely, as he had arrived at seven o'clock precisely for eleven years, a small round man in a good coat dusted with snow, carrying his stick and his smile and the expression of a man perpetually delighted by the weather regardless of what it was doing.

"Cold enough to crack bells!" he announced to the empty shop, stamping his boots, and then he saw Coral's face, and stopped stamping.

"Master Quillon," Coral said. "We need to talk about the stock paper delivery I received last night."

"There's no paper delivery scheduled until—" He caught himself. She watched him catch himself, watched the smile do something complicated, watched him glance at the front door, at the cellar hatch, at the stairs, cataloguing the room the way she'd once seen a pawnbroker catalogue a widow's wedding silver. "In the back, then," he said quietly.

In the back, the baby slept in the type drawer. Quillon looked down at him for a long moment, and Coral watched eleven years of softness rearrange themselves around something harder underneath.

"Where did you find him?"

"On the step. Wrapped in these." She laid the blanket open on the bench, pamphlets exposed, the note on top. "*Forget the face.*"

Quillon read the note twice. Then he picked up one pamphlet — only one, delicately, by the corner, as if it might go off — and looked at the woodcut, and put it down, and was silent for so long that the fire popped in the next room like a nervous cough.

"Burn it," he said at last.

"The baby?"

"*Everything,* Coral." His voice stayed soft; it always stayed soft; that was the frightening thing about Quillon, that his voice never rose no matter what fell. "The pamphlets. The blanket. The note. The gown. Scrub the drawer. Wash the rags. As for the child—" He passed a hand across his eyes, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned. "There are places. Foundling wheels. The Sisters at Saint Verene take them in, no questions, it's what the wheel is *for*. Tonight, before the city wakes. You'll take him yourself, you won't speak to anyone, and when it's done you'll come home and we will never—"

"You've read them." It wasn't a question. "You looked at one just now and you already knew what it said. Before you opened it."

Quillon's hands, resting on the edge of the bench, went very still.

"Fourteen years I've run this shop," he said, "without ever printing a lie big enough to hang us. Tariffs and funeral notices and playbills. Your father understood that. Your father—" He stopped himself again, and Coral noted, in the detached way of a compositor noting a cracked piece of type that will have to be dealt with later, that this was the second time tonight a man had almost said her father's name and swallowed it.

"He died at his case," Coral said. "Of a bad heart. That's what the register says."

"That's what the register says," Quillon agreed, "and registers are printed, Coral, and everything printed is printed by somebody, and somebodies have to go on living. Burn it. All of it. Tonight." He took his hat from the peg, and paused at the door, and did not quite turn around. "I'm sorry about the child. Truly. But mercy that gets us both killed is just murder with extra steps."

His footsteps faded down the street, precise as a clock.

Coral stood alone in the back room with a sleeping infant, forty-one pamphlets, a three-word note, and a fire that needed feeding.

She looked at the stove. She looked at the pamphlets. The stove was warm and hungry and right there, and burning them would take ten minutes, and then she would be safe, and neutral, and the woman she had been yesterday, the one who slept half-dressed and counted to thirty and asked no questions and remembered no faces.

The baby sighed in his sleep, a small, outrageous, trusting sound.

"Forget the face," Coral repeated softly. "Right."

She fed the stove one shovel of coal — for the baby, she told herself, strictly for the baby — and carried the pamphlets to the workbench, and struck a light, and did not burn a single one.

She began to read.

* * *

By the third page of the first pamphlet, her hands had stopped shaking and gone cold instead, which was worse. By the eighth page, she had stopped standing. Somewhere in the middle of the second pamphlet, the sun came up over Tanner's Bridge and filled the shop with ordinary grey light, and she did not notice, because she was reading a list.

A list of names. Forty-seven names. Farmers and ferrymen and a grain merchant and two boys of fifteen, dated the fourth of November, each with a line beside it describing how they died, and at the bottom of the list, in a formal secretary hand reproduced faithfully by whoever's press had set it — reproduced faithfully, Coral thought, *someone set this type, someone inked this forme, someone pulled this bar knowing* — the signature of the man whose initials were stitched inside a christening gown sleeping in a type drawer three feet away.

Orders signed. Dates confirmed. The massacre at Hallow Bridge, in the governor's own hand.

Coral laid the page down very carefully, the way her father had taught her to lay down a locked chase, so nothing shifted, so nothing fell.

Above her, in the drawer, the baby slept on, breathing the small steady breaths of the innocent, wrapped in the truth like a blanket — and Coral Dravenmoor, who had spent her whole life keeping her head down and her hands clean, understood with perfect clarity that she had until New Year's Eve, or until Herron Vail, whichever came first.

And she still had thirty-nine pamphlets to go.
