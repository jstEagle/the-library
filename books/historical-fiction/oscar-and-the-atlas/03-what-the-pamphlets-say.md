## 3. What the Pamphlets Say

The pamphlets had been sitting in the flour bin for two days.

Coral told herself she hadn't looked at them because there was no time to look at them — which was true in the way most lies are true. There was never time in the shop. Master Quillon kept her at the cases from dawn until the candles drowned in their own wax, and December made the daylight short and mean. There was a wedding invitation to set for the Harrowgate family, who wanted their names in gold ink though gold cost three times as much and looked, in Coral's opinion, like expensive mud. There were tax notices for the Governor's office, forty of them, each one identical, each one demanding money from people who had none. There was Quillon's own perpetual job, the *Vellmark Weekly Advertiser*, whose news was so carefully emptied of anything resembling news that it read like a letter from a man who had been hit on the head.

So yes, there was no time. And also, she did not want to look.

She knew what the papers lining the baby's blanket were. She had felt them with her fingertips that first night — the crisp bite of good rag stock, not the cheap wood-pulp sheets Quillon used for fishmongers' bills. Someone had printed those on a proper press with proper type, and had printed *many* of them, and had wrapped an infant in them like insulation against the cold, as if to say: this child is worth more than paper, but the paper is worth something too. Remember both.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. Coral had not forgotten the face. The face had a small furrow between its brows even in sleep, as if it had already heard bad news and intended to hear more.

On the third night, after Quillon had gone home to his wife and his gout and his decanter of plum brandy, after the apprentice Tam had been sent up to his cot above the stable, Coral locked the front door, banked the fire, and took the pamphlets out of the flour bin.

There were nine of them. She counted twice, because counting was a thing her hands did when her mind refused to move.

She carried them to the composing room at the back of the shop, where her father's lamp still hung from its hook over the stone sink, and she lit the candle, and she sat down on the low stool he used to sit on, and she began to read.

---

The first pamphlet was titled simply **THE TRUTH OF HALLOW BRIDGE**, in letters that marched across the page like soldiers who had finally been given honest orders. Beneath it:

*On the morning of the ninth of October, in the year 1792, the people of Hallow Bridge rose against the grain levy. They were millers, carters, and widows. They carried staves and one fowling piece belonging to old Anders Mott, who had not fired it since the border troubles of '74.*

*Governor Atlas Corvane dispatched the Fourth Regiment under Captain Edrell Voss. By evening, thirty-one of the people of Hallow Bridge were dead.*

Coral stopped. Not because she doubted it — doubt would have been a kindness — but because the fourth name down was one she knew. *Anders Mott.* He had come into the shop every autumn to print bills for his cider pressing, and every spring for his beehives, and once, memorably, to print a notice accusing his neighbor's rooster of crimes against the peace. He had haggled over the price of the rooster bill for twenty minutes and had paid, in the end, with a jug of cider and a bow that made her father laugh for a week.

He was dead. Thirty-one of them were dead, and here they were, listed by name, trade, and age, the way you'd list goods in a warehouse.

*Mott, Anders — miller, sixty-one.*
*Mott, Lena — his daughter, thirty-four.*
*Ondel, Petra — laundress, twenty-nine.*

An Ondel. She thought of the midwife two doors down, Sera Ondel, who had strong hands and a laugh like a cart going over cobbles. A sister? A cousin? She didn't know. She realized, with a small cold shock, how little she knew about anyone. She had spent six years keeping her head down so carefully that she had kept it down inside her own skull.

She turned the page.

The second and third pamphlets were copies of military orders — actual orders, reproduced in careful facsimile, the kind of work that required access to a government press or a very brave thief. *By command of His Excellency the Governor: the levy shall be collected in full. Resistance shall be answered in proportion.* And beneath, in a smaller hand that had been faithfully copied: *"In proportion" to be understood as tenfold. Make an example. No survivors among the ring-leaders; the rest may starve as a lesson. — A.C.*

*A.C.* Atlas Corvane. His initials sat at the bottom of the order like a signature on a wedding invitation, like a name on a tax notice, like the gilt letters on the facade of the Governor's House that caught the winter light and threw it back as if nothing ugly could possibly live behind them.

Coral set the candle down because her hands were no longer steady enough to hold it near paper. That was the rule, wasn't it — her father's rule, drilled into her before she could reach the upper cases: *never bring fire to where the truth lives.* It had been a joke about the shop. Now it was a joke about everything.

She read all nine. Orders, casualty lists, a testimony from a drummer boy who had deserted ("I beat my drum to march them in and I beat it to march them out, and the second time there was blood on my sticks and I never struck them again"), and a final sheet, printed on cheaper stock, addressed not to scholars or magistrates but to anyone who could read and half of Vellmark couldn't:

*They will tell you this never happened. They will tell you the bridge people were rebels and deserved it. We have the names. We have the orders. We have the hand that signed them. Ask your governor why his own seal sits at the bottom of a massacre.*

Coral sat in the darkening room with nine pamphlets and one candle and the enormous, humming silence of a building full of type.

Here was the terrible thing, the thing she would not say aloud to anyone, the thing that made her ashamed even alone: some part of her was *thrilled.*

Not at the deaths. Never at the deaths. But at the *print.* This was real printing — urgent, dangerous, alive. Every compositor dreams, somewhere in the secret attic of himself, of setting words that matter, words that will make hands shake when they're read, words that will outlive the paper. She had dreamed it herself, as a girl, setting her father's practice sentences: *the quick brown fox*, *now is the time*. She had imagined "now is the time" meaning something. And here it was, arrived at her door wrapped around a baby, and it meant exactly what she'd feared it would mean, and it was going to get someone killed, and the someone was most likely going to be her.

Knowledge, she decided, was like lead type. Useful, heavy, and poisonous if you held it in your bare hands long enough.

She heard the floorboard creak upstairs — Tam, turning in his sleep — and blew out the candle, and sat in the black listening to her own heart behave badly.

* * *

From the ledger of Master Aldous Quillon, printer, entered the same night in a hand less steady than his usual:

*Bought: tallow, 12 lbs. Ink varnish, 1 qt. Brandy (medicinal).*

*Note to self: burn the papers tomorrow. Burn them FIRST, before opening the shop, before the kettle, before anything. A man who burns papers before breakfast is a careful man. A man who reads them is a dead one.*

Quillon had not burned the papers. Quillon had stood in his kitchen holding the bundle over the stove and had found himself unable to let go, and had put them back in the flour bin with the excuse — a good one, really, almost clever — that burning them all at once would make a smell, and smoke from a chimney at four in the morning was the sort of thing neighbors noticed, and neighbors noticed things these days the way dogs noticed thunder.

This was true. It was also completely beside the point, and Quillon knew it was beside the point, and he poured himself a measure of brandy anyway, medicinal, and sat down heavily at his kitchen table where his wife had left bread and cold mutton he could not eat.

Forty years in the trade. Forty years of wedding invitations and tax notices and advertisements for hair tonic, and do you know why, Aldous, do you know why you've been safe all these years? Because you print what they tell you to print, and nothing else, ever, not once, not even the year your brother died and you wanted to put the true cause in the obituary instead of "a wasting illness." You swallowed that. You set "a wasting illness" in 14-point Baskerville and you went home and drank and you were safe.

Safe. And now a girl he employed — a good girl, a careful girl, a girl who kept her head down better than he'd ever managed — was sleeping twenty feet from nine pamphlets that could hang every person in the shop, and he had ordered her to burn them, and he had seen her face when she said "yes, Master Quillon," and he knew, the way printers know things, that she had not burned them yet and might not burn them at all.

Because he wouldn't have. That was the rotten core of it. If he were twenty-six, if he had her hands and her father's stubborn jaw, he'd have read every word by now and wanted more.

The brandy did not help. Brandy never helped; it only agreed with you.

There was more, of course. There was always more, and Quillon had been carrying the *more* for eleven years like a stone sewn into his coat. The resistance had come to him first in '81, hat in hand, quoting her father's name — Eriksen's old partner, the man who'd fled south — and asking for five hundred leaflets, just once, just this. And Quillon had printed them. And then it had been just-once again the next year, and the next, and somewhere along the way he had become, without ever deciding to be, the underground press of the eastern quarter, and the terror of it had grown familiar the way a bad tooth grows familiar, and he had told himself every year: this is the last year.

He had even, God forgive him, taken payment. Not much. Enough. The new press rollers hadn't bought themselves.

And now the child. Because Quillon had recognized the blanket. That was the thing he could never tell Coral, the thing that sat on his chest at night heavier than the stone in his coat: he had *set the type* on those massacre pamphlets, months ago, a rush job, a masked man with a southern accent, paid double, no questions — and the blanket the baby came wrapped in was woven in the Corvane household colors, deep blue and iron gray, and Quillon had held blankets like that exactly once in his life, when he'd done repair work on the Governor's private press and been searched twice on the way out.

Somebody from inside that house had walked through the snow to *his* door. Somebody from inside that house trusted the trade — trusted the old network, her father's network — with the governor's own grandchild.

Which meant the war everyone said was coming had already started, and the first shot had landed in his flour bin.

Quillon wrote in the ledger: *Burn papers tomorrow. First thing. Before kettle.*

Then he underlined *first* three times, because a man who has lied to himself for forty years knows exactly how much emphasis a lie requires, and poured one more measure, medicinal, and did not sleep.

* * *

Coral woke at dawn with the pamphlets under her mattress and a crick in her neck and the peculiar lightness of a person who has stopped pretending.

She opened the shop. She lit the stove. She set type for the Harrowgate wedding invitation, gold ink and all, and while her hands picked letters — H-a-r-r-o-w-g-a-t-e — her mind kept returning to the list of the dead, and specifically to the way the pamphlet had given each one a trade. *Miller. Laundress. Carter.* As if the typesetter had understood something she was only now understanding: that names alone can be forgotten, but a name with a trade attached becomes a whole life, and a page of whole lives is a weapon no magistrate can confiscate.

Whoever had composed those pages had loved the dead properly. Had given them dignity in metal and ink. Her father would have said — she could hear him saying it, that rumbling shop-floor voice — *that's the whole craft, Coral. Anyone can make marks. We make remembering.*

At half past eight, Master Quillon came in smelling of brandy and fear, and Coral watched him cross the shop with the studied casualness of a man crossing ice, and she said, "I burned them last night, Master Quillon. All nine."

He stopped mid-stride. For a moment his face did something complicated — relief and disappointment wrestling in the same small space — and then he said, "Good girl," in a voice that believed her completely and was therefore lying to her, because she had learned last night that reading a face was not so different from reading proof sheets: look closely enough and the errors showed.

"You'll want to check the flour bin's clean," she added. "Flour gets everywhere."

"Yes," said Quillon faintly. "Yes, it does."

He went to the bin. He looked into it for a long time. And Coral, sorting type with her back to him, saw reflected in the polished face of the imposing stone a small and terrible thing: her master, standing over an empty flour bin, looking not relieved but *lost* — like a man whose alibi has just walked out the door.

That was when she understood that Master Quillon knew exactly what those pamphlets were, had known before she did, and had wanted them burned for reasons that had nothing to do with her safety.

And that was when she heard the knock at the front door — three slow raps, official, patient — and through the frosted glass, the silhouette of a man wearing a hat with the brim pulled low, standing very still, the way men stand when they are being paid to wait.
