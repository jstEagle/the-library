## 4. The First Checkpoint

The morning of the delivery began, as all terrible mornings do, with the illusion of normalcy.

Coral woke before dawn to the sound of the baby breathing — a small, wet, determined sound, like a bellows worked by someone very small and very stubborn — and lay still for a full minute, cataloguing everything that could go wrong that day. It was a habit her father had taught her without meaning to: *count your misfortunes before breakfast, Cori, and none of them can surprise you.* He had meant it about type cases. She was applying it to soldiers.

She had three problems. First, the pamphlets: forty-one sheets describing the massacre at Hallow Bridge in detail no official broadsheet would ever dare print, currently hidden beneath a loose floorboard under the composing stone, wrapped in oilcloth like a thing that could suffocate if it heard its own name spoken. Second, the child, asleep at this very moment in the old type chest by the cellar stair, tucked into a nest made from Quillon's spare aprons. Third, and most immediately pressing, Master Quillon himself, who had appeared at the top of the stairs wearing his coat and an expression she did not like.

"You're taking the Alder Street run today," he said.

"The Alder Street run is on Wednesdays."

"It's Wednesday."

"It's Thursday," said Coral. "It has been Thursday since midnight. I would know. I was awake for all of it."

Quillon's eyes flicked — just once, quickly, involuntarily — toward the cellar stair, then back to her face. "A rush order. The chandler on Alder wants his invoices early. Take the cart."

"Since when does a chandler need invoices *early*?"

"Since he started paying us extra for it. Are you going to argue with money?"

Coral considered arguing with money. But arguing with Quillon required energy, and energy had become a currency she hoarded like a miser, spending only on essentials: feeding the baby, changing the baby, convincing Sera Ondel next door that the thin wail she'd heard through the wall on Tuesday had been "a cat." A very large cat. With opinions.

So she said yes, and began loading the cart, and told herself the cold knot in her stomach was merely breakfast arriving late.

* * *

Here is what Coral knew, though she pretended not to know it: there were now checkpoints between Vellmark's print quarter and the merchant streets. There had not been checkpoints between the print quarter and anywhere for eleven years, not since Governor Corvane had decided, in one of his periodic fits of generosity, that a city which trusted itself needed no soldiers at its elbows. The checkpoints had come back in November, quietly, the way rot comes back into a tooth — one day the street was a street, and the next day there was a sawhorse across it and two men in Corvane grey standing beside it with muskets they held as though the muskets were a fashion accessory they hadn't quite learned to carry.

Everyone in the print quarter had adjusted the way people adjust to anything: by pretending it had always been there.

Coral loaded the cart with genuine deliveries — the chandler's invoices, real ones, printed honestly two nights ago; a bundle of funeral notices for the Ondel family's neighbor; a ream of blank paper sold to a stationer who would resell it at a markup that should have been illegal. On top of all of it, she placed a crate of damaged type, the worn-out sorts her father had never been able to throw away because, as he'd put it, "metal remembers being useful, Coral, even when we don't." The crate clanked beautifully. It was the loudest object in the cart, and she wanted it that way. A search party finds what it goes looking for; give them something dull to find and they stop looking.

The baby went into the second type chest. This was not a decision she had made so much as a decision that had made itself over the past week, through the slow accumulation of necessity. The chest was oak, iron-banded, deep enough for a galley tray and shallow enough to pass for luggage. She had lined it with the softest rags in the shop. She had drilled holes — small ones, neat ones, drilled with a jeweler's bit at two in the morning while hating herself for how good she'd gotten at this sort of thing — along the upper rail where the bands met the wood, invisible unless you knelt and looked, which no one kneels and looks at a chest that rattles with lead type.

The pamphlets stayed home, under the floorboard. That had been the other decision, or rather the absence of one: she could not bring herself to destroy them, and she could not bring herself to move them, and so they sat beneath the floor of the shop like a heart beneath ribs, doing their quiet work whether she acknowledged it or not.

She wrapped the baby — fed, burped, changed, drowsy with milk and the particular warmth of a chest that smelled of metal and linseed — and settled him among the rags, and looked down at him for a long moment.

"Forget the face," the note had said. She had not forgotten the face. The face was the problem. The face was a small round argument against every sensible thing she had done since finding him, and it looked up at her now with the serene confidence of someone who has never once been searched at a checkpoint.

"Don't," she told him quietly, "make any noise I haven't approved in advance."

He yawned, magnificently insubordinate, and closed his eyes.

* * *

The checkpoint stood at the mouth of Tanner's Bridge, where the road narrowed between a tannery and a chandlery — the same chandlery, Coral noted with grim amusement, that supposedly needed its invoices early. Someone had dragged a military wagon sideways across the cobbles, and behind it stood four soldiers in grey, breath steaming, stamping against the cold. A sergeant with a clipboard — a clipboard, in December, held barehanded by a man whose fingers had gone the color of the ink Coral mixed for cheap job work.

There were three carts ahead of hers. She watched the procedure with the attention of a compositor reading another printer's galleys: pull forward, state your business, open your crates, endure the poking, go. The first cart, a fishmonger, took four minutes. The second, a woman with firewood, took two. The third was a brewer's dray that required both soldiers to climb into it, and while they climbed, the sergeant turned and looked directly at Coral.

Not at her cart. At her.

She knew that look. Her father had called it the proofreader's stare — the look of a man hunting for an error he is certain exists, simply because the document has been in front of him too long. Herron Vail's men, the apprentices whispered, had been asking questions in the print quarter all week. Questions about papers. Questions about a missing child.

*A missing child.* The word landed in her chest like a dropped case, every letter scattering.

The sergeant was walking toward her now. Coral did the thing she had decided to do, the thing she had rehearsed against the inside of her own skull while feeding the baby at four in the morning: she became bored. Profoundly, cosmically bored. The boredom of a woman who has been awake since midnight, whose hands smell of ink no soap will touch, who would trade her entire stock of em quads for five more minutes of sleep. Boredom was armor. Fear showed at the eyes; boredom fogged them.

"Business?" the sergeant said.

"Invoices," said Coral, in the flattest voice she owned. She held up the packet. "Chandler on Alder Street pays extra for early delivery, which tells you everything you need to know about chandlers."

Something flickered at the corner of the sergeant's mouth. Not a smile. The memory of one, maybe, buried somewhere under the frost. "Open the cart."

She opened the cart.

Here is what Coral learned in the next six minutes, kneeling on frozen cobblestones while strangers handled her father's type: terror is mostly arithmetic. You count. You count the soldier's fingers on the lid of the type chest — one, two, lifting, *don't lift it fully, let the weight argue for you* — and the chest comes up heavy and rattling, and the soldier peers in at forty pounds of dead metal and sees nothing but a workingwoman's tools, because men see what they expect to see, and what they expect to see is never a sleeping infant in a nest of aprons, because the world does not expect tenderness to be armored in lead.

The baby slept. She could hear nothing — the chest was too solid, the street too loud with wind — but she watched the soldier's face the way sailors watch weather, and the face said *heavy, boring, done.*

You count the seconds the sergeant spends flipping through invoices. One, two, three — he reads slowly, lips moving slightly, and she thinks, absurdly, *he'd have made a fair compositor, careful with his letters.* Four, five. The baby had eaten well. She had timed it. Milk was a sedative; she'd learned that from Sera Ondel, who dispensed childcare advice the way other women dispensed gossip, freely and with alarming authority.

You count, above all, the moments when luck leans over the board and puts its thumb on your side of the scale.

It happened twice.

The first time: the soldier searching the rear of the cart found the crate of damaged type, opened it, frowned at the worn-out sorts — letters rubbed smooth as river stones, faces gone blind with use — and asked what they were.

"Worn type," Coral said. "My father kept everything. Men of his generation didn't throw away metal. They thought metal had feelings."

The soldier looked at the crate, and at her, and something in her voice — the true grief underneath the flatness, leaking through despite her best efforts, the way water finds a crack — made him close the crate gently, almost respectfully, and set it back. "Mine keeps nails," he said. "Coffee tins of them. Won't part with a single bent one." And he moved on.

The second time: a commotion at the bridge. A woman — a grand dame in a fur-trimmed coat, escorted by a harried servant — arrived in a sedan chair and demanded, at volume, to be carried across Tanner's Bridge immediately, because her daughter was expecting and she intended to be present for the event regardless of governors, soldiers, or the general collapse of civilized order. The sergeant turned toward the noise with the expression of a man watching his morning die. His colleague abandoned the cart queue entirely to deal with her.

For perhaps ninety seconds, nobody was watching Coral's cart at all.

She stood very still. She thought about the floorboard back at the shop, and the oilcloth bundle beneath it, and the names written on those pages — names of men shot at Hallow Bridge in October, dates, orders signed in a hand she now recognized from proclamations posted on every church door in the city. She thought about the fact that she was, at this moment, technically innocent of everything except knowing. Knowing was not yet a crime. Knowing was becoming one daily, the law tightening around it like a press screw.

The sergeant returned, rubbing his temples. "Go on, then," he said, waving the clipboard at her with the weariness of a man who has discovered that paperwork cannot actually hold back winter. "Tell your chandler some of us deliver on schedule without being paid extra."

"I'll pass along your regards," said Coral, and clicked her tongue at the horse, and did not breathe properly again until the bridge was three streets behind her.

* * *

She delivered the invoices. The chandler, a round man named Petch with flour-white eyebrows, counted them twice and complained that the third page was smudged, which it was not, and paid her extra anyway, and asked after her health in the tone of a man who had heard rumors about the print shop and was probing for gossip. She gave him nothing. She gave him less than nothing. She gave him a smile so bland it could have been printed in bulk.

Then she sat in the cart behind the chandlery, out of sight of the road, and shook.

It came on all at once, the way it always does — the delayed invoice of fear, presented for payment the moment the creditor leaves. Her hands trembled against her skirts. Her breath came short and hot in the cold air. Somewhere behind her sternum her heart performed the work of an apprentice gone mad with a mallet, banging away at nothing.

In the type chest, faintly, the baby made a sound. Not crying. A small, sleepy, questioning noise — the noise of a person checking whether the world was still there.

"Yes," Coral said aloud, her voice cracking in the middle like bad type. "I'm here. We're fine. We passed."

We. She heard it leave her mouth and stared at the empty street ahead as though the word might come back and be repossessed. *We.* Two days ago it had been *the child,* a noun, cargo, a problem with a heartbeat. Now it was *we*, and she had not authorized the change, and there was no one to complain to about it.

She got down, knelt by the chest, and lifted the lid an inch. Two dark eyes blinked up at her from the nest of aprons, unfathomably calm, as if being smuggled past armed men were a service she provided and he had merely found it adequate.

"You have no idea," she whispered, "what you cost me just now."

He reached up one hand and closed it around her little finger, and held on with the astonishing grip of infants, who apparently enter the world already prepared for betrayal and determined to prevent it.

Coral sat on the cold step of the cart and let him hold on, and thought about the soldier with the coffee tins of bent nails, and about her own father's crate of dead letters riding openly in the bed of the cart, and about the floorboard back home with its secret pressed against the joists. Everything hidden was also, somehow, being carried. That was the trick no one warned you about. You could bury a truth under your feet and still haul it everywhere you went.

She closed the chest. She drove home.

* * *

Quillon was waiting in the doorway when she returned, and he had the look of a man who has spent the morning losing arguments with himself.

"Well?" he said.

"Well, what?"

"The checkpoint. Did they—"

"They looked at my type," Coral said, climbing down, unhitching nothing, keeping her voice level with an effort that cost her more than the checkpoint had. "They read my invoices. One of them was bored. Boredom, Master Quillon, turns out to be worth more than courage these days. Cheaper, too."

Quillon nodded too many times. He took the horse's bridle, patted its neck, didn't meet her eyes. "Good. Good. That's — good."

And then, as she brushed past him into the shop, he said the thing she would turn over all night, the sentence that slid under the door of her mind and refused to leave:

"There are people," Quillon said, to the horse, to the street, to no one — "people who would pay very well to know that kind of route is safe."

Coral stopped on the threshold. Behind her, muffled by oak and iron, the baby sighed in his sleep. Ahead of her, in the dim of the composing room, her father's cases gleamed in the morning light like a field waiting to be planted — and Master Quillon stood among them, wringing his cap in his hands, wearing the face of a man about to ask her for something enormous.

"Coral," he said. "We should talk."
