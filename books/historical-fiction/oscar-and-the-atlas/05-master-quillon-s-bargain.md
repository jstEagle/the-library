## 5. Master Quillon's Bargain

The snow came down on Vellmark like a printer's mistake — too much of it, all at once, and no way to correct the page. Coral watched it from the shop window on the morning of the nineteenth of December, her breath fogging the glass, her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory that had gone cold an hour ago. Across the street, a squad of Corvane's soldiers marched past the chandler's shop in their grey coats, their boots leaving prints that the snow filled in behind them, patient and thorough, as though the city itself were eager to forget they had ever passed.

She had not slept. The baby had slept, which was more than she could say for herself. He lay now in the type chest — the deep bottom drawer where her father had kept the display faces, the fat ornamental letters used for broadside headlines, now lined with a folded blanket and smelling faintly of lead and linseed oil. He slept the way only the very young and the very guilty can sleep: completely, shamelessly, his small fist curled against his cheek as though the world were a thing he had already decided to trust.

Coral envied him. She had decided, at roughly three in the morning, that the world was not to be trusted, and that she was a fool for having carried it past the first checkpoint in a fish cart, her heart hammering so loudly she'd been certain the soldier at the rope would hear it over the creak of the wheels.

And Quillon — Quillon had not come down from his rooms above the shop since.

That was the thing that worried her most, she realized. Not the soldiers, not the pamphlets hidden beneath the floorboards of the delivery cart where it sat in the alley, not even the child. Master Quillon Aldous Vey was a man of habits as fixed as type metal. He rose at six. He took his breakfast — black bread, hard cheese, and one small glass of plum brandy "for the digestion" — at half past. He was at his desk by seven, complaining about the quality of the paper merchants' stock before his second sip of tea. In the eleven years Coral had worked for him, she could count on one hand the mornings he had failed to appear, and every one of them had involved either gout or a funeral.

Today was neither. Today, when she had knocked on his door at seven with his tray, she had heard him moving about inside — the slow shuffle, the creak of the floorboard by the window that he always stepped on because he refused, on principle, to have it mended — but he had not answered. She had left the tray. The brandy glass had sat untouched when she'd collected it an hour later.

A man who refused his plum brandy was a man in trouble.

* * *

She found her answer at noon, when he finally came down the stairs.

He had dressed for it, she saw at once. That was the second alarming thing. Quillon owned two coats: the ink-stained working coat he wore daily, and a good dark coat with brass buttons that he wore to funerals and to the annual dinner of the Vellmark Stationers' Guild, where he drank too much and argued about serifs. He was wearing the good coat. He had shaved. There was a small cut on his jaw where the razor had slipped, and he had dabbed it with the little styptic pencil he kept for emergencies, which gave him the look of a man who had prepared for a solemn occasion and been ambushed by his own face.

"Coral," he said. "Would you come into the back room, please."

It was not a request. It was also not, she noticed, the voice he used for instructions about paper stock. It was quieter than that. Careful. The voice of a man who had rehearsed something on the walk down thirteen stairs and was now discovering that rehearsal is no substitute for courage.

The back room was the oldest part of the shop, the part her father had known, with the low ceiling and the window that looked out on the yard where the delivery cart stood under its tarpaulin of snow. There was a table there, and two chairs, and a stove that Quillon lit in winter with the miserly precision of a man who believed warmth was a privilege to be earned. He lit it now. Then he sat down across from her, folded his hands on the table, and looked at her for a long moment with an expression she had never seen on him before.

It was, she realized with a small shock, fear. Not the everyday fear of a printer whose governor was a butcher — that fear Coral knew how to read, knew how to share, knew how to fold away each morning like the shop's shutters. This was a specific fear. A fear with a name and an address.

"You went past the rope at Miller's Bend yesterday," Quillon said.

"The delivery for the chandler."

"The delivery for the chandler." He repeated it slowly, as though tasting the words for poison. "And the soldiers searched your cart."

"They searched the cart. They didn't search the cart."

"No," Quillon said. "They didn't." He drew a breath. "I watched from the window of the tallow merchant's, where I had gone to settle an account I did not need to settle. I watched a corporal of the Ninth put his hands on that cart's sides, and I watched him find nothing, and I have not been able to hold a pen straight since. Do you understand what I am telling you, Coral? I stood in a warm shop and let a nineteen-year-old girl carry my death past a checkpoint in a fish cart, and I watched, and I did nothing."

Coral said nothing. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the front of the shop, the baby — who had been moved, in an act of either great courage or great folly, from the type chest to the wicker basket behind the counter — made a small sound and settled again.

"You knew," she said at last. "When you told me to burn everything. You knew what it was."

"I knew it was trouble." Quillon's hands unfolded, folded again. "I did not know whose trouble. There is a difference, and I have been hiding behind it for six days like a coward behind a wall." He looked at her, and something in his face shifted — the fear giving way, briefly, to something older and wearier. "Your father would have known whose trouble it was inside of a minute. He'd have read the first paragraph and known the hand that set it."

The words landed in the room like a dropped composing stick. Coral felt her spine straighten without her permission.

"What do you know about my father's hands?"

* * *

What Quillon knew, it emerged, was considerably more than Coral had ever suspected, and he told it to her the way a man confesses to a priest who is younger than he is — in pieces, with long pauses, staring at the stove.

He told her that the press in the front room — her father's press, the old Albion with the iron frame and the counterweight that sang when it swung — had never printed only almanacs and chandler's invoices. He told her that in the years before the crackdowns, before Corvane had closed the free presses one by one and called it *restoring order*, her father had set type at night by a shuttered lamp: pamphlets, broadsides, the names of men Corvane's soldiers had drowned at the ferry crossing, the names of the villages taxed into starvation. He told her that when the soldiers came for the printers of Printer's Row — and Coral remembered that night, she had been eleven, she remembered the torches and her mother's hands over her ears — it was not an accident that the Dravenmoor shop had been spared. It had been spared because her father had agreed, in exchange for his daughter's safety, to print nothing true again.

"And then he died anyway," Quillon said quietly, "three years later, of his heart, at his case, with a half-set page in his stick. And I took over the lease, and I told myself the bargain died with him. And I was wrong, Coral. I was wrong, because the trouble did not die with him. It only changed hands."

He stood, went to the corner where the paper stock was kept, and pulled out the bottom ream shelf. Behind it, set into the stone, was a cavity Coral had swept past ten thousand times and never seen. From it he drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, and no larger than a hymnal.

He set it on the table between them and did not touch it again.

"The massacre at Hallow Bridge," he said. "The one your foundling was wrapped in. The pamphlets the mother used — those were printed from copies of copies, third-hand, the way rumor travels. What is in this packet is not third-hand. It is the orders themselves. Signed. Dated. In Corvane's own hand, or near enough that a court would call it near enough. The muster rolls of the men who did the killing. The pay ledgers that show who paid them. Names, Coral. Forty names of the dead, and the names of the men who killed them, and the signature that ordered it."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Coral looked at the little oilcloth packet the way she might have looked at a live coal.

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because there is a contact beyond the river. A man who can carry it further — to the coastal presses, to the foreign papers, to places where Corvane's writ doesn't run and his soldiers can't swim. It must be there before the year turns, because after the first of January the passes close and the river freezes solid and Corvane's patrols can walk across it in a line. And because I am sixty-one years old, I have gout, and I cannot walk to the end of the street without resting." His voice cracked, and he mastered it. "I have carried this thing for four years, Coral. Four years, under my floor, under my paper stock, while I printed wedding invitations and tax notices for the man's government with the same hands. I told myself I was keeping it safe. I was keeping myself safe. There is a difference, and I am done hiding behind it."

"Then give it to me and I'll take it to this contact myself. Why the speech?"

"Because," Quillon said, "you could instead walk out that door right now, go to the governor's house, and hand it over. You'd be paid. You'd be safe. You'd be the woman who saved the city a great deal of unpleasantness. And I could not stop you, and I would deserve whatever came to me for having shown you where it was." He met her eyes. "I am not giving you an order. I have no right to give you an order. I am asking."

* * *

Coral looked at the packet for a long time.

She thought about the fish cart, and the corporal's bored hands, and the sound of her own heart. She thought about the note in the blanket — *forget the face* — and the mother who had written it, whoever she was, a woman with a baby and a death warrant and nowhere in the world left to go. She thought about her father at his case, the half-set page, the heart that had stopped, and the bargain that had bought eleven quiet years and saved nothing.

And underneath all of it, in the place where she kept the things she did not say, she thought about the shop. Her father's type cases. The press that sang. Eleven years of setting other people's words for other people's purposes — invoices, almanacs, the chandler's notices, the tax proclamations — skilled work, honest work, work that belonged to no one and meant nothing. She had told herself for eleven years that this was enough. That a woman alone in a city like Vellmark should be grateful for enough.

But she had carried a baby past a checkpoint in a fish cart, and she had never in her life felt so much like herself.

"The shop," she said.

Quillon blinked. "What?"

"You said you'd been thinking. You didn't rehearse all that just to ask. You're a businessman; you came down in your good coat because you intend to close a bargain. So close it. What are you offering?"

For a moment he looked almost young — caught, flushed, the razor-cut standing out on his jaw. Then he laughed, a short, surprised bark, and pressed his hand flat on the table.

"The shop," he said. "The lease, the press, the type, the goodwill, the whole cursed enterprise. I have no children. I have no heirs but a nephew in Harrowgate who thinks printing is beneath him and writes to me twice a year to say so. If you do this — if you carry the packet to the contact beyond the river before the year turns, and if we are both alive and free at the end of it — the Dravenmoor shop becomes the Dravenmoor shop again. Your name over the door. Your father's press in your hands. I'll sign the papers tomorrow."

"That's a great deal of money for a walk across a river."

"It is not money," Quillon said. "It is the only currency I have left that isn't stained. And it is less than your father was paid, which was nothing, and more than I deserve, which would also be nothing." He pushed back his chair and stood, and for the first time that morning he looked like the Master Quillon of the good days — the one who argued about serifs and drank too much brandy and had once, Coral now understood, been a man worth knowing. "Think on it. Not for long — the river doesn't wait, and neither, I suspect, does whoever left that child at our door. But think on it."

He went to the door. He stopped there, with his hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the night the soldiers came for Printer's Row, your father sent me and the packet to the tallow merchant's cellar and stayed in the shop himself to answer the door. He told me: *Quillon, the type is the inheritance. The man is only the caretaker.*" Quillon's voice was rough. "I have been the caretaker for eleven years. It is time the inheritance went home."

He left her alone with the stove, and the packet, and the sound of the snow.

* * *

She did not go back to work. She sat in the back room while the light went grey and then blue, and the packet sat on the table like a guest who would not leave, and in the front of the shop the baby woke and began, with the impeccable timing of his kind, to cry.

Coral went to him. She lifted him out of the wicker basket, and he stopped crying at once, which she suspected was less a compliment to her than a commentary on the basket, and she stood with him by the window while the streetlamps were lit one by one down the lane — the lamplighter working his way along through the falling snow, patient and methodical, lighting the dark one small flame at a time.

"You're expensive," she told the baby. "You know that? A whole shop. My father would have haggled."

The baby looked at her with the grave, unfocused solemnity of the very young, as though he were considering her offer and would have his answer by spring.

And it was there, in the lamplight, with the packet waiting in the back room and the river waiting beyond the city and the year running out of days like sand through a cracked hourglass, that Coral Dravenmoor understood that she had already decided. She had decided six days ago, at the door, in the snow, when she had bent down and picked the bundle up instead of walking past. Everything since — the fish cart, the checkpoint, the speech in the good coat — had only been the slow type of a page already set.

She was carrying the packet. She wanted, with an ache that surprised her, to belong to something — even if the something was only a truth, and a child, and a press that sang.

She was carrying the packet across the river, and she was taking the shop, and she was going to do it before the year turned or not at all.

She was still standing there, the baby warm against her chest, when there came a knock at the shop door — three slow, deliberate raps, the kind that are not asking permission — and through the frosted glass she saw, silhouetted against the lamplight, the grey coat and the long shadow of a man she had never seen before, and would remember for the rest of her life.
