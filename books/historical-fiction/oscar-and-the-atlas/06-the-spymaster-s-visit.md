## 6. The Spymaster's Visit

The knock came again.

Three raps, patient as arithmetic. Coral stood in the middle of the composing room with the baby's warmth still fading from her chest — she had tucked him into the type chest not a minute before, beneath the false floor Quillon had built for smuggled matrices, and now the whole shop seemed to hold its breath with her, every tray and galley and hanging sheet of proof listening.

She crossed to the door and opened it on the latch chain.

The man outside was tall in the way that furniture is tall — without effort, without apology. Grey coat, grey eyes, a face like a page someone had decided not to print on. Snow had settled on his shoulders and had not melted, as though even his body heat were a matter of official restraint.

"Master Quillon's establishment," he said. It was not a question. Nothing about him would ever be a question; questions were for people who needed answers.

"He's at supper," Coral said.

"Then you are the typesetter. The daughter of Eriksen—" he paused, consulting some interior ledger, "—of Dravenmoor."

"Coral Dravenmoor."

"Herron Vail." He did not offer a hand. He offered, instead, a paper bearing the governor's seal, and waited while she read it with the chain still fastened, which he permitted with an expression suggesting that the chain was a courtesy he was extending to her rather than an obstacle she was imposing on him.

*By order of His Excellency the Governor, all houses of trade within the river district are subject to inspection...*

"You may come in," she said, and unhooked the chain, and hated herself for how steady her voice was.

---

Herron Vail entered the print shop the way water enters a hull: quietly, finding every low place.

He did not go first to the press, which is what she expected — the press was the heart of any house of printing, and men who wanted to seem impressive went straight for hearts. Instead he stopped just inside the door and let his gaze travel the room the way a customs officer travels a manifest. The type cases along the wall, their little brass labels. The drying lines strung with sheets. The stone sink where she washed ink from her hands until her knuckles cracked in winter. The composing stick lying on the stone where she'd set it down mid-sentence when the knock came.

He picked up the composing stick.

"You were setting something," he said.

"A funeral notice," Coral said. It was true. It was always true; that was the trick her father had taught her without knowing it — keep one honest thing on the stone at all times, and everything else can hide behind it. "Old Mistress Halloway, up on Rope Street. Died Thursday."

"Thursday." He turned the stick over and read the half-set line upside down with the ease of a man who had learned to read in mirrors. *Died in the faith of...* "The church gets its notices done elsewhere, I believe. Why does Master Quillon send them to you?"

"Cheaper. And I don't drink his stock."

Something happened at the corner of Vail's mouth that on another face might have been a smile. On his it was more like the memory of one, filed away for future use.

"My compliments to your economy," he said, and set the stick down precisely where it had lain, at precisely the angle, so that she understood he had noted the angle too.

---

*She lies well,* Vail thought, standing in the warm ink-smelling dark of the shop while the girl lit lamps and moved through the room with the unhurried competence of someone who has nothing behind her but a dead father and a trade. *Not brilliantly. Brilliant liars decorate. She strips things down — funeral notices, cheapness, chapped hands. She gives me a smaller world than the one she lives in and hopes I will fit inside it.*

He had read eleven hundred faces in his career, or thereabouts; he kept no exact count, because exact counts invited vanity, and vanity was the first door through which error entered a man. This one — Dravenmoor's daughter — had the particular stillness of a person who has recently begun carrying something. Not guilt. Guilt fidgets. This was weight, distributed carefully, the way a mule distributes a load it has been taught not to spook under.

And somewhere in the city this week, a load had gone missing. A child. Papers.

He watched her trim a lamp wick and thought: *You know exactly what I am here about, and you have known since you saw my coat through your frosted glass, and you have already decided what I will be permitted to see.*

Good. Decisions could be mapped. Decisions had edges, and edges could be pressed.

---

Vail drifted along the type cases the way other men drift through galleries. At the case marked with the worn brass letters he stopped, and ran one finger down the rows of sorts, and Coral felt the hair rise on her arms, because it was her father's case — the upper case, the capitals, the one piece of the shop that had never been Quillon's, that had come to her across a funeral and a will and twenty years of her own hands.

"These are old," Vail said. "Older than the rest."

"They came with the shop."

"No," he said mildly. "They came with a man. There is a difference. Tools carry their owners' habits. See here—" he tapped the row of capital Q's, "—worn on the left shoulder only. Your father pulled toward himself when he lifted. A careless habit. Or a hurried one." He looked at her. "Did he hurry much, your father?"

"He set type for forty years. Hurrying is how printers die."

"Yes," Vail said softly. "It is."

He moved on. He examined the press itself — Quillon's fine new Stanhope, bought two years ago with money nobody asked about — and gave it less attention than he had given the sink. Then, in the corner, half-shaded by a hanging rack of wet posters, he found what Coral realized, with a slow drop of the stomach, that she had forgotten: her father's punch. The silver type punch, the one tool she owned outright, the last thing made by his hands, sitting out on the ledge by the window where she had been cleaning it that afternoon and where the baby's crying had driven every other thought from her head.

Vail picked it up. Turned it. Held it to the lamp so the light slid down the steel shaft and pooled in the cut letter at its tip — an O, deep and clean as a well.

"Beautiful," he said. "Silver-faced. One doesn't see that outside a mint. Punchcutting is a dying art, they say. The patience it takes — months for a single letter, cutting steel against steel under a lens." He weighed it in his palm. "A man who can do this can wait out anything. Did he teach you patience, Mistress Dravenmoor?"

"He died before he could teach me much of anything."

"Mm." Vail set the punch down — gently, aligned parallel with the ledge's edge, another courtesy — and turned to face her fully for the first time since entering. Up close his eyes were the pale grey of ice over moving water, and she understood suddenly that the stillness was the surface and the movement was underneath, and that he preferred it that way, that stillness was his instrument the way her composing stick was hers.

"Now," he said. "To business, so that we needn't pretend this is about funeral notices any longer."

---

*Here it comes,* Coral thought, and was surprised to find that beneath the terror there was a cold, clear layer of her mind taking notes, the way her father had taken notes on everything — prices, weather, the grain of different papers. Some part of her had already begun setting this scene in type, arranging it into columns, and that part was calm, and she clung to it like a rail.

"The governor's office has lost several items this month," Vail said. "Perhaps you've heard gossip. Cities love gossip; it is the one commodity cheaper than ink."

"I don't hear much gossip. Ink keeps me busy."

"So I'm told. So I'm told." He began to walk, slowly, a circuit of the room, and she understood that the walking was the interrogation — that he would talk to the shop and let her listen, because a man reads a face best when the face believes it is not being read. "An officer of the household guard reports that papers have gone missing from his Excellency's private archive. Correspondence of a sensitive nature. Military orders, perhaps. The kind of paper that certain elements in this city would pay a great deal to possess, and a great deal more to publish."

He reached the type chest — the big one, Quillon's, with its iron bands — and rested a hand flat upon its lid, and Coral's heart performed a single enormous beat and then went very quiet, the way a bird goes quiet in a closed fist.

"And there is a second matter," Vail said. "Less public. A child. An infant, abandoned — or delivered; the distinction interests me — at a house in this district some nights ago. Wrapped, curiously, in printed material. The household guard has instructions to recover both the child and the material. Quietly." He glanced back at her. "You see the shape of it? Two missing things, and everyone agrees they are connected, and no one agrees on why."

"People abandon babies in Vellmark every winter," Coral said. Her voice came out level. She could hear herself being level and marveled at it distantly, as if listening to another woman print.

"They do. Cold does terrible bookkeeping." He lifted his hand from the chest. "But this child was healthy. Well-fed. Recently nursed. Someone loved it enough to feed it and was desperate enough to give it away, and that combination is rarer than you would think — usually it is the reverse. Desperation loves and cannot feed." He tilted his head. "You are not married, Mistress Dravenmoor?"

"No."

"A pity. You have capable hands. Hands tell the truth faster than faces, did you know that? Faces can be trained; hands were born honest." He smiled his filed-away smile. "Yours have ink in every crease. A working woman's hands. Nothing hidden in them at all."

He said it the way a man lays a card face-down on a table.

---

*She did not flinch at the word "child."* Vail catalogued it. *Nor at "printed material." Either she is innocent, or she is very good, or — the third possibility, the interesting one — she is guilty and has already spent her flinch somewhere I was not looking for it. People budget their fear. A novice spends it all at once. A professional rations it. Where did you spend yours, printer's daughter?*

He knew the shop was not the whole of it. Shops had cellars; cellars had floors; floors had spaces between themselves and the earth, and in twenty years of this work he had found more truths under floorboards than in any archive in the capital. But he would not lift floorboards today. Today was for mapping. Today he would stand in this warm room among the smells of ink and tallow and — yes, under it, faint but unmistakable, milk — today he would plant himself like a seed and wait for her to grow around him.

Because she had moved. Twice. Small movements, corrections, the body tidying up after the mouth: once when he touched the type chest, her weight had shifted onto her back foot, a half-inch of withdrawal, instantly corrected. Once when he mentioned nursing. He filed them where he filed everything, in the great cold archive of himself, cross-referenced against the date, against the district, against the name Quillon, whom he had been watching for six years and whose loyalty, like bad mortar, had finally begun to show its cracks this season.

Quillon printed for both sides. Everyone knew; knowing was permitted, even profitable, so long as both sides believed themselves the favored customer. But the governor had grown tired of balance. Balance, his Excellency had begun to say, is what a scale does while it decides.

And scales decide. That was the season's lesson. The whole century was a scale deciding.

"Master Quillon will return soon?" Vail asked.

"Any time."

"Then I will leave a card, and spare us all the theater of waiting." From his coat he took a grey card, blank except for a small stamped seal, and laid it on the composing stone beside the funeral notice, square with the stone's edge, of course. "If anything should come to your attention — stray papers, stray infants, stray loyalties — you may send word. Discretion is guaranteed. Rewards," he added, and for the first time his voice warmed by a single degree, like a stove fed one coal too many, "are considerable."

"I'll remember."

"I believe you will. Memory is a family trait in this trade." He drew on his gloves, finger by finger, unhurried. At the door he paused, his back to her, and said, without turning:

"The Hallow Bridge notice. Set it well. Names, dates, full columns. The governor likes completeness in his obituaries."

And then the grey coat was gone into the snow, and the three raps of his departure were only the sound of the door settling in its frame.

---

Coral stood a long time without moving.

Then she went to the type chest, knelt, and lifted the false floor, and the baby looked up at her from the blankets — awake, silent, watching her with the grave dark eyes of infants who have already learned the world requires watching — and she put her hand on his small rising chest and felt the heartbeat going on underneath, stubborn as a press.

On the composing stone, Vail's grey card lay square beside the notice for old Mistress Halloway, and Coral looked at the two pieces of paper side by side for a long moment.

Then she picked up her father's silver punch from the window ledge, closed her fingers around it until the letter O bit into her palm, and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that the man had seen everything — the chest, the milk-smell, the lie balanced on the truth of a funeral — and had chosen, tonight, to take none of it.

Which meant he didn't need to.

Which meant he already knew where everything was, and was simply giving her enough rope to hang the whole district — Quillon, Sera, Ivor Kade, herself, and the child — in one knot he could pull before New Year's Eve.

Outside, the snow went on falling over Vellmark, softening the roofs, filling the streets, covering the tracks of every living thing, and Coral blew out the lamps one by one and sat down in the dark beside the chest, with the baby breathing below her like a small tide, and began, silently, to plan.
