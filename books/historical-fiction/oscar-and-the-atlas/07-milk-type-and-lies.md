## 7. Milk, Type, and Lies

The baby cried for three days before Coral learned the trick of him.

Not that there was one trick. There were eleven tricks, and he rotated through them with a tyrant's whimsy — hungry, wet, cold, bored, lonely, startled by his own fist, offended by the draft under the door, and once, memorably, furious about nothing at all while the church bells rang noon. She had tried everything her exhausted mind could invent: walking him along the rows of type cases, humming the only song she half-remembered from her mother, holding him against her shoulder so he could stare at the great black bulk of the press like a congregation at a sermon.

Nothing worked until the fourth morning, when she sat down on the floor between the galley trays to eat her bread and he simply stopped.

He lay in the crook of her arm, red-faced and damp, watching her chew with an expression of profound grievance slowly giving way to fascination. Then he yawned, a vast theatrical yawn that seemed to unjoint him entirely, and fell asleep as though sleep had been his plan all along and the crying merely a negotiation tactic.

Coral sat very still, bread halfway to her mouth, and looked down at him.

*Well,* she thought. *That's blackmail.*

She finished her breakfast one-handed, which was a skill she had not possessed a week ago, and considered the situation with the same cold pragmatism she brought to a difficult page of composition. The child needed milk. The child needed warmth. The child needed, apparently, to be held by someone who smelled of ink and tallow, because every attempt to leave him in the blanket-lined drawer of the proofing table produced howling within ninety seconds.

And none of this, she reminded herself, was her problem. She was a courier now, or nearly one. Master Quillon had made his offer; she had not yet answered it. The pamphlets still lay hidden beneath the floorboard by the wash-up sink, wrapped in oilcloth, each one a small paper bomb with Corvane's signature ticking inside it. Her job was to keep her head down, set type for whoever paid, and get the evidence across the river before the year turned.

The baby was not part of the arrangement. The baby was, if anything, a liability with a heartbeat.

"You," she told him quietly, "are going to be the death of me."

He slept on, unbothered, one small fist curled against her collarbone, and something in her chest did a thing she refused to name.

* * *

Naming him was Quillon's fault, though he never knew it.

He came by on the fifth day, nervous as ever, checking the shop's front room with the furtive glances of a man who had recently discovered that windows face outward. He peered at the press, at the type cases, at Coral herself, as though all three might be concealing contraband.

"The drawer," he said. "In the proofing table. It's been moved."

"I reorganized."

"You *reorganized.*" He said the word the way other men said *heresy.*

"Master Quillon." Coral kept her voice level and pleasant, the voice she used with difficult customers. "You told me to burn everything. I have not burned everything. That is already more disobedience than you asked for, and you will get no confession out of me beyond it. Now — do you want the Corvane almanac pages composed by Friday or not? The governor's own printers are behind schedule, and their money spends the same as anyone's."

This was true, and it was the shop's great joke: they printed for both sides. Last month it had been a run of sermons praising the governor's piety; this week, the official almanac, with Corvane's portrait on the first leaf looking stern and statesmanlike above the caption *THE ATLAS OF VELLMARK.* Coral set the portrait's border herself, and took a certain private satisfaction in kerning it beautifully. If the man wanted his face surrounded by ornament, she would give him ornament worthy of the lie.

Quillon wrung his hands, glanced at the drawer again, and visibly decided that ignorance was a garment he could still wear if he buttoned it quickly enough. He left without answering the question about the almanac, which meant she would have to answer it herself, which meant she was running the shop now, which was — she noted with some irritation — exactly what she'd been doing since her father died.

When the bell over the door faded, she went back to the proofing table and lifted the baby out of the drawer.

"That's no place for you anyway," she murmured. "Drafty."

It was while she was settling him into the warm hollow of the type chest — the big double case near the stove, lined with a folded apron, its compartments empty since her father's death because no one else could read his arrangement of sorts — that the joke arrived. She looked down at the small scowling face, at the way even asleep he seemed to be issuing decrees, and thought of the almanac portrait waiting upstairs in the forme.

*The Atlas of Vellmark.*

"Atlas," she said, testing it.

The baby opened one eye.

"No," she told him firmly. "Absolutely not. It's a bitter joke, not a christening. You're a bundle of seditious pamphlets with lungs. You don't get a name. Names are for things we intend to keep."

But that evening, feeding him goat's milk warmed in a cup set in the ash beside the stove — goat's milk bought from the widow Brann two streets over, who asked no questions because she was paid in printed handbills for her late husband's memorial service, such being the economy of Vellmark — Coral caught herself saying it aloud.

"Hush, Atlas. Hush, Atlas, you little tyrant, that's my thumb, not a teat."

She stopped. Listened to her own voice in the empty shop. And understood that the joke had already done what jokes do: it had walked in wearing a costume and taken up residence.

*Atlas.* Because he carried the whole weight of something enormous on a body the size of a loaf of bread. Because the name was a curse and a shield at once — no one hunting a missing Corvane heir would look for a child called by the governor's own proud title. And because, she admitted in the honesty of the hour past midnight, when the stove ticked and the frost ferned the windows: because it was hers. However briefly. Whatever came.

"Forget the face," the note had said. But nobody had said anything about forgetting the name, mostly because there hadn't been one.

She fed him, and burped him against her shoulder with a technique discovered by trial and error and one unfortunate incident involving her only clean shawl, and when he slept she laid him in the type chest and stood over him a long moment with the candle.

"Don't get attached," she advised herself.

* * *

Sera Ondel came on the ninth day, ostensibly about flour.

Coral heard her boots on the frozen lane before the knock — the brisk, unhurried tread of a woman who had delivered half the district and buried a good portion of the rest. Sera Ondel was sixty, built like a dock crane, with forearms that had turned more babies than the town midwife's ledger could honestly account for and eyes that had seen everything twice and believed most of it.

"Coral Dravenmoor." She planted herself in the doorway with a sack of flour she had clearly invented as a pretext. "I've come about the flour."

"We didn't order flour."

"No," Sera agreed, stepping inside and closing the door behind her with her heel. "Funny thing, that. Widow Brann says you've been buying goat's milk. Half a jug, every second day, regular as bells. Says you take it warm." She set the flour down on the composing stone with the finality of a gavel. "Now, I've known you since you were a girl with ink to your elbows, Coral, and I'll say plainly what I've come to say: you've got the look of a woman hiding a baby, and I'd take it kindly if you'd stop wasting both our time."

Coral's mind went white and then very fast. Deny. The word arrived first, old habit, the whole architecture of her careful neutrality rising around her like scaffolding. *What baby. What a strange thing to say. Perhaps the milk is for baking—*

From the type chest by the stove came a sound. Not a cry. Worse. A small, satisfied, waking snuffle — the sound of an infant deciding the world was worth inspecting again.

Sera Ondel did not move toward the chest. She folded her arms and waited, with the patience of a woman who had outlasted forty years of laboring mothers insisting they couldn't possibly, wouldn't possibly, and then delivering in the cart outside.

Coral exhaled. "How long have you known?"

"Since the milk. The flour is so you'd open the door. You haven't opened your door to a neighbor since your father died, and grief makes people rude, but not *that* rude." She crossed the room, looked into the type chest, and her weathered face went through several rapid changes — surprise, tenderness, and then something harder, something professional. She picked the baby up with the ease of long practice and turned him toward the light, examining him the way Coral examined a fresh proof: quickly, thoroughly, missing nothing.

"Well-fed," she pronounced. "Clean. Warm. Whoever's minding him knows what she's about, for an amateur." A pause. "Whose is he?"

"I don't know."

"Coral."

"I truly don't. He was left at the door. Wrapped in—" She stopped.

"In what?" Sera's voice had gone quiet in a new way.

"Papers."

The midwife looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a patrol went by on the lane, boots crunching, a soldier laughing at something. Neither woman breathed until the sound faded.

"Right," Sera said finally. "I'm not going to ask what papers. Here's what I know, and you can weigh it: there are soldiers asking after a missing infant at the market. Not loudly. Quietly, which is worse. A lieutenant came to my door Tuesday wanting to know if any births had gone unregistered this season. I told him the truth, which is that half the births in Tanner's Quarter go unregistered because registering them means the tithe-man comes, and mothers would rather lie than starve." She bounced the baby gently on her forearm, an unconscious motion, older than any revolution. "He wrote it all down very carefully. Men who write things down carefully are the ones to fear."

"He's not mine," Coral said. "I'm minding him. Temporarily."

"Mm." Sera settled onto the stool by the stove, uninvited, with the air of a woman taking possession. "And I'm temporarily sixty years old. Pass me that cup, girl, and tell me what you're feeding him, because goat's milk straight is too rich, you'll want it watered with a spoon of barley water, and where did you learn to swaddle, because this wrapping is a disgrace and we'll fix it directly."

It was not permission. It was annexation. Coral found herself fetching the cup, the barley, the disgraced blankets, and being corrected on each in turn — the milk watered just so, the swaddle snug at the shoulders and loose at the hips ("you'll ruin his hips, and then what will he walk on"), the type chest pronounced acceptable but moved closer to the stove.

"Why?" Coral asked at last, when the baby was rewrapped, fed, and sleeping in demonstrably superior fashion. "Why help? You don't know what he is. You don't know what I am. Helping me could cost you everything."

Sera Ondel looked into the fire. When she spoke, her voice had lost its briskness.

"I've brought four hundred children into this city, girl. Maybe more; I stopped counting at four hundred because counting them meant remembering the ones that didn't draw breath, and a woman can't carry that many names. In all those years, do you know what I learned?" She turned back, and her eyes were bright and hard. "Nobody asks to be born. Nobody picks their father, or their city, or their time. This one" — she nodded at the chest — "didn't pick whatever war he was born into the middle of. And when the soldiers come asking, quiet-like, after babies — well. I've buried enough of this city's children. I won't help bury another one to spare myself."

She stood, gathered her flour sack — which, Coral noticed, she left behind — and paused at the door.

"I'll come Tuesdays and Fridays. Milk, barley, and I'll teach you what your mother should have. And Coral—" She fixed her with a look. "Whatever papers those are, and whoever wants them: children grow faster than secrets. Sooner or later you'll have to decide what he is to you. Best start thinking on it now."

The door closed behind her. The shop was very quiet, just the tick of the stove and the small wet breathing from the type chest.

Coral stood in the middle of her father's shop, ink-stained, flour-dusted, absurdly close to tears, and realized she could not remember the last time someone had entered this room and left it warmer than they found it.

*Atlas,* she thought. *Look what you've done. Three days in my care and you've already recruited a midwife.*

* * *

The Tuesdays and Fridays became a rhythm, and the rhythm became, without Coral's noticing or consenting, a life.

Sera taught her the barley-water trick and the hip-safe swaddle and the particular bounce — two fingers under the chin, a slow figure-eight — that dissolved the evening colic like salt in warm water. She brought a knitted cap that had outlasted three of her own grandchildren's heads, and clucked over the draft from the door, and stuffed the gap with rag without asking. She told stories while she worked: the great flood of '74, the tanner's wedding where the groom fainted twice, the year the river froze solid enough to hold a fair. Coral, who had spent three years keeping conversation at arm's length the way one keeps a stranger's dog at arm's length, found herself talking. About her father. About the smell of the ink he'd mixed himself, linseed and lampblack, that no supplier had ever matched. About how the type cases still held his arrangement, his logic, his hands in wooden form, and how setting type in them felt like speaking with the dead in a language only they knew.

Sera listened the way she did everything — thoroughly, without flinching.

On the second Friday of the new arrangement, she stayed late, and the three of them sat by the stove while snow came down soft and businesslike outside, and the baby — Atlas, though never aloud, never where anyone could hear — lay in Coral's arms watching the firelight with the grave attention of a scholar.

"He watches everything," Sera observed. "Takes it all in. Mark me, this one will remember these days, somewhere down deep, even if he never knows them for what they were."

"What, the ink and the cold and the goat's milk?"

"The being loved, girl. Babies remember that the way stone remembers rain. Long after they've forgotten the shape of it."

Coral looked down. The baby had gripped her thumb — the whole of his hand barely spanning two of her knuckles — with the fierce, unreasonable grip of the very young, who have not yet learned that holding on costs anything.

"I'm minding him," she said. "Temporarily."

"Of course you are." Sera rose, stiff-kneed, and began gathering her things. At the door she stopped, and for a moment the briskness dropped away entirely, and Coral saw how tired the woman was, how much winter weighed on her, how heavy the simple mercy of showing up twice a week must be.

"There's talk at the market," Sera said quietly. "More soldiers this week. And a man asking questions who isn't a soldier — fine coat, soft voice, writes nothing down. That's the kind that frightens me worst, Coral. The ones who write it all down, you can rob the paper. These others carry it here." She touched her own temple. "I don't like the look of him. I don't like that he was seen standing across the lane from this shop yesterday, just looking at the windows."

The fire ticked. The baby slept. Somewhere across the frozen city, in rooms Coral could not imagine, plans were being drawn that had her name in them, or would soon.

"When he comes," Sera said, "and men like that always come — you send for me. Whatever hour. Do you understand? Whatever you've decided he is to you by then, you won't face it alone. That's not a kindness, girl. That's a condition."

She let herself out. Coral bolted the door, banked the fire, and stood a long time in the dark with the baby against her shoulder, listening to the wind work at the shutters.

Across the lane, in the shadow between the chandler's and the wall, a figure detached itself from the deeper shadow and walked away unhurried, boots silent on the snow, carrying what he had seen here in the vault behind his eyes — the lamplight, the silhouette at the window, a woman rocking a child in a shop that should have been dark.

Herron Vail did not write things down. He never needed to.

He had already begun to remember her face.
