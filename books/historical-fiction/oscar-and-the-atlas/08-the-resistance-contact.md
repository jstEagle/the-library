## 8. The Resistance Contact

The bookseller's shop was called The Gilded Page, which was a lie twice over — nothing in it was gilded, and half of it was not, strictly speaking, pages. Coral found it on Tallow Row on a grey Tuesday morning, tucked between a coffin-maker and an establishment that sold nothing but pickled eels, its sign hanging by one chain so that it read *The Gilded P* to anyone approaching from the river side.

She had walked past it three times before she made herself go in.

Quillon's instructions had been precise in the way of a man who wanted no part of what he was describing. *Ask for a book on river hydrography. If he asks who sent you, say "a man who owes a debt." Do not linger. Do not look at anything longer than a customer would.* He had pressed a coin into her hand for the purchase — an actual coin, for an actual book, because as Quillon had said with a thin smile, "the best cover story is one that costs you something."

The bell over the door gave a tired jangle. Inside, the shop smelled of dust and leather and pipe smoke, shelves rising crooked toward a ceiling lost in shadow. Books lay everywhere in drifts, stacked sideways, piled on chairs, propped open like sleeping birds. A cat — enormous, orange, and visibly offended by existence — occupied the only clear patch of counter.

"Closed," said a voice from behind the tallest shelf.

"The sign says open," Coral said.

"The sign also says *Gilded*. A man must be allowed his fictions."

He emerged then, and Coral revised several assumptions at once. She had expected someone lean and watchful, a creature of corners. Ivor Kade was broad as a barrel, fifty-odd years old, with a beard going to iron-grey and ink stains up both forearms like a printer's — though a bookseller's hands would know ink too, she reminded herself. His eyes were small and quick and missed nothing, including, she suspected, the fact that her coat did not fit her the way a coat should fit a woman carrying nothing.

She set the coin on the counter beside the cat, who sniffed it with contempt. "I'm looking for a book on river hydrography."

"A popular subject this season," Kade said mildly. "Who recommended it?"

"A man who owes a debt."

Kade looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached beneath the counter, produced not a book but a key, and turned the shop's sign to *Closed* with the unhurried air of a man closing early because business was bad, rather than because business was about to become extremely dangerous.

"Through the back," he said. "Mind the cat. He bites printers."

"I'm not a printer."

"No?" He glanced back over his shoulder. "You've got type metal under your thumbnail and a compositor's callus on your right middle finger, and you walk like someone who has spent twenty years standing at a stone. But do go on telling me you're not a printer."

* * *

The back room held a stove, two chairs, a table scarred with cup-rings, and more books, though these were shelved properly, spines out, alphabetized — the working collection, Coral thought, the ones that mattered. Kade poured tea from a dented pot without asking whether she wanted any, which struck her as either hospitality or habit, or possibly interrogation technique.

"You're Dravenmoor's daughter," he said.

The cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "I don't recall saying my name."

"You didn't. You didn't have to." He settled into the other chair, which groaned. "You have Aldric Dravenmoor's hands. I'd know them anywhere. He had the same way of holding a piece of type — like it might apologize to him if he gripped it too hard." Something moved across his face, quick as a fish under ice. "I knew your father, girl. Before you were born, and after. We were friends. Of a particular kind."

"What kind?"

"The kind where we never met in daylight and never used each other's names." He sipped his tea. "It was a very warm friendship, considering neither of us would have recognized the other in church."

Coral set her cup down carefully. Her heart was doing something unhelpful. "Then you knew what he printed. After hours. The things he never told me about."

"I knew some of it."

"He told me he did job work. Bills of lading. Wedding invitations." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Twenty years I worked beside him and he never once—"

"Of course he didn't." Kade's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "What would you have done with it? At eight? At twelve? At sixteen, when Vail took over Corvane's intelligence service and started keeping lists? Your father wasn't lying to you, Miss Dravenmoor. He was armoring you. There's a difference."

"There isn't," she said. "Not from where I stand."

Kade studied her across the steam rising between them. Outside, the coffin-maker's saw whined through a board; the sound went on far too long, like the city itself clearing its throat.

"Do you know how your father died?" he asked.

"He fell ill. In the spring of '85. Fever of the lungs. I nursed him for six weeks."

"That's what the doctor wrote, yes. And the doctor was paid to write it." Kade turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Aldric printed a pamphlet that winter. An account of what happened at Marrow Field — the grain riot. Eleven dead, though the official count was two, and both of those 'regrettable accidents involving their own knives.' Your father set the true names, the true number, and the order that preceded it, signed by a captain reporting directly to Governor Corvane. Two hundred copies. Distributed in a single night."

Coral sat very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, the cat performed a slow, thunderous patrol across the floorboards.

"The governor's men came to the shop on the fourth of March," Kade went on quietly. "Your father had warning — an hour of it. Enough to burn the forms and scatter the type. Not enough to leave. He chose to stay, because if they searched the house and found evidence the printing had come from there, they wouldn't stop at him. They'd have taken the whole street for questioning. So he sat down at his own composing stone and waited for them, and when they asked if he was the printer, he said—" Kade's voice caught, just slightly, on the shape of words he had clearly carried for seven years. "He said, *I am the printer.* Nothing else. No denial. He gave them one sentence and saved forty families with it."

"They said fever of the lungs."

"They said whatever the family needed written. Your mother had died the year before. There was only you." He met her eyes. "They let him keep his dignity and his death certificate, Miss Dravenmoor, because even then, Corvane preferred his monsters quiet. It is the single mercy I know of in that whole affair, and I am not certain it was a mercy at all."

Coral discovered that she had gripped the arms of the chair hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She made herself let go, finger by finger, the way her father had taught her to loosen a locked-up form — evenly, patiently, so nothing sprang.

"All those years," she said. "All those nights he stayed late. I thought he was catching up on job work. I brought him supper. I brought him *supper*, Mr. Kade, while he was—" She stopped, because the next word was going to be *printing the truth about massacres*, and she could hear how it would sound, and it sounded exactly like the truth, which was the problem.

"He knew you brought him supper," Kade said. "He talked about it. The mutton pie, the little pickles. He'd sit in a safehouse eating cold pie at two in the morning and tell us his daughter made better pickles than any chandler in Vellmark, and that one day—" Kade stopped himself. Cleared his throat. Looked, briefly, at the ceiling, where the cat was now staring down at them with an expression of vast judicial disappointment.

"One day what?"

"One day you'd take over the shop," Kade said. "And he hoped to God you never would have to know why it mattered."

* * *

She did not cry. She was surprised by that, afterward — she had expected to, had braced for it the way one braces for a wave. Instead something else happened: the grief rearranged itself. It did not get smaller. It got *larger*, but differently shaped — no longer the grief of a daughter for a father taken by illness, ordinary and meaningless as weather, but the grief of an heir for an inheritance she hadn't known she'd been given. Seven years of mourning a lie, and the truth underneath it was worse and better in equal measure.

"So Quillon works for you," she said at last, when her voice would behave.

"Quillon works for Quillon," Kade said. "As he always has. He prints for us when the risk suits him and refuses when it doesn't, and charges us double either way, and I pay it, because he is the finest pressman east of the mountains and cowards make excellent craftsmen — fear teaches precision." He smiled faintly. "But yes. The route he's offered you runs through me. Which brings us to the matter at hand."

He rose, went to a shelf, and drew out a slim volume bound in plain blue boards — *A Treatise Upon River Hydrography*, published, according to the spine, in 1761. He opened it. The center had been hollowed with a knife, and inside lay a folded packet sealed with black wax.

"This goes to a contact in Ferrenholt, past the border. You'll carry it as part of a legitimate delivery — Quillon will provide invoices, real ones, for real customers along the way, so every checkpoint has paperwork to bore it. The packet contains the Hallow Bridge accounts. Names, dates, orders. Originals." He held it a moment before handing it over, and his small quick eyes were suddenly not quick at all, but very still and very old. "Before you take it, understand what you're carrying. Not paper. People. Every name in that packet is a household that was emptied. Some of them I knew. Some of them your father would have known. This is why he died, Miss Dravenmoor — so that someday someone could put all of it in one place and hand it to someone willing to carry it. He just didn't think it would be you. Neither did I. God forgive us both for that."

Coral took the packet. It weighed almost nothing, which seemed impossible, and everything, which felt accurate.

"There's something else," Kade said. He hesitated — actually hesitated, this barrel of a man who had faced down soldiers' questions in a shop full of seditious books. "Quillon tells me there's a child involved. That you've been seen caring for an infant."

Coral kept her face still. Sera Ondel had warned her: *the city talks, love, and talk travels faster than any courier.* "There's a child whose welfare became my concern," she said. "That's all anyone needs to know."

"Mm," said Kade, in the tone of a man filing something away. "For what it's worth — and it may be worth nothing — whoever left that child left it with a printer. Not at a church door, not at an orphanage. A print shop. Wrapped, I'm told, in pamphlets." He tapped the blue boards gently against his palm. "In my experience, when someone chooses a printer's door, they are making a request. Not of the printer. Of the trade. They are asking the trade to remember. And the trade remembers, Miss Dravenmoor. It's the one thing we're better at than governments."

The cat descended from the upper shelves, crossed the room with the gravity of a magistrate, and leapt onto Coral's lap without invitation, turning three times and settling, a warm dead weight of orange fur. Kade raised his eyebrows.

"He likes you," he said. "He's bitten two couriers this month alone. Make of that what you will."

"I'll make nothing of it," Coral said, scratching the animal's ears. "I'm not staying. I deliver, I come back, I go back to setting type for whoever pays."

"Of course," said Kade, and his smile was kind and unconvinced. "Your father said much the same thing, the first time I asked him to print something dangerous. Sat right where you're sitting, in fact — well, stood, he distrusted that chair — and told me he was a tradesman, not a martyr, and that he had a baby daughter and no interest in heroics." He picked up the teapot and refilled her cup. "Then he went home and set eleven names in twelve-point Baskerville, because he said the dead deserved a good face."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, the grey afternoon folding itself away. Coral stood, dislodging the cat with an apology, and tucked the hollowed book into her coat, against her ribs, where it beat against her side like a second heart.

At the door she paused. "Mr. Kade. The pamphlets. The ones the child was wrapped in — the Hallow Bridge account. Did my father ever print anything like them? Before?"

Kade was quiet a moment.

"He printed the truth," he said. "Every time he could, however he could, for fifteen years. Hallow Bridge is only the newest page in a very long book." His voice dropped. "And now the book needs a new printer. Think on whose hands it was always meant to fall into."

Coral stepped out into the cold of Tallow Row, the coffin-saw whining somewhere behind her, the pickled-eel smell thick on the wind. She had gone in as a courier. She came out as something else — she did not yet have the word for it, but she could feel it setting in her chest like fresh-cast type, hardening, ready for the stone.

Behind her, the shop bell jangled once, and went silent.

She was halfway home before she remembered that Quillon had told her not to linger, not to ask questions, not to look at anything longer than a customer would — and that she had broken every instruction within the first five minutes, and that Ivor Kade had known she would, because he had known her father, and her father had been exactly the same way.

What she did not see, because no one ever did, was the boy in the chandler's doorway across the row, watching her pass, counting her steps until she turned the corner — and then running, fast, toward the Governor's House, to tell a man in grey that Dravenmoor's daughter had finally gone to the bookseller's.

Vail had been waiting seven years for a Dravenmoor to make that mistake.

He would not have to wait much longer.
