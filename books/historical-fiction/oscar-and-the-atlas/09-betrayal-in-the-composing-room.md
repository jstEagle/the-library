## 9. Betrayal in the Composing Room

The trouble, as Coral Dravenmoor would later reflect, began not with soldiers or spymasters but with a boy named Pip Wrenn and half a bottle of plum brandy.

Pip was fourteen, all elbows and ink stains, apprenticed to Master Quillon on the understanding that he would learn a respectable trade and ask no questions. He had so far mastered neither. He could not set a line of type without transposing two letters — his broadsheets had recently announced a *Public Hanging*, which had caused a small riot until someone noticed it should have read *Public Hearing* — and he asked questions the way other boys asked for bread: constantly, hungrily, and without any expectation of satisfaction.

On the fourth evening before New Year's Eve, Coral came down to lock up the shop and found Pip sitting in the composing room with his back against her father's old type case, a bottle cradled in his arms like an infant, weeping.

"Pip." She set down the lantern. "What in God's name—"

"He's going to do it," Pip said, with the enormous dignity of the very drunk. "He's going to *do* it, Miss Dravenmoor, and I said I wouldn't have any part of it, and then he gave me some brandy to settle my nerves, except it's settling them right out the bottom."

"Slowly. Who is doing what?"

"Master Quillon." Pip hiccupped. The word *Quillon* seemed to cost him something; he winced around it as though it were a bad tooth. "He went out tonight. Through the Tanner's Quarter, in his good coat, which he only wears when he's frightened or groveling. And I followed him because I'm not stupid, whatever he says, and I saw where he went."

Coral crouched down. The shop smelled of lamp oil and cold metal, and beneath it, faintly, the sourness of spilled brandy. Somewhere upstairs, in the room behind the drying racks, the baby slept in a laundry basket lined with a blanket she now knew contained more than wool.

"Where did he go, Pip?"

"The Vane Street house." Pip pronounced the name carefully, the way one pronounces a curse. "Where Herron Vail keeps his office. There were lamps lit in the top windows, and a man let him in without knocking, and I waited near the coal cellar for — I don't know how long — and when he came out he was walking different."

"Different how?"

Pip considered this with the gravity it deserved. "Lighter," he said at last. "Like a man who's put down something heavy. Like he'd been carrying a stone all winter and somebody finally told him he could drop it."

Coral felt the cold come up through the floorboards, through her boots, through the soles of everything she had believed about the last three weeks.

"What did he give them, Pip? Do you know what he gave them?"

"I heard him talking at the door." The boy's voice dropped. "I wasn't meant to hear. He was saying something about a route. About a woman who'd carry papers past the river gate, and about a contact — a bookseller — and he said—" Pip stopped. His eyes, glassy and miserable, fixed on hers. "He said there was a child involved, miss. He said the child was the thing worth the most money, and that if they moved quickly they could have all of it by tomorrow's dark."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still. Coral stood up slowly, and found that her hands, hanging at her sides, had curled into fists so tight her nails bit her palms. She did not remember making them.

"Miss Dravenmoor?" Pip said. "You've gone a funny color."

"Have I." Her voice came out flat and strange. "Drink your brandy, Pip. All of it. Then go home to your mother's and don't come back to this shop tomorrow. Or the day after. Or ever, if you can help it."

"But my apprenticeship—"

"Your apprenticeship is over as of this moment, and consider yourself lucky. If you're very quiet and very gone, you may live to be terrible at some other trade entirely."

He blinked at her. Then, being Pip, he began to cry again — great honest sobs that shook his whole narrow frame — and she found herself, absurdly, patting his shoulder, this sniveling drunken witness to her ruin.

"You did well," she told him. "Do you hear me? Whatever else happens, you did the single best thing anyone has done in this shop since my father died. Now go. Out the alley door. Don't take the main street."

He went, weaving, pausing at the threshold to say, "He gave me the brandy because he felt guilty," and then, with sudden terrible clarity: "He's been feeling guilty for weeks, miss. I thought it was about the prices."

And then he was gone into the snow, and Coral stood alone in the composing room among her father's type cases, and the fury arrived.

It did not arrive all at once. It came in layers, the way frost comes across a window — first at the edges of things, then creeping inward, until the whole pane of her composure was white with it. Quillon. Master Quillon, who had wept over her father's coffin. Who had taught her the difference between a good impression and a ghosted one. Who had sat across from her not ten days ago and offered her the shop itself, the presses, the type, her father's life's work, as payment for carrying the truth past the river — and who had, all the while, already priced that truth for sale to the man who would burn every press in Vellmark rather than let it speak.

She thought of him saying *you're the only one I trust, Coral*. She thought of how he had looked when he said it — the slight downward tilt of his head, the careful folding of his hands. She had taken it for humility. It had been rehearsal.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty shop, to the cases of type, to her father's ghost wherever it was lurking. "Well, damn you, Aldous Quillon. Damn you twice."

Her voice cracked on the second *damn*, and she hated that, and she wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, hard, and got to work.

Because that was the thing about fury, she discovered: it was excellent fuel but a poor compass. It wanted her to march up Vane Street and hammer on Vail's door and tell him exactly what kind of man bought children. What she actually needed to do was quieter and much harder. She needed to be gone before tomorrow's dark. Which meant she needed to be gone now.

*

The baby woke the moment she lifted him, as babies always know, and regarded her with the solemn, unforgiving stare of the very young.

"Don't look at me like that," Coral muttered, wrapping him in the blanket — the blanket, pamphlets still sewn into its lining, because burning evidence she might need seemed premature even now, though she was beginning to revise her feelings about keeping anything at all. "We're leaving. Yes, at this hour. No, I don't care that you were comfortable. Your bedtime arrangements are the least of our problems."

Atlas — she still called him that privately, the bitter joke that had stopped being funny somewhere around the first time he gripped her finger with his entire fist and refused to let go — made a sound halfway between a complaint and a question, and then, deciding the matter was beyond him, tucked his head under her chin.

She packed the way her father had taught her to pack a forme for pressing: everything locked tight, nothing loose, no piece included that did not earn its place. The full evidence packet — Quillon's copies of the Hallow Bridge orders, the muster rolls, the letters — went into a false bottom of the type chest, beneath three trays of twelve-point Caslon that weighed enough to discourage casual curiosity. The baby went into a market basket with a false lid, holes bored in the sides, blankets inside. It was not a cradle. It was barely furniture. But it was the best disguise a child could wear in a city that had begun, she suspected, to sprout Vail's eyes on every corner like mold on a damp wall.

She left a note for Quillon. It took four drafts.

The first said simply, *I know.* The second added several anatomically specific suggestions. The third observed that he had sold a child's life for his own skin, and wondered whether the coin jingled nicely. The fourth — the one she folded and weighted under the stone on the counter — read:

*Aldous. You taught me that a lie set in type is still a lie, just heavier to carry. I hope yours sits well on you. I have taken what is mine: my father's chest, my father's courage, and none of your promises, which were never yours to give. When they come for you — and they will, men like Vail always eat their purchases last — remember that I warned you in the only way that costs me anything, which is by telling the truth.*

*Coral.*

It was too long. It was too proud. It was, she admitted, folding it, possibly the most self-satisfied letter ever composed by a fleeing woman at midnight. She almost rewrote it again. She didn't. Some things a person was entitled to.

*

Getting out of the shop was the easy part. Getting out of the quarter was another matter.

Vellmark in the last days of December wore its curfew like a shroud. Snow fell in fat, indifferent flakes, muffling the streets, and between the muffled streets stood the patrols — pairs of soldiers with lanterns, breath steaming, boots crunching in that slow rhythm of men who are cold, bored, and armed, which Coral had learned was either the safest combination in the world or the most dangerous, depending entirely on their mood.

She kept to the tanners' lanes first, where the stench hung even in winter and no soldier patrolled longer than he had to. The basket rode against her hip, warm and breathing, and twice the baby stirred and she stopped dead in doorways and hummed — tunelessly, desperately — until he settled. She hummed the printing song her father used to sing when a job went well, the one about the devil and the compositor, and thought bitterly that Quillon had never once sung it. Quillon whistled. Whistling was cheaper.

The problem revealed itself at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where she intended to cut toward Sera Ondel's door and instead found a checkpoint she had never seen before — two lanterns, a brazier, four men, and a sergeant reading a paper by firelight with the particular concentration of a man looking for a specific face.

Vail's description. It had to be. *A woman, alone, likely with a child.*

Coral pressed into the shadow of a doorway, heart slamming, and did arithmetic. Four soldiers. One lane in, one lane out. A baby who had, in a stroke of catastrophic timing, chosen this exact moment to wake.

He began to fuss — small sounds first, then less small ones. She bounced him. She hummed. She offered him the corner of her sleeve, which he accepted with the air of a connoisseur agreeing to sample the vintage. For thirty seconds, silence held.

Then a cat knocked over a crate two streets over, and Atlas startled, and produced a cry — not the thin mewl of hunger but the full-throated, red-faced outrage of an infant with opinions, and every lantern at the end of Cooper's Lane swung toward the sound.

"*Who's there?*" The sergeant's voice, cracking with cold and irritation. "*Come out where I can see you!*"

Coral stood very still in the doorway, doing the last of the arithmetic, and found the sum was this: run and be chased, or walk out and be seen. Neither was survivable. Unless—

Unless she was nobody. Unless the woman in the doorway was not a courier with contraband sewn into a blanket, but something so mundane, so pitiful, so *boring* that four cold men would wave her past just to stop hearing the baby.

She stepped out into the lantern light, hugging the basket, letting her shoulders shake — not acting, exactly, but borrowing freely from the genuine article, the fear that was already there and merely needed shaping.

"Oh — oh thank heaven, sirs, please—" Her voice came out high and ragged. "My sister's babe, sir, she's sick with the fever and I've walked half the city for the midwife on Ondel's Row and I'm lost, I'm completely lost, please don't shoot us—"

"No one's shooting anyone," the sergeant said wearily. He came close, raised his lantern, and looked at her — really looked, a long moment, and Coral watched his eyes travel over her face and knew, with a lurch, that he was comparing it to the paper in his pocket. "You alone?"

"Just me and the little one, sir."

"The mother's sick?"

"Burning up, sir. Since Tuesday."

Something moved behind the sergeant's eyes — fatigue, or December, or the memory of some fevered sister of his own. He lowered the lantern.

"Ondel's Row is past the granary, left at the shrine, third turning. Keep the babe quiet." He paused. "Curfew's lifted till dawn on account of the holiday preparations. Anyone asks, you're a midwife's runner. Say Sera Ondel sent you; she's known to the garrison."

"Thank you, sir. God keep you, sir."

"They mostly don't," he said, and waved her through, and turned back to his brazier and his paper and his cold.

Coral walked — did not run, *walked*, each step a small act of tyranny over her own legs — down Cooper's Lane, past the granary, past the shrine, until the lantern light was a memory and the lane bent away toward Ondel's Row. Only then did she stop, behind a woodpile, and lean against the frozen timber and shake, properly this time, with the basket held to her chest like a drum.

Inside, absurdly, Atlas had gone back to sleep.

"You," she whispered into his wool-wrapped warmth, "are going to be the death of me, and I want you to know that I have decided to forgive you in advance."

*

Sera Ondel opened her door before Coral finished knocking, which told Coral everything about what the midwife already knew.

"I heard the patrol," Sera said, pulling her inside into candlelight and the smell of herbs and hot water. "And I heard a woman's voice lying beautifully. Sit down. Give me the child."

"How did you know it was—"

"Coral." Sera already had the baby, already had him unwrapped with the swift economy of twenty years' practice. "In eleven days I have watched you invent three sisters, a dead aunt, and a fictional errand involving goose fat. You are many things, but subtle is not one of them. Now. Tell me why you're out after curfew looking like a woman whose world has ended, and why you're carrying my godson in a market basket."

So Coral told her. The Vane Street house. The lighter step. The route sold, the bookseller named, the child priced. She told it flatly, without drama, because drama was a luxury and besides, she was saving what remained of her voice for the important part.

"—so by tomorrow's dark they'll have everything. The route, Ivor Kade, the safe houses. Possibly me. Certainly him." She looked at the baby in Sera's arms. "I can't stay anywhere they'll think to look. Which means I can't stay with you, and I can't stay at the shop, and I can't stay anywhere in this city, and I don't—"

"Stop." Sera did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "First: you will stay here tonight, in the back room, because no one searches a midwife's house for a hiding child — we're supposed to have those, it's practically professional. Second:" she bounced Atlas gently on her knee, and her face, usually so composed, went grim as flint, "I delivered half the officers' wives in this garrison, and half the soldiers were born in this room, and Herron Vail himself has a niece whose labor I sat with for two days last spring. This street protects its own. Third—"

She stopped. In the silence, Coral heard it too: footsteps outside. Not the crunch of a patrol — softer, hurried, uneven, the walk of a man in a good coat moving fast through snow because he had somewhere to be and something heavy on his conscience.

Then fists on the door. Three quick blows.

Sera rose, handed the baby back to Coral, and blew out two of the three candles with a pinch of her fingers. Through the window's frosted pane, the silhouette on the stoop shifted, hunched, familiar — the particular slope of shoulders Coral had known for six years, the shoulders of the man who had taught her kerning and betrayed her for coin.

"Aldous Quillon," Sera murmured. "At my door. At this hour."

"Yes," Coral said. She stood in the shadows with the child against her heart and the evidence beneath the type chest across town, and she found that the fury had burned down overnight to something colder and more useful. "He knows I'd come to you. He's come to warn me — or to finish selling me."

Through the door, low and ragged, Quillon's voice: "Sera. Sera Ondell, I know you're awake. Please. I need to speak with her. I've done a terrible thing, and there isn't much time."

Coral looked at the door. She looked at the baby. Trust, she was learning, was a currency — and hers had just been devalued to nearly nothing.

But the knock came again, and beneath the guilt in that voice she heard something else, something that sounded almost like fear for someone other than himself.

"Let him in," she said quietly. "But Sera — stand between us, and keep a poker near."
