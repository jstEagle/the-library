## 10. Through the Tanner's Quarter

The apprentice's warning was still ringing in Coral's ears when Sera Ondel arrived at the cellar door with a basket of turnips and no intention of discussing them.

"They came for Quillon at dusk," the midwife said, setting the basket down and pulling back its cloth to reveal not turnips but a bundle of dry swaddling clothes, a flask of goat's milk, and a small clay jar of honey. "Three men in grey coats. Not soldiers — soldiers wear their stupidity proudly. These ones were quiet about it."

Coral had known since sundown. She had spent those hours doing what she always did when terror came: working. She'd packed the type chest with straw, lined it with her father's old apron, and drilled air holes in the lid with an awl while the baby — Atlas, though she'd begun saying the name without irony now, which frightened her more than Vail did — slept against her breast with the boneless trust of the very young.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't an accusation. Sera Ondel had been bringing milk for eleven days and asking no questions for ten of them, which was its own kind of confession.

"I've caught babies for thirty years, girl. I know when one is being kept somewhere unusual." Sera was already folding swaddling bands with brisk economy. "I also know Herron Vail pays his informants in silver, and half this street would sell their grandmother for less. So. Are we going, or are you going to stand there looking noble until they kick the door in?"

"We."

"Good." She handed over a heavy shawl the color of wet slate. "Wear that. Wrap him under it, against your left side, so your heart sounds cover any noise he makes. And Coral—" Sera's voice dropped into something gentler than Coral had ever heard from her, "—when I say walk, you walk. When I say run, you run. Do not stop to be brave. Bravery is a luxury for people who aren't carrying the whole truth in a blanket."

The baby woke as she lifted him, and made a small questioning sound, and Coral pressed her lips to the crown of his head before she could think better of it. Then there was no time for thinking better of anything at all.

* * *

The Tanner's Quarter sat along the eastern edge of Vellmark like a bruise, where the river bent and the city's refuse went to be made into leather. Even in December the smell was monumental — lime pits and dung and rotting flesh, a reek so total that respectable citizens crossed the river rather than pass through it. Which was precisely why Sera had chosen it.

"No one searches the Tanner's Quarter," she said as they slipped out the shop's rear door into the alley. "Vail's men are city men. They'll take the bridge road, the boulevards, the gates. They will not go wading through offal if they can help it."

"And if they do?"

"Then we're in trouble and I'll say something cutting about it afterward."

The night was hard-frozen, the mud frozen into ridges like the knuckles of some buried giant. Coral carried the type chest slung across her back with rope — inside it, beneath the straw and the sleeping weight of the child, lay the pamphlets wrapped in oilcloth, the massacre at Hallow Bridge in Corvane's own hand. Forty pounds of lead and paper and one heartbeat. Her shoulders had stopped complaining about the weight two days ago; now it simply lived in her body, the way grief did.

They moved through alleys Sera clearly knew well, past shuttered dyers' sheds and the dark mouths of courtyards. Twice they heard boots on stone ahead, and twice Sera pulled them into doorways with the calm of a woman who had delivered children during bombardments and found it only moderately inconvenient.

The first sweep came at the mouth of Cooper's Lane.

Grey coats, four of them, moving with lanterns hooded to slits. Coral heard them before she saw them — the particular rhythm of men searching, unhurried, thorough. Sera's hand closed on her wrist and drew her down a stairwell into a sunken yard where tanners' vats stood empty for winter, great wooden troughs furred with lime.

"In," Sera breathed. "Under the lip. Don't breathe through your nose."

Coral folded herself into the shadow beneath the vat's rim with the chest against her chest and the baby between them, a warm comma of weight. Through a gap between the boards she watched the lantern light slide up the lane, and her whole body became a held breath.

The baby stirred.

She felt it before she heard it — the small flexing, the gathering inhale that precedes either contentment or catastrophe. Her hand moved without instruction from her mind, found his fist through the wrapping, and closed around it. His fingers gripped hers. The inhale became, instead, a long sigh, and his grip tightened once, and he settled again, trusting her absolutely.

*He trusts me,* she thought, absurdly, crouched under a lime vat in a freezing yard with the spymaster's hounds twenty feet away. *This child whose grandfather kills people like my father. He trusts me.*

The lanterns passed. The boots passed. A soldier said something bored to another soldier, and someone laughed, and then there was only wind and the distant groan of the river ice.

Sera counted to sixty under her breath before she moved. "Well," she whispered. "That was nearly educational."

* * *

They crossed the open ground beyond the vats at a crouch, and here the quarter showed its true face: smoke hanging low from the curing fires that never fully went out, the frozen lye streams glittering like veins of tin, hides stretched on frames like sails of some wrecked and terrible ship. Coral had never been here after dark. Few had. The workers' huts leaked orange light around their shutters, and from one came the sound of a fiddle played badly by someone who didn't care.

A woman stepped out of the smoke directly into their path, and Coral's heart slammed against her ribs — but Sera only nodded to her, and the woman nodded back, and stood watch in the middle of the track as they passed, facing the way they'd come.

"You know everyone," Coral murmured when they were clear.

"I delivered half this district," Sera said. "The other half I fished out of the river when the tannery work took their lungs. These people have been owned by the smell for three generations, Coral. Nobody powerful comes here. That means nobody powerful has ever done them a kindness either — and they remember every one they've ever gotten or given." She glanced sideways. "That is the arithmetic of the poor, girl. Every kindness has a price, and every price gets paid eventually. Remember that tonight. Someone will pay for walking us through."

"I could come back—"

"You could come back when it's safe to come back, and buy bread from them at fair prices, and let their children read your pamphlets free. That's how you repay it. Not with gratitude. Gratitude is cheap." Sera said it flatly, but Coral heard something underneath it, older and sadder — a woman who had spent thirty years watching kindness fail and offering it anyway.

The baby needed feeding near the old flax mill, and they risked a doorway to do it, Sera standing lookout with her shawl up against the smoke. Coral fed him in the dark by touch alone, and he ate with the ferocious seriousness of infancy, one small hand splayed against her collarbone.

"When this is over," Sera said quietly, not turning around, "you should keep him."

"He isn't mine."

"Neither is anyone, strictly. But 'mine' is a word people make true by deciding." She paused. "My husband decided it about me forty years ago and I never managed to argue him out of it."

"You never told me you had a husband."

"There's a great deal neither of us has told the other. That's how friendship works — you trade secrets slowly, over years, and pretend not to notice the exchange rate." Sera turned then, and in the orange light leaking through the shutter her face was suddenly very old and very kind. "I'm telling you one now: whatever you're planning, whatever you think you owe these papers or these people or your dead father — that baby doesn't care about Corvane. He cares whether your arms are steady. Don't forget which debt is real."

Before Coral could answer, the fiddle in the hut stopped.

In its place: whistles. Short, sharp, military. Two notes repeated, moving closer along the quarter's western edge — the sound of a search line closing.

"They brought dogs," Sera said, and all the warmth went out of her voice like water out of a cracked jug. "Move."

* * *

What followed, Coral would remember for the rest of her life in fragments: running with the chest bouncing against her spine while the baby, miraculously, mercifully, slept on; Sera hauling her through a gap in a fence that tore Coral's coat and drew blood along her forearm; the sudden stink of a fresh lime pit steaming faintly in the cold as they skirted it; a dog's cry, high and frustrated, somewhere behind and gaining and then — blessedly — losing them as Sera led them down into a drainage channel where the year's first ice crackled underfoot and masked their scent with salt and chemical rot.

At the far end of the channel, where it opened onto the towpath along the river, a boy of perhaps twelve waited with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light and a hand-cart piled with hides.

"Tante Sera," he said. "Papa says take the cart, take the towpath, and don't look at the tollhouse fire."

"The tollhouse fire?"

"It's burning nice and bright tonight." The boy grinned, gap-toothed. "Very warm. Very distracting."

Sera pressed something into his hand — a coin, Coral guessed, though it vanished too fast to see — and ruffled his hair with a roughness that meant tenderness in her language. "Tell your papa he owes me for the Ondel twins and I'm calling it settled."

They took the cart. Coral rode it, actually rode it, sitting among stinking hides with the chest in her lap like the world's most dangerous cargo, while Sera walked it along the towpath like a tanner's wife hauling stock, and the tollhouse fire bloomed orange against the low clouds half a mile downstream, drawing grey-coated men toward it like moths.

Halfway along the path, Coral realized she was shaking. Not from cold. From the specific tremor that comes when the body stands down after holding itself at attention too long. She put her hand flat on the chest lid and felt, through straw and wood and wool, the tiny rise and fall of the child's breathing, and the shaking slowed, and did not stop.

"Sera." Her voice came out strange. "Why? Truly. You could lose everything. Your house, your practice — they hang people for less than what you've done tonight."

Sera walked several steps before answering. The cart wheels crunched on frozen mud. Somewhere across the river, a church bell rang the three-quarter hour, indifferent as ever.

"When my daughter was born," Sera said at last, "the midwife who came for me was a woman named Petra Voss. This was during the last troubles, when Corvane was young and hungry. She came through checkpoints worse than anything tonight, because I sent for her, because there was no one else. And she came knowing they'd shot two printers that week for lesser crimes than carrying a bag of tools." She shrugged. "She saved my daughter's life and went home, and a month later they took her. I was nineteen years old and I hid under my bed while they marched her past our window."

The bell finished. The ice groaned on the river.

"I have been paying that debt for forty years," Sera said. "Tonight you're just the latest installment. Now stop talking and keep your head down — the gate's ahead, and gate guards are the stupidest form of human life God ever suffered to exist, but even stupid men notice crying."

* * *

The river gate was a timber arch lit by one lamp, manned — as Sera had predicted — by exactly the sort of guard she'd described: a broad, red-nosed man stamping his feet and wishing himself anywhere else. He looked into the cart, saw hides, smelled them, and recoiled.

"Stock for Kessel's yard," Sera said, in the tone of a woman who had been boring officials for decades. "And unless you want to carry it yourself, brother, wave us through. My feet are dying and my patience died an hour ago."

The guard waved them through. He did not look at Coral, curled among the hides with her heart hammering and the chest in her lap, nor at the small shape hidden beneath her coat. Why would he? No one powerful ever came through the Tanner's Quarter, and nothing worth finding had ever smelled that bad.

Only when the arch was fifty yards behind them did Coral allow herself to lift her head. The river ran black beside the path, broken with plates of grinding ice. Ahead, past the last warehouses, the country began — fields, hedgerows, and beyond them the dark suggestion of hills, and beyond *those*, invisible but suddenly, overwhelmingly present, the border.

She started to laugh. It came out wrong, half sob, and she clamped it down.

"Don't," Sera said, without looking at her. "Not yet. Laughing at the gate is how fools die. There's a farmhouse two miles on where you can shake apart properly, with a roof over you." She paused, and then added, quieter: "There's also a man waiting there. Bookseller, they tell me. Says he has business with the woman carrying Hallow Bridge in a type chest. Name of Kade."

Coral's arms tightened around the chest. "Ivor Kade knows my name?"

"He knows a great deal, apparently." Sera's voice was carefully neutral. "Including, I'd guess, more about your father than you do. So decide before you get there, girl — how much of yourself you're willing to hand over to strangers. Because everyone in this story wants something from you. The governor wants the child. The resistance wants the papers. And the bookseller—" she glanced at Coral, and the lamplight caught something complicated in her eyes, "—the bookseller wants *you*. Whether that's better or worse, I honestly couldn't say."

Ahead, the farmhouse window glowed like a single held ember in the dark. Behind them, far back in the smoking quarter, a whistle sounded — two short notes, then silence, then two more.

Closer than before.

Sera quickened her step, and Coral, with the sleeping child against her heart and the massacre against her spine, followed her into the dark.
