## 11. The Second Checkpoint

The coffin was oak, and it was the finest thing Coral had touched in weeks.

She'd found the funeral party at the Tanner's Quarter crossroads just past dawn — a widow named Marta Brune, her two grown sons, and a hired carter, all of them standing around a cart like people who had forgotten what they were waiting for. The carter had quit on them, apparently. Walked off into the frozen morning without so much as a by-your-leave, which in Vellmark that winter was less a betrayal than a career move.

Coral had stood in the shadow of a tannery wall with the baby strapped against her chest beneath her coat — he was asleep, thank every merciful saint, and warm as a loaf — and she had done arithmetic.

Three soldiers at the river gate. One road to it. A woman alone with a bundle draws eyes. A woman walking with a grieving family does not.

"You'll want someone to sit with him," she said, stepping out of the shadow before her courage could file a formal objection. "The dead, I mean. Nobody should ride to burial alone."

Marta Brune looked her up and down with the particular thoroughness of a woman who had buried a husband and raised two sons through a governor's martial law and had precisely no patience left for nonsense. "And who are you?"

"Coral. I'm traveling to my sister's past the river. I can pay for the seat." She had eleven coppers and her father's silver type punch, and she was prepared to spend neither lightly. "My mother died in autumn. I know how the roads are."

Something in that last sentence landed. Grief recognized grief the way dogs recognize each other at a distance — no introductions required.

"Five coppers," said Marta's elder son, a broad young man with his father's jaw and his mother's habit of deciding things quickly. "And you carry the wreath so your hands aren't free for gossip."

"I don't gossip," said Coral, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true.

* * *

So it was that Coral Dravenmoor rode toward the second checkpoint of her life in a funeral cart, holding a wreath of winter holly in her lap, with the evidence of Governor Atlas Corvane's massacres sewn into the lining of a coffin six feet from her knees, and the governor's own grandson sleeping under her coat directly above her heart.

There were moments — this was one of them — when the absurdity of her situation rose up in her throat like laughter and she had to bite down hard on it, because a woman who laughed on the way to her mother's funeral would be remembered, and remembered was the one thing she could not afford to be.

The baby — Oscar, she was calling him now in the privacy of her own skull, though she hadn't told anyone, not even Sera, not even herself out loud — stirred once as the cart jolted over a rut. She shifted the wreath to cover the motion and began, silently, to compose an epitaph for her own composure:

*Here lies Coral Dravenmoor's careful life. It died of pamphlets.*

"His name was Aldous," Marta said suddenly, beside her. She nodded at the coffin. "Forty-one years. He mended nets. Never made anyone rich, never made anyone sorry, except me, now." Her voice didn't waver. It had gone past wavering into something flat and permanent, like ice on a pond. "They say the river gate takes an hour now. Searches. My husband waited forty years to be buried and they'll make him wait another hour in the cold because some clerk is frightened of paper."

"Frightened men make thorough clerks," Coral said.

"Mm." Marta looked at her sideways. "You've got a way of saying nothing that sounds like something. My husband was the same. Drove me mad."

Coral decided she liked Marta Brune enormously and would probably betray her trust within the hour, and hated herself for both facts in equal measure.

* * *

The river gate had been a tollhouse once, back when Vellmark's river was a road instead of a border. Now the arches were bricked half-shut and the remaining gap was framed in timber palisade, and above it flew the Corvane colors — black atlas beetle on gray — hanging limp in the windless cold like something that had given up.

The queue ahead of them was short. That was worse. Short queues meant time, and time meant conversation, and conversation meant questions.

Coral ran the plan through her head again, the way you worry a loose tooth. The pamphlets — thirty-two of them, folded flat, wrapped in oilcloth — were inside the coffin's false bottom, which was not actually a false bottom but a gap between the inner box and the outer shell that honest coffins have anyway, for drainage, or dignity, or whatever coffins have gaps for. Sera Ondel had sewn them in two nights ago by candlelight, saying nothing the entire time except, at the end, "If a soldier shakes it, we're finished," and then, after a pause, "So don't let them shake it."

The baby was the harder problem. You could hide paper. You could not hide a heartbeat. But a sleeping infant under a mourning woman's coat, at a funeral, in winter — that was not hidden, exactly. That was simply unremarkable. Grief came with bundles. Half the women at any burial were carrying something: bread, brandy, a child too young to leave behind.

What Coral could not account for was the guard.

She saw him as the cart rolled into the gate's shadow: young, tall, wearing the Corvane gray with a fur collar that marked him as more than common soldiery, and he had a face that did not fit the uniform. Not soft, exactly. But unfinished. Like type that had been cast but not yet set into a line — all the metal there, waiting to find out what sentence it belonged to.

"That's Lord Edran," muttered the younger son, keeping his voice low. "Corvane's sister's boy. They say the old man keeps him at the gates on purpose. Testing him."

"Testing him how?"

"How do you test a dog? You see if it bites."

The cart stopped. A sergeant with a ledger waved them forward, and Lord Edran came around to the side of the coffin, and Coral discovered that her heart had opinions about the situation and was expressing them at considerable volume. She pressed the wreath against her chest and thought: *I am a woman going to bury my mother. I am tired. I am cold. I am nobody.* It was almost easy. She had been nobody for years. It was the one role she could play without rehearsal.

"Papers," said the sergeant.

Marta produced them. Death certificate, transit permit, parish seal. The sergeant read them the way soldiers read everything — slowly, suspiciously, as though the words might rearrange themselves into treason when he wasn't looking.

"Open it," he said.

"My husband," Marta said, "has been dead four days."

"Regulations."

"He has been *dead*, sergeant. Four days. In my parlor. Because your regulations closed the burial grounds across the river, and now your regulations delay him at the gate. If your regulations would like to dig the grave themselves, they're welcome to the shovel."

There was a silence with a great deal of weather in it.

Lord Edran had gone very still. Coral watched him — couldn't help watching him — and saw something pass over his face that she recognized, because she had spent three weeks wearing it herself: the look of a person discovering that the machine they serve runs on exactly this. On dead men kept waiting. On widows shouted down. On the word *regulations* deployed like a truncheon against the grieving.

"Open the outer lid only," Edran said quietly. "No need to disturb the shroud."

The sergeant glanced at him, surprised, then obeyed. The outer lid came up. Cold air moved through the gap between the boxes, and Coral felt the oilcloth packets shift a quarter-inch and stop, held fast by Sera's stitching, and she kept her face fixed in the mild blankness of a mourner thinking about her mother, while inside her skull a small voice screamed with the discipline of a professional.

Edran leaned over the coffin. He looked down at the shrouded shape of Aldous Brune, net-mender, forty-one years, never made anyone sorry, and he stayed looking for longer than inspection required.

"What did he die of?" Edran asked.

"The fever," Marta said. "The one going through the Tanner's Quarter since the granary burned. You'd know it. Your uncle's garrison burned it."

"Marta," said the elder son, warningly.

"No, let her." Edran straightened. His voice was still quiet. "The granary was condemned. There was an order. Whether the order was necessary—" He stopped himself, and Coral watched him stop himself, watched the sentence get locked away somewhere behind his teeth, and thought: *there it is. The crack. Small as a hairline in a typeface, but ink finds every crack there is.*

"Close it," Edran said. "Let them through."

The sergeant hesitated. "My lord, the woman—"

"—is carrying a wreath and a grudge, and both are legal." Edran's eyes moved then, for the first time, to Coral. Just briefly. Just a flicker — the kind of look a man gives a stranger when he is cataloguing faces out of habit, because his uncle's spymaster has taught him that faces are inventory. Coral met it with the flat patience of the genuinely bereaved, and offered up a silent prayer to no one in particular that the baby, under her coat, would go on sleeping through the single most dangerous nap of his short life.

The baby chose that moment to hiccup.

It was a tiny sound. A sound like a cork leaving a bottle, if bottles were the size of walnuts. In the gate's cold acoustics it might as well have been a gunshot.

Nobody moved. The sergeant frowned. Edran looked at her chest, where the wreath sat, where under the wreath and under the wool a small warm weight lay utterly still, having delivered its hiccup and returned to sleep with the magnificent indifference of the very young.

"Grief does strange things to the body," Coral said, before anyone else could speak. "Stomachs especially. My mother would have called it nerves."

It was, she reflected later, the strangest true statement of her life.

Edran studied her a moment longer. Then he stepped back from the cart and made a small gesture, and the barrier lifted, and the cart rolled through the arch and out onto the river road with the ice gleaming ahead and the border a gray line two miles off.

Coral did not breathe properly until the gate was out of sight.

* * *

They buried Aldous Brune at noon, in a churchyard where the ground was iron and the sexton used a pickaxe and apologized the whole time. Coral stood at the edge of the little gathering with her hood up and the baby — Oscar, her Oscar-for-now, the governor's grandson, the evidence with a heartbeat — breathing against her collarbone, and she listened to Marta Brune say the words over her husband, plain words, no Latin, no politics, just *he was here and he was good*, and something in Coral's chest came loose that she hadn't known was tied down.

Because it was grim, wasn't it. It was grim past all joking, that the truth about Hallow Bridge had crossed the same river as a net-mender's coffin, smuggled among the mourners of a man who had never hurt anyone, guarded by the nephew of the man who had hurt everyone. Power's whole architecture, riding in one cart: the dead man, the widow, the soldier-boy with doubt in his eyes, and her, the courier, holding holly.

"You cried," Marta said afterward, catching her at the lychgate. Not accusingly. Wonderingly.

"A little."

"For my husband?"

"For mine," Coral said, before she could stop it. "For everyone's."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Women like Marta — women who had buried things and gone on — could weigh a sentence like that and tell you its exact contents. Coral watched her do it. Watched her decide, deliberately, not to ask.

"Whatever you're carrying," Marta said, "carry it to your sister and put it down. Roads are long enough already." She pressed something into Coral's hand — a heel of bread and a copper, and closed Coral's fingers over both. "Aldous mended nets. He always said the trick isn't the knot. It's knowing which holes are worth mending and which you let the fish through."

Then she walked away across the iron ground, upright, finished, and Coral stood at the lychgate holding bread and a copper and a sleeping child and thirty-two reasons a government should fall, and she thought: *I will remember her name. Whatever else happens, I will remember Marta Brune.*

Behind her, on the far bank, faint with distance, the bells of Vellmark began to ring the hour — and stopped. All of them. Mid-peal, all together, the way they'd been ordered silent twice before in her lifetime, both times before bad news.

Oscar woke, and looked up at her with the grave, unfocused stare of the newly fed, entirely unaware that his grandfather had just silenced a city.

"Hold that thought," Coral told him, and turned toward the mountains.
