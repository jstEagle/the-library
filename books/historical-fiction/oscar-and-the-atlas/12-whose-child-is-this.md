## 12. Whose Child Is This?

The safe house was a bookshop that had stopped pretending to be anything else. Its shelves stood half-empty, its ledgers were cooked to look like failures, and the back room — where Ivor Kade now poured tea for Coral from a pot older than either of them — smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular melancholy of books nobody had bought in years.

Coral sat on a crate of unsold sermons, which she felt was fitting, and tried not to look at the basket beside her.

The baby slept in it anyway, loudly. He had inherited his mother's gift for sleeping through important conversations and his grandfather's, apparently, for making everything about himself.

"You're staring," Ivor said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're staring *while* thinking. It's very unnerving. Your father did the same thing. He'd stare at you like you were a page he'd already set and found an error in."

"My father," Coral said carefully, "has been dead eleven years, and yet everyone in this city seems to have known him better than I did. First Sera. Now you. Next I suppose the checkpoint soldiers will tell me he taught them their letters."

"He might have." Ivor settled into the chair across from her, a narrow man built like a bookmark, all edges and patience. "That's rather the point of what I have to tell you."

Outside, Vellmark's curfew bells rang the second hour after dark. Somewhere in the Tanner's Quarter, three days behind them now, Herron Vail's men were still kicking in doors and finding nothing but frightened tanners and bad air. Coral had crossed the river with a coffin, a grieving family who weren't grieving, and a heartbeat under her coat that hadn't stopped once since Hallow Bridge.

She had earned this tea. She intended to drink it before the world turned over again.

"Tell me," she said.

Ivor glanced at the basket. Then at the door. Then at the basket again, with the expression of a man deciding whether to jump into cold water all at once or one toe at a time.

He jumped.

"The child is Atlas Corvane's grandson."

The tea went down wrong. Coral coughed, set down the cup with exaggerated care so as not to throw it, and said, "No."

"Yes."

"No. Look at him." She pointed at the basket, where the baby had one fist raised in his sleep like a tiny orator mid-speech. "Look at him. He's — he's *small*. He's red. He has a birthmark shaped like a bean on his ear. Corvane is a mountain in a uniform. That child couldn't oppress a bowl of porridge."

"Coral."

"His daughter," she said, catching up with herself, because she was not stupid even when she was panicking. "Lysandra Corvane. The one who married the Harrowfield man and then didn't. The one they say—" They said a great many things about Lysandra Corvane, most of them invented by pamphleteers on both sides, and Coral had set type for at least six of the inventions herself, which made it difficult to remember which ones she'd believed.

"She carried him out," Ivor said quietly. "Three weeks ago. Out of the Governor's House, past her father's own guard, with nothing but a wet nurse's cloak and a plan she'd been building since the day he was born. And when the wet nurse turned back at the first checkpoint — lost her nerve, or lost her life, we've never learned which — Lysandra kept going alone. Until she couldn't anymore. Until she found your door."

Coral looked at the baby. The baby, sensing a dramatic moment, chose it to wake up, regard her with two grave blue eyes, and begin to fuss.

"That note," she said. "*Forget the face.*"

"Lysandra wrote it. If anyone ever traced the child, she wanted whoever held him to be able to put him down and walk away. No name, no claim, no crime." Ivor's mouth twisted. "It was meant as mercy. To you, I mean. Though I notice you didn't forget the face."

"I set type for a living. I don't forget faces; I remember them in reverse, white on black." She lifted the baby out of the basket because he was winding up toward a wail and because, frankly, holding him gave her hands something to do that wasn't shaking. He settled against her shoulder with a sigh, one small hand closing in her collar. "So the whole city is hunting him. Not just Vail. *Corvane*. The governor's own blood, smuggled out to—"

"To end it," Ivor said. "The line. The name. The dynasty." He leaned forward. "You've read the pamphlets, Coral. You know what's signed at the bottom of the Hallow Bridge orders. Lysandra grew up in that house. She watched what her father was, and she watched what her son would be raised to be, and she decided that the kindest thing she could do for the world — and the cruelest thing she could do to her father — was to make sure there was no heir. No little Atlas the Third learning to sign massacre orders with a steady hand."

The fire ticked in the grate. The baby breathed against her neck, warm and damp and entirely unaware that he was, at four months old, the most dangerous object in the province.

"So she's dead," Coral said. Her voice came out flat. "Or taken."

"We don't know." Ivor's honesty was brutal and she was beginning to be grateful for it. "Vail's men swept the river district the night after she left him. There's been no body, no arrest, no announcement. Which means either she escaped north, or Corvane is keeping her disappearance very quiet indeed. A governor can survive rebels. He cannot survive the story of a daughter who fled him."

Coral walked the length of the back room. Four steps, turn, four steps back. The baby rode her shoulder like a sailor riding a slow ship, drooling thoughtfully into her collar.

"Say I believe you," she said. "Say it's true. What am I supposed to do with that?"

"Do?" Ivor spread his hands. "Nothing different. Deliver him to the contact at Marrow Ford as planned, along with the packet. The resistance will—"

"The resistance will what?"

Ivor paused.

It was a small pause. A professional pause. But Coral had spent her life reading other people's proofs, catching the errors between the lines, and she caught this one.

"What," she repeated, "will the resistance do with the grandson of Atlas Corvane?"

"They'll keep him safe."

"Ivor."

"They'll keep him *safe*, Coral, and when the time comes—"

"When the time comes," she said, "they'll stand him up on a stage somewhere with a sash on him and tell the crowd whose blood is in his veins. Won't they. He's not a refugee. He's a *banner*. 'Behold, the tyrant's heir, and behold, he is ours.'" She heard her own voice rising and made herself stop, made herself breathe, because the baby flinched at loud noises and so, increasingly, did she. "They'll use him exactly the way Corvane would have used him. Just with better slogans."

Ivor didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He sat there with his tea going cold and his honest face doing honest arithmetic, and he said, "A living symbol ends wars faster than ten thousand pamphlets. Fewer people die, Coral. Isn't that worth—"

"Worth what? His whole life?" She turned to face him, the baby a warm weight against her chest. "You want to hand a child to a cause the way Quillon handed my route to Vail. Everyone keeps telling me this boy is evidence. Cargo. Leverage. Nobody — *nobody* — has asked me once whether he eats enough, or whether his ears hurt from the cold crossing, or why he cries at bells." She swallowed. "He cries at bells, Ivor. Every time. Somebody rang a bell over his cradle once, something bad, and I don't know what it was, and apparently neither does anyone else, because everyone's too busy asking what he's *for*."

The silence stretched. In it, the baby yawned enormously, displaying approximately four teeth's worth of ambition, and put his head down on her shoulder.

Ivor set down his cup. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — less the liaison, more the bookseller.

"Your father asked the same question once," he said. "Not about a child. About a pamphlet. We brought him proof of the Saltmarsh drownings — names, dates, orders. And he read it all night, and in the morning he printed it, and then he asked me: *who does this belong to now?* And I told him the truth belongs to everyone. And he said no." Ivor smiled, faintly, at some memory twenty years deep. "He said truth belongs to whoever carries it, and carrying it costs, and so you'd better be sure you're carrying it for the right reason. Not for the cause. Not for the money. For the *carrying*."

"And then he died for it."

"And then he died for it," Ivor agreed softly. "And I have regretted my answer every day since, because I think he knew, even then, what I'm only now learning: that the moment a truth becomes a *weapon*, it stops being true. It becomes just another thing men point at each other."

Coral looked down at the baby. Oscar's eyes — no. She hadn't named him. Naming him would mean something. She resolutely did not name him, and failed completely, and thought *Oscar* anyway, the way you think a prayer.

"If I give him to the resistance," she said slowly, "he grows up to be a flag. If Corvane gets him back, he grows up to be a knife. If I leave him at a church door, he grows up lucky, maybe, if the priest is kind, and never knows why soldiers ask questions about him for the rest of his life." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "There's no version of this where he's just a child, is there? Not anymore. Not in this city. His face was forgotten the way the note asked, and it didn't matter, because his *blood* is famous."

"Not in every city," Ivor said.

She looked up.

"North past the pass," he said. "Across the border. There are farms up there where the war is a rumor that arrives late and badly translated. Where a boy could grow up being nobody's grandson and everybody's neighbor." He held her gaze. "I shouldn't be telling you this. My instructions are explicit. Marrow Ford, the packet, the child, New Year's Eve. But I watched you cross a military checkpoint inside a coffin with that baby against your heart, and I watched you just now choose his crying over my argument, and I think — I think Lysandra Corvane chose your door for reasons even she may not have fully understood."

The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs, the shop's ancient cat knocked something off a shelf with the deliberate violence of cats everywhere.

"I can't decide this tonight," Coral said finally.

"No," Ivor agreed. "But you'll have to decide it soon. The packet goes to Marrow Ford by the thirtieth or the whole network goes dark — and Coral?" He rose, gathering his coat, and paused at the door. "The packet and the child are listed separately in my instructions. Two deliveries. Whatever you decide about one doesn't have to bind the other. Remember that. Most people don't, and it costs them."

He let himself out. The bell above the shop door jingled, absurdly cheerful.

Coral stood alone in the lamplight with the grandson of the most feared man in the province asleep on her shoulder, and she did the only sensible thing left to do: she checked his diaper, discovered it required immediate attention, and muttered, "Well. *You're* no banner."

He opened one eye at her, unimpressed, and she began to laugh — quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a woman standing at the edge of a very tall decision — and that was when she noticed, for the first time, the stitching at the blanket's hem. Fine, careful stitches, in thread slightly newer than the wool. A seam that wasn't a seam.

A lining.

She carried him to the lamp, laid him gently in the basket, and picked open the stitches with her thumbnail, the way she'd pick a kerned letter loose from a locked form.

Inside the lining, folded into a square no bigger than a playing card, was a letter. And across it, in a fine, educated, desperate hand, a single line:

*If you are reading this, you are the person I trusted most in the world, though we have never met.*

Coral's hands, so steady over a composing stick, so steady inside a coffin at a checkpoint, shook now.

She broke the seal.
