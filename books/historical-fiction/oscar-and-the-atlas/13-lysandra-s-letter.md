## 13. Lysandra's Letter

The letter had been there the whole time.

Coral found it on the fourth morning at Sera Ondel's house, in the worst possible way — which was to say, the baby had been sick in the night, and she had stripped the blanket from him in the grey light before dawn, and she was scrubbing it in a basin of water that went from clear to grey to something like ink when she noticed the seam.

It wasn't a seam. It was a pocket. Someone had sewn a second layer into the lining of the wool, fine stitches, careful ones, the kind of stitching her father used to do when he mended the canvas covers of the type cases because he never trusted anyone else's hands. And inside the second layer, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly the kind of washing she was doing right now, was a letter.

She sat down on the floor of Sera's kitchen with wet hands and read the superscription twice.

*To whoever is holding my son.*

"Well," said Coral, to nobody. "That's me."

The baby — Atlas, she still called him privately, though the joke had gone sour somewhere along the river and now she mostly just called him *little one* — was asleep again in Sera's spare cradle, which was actually a laundry basket lined with a shawl, because Sera Ondel did not own a cradle and had opinions about people who expected her to produce one overnight. The kitchen smelled of tallow and bread and the faint iron tang of the tanner's quarter drifting through the shutters. Somewhere outside, a dog was complaining about the cold in the long-suffering tone of a dog who has been complaining about the cold since October.

Coral broke the seal.

* * *

*To whoever is holding my son:*

*I don't know your name. I have imagined you for three weeks now, every night while he sleeps beside me, and I have made you tall, short, old, young, kind, reluctant, frightened. I hope you are frightened. I hope you are also stubborn, because only a stubborn person will still be reading this by the third paragraph.*

*My name is Lysandra Corvane. You know the name. Everyone knows the name. I watched a fishmonger in the Lower Market spit on the ground when my father's carriage passed, and then look over his shoulder as though the cobblestones themselves might report him. That is what our name does. It makes men afraid of the ground they stand on.*

*I am not writing to ask forgiveness. I am writing so that you understand what you are carrying, besides a child who cries at thunder and has recently discovered his own feet, which delights him beyond all reason.*

*You have the pamphlets. I put them in the blanket myself, stitched into the lining with these same hands, and if you are reading this letter you have found them or you soon will. Read them. I know what they say. I helped choose which ones to include. There were so many, and we could only take the weight of a few, and I lay awake choosing between massacres the way a merchant chooses between bolts of cloth. Hallow Bridge went in because of the date. My father signed the order on my birthday. He gave me a necklace that year. I wore it to dinner while he told the story of the bridge, how the crowd had been dispersed, how regrettable the losses, and I sat there with his garnets at my throat and said nothing, and I have been saying nothing for twenty-six years, and I am done.*

*Here is the thing I need you to know, stranger, whoever you are.*

*There was a generation of printers in Vellmark, twenty years ago, who printed the truth about the Granary Riots. You may be too young to remember. My father was not. He remembered every name. On a single night in March — they called it the Quiet Night afterward, because the city pretended it hadn't happened — his soldiers went down the print streets with lists. Eleven shops burned. Fourteen men died. Some in their shops, some dragged into the square. One of them, I learned later, was named Dravenmoor.*

*I don't know if that name means anything to you. If it doesn't, skip this paragraph. If it does — if you knew him, if you loved him, if you are sitting somewhere holding this letter with his blood in your history — then I cannot tell you anything that will make it better, and I am not going to insult you by trying. I will only tell you this: I found the list once, in my father's study, in his own hand. Not the soldiers' names. His order. He wrote it himself, at his own desk, with the same pen he used to write my birthday letters. I held the paper and I understood that the man who tucked me into bed and the man who wrote that list lived in the same body, and that I was the daughter of both of them, and that someday everything he built would pass through me into another pair of hands.*

*I looked at my son that night — he was two weeks old — and I saw my father's mouth on him. Corvane mouths run in the family; we all have the same stubborn lower lip. And I thought: this child will inherit a throne built on a list. And I thought: unless someone puts him down somewhere else.*

*So here is what I am asking, and I know the size of it.*

*Do not give him to the resistance. They will make him a banner. Do not give him back to my father; he will make him an heir. Do not give him to anyone who wants him for what he is. Give him to someone who wants him for nothing at all — for his weight, and his crying, and his ridiculous feet. A farm family. A widow. Anyone whose door he can grow up behind without the name following him like smoke.*

*He is not innocent. No one is; I've stopped believing in innocence, it's a word powerful people use about their children and powerless people use about their dead. But he is unwritten. That is worth more than innocence. Everything my father ever did was written — signed, sealed, entered into the record of the world. This boy is a blank page. Keep him blank.*

*If you are reading this, then I failed to escape, or I succeeded and chose not to take him further myself, and either way you are standing where I stood, holding what I held. I'm sorry. It is a terrible thing to hand a stranger your whole heart in a bundle of wool and sedition. I have done it anyway. Forgive me. Or don't, and carry him safely regardless. Stubbornness will serve.*

*One more thing, and then I will let you go.*

*Whatever you decide to do with the pamphlets — burn them, deliver them, print them until the paper runs out — decide it yourself. Not for my father's enemies, not for mine. Everyone who touches this child will want to tell you what he is for. Don't listen. The moment he becomes useful, he becomes theirs. He was never for anyone. He was only ever mine, for six weeks, and now he is no one's, and no one's is the best thing a person can be in Vellmark this winter.*

*His name — his real one, the one on no register anywhere — is Oscar.*

*I chose it out of a book of saints my mother owned. It means, if the book is to be believed, friend of God. I liked the smallness of that. Not servant of God, not soldier of God. Friend. Something chosen, not commanded.*

*Be well, stranger. Be stubborn. Forget my face if you ever learn it.*

*L.C.*

* * *

Coral read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases, very carefully, the way her father taught her to fold a proof you intended to keep, and she sat on Sera Ondel's kitchen floor and did not cry, because apparently she was constitutionally incapable of it, but something in her chest rearranged itself with an audible click, like a forme being locked into the chase.

Oscar.

Not Atlas. She'd been calling him Atlas for a week — a bitter little joke, the tyrant's name on a squalling infant, private revenge in a nickname. And all the while he'd had a real name, a small one, a chosen one, stitched into a blanket by a woman who had picked it out of her dead mother's book of saints because she wanted her son to be a friend and not a soldier.

"Your mother," Coral said to the laundry basket, "was smarter than everyone in this entire city."

From the basket, Oscar kicked one ridiculous foot in agreement.

Sera came in from the yard with an armful of frozen linen and took one look at Coral's face. "What's happened?"

"Nothing." Coral got up off the floor, knees cracking. "Everything. I found a letter."

"In the blanket? I told you that blanket was too well made to be honest." Sera dumped the linen in a heap and peered at her. "Bad news?"

"No." Coral turned the letter over in her hands. "It's the strangest thing. It's the worst news I've ever gotten and it's good news. It's—" She stopped, because she didn't have a word for it, and Sera Ondel was not a woman who waited patiently for people to find words.

"Is it about the boy's mother?"

"Yes."

"Alive?"

"I think so. I think she gave herself up so he could get out." Coral looked toward the basket. "She wants him given to a family. A farm family, a widow, anyone. She wants him to grow up blank. No name, no cause, no throne."

Sera snorted. "And the resistance wants him as a figurehead, and the governor wants him as an heir, and the spymaster wants him in a sack at the bottom of the river. Everyone in this story has plans for him except him." She began folding linen with the violence of a woman who found folding therapeutic. "What do you want?"

"Me?"

"You're the one holding him. The letter says so. *To whoever is holding my son.* Well. Whoever. That's you, girl. So what do you want?"

Coral opened her mouth to say *I want to deliver the evidence and go home to my shop*, which was the true answer, or had been true, or had been the shape of true before the letter. What came out instead was:

"My father printed truths. Twenty years ago. The same generation the letter talks about." Her thumb moved over the oilcloth. "I always thought he just... died. Printers die. Ink in the lungs, bad winters, whatever you tell children. But Ivor Kade started to say something, back at the shop, before everything went wrong, and I wouldn't let him finish. And now this woman writes fourteen names down on a page and one of them might be—"

She couldn't say it. Sera waited, folding.

"If it's him," Coral said finally, "then I've spent my whole life setting type in the shop he died protecting, thinking it was just a shop. Thinking it was just work. And she—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She mastered it. "She killed him. Not her personally. Her father, with her birthday necklace on. And she's the one person in the world who understands exactly what that means, and she's probably in a cell, or worse, and she'll never know whether the blanket made it."

Sera set down the linen. She crossed the kitchen, took Coral's chin in one flour-dusted hand, and tilted her face up like a physician examining a symptom.

"There it is," she said. "I wondered when you'd crack. You've been holding yourself together with work since the night you found him. Work's good glue, mind, but it isn't bone." She released her. "Grieve however you like, but grieve *forward*, girl. Backward grief drowns people in this district — I've pulled enough of them out of the river to know."

"That's the least comforting comfort I've ever received."

"I'm a midwife, not a poet. Comforting is for people with time." Sera glanced at the basket, where Oscar had woken and was conducting a one-child protest against the concept of morning. "Now. Are you going to tell me what you're going to do? Because you've got the look of a woman composing a sentence she hasn't finished."

Coral looked at the letter. At the basket. At the window, where the light over the tanner's quarter was doing its usual grey impersonation of daylight.

"He needs a family," she said slowly. "A real one. Across the border, past the checkpoints, where no one asks questions. And the pamphlets need to reach more than one pair of hands, because a truth that lives in one place can be killed in one place." She heard herself say it and was surprised, the way you're surprised by your own handwriting sometimes. "And I need to know about my father. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"Hm," said Sera, which from Sera was a round of applause.

"And I want to print something," Coral added, almost to herself. "I don't know what yet. But I keep thinking about ten thousand copies, and I keep thinking about a press sitting idle in a cellar, and I keep thinking that Lysandra Corvane wrote *decide it yourself* and nobody — not Quillon, not the resistance, not her — gets to decide for me."

"There she is," said Sera.

But the morning had one more thing to teach her. It came an hour later, in the form of Tam, the drunk apprentice, hammering on Sera's door with none of his usual drunkenness and all of his fear, breathless in the doorway with frost in his hair.

"Coral. Coral, you have to move. Now." He grabbed the doorframe like the street was tilting. "Quillon talked. Vail has half the route — the river gate, the coffin trick, all of it. And there's worse." He swallowed. "Vail's men were asking about the shop. About your father. About who else was printing that winter. They're not just looking for you anymore, Coral. They're looking backward."

Coral's hand closed around the letter in her pocket.

Backward. Toward a list of fourteen names, written in a governor's hand, in a study with a birthday necklace hanging above the desk.

"They're coming here?" she asked.

"Not yet. Hours, maybe. Vail likes to be thorough." Tam's eyes flicked to the basket, and back. "Thorough is the problem. Thorough finds things."

Coral was already moving — blanket, basket, bread, the oilcloth packet, the letter pressed flat against her ribs where it could feel her heartbeat. Outside, the bells of Vellmark rang the hour, indifferent as ever, and somewhere across the frozen city a man with a patient face was opening a file with her father's name in it.

She had meant to grieve forward. The world, it seemed, intended to make her run forward first.
