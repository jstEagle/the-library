## 14. Vail Closes the Net

The apprentice came at half past nine in the evening, drunk enough that he had to hold the doorframe to remember which way doors opened.

His name was Tam Wicker, and he was sixteen, and he had been drinking the governor's health in a tavern called the Drowned Bell because it was safer to toast the governor than to refuse. He stood in the doorway of Sera Ondel's kitchen, where Coral had been mending a shirt she did not care about, and he said, very carefully, the way a man walks across ice:

"They've taken Master Quillon."

The shirt stopped moving under Coral's needle.

"Taken him where?"

"Soldiers. Four of them and a closed carriage, and Herron Vail himself standing on the step like he'd come to buy type." Tam's eyes were glassy but underneath the glass there was something awake and frightened, an animal that had been drinking to drown itself and found the water full of teeth. "I got out the back. I ran. I ran here because —" He stopped. He looked at the baby, who was asleep in a laundry basket lined with Sera's good flannel, and his face did the arithmetic of a boy realizing exactly how much trouble he had run toward.

"You ran here," Coral said quietly, "because you're the only one left who knows I'm not dead."

"I don't know that either," Tam said miserably.

Sera Ondel, midwife, widow, keeper of the neighborhood's secrets the way other women kept sugar, set a cup of watered wine in front of the boy and said, "Drink that and tell it again, slow, from the beginning."

So he told it again, slow, from the beginning: how the soldiers had come without drums or noise, which was worse than drums; how Vail had walked through the shop touching nothing, looking at everything; how Quillon had stood behind his desk with his hands flat on the wood like a man steadying himself against weather, and said, "Master Vail, this is a printing house, we print wedding invitations and grain prices," and how Vail had smiled — Tam shuddered describing the smile — and said, "Yes. Let us discuss what you print."

"And then?"

"Then they put him in the carriage. And Vail stayed. He's still there, missus. He told the neighbors the shop would be open tomorrow, normal hours, and he sat down in Master Quillon's chair."

Coral looked at the baby. Atlas slept the way only infants and the very foolish sleep — completely, arms flung wide, as though the world were a thing that could be trusted. She thought about the packet of papers sewn into the false bottom of her father's old traveling trunk, currently sitting in Sera's back room under a bolt of linen. She thought about the route written in her head, memorized and burned: river gate, coffin cart, mountain pass, border. She thought about how many people knew pieces of it.

Quillon knew all of it.

"Oh, Quillon," she said softly. Not in anger. In something worse — in understanding. She had known for three days, since the drunk apprentice first stumbled past her with his eyes down, that Quillon was afraid in a new way, a sweating, door-checking way. She had told herself he was too vain to break, too proud of his cleverness, too invested in his own legend as the resistance's brave little printer. Vanity was a kind of armor. She had counted on it.

But vanity was also a hinge, and fear was a hand on the door.

"He won't last a night," she said. "Not against Vail. Not real torture — Vail doesn't need real torture. He needs a warm room, a bottle of good brandy, and four hours of pretending to be your friend. Quillon will tell him everything by midnight and believe by morning that he'd planned to all along."

Tam looked like he might be sick into the wine. "Then you have to run. Tonight. Now."

"Yes," Coral said. And then, because her mind was her father's mind and it worked the way a press works, steadily, one sheet at a time: "No. Not yet. Listen to me, both of you. If I run tonight, I run blind. Vail will have watchers on every road out of this city by dawn, and he'll know my face is the one worth watching for. I need until morning. I need to know what Quillon gives him and what he holds back, because what Quillon *doesn't* know is the only road I'll be safe on."

"And how will you know that?" Sera asked.

Coral looked at Tam. Tam looked at his wine.

"There's a coal chute," Tam said reluctantly, "behind the shop. It comes up in the cellar. I've used it for — deliveries. Ones Master Quillon didn't want the soldiers counting."

"Of course you have," Sera said, with the tone of a woman filing away a future conversation.

"I can't go back there," Tam whispered. "If Vail sees me —"

"He won't see you. You're going to stand in the alley and watch the front door, and if anything comes out of it — carriage, soldiers, Vail himself — you whistle the tune from the fish-market song, the rude one about the eel."

"The one about the — yes. Yes, I know that one."

"Good." Coral stood, and picked up the baby, who woke just enough to frown at being handled and then decided, generously, to forgive it. "Because tomorrow morning, whatever happens, I'm going to need to move faster than fear does. And fear is fast. But it isn't organized."

* * *

Herron Vail did not believe in dungeons.

Dungeons, he had explained once to a subordinate who admired them, were theaters. They announced their purpose before the performance began, and men arrived already rehearsed in heroism. A comfortable room, on the other hand — a fire, a decanter, two chairs — gave a man nowhere to perform. There was only himself and the silence, and silence always won.

He sat now in Master Quillon's own chair, behind Master Quillon's own desk, in the shop above, while downstairs in the cellar — not a dungeon, merely a cellar, with a brazier and a table and a second, less comfortable chair — Master Quillon discovered what silence could do.

Vail had read everything in the shop by lamplight. The ledgers were dull and honest, which meant they were false, which meant Quillon was competent. The type cases held nothing seditious, which meant they'd been cleaned recently, which meant Quillon was frightened. Frightened and competent. His favorite combination. Competent men broke slowly and gave up much; frightened men broke quickly and gave up everything; the intersection of the two was where the interesting truths lived.

At midnight he went down the stairs.

Quillon sat in the uncomfortable chair with his hands between his knees, and he had been crying, though he would deny it later, and he started talking before Vail had even sat down.

"I want you to understand," Quillon said, "that I never wanted any of this. I am a tradesman. My father was a tradesman. We print what pays—"

"Wedding invitations," Vail agreed pleasantly. "Grain prices. Sit, sit, no — stay standing if you prefer. Some men think better upright." He poured two glasses of the brandy he'd brought himself, having no intention of drinking Quillon's, and pushed one across the table. "Now. On the twenty-third of December, a child was left at this shop's door. On the twenty-fifth, a delivery cart left this shop bound for the river district, driven by your compositor, Coral Dravenmoor, carrying a type chest heavier than any type chest has a right to be. Tell me about the chest."

"I don't — there was no —"

"Master Quillon." Vail settled back, warm, reasonable, almost tender. "I have been doing this work for nineteen years. In that time I have learned that lies are like bad kerning — the eye catches them at once, and everything after them looks crooked. You are going to give me the truth, and then we are going to drink this excellent brandy, and in the morning you are going to sign a statement, and none of this will ever have happened to anyone who matters. Whereas if you make me work for it—" he gestured gently at the brazier, at the iron poker leaning beside it, at the whole modest grammar of the room "—then it happens to you, and continues happening, and I remain exactly as comfortable as I am now. That is the entire negotiation. Take your time."

Quillon took his time. It lasted eleven minutes.

What he gave up, in a voice that grew smaller with each sentence, was half the truth, which is the most dangerous fraction there is. He gave up Coral's name — Vail already had it. He gave up the child's existence — Vail already suspected it. He gave up the fact that Coral had gone to ground somewhere in the Tanner's Quarter with a midwife named Ondel — this was new, and Vail wrote it down. He gave up the existence of a contact beyond the river, a bookseller — this was newer, and Vail's pen slowed with pleasure.

What he did not give up — because he did not know it, because Coral had never told him — was the route. River gate, coffin cart, mountain pass. What he did not give up was the location of the original proofs, because Coral had moved them out of the shop on the second night, sewn into the lining of her father's trunk, and Quillon had searched for them afterward and failed, and had been too ashamed to admit his failure even to himself.

And what he did not give up, because some stubborn printer's sinew in him refused even now, even broken, even weeping into another man's brandy, was Ivor Kade's name. He gave "a bookseller beyond the river" and then, when Vail pressed, he gave a street, and when Vail pressed harder he gave a city, and there his courage — such as it was, such a small, dented, ridiculous coin of it — ran out, and he said, "I never met him. She met him. I swear it on my trade, I swear it on my hands, I never saw his face."

Vail believed him, which annoyed him. He preferred his witnesses unreliable; reliable ones limited where you could point the question.

He stood, buttoned his coat, and looked down at Quillon, who had folded forward over the table like wet paper.

"You'll sign in the morning," Vail said. "And Master Quillon — thank you. Truly. Most men make me waste the poker." At the stairs he paused. "One more thing. The girl. Coral Dravenmoor. Her father printed pamphlets too, in the old troubles. Did you know that?"

Quillon lifted his head. His eyes were ruined and red-rimmed and, beneath the ruin, calculating — the last flicker of the vain, clever man, trying to guess which answer hurt least.

"No," he said.

"Mm," said Vail, and went up the stairs, and did not believe him, and filed it away where he filed everything, in the cold room at the back of his mind where nothing ever rotted.

* * *

Coral heard none of this. What she heard, crouched in the coal chute with the baby strapped to her chest and a mouthful of soot-flavored air, was fragments — voices filtered through floorboards and brick, rising and falling like weather. She heard Quillon's voice crack twice. She heard long silences that were worse than shouting. She heard, once, Vail laugh — a short, pleasant sound, the worst thing she had ever heard.

Atlas, mercifully, slept. She had dosed him with Sera's poppy syrup, a thimbleful, the smallest dose, and spent the entire climb across the rooftops pressing two fingers under his jaw to confirm the small drumbeat of him. Now he breathed against her collarbone, a warm weight, and she stood in the dark among the coal dust and tried to breathe as quietly as he did.

*Don't think about Quillon,* she told herself. *Think about the route.*

River gate. Coffin cart. Mountain pass. Three checkpoints, two of them now forewarned — no, possibly all three, depending on what Quillon had given up and what Vail chose to believe. The route in her head was worth exactly as much as its secrecy, and its secrecy had died tonight in a warm room over good brandy.

Which meant she needed a new route, and she needed it before dawn, and the only man in Vellmark who drew routes for a living was a bookseller who might, by morning, be dead.

She waited until the bells rang three. She waited until boots crossed the floor above her, twice, unhurried — Vail walking her father's shop like a man walking through a museum of things he intended to own. She heard him pause, once, near the old press, and she heard the small metallic sound of a type case being slid open, and shut.

Then the front door, and the carriage, and quiet.

She climbed out through the chute into the alley, gray with coal, smelling of the inside of the earth, and Tam Wicker materialized beside her so suddenly she nearly struck him.

"The eel song?" she whispered.

"Nobody came out. Nobody whistled. I whistled it to myself twice just to keep in practice, sorry."

"It's fine, Tam." She looked back at the dark bulk of the shop. Somewhere in there was her father's press, her father's type, forty years of her family's life, and Herron Vail sleeping — did he sleep? — behind its door. "Go home. Sleep. If anyone asks, you were at the Drowned Bell all night and you remember nothing, which should be easy, since it's nearly true."

"What will you do?"

Coral shifted the baby higher. Down the alley, the sky over the river district was going the color of dirty pewter, and the frost lay on the cobbles like a skin waiting to be broken.

"What Quillon couldn't do," she said. "I'm going to spend the truth instead of hoarding it. And I'm going to do it before Vail figures out that the half he bought isn't the half that matters."

She turned west, toward the river, toward a bookseller's door that might already be watched, carrying the only things she had left: a sleeping child, a memorized lie, and ten thousand words in a dead man's handwriting.

Behind her, in the shop, the press stood silent in the dark, patient as a loaded thing.
