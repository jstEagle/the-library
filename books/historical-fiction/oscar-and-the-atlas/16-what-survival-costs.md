## 16. What Survival Costs

The commander of the mountain pass tollhouse was a small man with a large mustache, and he wore the mustache the way other men wore rank — as though it had been earned through years of faithful service.

Coral saw him first from the sled road, a dark figure pacing before the tollhouse stove, and her heart performed a small treacherous leap of hope. Small men, she had learned in three weeks of flight, were easier than large ones. Large men liked to be feared; small men only wanted to be respected, and respect could be bought for the price of a little listening.

Then she saw the notice nailed beside the gate, its corners curling in the wind that came down off the pass like something with an errand.

*WANTED FOR QUESTIONING: a woman traveling alone or with child, fair-haired, answers to no name given. The child may be described as healthy, under one year, and uncommonly calm. Reward sufficient to retire upon.*

Uncommonly calm. She looked down at the basket on her lap, where Oscar — she had begun, helplessly, to think of him by that name — was gnawing on the wooden handle of a type stick she'd let him keep. He gazed up at her with his enormous gray eyes and drooled slightly, which was, at least, honest.

"You," she whispered, "are going to be the death of me."

He kicked his legs, pleased.

The sled driver — a taciturn man named Brann who had accepted her coin at the last village without asking why a widow with an infant wished to travel toward the border in the dead of winter, which either meant wisdom or indifference, both of which she blessed him for — reined in before the tollhouse and spat thoughtfully into the snow.

"Commander's in a mood," he said. "Turned back two carts this morning."

"Did they have children?"

"Didn't look." He shrugged. "Widow Vessel, is it?"

"Widow Vessel," she agreed. It was the fourth name she had worn since leaving Vellmark, and she was beginning to feel like a set of type that had been redistributed too many times — all the letters still there, but nothing spelled what it used to.

She climbed down. The cold up here was not the river city's damp cold; it was a dry, glittering cold, the kind that made the air feel like it might shatter if you spoke too loudly. The mountains rose on either side of the pass like the shoulders of some patient animal, and somewhere beyond them, she had been promised, was the border, and safety, and an end.

She had stopped believing in ends. But she kept walking toward them anyway.

* * *

Inside the tollhouse it was almost painfully warm, and it smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular boiled-cabbage melancholy that seemed to afflict every military kitchen in the province regardless of altitude. The commander sat behind a desk that had clearly belonged to someone else once — a customs officer, perhaps, or a toll collector from the old days, when this building had taken coins instead of oaths.

Behind the commander, on the wall, hung a portrait of Governor Atlas Corvane.

It was, Coral thought, a terrible likeness. Whoever had painted it had flattered the jaw and softened the eyes, and the result was a man who looked merely stern rather than what he actually was, which was the kind of face you saw in nightmares and then woke grateful to find it wasn't real. She wondered if Corvane had ever seen it. She wondered if he'd approved. Powerful men so often preferred the version of themselves that painters gave them.

"Mistress?" The commander had risen. Up close, the mustache was even more magnificent — iron-gray, waxed, curled at the tips with what must have required a small mirror and considerable vanity each morning.

"Vessel," she said, dipping a curtsy that hurt her knees. "Elma Vessel. I'm traveling to my sister's, over the border. Her confinement is near and she's asked for me."

"Sister's name?"

"Marta. Marta Vessel." She had prepared this. Names within names, like nested boxes.

"And your late husband? What did he die of?"

"Fever." This was true, in a way. Everything she'd lost could fairly be called a fever — the fever in the streets, the fever in men's hearts, the fever that had come off the presses in her father's shop long before she was born.

The commander grunted and consulted a ledger. Beside the desk, a soldier stood with a carbine held loosely, looking bored in the way of young men who had joined an army expecting glory and received instead a stove and a doorway. The baby, mercifully, slept.

"You'll understand," the commander said, not unkindly, "that we've orders regarding travelers with infants. There's a child missing from the governor's own household, and there are those who would use such a child against the state."

"The state has my sympathy," Coral said, "but not my child. My daughter died in the autumn." She let the lie sit between them, small and terrible. "I carry my sister's boy for her. She was too far along to travel herself."

Something moved behind the commander's eyes — pity, maybe, or the memory of a wife, a sister, a loss of his own. For a moment she thought it would be enough. Then he straightened, because duty is a coat some men cannot take off even when it chafes.

"I'll need to see the child's papers."

"Papers." She smiled, and it cost her more than the curtsy had. "Commander, I'm a midwife's widow from a village you couldn't find on any map. My nephew was born at home, as children are born everywhere. Do you suppose the birthing stopped to wait for a clerk?"

A muscle in his cheek twitched — amusement, she thought, quickly suppressed. "Nevertheless."

"Nevertheless," she agreed softly.

They regarded each other across the desk. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. In her basket, Oscar stirred and made a sound — not crying yet, just a small questioning syllable, the sound of a person checking whether the world was still worth complaining about.

The commander glanced at the basket. Then at her face. And Coral understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he knew. Not everything — but enough. Enough that he was giving her a chance to make it easy, the way a card player gives warning before he turns over the winning hand.

"Mistress Vessel," he said quietly, "perhaps we should speak alone."

* * *

The soldier went out to check the sled runners. The door closed. The commander poured two cups of something from a dented pot on the stove — not tea exactly, but hot, which in that country in that season counted for nearly the same thing — and pushed one across the desk.

"Sit," he said. "Your legs are shaking. Don't insult us both by pretending they aren't."

She sat. The cup warmed her hands. She did not drink; drinking meant swallowing, and swallowing meant trusting, and she had run out of trust somewhere back along the river road.

"My name is Doss," the commander said. "I've held this post for eleven years. You know what happens at a place like this, all those years? Nothing. Snow. Sleds. Smugglers bringing brandy down and wool going up. Once a dancing bear." He said this with genuine regret. "Eleven years of nothing, and now a notice comes down from the capital saying I'm to watch for one particular infant, and if I find him, I'm to hold him and send word, and men will come, and there will be questions, and my quiet pass will fill up with the kind of people who ask questions with pliers." He drank. "So you'll understand that my interest is not entirely patriotic."

"No," Coral said. "I imagine it isn't."

"I'm told the reward is enough to retire upon." He turned the cup in his hands. "I'm fifty-three years old. My knees hate these mountains. A man could dream."

"What will you do, Commander?"

"That depends," he said, "on whether the woman in front of me is a kidnapper or a courier. Kidnappers lie badly and look at doors. Couriers—" he gestured at her with the cup— "sit very still and count their exits. You've been counting since you sat down. The window's barred, incidentally. Has been for years. Bears."

Despite everything, despite the terror sitting on her chest like a cat, Coral almost laughed.

"So here is what I know," Doss continued. "I know the description says *uncommonly calm*, and I've watched you with that basket, and you touch it every few minutes the way a man touches a wound — not to soothe it, but to be sure it's still his." He set down the cup. "I know the governor's household is not a place anyone flees *toward*. And I know that whatever's in that blanket lining that you keep adjusting, it matters more than the child, because you'd have left the child with the midwife in Harrow Fen if carrying him weren't necessary."

Her blood went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"Mistress." He leaned forward. Mustache first, then the rest of him. "I don't care what you mean. That's the point. I have spent eleven years learning exactly how much not to know, and I am very good at my work. But there are soldiers outside, and one of them can read, and the other one wants a pension, and I cannot simply wave you through like a cart of turnips. If you're searched and found, it lands on me. So either give me a reason to search you thoroughly and find nothing, or—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before naming the price of the knife— "give me a reason to be tired tonight. Old men get tired. Nobody questions a tired man's memory."

Coral sat very still.

She thought of the bundle at the shop door, three weeks and a lifetime ago. She thought of Quillon's sweating hands, of Ivor Kade's sad knowing smile, of Sera Ondel pressing warm bread into her arms and saying *go, girl, go.* She thought of Herron Vail's voice, soft as dust, asking about her father's tools.

And she thought of the thing in her bodice, sewn into the false seam Sera had made, flat and cold and heavier than anything else she carried.

Her father's silver type punch.

Every punch in the Dravenmoor shop was steel except one — the master punch, cast in silver the year her father finished his apprenticeship, the one he'd worn on a cord around his neck and pressed to her palm when she was nine years old and cutting her first letter. *Feel that?* he'd said. *That's the shape of a truth, little one. Everything ever printed began as a piece of metal like this, struck into copper, filled with lead. Truth starts harder than it ends.* She'd worn it herself after he died. Through the checkpoints. Through the tanner's quarter. Through the coffin and the river gate and every mile since. It had lain against her heart through all of it, and she had told herself it was armor.

But armor is for keeping. And survival, she was learning, is a merchant that takes payment in the only coin you truly own.

She reached into her bodice.

The cord came away over her head with a whisper. The punch caught the firelight — a small silver rectangle, no longer than a finger, the letter *D* cut deep into its face in her father's sure hand. The first letter of Dravenmoor. The first letter of the alphabet he taught her. Just a piece of metal. Just the hardest part of every truth she'd ever set in type.

She laid it on the desk between them.

Doss looked at it for a long moment. He picked it up — carefully, the way one picks up a sleeping infant or a live coal — and turned it in the light. He read the letter. He was, she realized, one of the few soldiers she'd met who could read at all.

"This is old," he said.

"It was my father's."

"He was a printer?"

"He was a printer."

Doss nodded slowly. Something passed over his face that she couldn't name — recognition, maybe, or grief for a category of men rather than a specific one. Eleven years of watching the world go by a mountain pass teaches a man things.

"There was a printer in Vellmark," he said quietly. "Years ago. They came for the shops one night, the governor's men. Burned what wouldn't fit in their carts." He set the punch down and slid it, deliberately, into his breast pocket, over his heart, where medals go. "My brother set type in that city. He got out ahead of them. He never printed again — too frightened. But he talked about the work the way fishermen talk about the sea. Like something you can't quit loving just because it tries to drown you."

Coral's throat closed. She made herself say: "What was his name?"

"Does it matter? He's dead now too. Fever." His mouth twitched, the smallest acknowledgment of the word they'd traded earlier, the currency of widows. "Mistress Vessel. Elma. Whatever you're called." He rose, took the ledger, and made a great show of writing in it. "I'm recording that a widow named Vessel passed north today with her sister's child, papers irregular but disposition respectable, and that the commander, being tired after a long winter, declined to perform a full search." He blotted it. "I'm also recording that the notice on my wall blew down in the night, and I haven't had time to nail it back up. These things happen."

"Commander—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't thank me and don't remember me. Both are dangerous. Go while the light holds. The pass takes four hours and it lies to strangers — what looks like the top never is." He walked her to the door, and there, where the soldier couldn't hear, he added, low: "Whatever's in that lining — the papers, the proof, whatever it is — it won't be safe anywhere until there are ten thousand copies of it and no original worth stealing. A man who once loved a printer taught me that." He opened the door onto the white blaze of afternoon. "Go home to your sister, Widow Vessel. And tell your brother printers the mountains remember them kindly."

She took the basket and stepped out into the cold before her face could do anything that would undo them both.

* * *

The pass lied, exactly as promised.

What looked like the summit from the tollhouse was a shoulder; the shoulder revealed a ridge; the ridge unfolded into another climb. Brann drove the sled in silence, and Coral sat wrapped in blankets with Oscar against her chest, watching the world narrow to snow and stone and the laboring breath of the horse.

Somewhere around the second hour, the trees gave out. Above the tree line there was nothing but wind-scoured rock and the blue-white glare, and the sky so close it felt less like weather than architecture. Oscar woke, considered his surroundings, and decided — with the unfailing judgment of infants — that this was acceptable, provided breakfast followed.

She fed him under a shelf of rock while Brann watered the horse. The wind came off the ice fields and sang in the rocks like a choir rehearsing somewhere underground. Oscar's fists gripped her collar. His eyes, half-lidded at the milk, were the exact gray of the glacier light.

*You should have been somebody's whole life,* she thought. *Somebody who gets to keep you.*

And then, because the altitude loosens things, because grief is patient and waits for thin air and silence, the rest arrived.

She thought of her father's hands setting type by lamplight, the little click of each letter finding its place. She thought of the silver punch lying warm against her skin for twenty years, through every ordinary day — through the mornings when the shop smelled of ink and coffee, through the nights when she'd fall asleep over the composing stone and he'd carry her to bed without waking her. She thought of how she had believed, right up until she'd laid it on that desk, that she would die before giving it up. And how, when the moment came, she had given it up in four seconds flat, because that is what survival costs, and survival always sends the bill to the same address: the last thing, the dearest thing, the thing you swore.

She had thought she was paying to save the child. She was. But she had also been paying to save the pamphlets, the proofs, the truth — and some traitorous part of her knew the truth would have survived the loss of her. Only she would have known what she'd lost. Only she would carry the hollow place where the silver used to be.

That was the arithmetic nobody warned you about. You didn't trade gold for your life. You traded the things that made the life worth the price.

The tears came without ceremony — not the elegant, single-track grief of stories, but a full, ugly, hiccupping ruin of it, the kind that makes your nose run and your chest ache and frightens babies. Oscar blinked up at her, alarmed, and then did the only thing he knew to do, which was to reach up and pat her chin with a milk-scented hand, twice, firmly, as though knocking on a door he intended to have opened.

Which, of course, undid her completely.

Brann, to his eternal credit, studied the horse's harness with tremendous concentration and heard nothing at all.

She cried until there was nothing left, which was not long — grief, like air at altitude, runs thin — and then she wiped her face with her sleeve, and kissed the top of Oscar's head, tasting snow and baby, and laughed at herself, one short broken note that the wind took and scattered.

"Well," she said to him. "That's that, then."

The punch was gone. Her father's letter *D* was in a soldier's pocket on the other side of the mountains, and someday — she chose to believe this, the way you choose to believe spring — some tired old man would take it out on bad nights and hold it, and it would not be payment, it would be company.

Everything else she still carried. The proofs. The letter from Lysandra, sewn into the blanket's second seam. The child. Herself, mostly.

The light was failing when they crested the true summit at last, and below them the land fell away into darkness — and there, far down in the black, a scatter of lights. A valley. A village. Beyond it, the border, invisible but suddenly real, the way a threshold becomes real only when you're standing on it.

Brann grunted. "Kestrel Hollow. Safe house is the barn with the green door, past the mill. Someone'll be waiting." He glanced at her sidelong. "Whoever they are."

Coral settled Oscar into the basket, tucked the blankets close, and looked down at the lights that meant the end of running — and felt, instead of relief, a faint unease she couldn't name. Ivor Kade had sent word of the safe house. The resistance would be there. The people who had promised, through Ivor, to see the evidence printed and the truth loosed on the world.

*Ten thousand copies,* Commander Doss had said. *No original worth stealing.*

She hugged that thought to her like a coal as the sled began its long descent into the dark.

The barn, when they reached it, stood apart from the village at the edge of a frozen field, and its green door was already open, and lamplight spilled out across the snow. Three figures waited inside, and even before Brann halted the sled, even before she climbed down with the basket and the cold seized her lungs, Coral understood from the way they stood — squared, armed, unhurried — that these were not farmers.

One of them stepped forward, a broad woman with a scar through one eyebrow, and looked not at Coral but at the basket, for a long, appraising moment, the way a man looks at a strongbox.

"You're the courier," the woman said. It was not a question. "Good. We've been waiting for the cargo."
