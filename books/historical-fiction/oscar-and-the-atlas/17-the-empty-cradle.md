## 17. The Empty Cradle

The safe house was a mill, once — Coral could tell by the way the floorboards sloped toward where the waterwheel had been, and by the great empty axle-hole in the far wall that let the wind in like an old man whistling through his teeth. Somebody had stuffed it with sacking years ago and somebody else had stolen the sacking for bedding, and now the wind came back every night to complain about it.

She had been there four days. Long enough to learn that the resistance ate turnips at every meal out of what seemed to be principle, and long enough to learn the names of the hardliners who had come up from the river valley to meet her.

There were three of them. Maren Ostrow, broad-shouldered and gray-braided, with a voice like a door slamming. Her brother Dannel, who said little but arranged his face a great deal. And a lean, courteous man called Ferren Hale, who wore a scholar's coat gone shabby at the cuffs and smiled the way ice smiles on a pond — beautifully, and as a warning.

They had come to see the pamphlets. They stayed to look at the baby.

Coral sat by the fire with Atlas — she still would not say the name aloud, even privately it felt like theft — asleep against her shoulder, while across the room Ivor Kade spread the evidence packet on a trestle table: the massacre accounts from Hallow Bridge, the muster rolls, the orders bearing Corvane's own hand. The originals were ash; these were Quillon's proofs, pulled in secret over three years. It was everything. She knew it was everything because of how quietly they looked at it.

Maren read first. She read the way other people prayed.

"Forty-one names," she said at last, not looking up. "I counted. My cousin's wife is on this page." She tapped the paper once, gently, as if it might bruise. "Governor's seal. Governor's signature."

"There are more pages," Coral said. "The signature repeats."

"I know it repeats." Maren lifted her head. "I've spent eleven years repeating it myself, madam, to rooms exactly this size, to people who told me I was hysterical. Eleven years, and here it is in ink, with a date, and a man's name signed under it like he was ordering wine." She stood. Something moved through the room when she did — Dannel straightening, Hale folding his hands — the way weather moves through wheat. "This ends him. Not a riot. Not a raid. *Him.*"

"It will," Ivor said carefully, "if it reaches enough eyes."

"That is why we came." Maren turned, and Coral saw her gaze go — inevitably, the way all gazes in that room had gone for four days — to the child sleeping on Coral's shoulder. "And that is why we brought what we brought."

Coral had noticed the bundle earlier: a lacquered case Dannel carried, which she'd assumed held documents. Dannel set it on the table and opened it now, with the tenderness of a man handling relics.

Inside, on faded velvet, lay a christening gown.

It was fine work, decades old, yellowed at the folds. Someone had embroidered along its hem, in thread gone brown, a crest Coral did not need anyone to explain. An atlas bear, standing on a globe.

"The Corvane christening robe," Hale said softly. "Worn by the governor himself, and by his son, and kept in the family chapel at Greywater until certain friends of ours liberated it last summer. We didn't entirely know why we wanted it." He smiled his pond-ice smile. "Now we do."

The fire popped. The wind complained through its hole in the wall. Coral found that her arms had tightened around the baby without asking her permission, and that he, sensing it or simply being a baby, had stirred and grumbled and gone back down.

"I don't understand," she said, though a cold part of her understood perfectly.

"It's simple," Maren said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it worse. "In spring — or sooner, if the pamphlets do their work — Corvane falls. He falls hard, and Vellmark wakes up with no government and forty-one dead at Hallow Bridge and twenty years of graves behind it, and every man with a sword and a grievance starts asking who rules next. And history tells us, Mrs. Eriksen—" 

"Coral."

"—*Coral,* that whoever holds a symbol holds the morning." Maren crossed to the fire and crouched before it, close enough that Coral could see the gray in her braid was earned, not inherited. "A republic needs more than pamphlets. It needs a story people can love. And here—" she nodded at the sleeping weight on Coral's shoulder, "—here is the last of the tyrant's blood, an innocent, smuggled out by his own mother to *break the line*. We don't have to kill the dynasty, girl. We can *convert* it. Raise him free. Raise him among us. Let the whole country watch the Atlas cub grow up pulling turnips and learning letters in a free school, and when some general raises the old flag, the people answer: *no — we have the heir, and the heir is ours.*"

"He wouldn't be ours," Coral said. "He'd be theirs. Yours. A coat you put a boy inside."

"A cradle, then," Hale said pleasantly. "A very grand one. With guards, and tutors, and a nation's gratitude—"

"A cage with gratitude painted on the bars." Coral heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "You want to dress him in that—" she nodded at the robe, "—and stand him up at rallies, and call it freedom. Master Hale, I have spent a month carrying this child past men who wanted him dead, and every single one of them wanted him dead or wanted him owned, and I am telling you plainly that I cannot tell the difference anymore from where I'm sitting."

Maren's jaw set. "He is Corvane's grandson."

"He is a *baby*," Coral said. "He likes barley porridge and hates the wind. He has a birthmark on his left heel shaped like nothing at all. That's the whole of what I know about who he is, and it's more than any of you know, because none of you have changed him at three in the morning while soldiers knocked on doors downstairs."

"And you have." Hale tilted his head. "Which makes you attached. Understandably. Which is why—"

"Don't," Ivor said.

It was the first time Coral had heard Ivor Kade raise anything above a bookseller's murmur, and it landed in the room like a dropped book in a silent shop. Everyone looked at him. He stood by the trestle table with one hand still resting on the proofs, and Coral watched the argument happen behind his face — she could nearly see the two parties in it, the cause and the conscience, taking turns at the podium.

"Maren," Ivor said, quieter now. "Eleven years you've fought for this. You deserve your victory. But listen to what she's actually saying, because she's saying it better than I ever managed to." He looked at Coral, and there was an apology in it already, which frightened her more than the robe did. "The pamphlets end Corvane. The child doesn't need to be anything. The moment we make him a symbol, we've built the Atlas again with new masonry — a throne made of a person instead of a chair. Isn't that the entire thing we're burning down?"

"The entire thing we're burning down," Maren said, rising, "is a system where one family's blood decides the fate of millions. And you propose to solve it by letting that same blood wander off into the world unwatched, to be found someday by someone worse than us? By Vail? By whatever general comes after? At least we'd raise him *kindly*, Kade. At least with us he'd be loved and educated and—"

"Owned," said Coral.

"*Kept safe!*"

"By whom?" Coral stood, and the baby woke properly then, blinking, and began to fuss, and she rocked him absently, automatically, the way she now did everything — with half her mind always holding him. "Who keeps him safe, Maren? Your guards? Your tutors? The day he's a symbol, every enemy you have becomes his enemy, and every friend you have stops seeing him. You'll call it protection. He'll call it prison, when he's old enough to call it anything, and if he's anything like his mother — and I've read her letter, I've held her words in my hands — he'll be exactly the sort of person who breaks out of cages and burns down dynasties. You'd be raising the next revolution against yourselves."

Silence. The wind filled it, whistling its complaint.

Dannel spoke for the second time in four days. "She's right," he said, to nobody, to the table. "You know she's right, Mare. I told you in the cart."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked suddenly, enormously tired — a woman who had carried eleven years up a mountain and been told at the top that the view was wrong. "Then what?" she asked, and her voice cracked on it, and Coral understood that underneath the iron was grief, forty-one names' worth, and that iron was just what grief looked like after enough winters. "What do we *do* with him, printer's daughter? Bury the name? Give him to a farm family and pretend? And in thirty years some ambitious man digs up his bloodline and parades *him* anyway, and he's had no one, no story, no armor — nothing but a lie about who he is. Is that mercy?"

"It's a chance," Coral said. "It's more of a chance than a cage gives. That's all any of us get. Ask me how I know."

Nobody asked. Everybody knew.

* * *

They argued until the candles drowned themselves in their own wax, and then longer, by firelight, and the argument went around and around the same track like a cart horse: symbol versus child, story versus soul. Hale produced plans — schools, ceremonies, a regency council of respected citizens — each one more reasonable than the last, and each one, Coral noticed, requiring that the child be given to them. Reasonableness, she was learning, was just persuasion wearing its good coat.

Ivor argued with them. That was the thing that surprised her most. He argued well, patiently, citing their own pamphlets back at them — passages about the rights of persons, about tyranny dressed in necessity — and she realized he had been arguing this exact fight inside himself since the river gate, maybe since the day her father died, and tonight the two halves of him had finally met in a room and were going at it tooth and claw.

At some point past midnight, Maren threw up her hands. "We don't decide this tonight," she said. "We're tired and we're grieving and the girl is stubborn as type metal. We rest. We decide at dawn." And her eye found Coral's across the fire with a look that promised dawn would be conducted under different management. "The proofs stay with us tonight. As security."

"No," said Coral.

"Coral." Ivor's voice, low, urgent. "Let them hold the papers. It changes nothing. You keep the child—"

"The papers are the only reason they haven't simply taken both," Coral said. She heard how it sounded. She said it anyway. "As long as I hold what they need, they negotiate. The minute they hold everything, I'm a nursemaid with opinions." She gathered the proofs off the table herself, page by page, feeling Hale's smile cool by degrees, feeling Maren's silence sharpen into something with an edge. "Tomorrow, then. Dawn. Bring your best arguments. I'll bring mine."

"And what are yours?" Hale asked, silk over steel.

Coral tucked the proofs inside her coat, against her heartbeat, where the blanket lining used to be. She thought of her father's cases, of Sera Ondel's hands, of a coffin riding through a checkpoint full of grief, of a silver punch left on a tollhouse table. Everything she'd paid. Everything that had paid her.

"You want a story the country can love," she said. "So do I. Mine just happens to be true, and it doesn't need a costume." She touched the coat where the papers lay. "Dawn."

* * *

But dawn, as it turned out, had other appointments.

Coral slept sitting up with the baby in the crook of her arm and the proofs beneath her, and woke once in the black hours to the sound of the wind changing — not complaining now, but pushing, urgent, the way wind pushes ahead of weather — and woke the second time to Ivor's hand on her shoulder, shaking her, and Ivor's face in the last gutter of the fire, gray as his coat.

"They're gone," he whispered.

"Who's—"

"Dannel took the horses an hour ago. Maren's packing. Hale sent a rider down the pass before midnight — I watched him go and told myself it was nothing, God forgive me, I *told myself*." He dragged a hand down his face. "Coral — Hale didn't send word to the committee. There's no committee courier on this road in winter. There's only one thing between here and Vellmark worth sending word about."

The baby stirred. Outside, far down the frozen valley, so faint it might have been the wind playing tricks, came the sound she had learned to dread across three checkpoints and a hundred miles: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast.

Vail's description had reached a tollhouse commander in nine days. Coral did the arithmetic she did not want to do, and it came out ugly.

"How long?" she asked, already on her feet, already reaching for the blanket, the sling, the satchel, her hands doing four things at once the way they'd taught themselves to.

"If the rider went at midnight and they rode through the dark hours — hours. Maybe less." Ivor hesitated in the doorway, torn open between the cause sleeping in the next room and the woman bundling a child in front of him, and Coral saw him choose — saw the choosing cost him — as he said, "The back trail goes down through the millrace. It's flooded half-frozen, it's brutal, it's the one direction nobody watches because nobody's fool enough. I can buy you time at the front of this house. I don't know how much. Coral—" he caught her arm, and his bookseller's voice finally broke altogether, "—I believed in them. Eleven years I believed the worst thing about the revolution was that it might lose. Tonight I'm afraid the worst thing is that it might win *this way*. Go. Don't look back. And whatever happens—"

Whatever happened, there wasn't time to hear it. Hoofbeats in the valley, closer now. Maren's boots crossing the loft overhead, heavy and deliberate, coming down.

Coral pulled the blanket over the baby's face, pressed the proofs against her heart, and ran for the millrace and the freezing dark.
