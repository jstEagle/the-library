## 18. Back Across the River

Sera Ondel held the baby at arm's length and looked at Coral the way she looked at a patient who had insisted on standing up too soon after a fever.

"You want to go *where*?"

"Vellmark."

"Vellmark," Sera repeated, as though tasting a spoiled egg. "The city where your face is on a warrant. Where the man hunting you has more soldiers than the city has chimneys. That Vellmark."

"The proofs are still there," Coral said. "Under the floor of the composing room. Corvane's own signature, his own hand, on orders for Hallow Bridge. The pamphlets are copies — good ones, my father's press made good copies — but copies can be called forgeries. The originals can't." She paused. "And they're the only things left in that shop that are mine."

"That last part is the reason you're going, and we both know it."

Coral didn't deny it. It was true, and it was also not the whole truth, which was the most honest kind of thing she'd said all winter.

The safe house was a dairy farm two valleys past the border, all whitewash and low beams and a smell of scalded milk that clung to everything, including — she discovered — the baby, who had taken to it with the enthusiasm of a small emperor annexing territory. He was four months old now, or thereabouts; nobody knew exactly, and Sera had declared that his birthday was therefore the day he'd arrived on Coral's doorstep, "because every soul deserves one day that's certainly theirs." He had learned to laugh in the past week, a startling, honking sound like a goose being tickled, and he laughed hardest of all when Coral pulled type sorts out of her pocket one by one and let him try to eat them.

She had promised herself she wouldn't name him. Naming was claiming, and claiming was a debt, and she had already paid so many.

She was losing that promise the way a dam loses — slowly, then all at once. Last night he'd fallen asleep against her collarbone with one fist closed around her thumb, and she had sat in the dark thinking, *Oscar*, and then thought, *no*, and then thought it again anyway.

So: Vellmark. Before she could start calling him something. Before the wanting became a wound.

"You'll stay until I'm back," Coral said. "Two weeks. Three if the river ice is bad."

"And if you're not back?"

"Then Ivor takes him further east, and you forget my face, and you tell anyone who asks that the baby was left by a tinker's widow."

"I've already told everyone he was left by a tinker's widow," Sera said. "I told the priest. I told the miller's wife. I told a tax collector, who believed me, which proves the gods have a sense of humor." She bounced the baby gently. "What I have *not* told anyone is why you're walking back into a city full of men with guns over a stack of paper. So tell me that."

Coral looked at the fire for a while.

"Because everyone wants to own this," she said finally. "Corvane wanted it burned. The resistance wants it delivered. Quillon wanted to sell it. Everyone who touches it wants to *use* it." She flexed her ink-stained fingers. "My father used to say that a proof sheet isn't finished until someone pulls it off the press with their own hands. Nobody else gets to finish this. Not them."

Sera studied her a long moment. Then she shifted the baby onto her hip, went to the dresser, and began packing bread, hard cheese, and a flask of something medicinal into a satchel with the brisk violence of a woman who expressed love exclusively through logistics.

"You'll take the south road to the ferry at Greywether," she said. "Not the bridge — the bridge has a garrison since midwinter. And you'll wear this." She threw a folded garment at Coral's head. It turned out to be a widow's shawl, grey and enormous. "You look like half the women in three provinces. Grief is the best disguise in the world. Nobody looks closely at a grieving woman; it makes them afraid they'll catch it."

"Sera—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me implies you might die, and if you die, who's going to come back and fix my churn? It squeaks."

* * *

The river at Greywether had frozen in long grey plates that ground against each other like teeth, and the ferryman crossed it anyway, because ferrymen always do, poling through gaps in the ice with the serenity of a man who has decided that drowning is somebody else's problem. He took Coral's coin without looking at her face, which confirmed Sera's theory about grief as camouflage, or possibly just confirmed that ferrymen only see coins.

On the far bank, Vellmark lay under its winter smoke like a city holding its breath.

She had expected it to hurt. It did, but not where she'd expected. It wasn't the spires or the river gate or the bell towers. It was the *smell*. Every city has a smell, and Vellmark's was river mud, coal smoke, roasting chestnuts from the corner by the Tanner's Quarter, and underneath it all, faint as a signature, the sour-metal tang of printing ink drifting from the shop districts. She had been breathing that smell since before she could walk. Her father used to joke that she hadn't been born, she'd been *set* — one line at a time, letter by letter, into a form too tight to fall apart.

She stood on the ferry landing longer than was wise, letting the cold bite her cheeks so she'd have an excuse for wet eyes.

Then she put her head down and walked, and the city swallowed her, and to her surprise the swallowing felt almost easy. That frightened her more than anything else. Four months a fugitive, and here she was moving through checkpoints and patrol streets like water finding its old channels. The fugitive had learned the hunter's first lesson: nobody suspects a woman carrying turnips.

She carried turnips.

* * *

The shop stood on Binder's Lane where it had always stood, squat and dependable, with the faded painted sign — DRAVENMOOR & SON, and beneath, in smaller letters someone had once tried to paint over, AND DAUGHTER, WHICH FATHER INSISTED ON ADDING HIMSELF.

The sign nearly undid her. She stood in the shadow of the cooper's yard opposite and stared at those words, AND DAUGHTER, until a patrol came down the lane and she had to become a turnip-woman again, bent and dull-eyed, examining nothing.

But it was the windows that told her what she needed to know.

The shop glowed. Lamplight behind glass, regular as a pulse, and shadows crossing it — not the wandering shadows of a burglar but the ordered shadows of an office. A sentry stood at the door, stamping his feet against the cold, wearing the slate-blue coat of the Governor's Intelligence Service rather than the red of the regulars.

Vail had moved in. Of course he had. The shop was known to be hers, known to be the center of everything, and Herron Vail was the sort of man who slept inside other people's lives the way a spider sleeps in the middle of a web.

*He's using my father's desk,* she thought, with a fury that surprised her by how clean it was. Not fear — fury. For four months fear had ridden her like a jockey, and now, standing in the dark across from her own stolen life, she found the jockey had dismounted and something else had climbed into the saddle.

She circled the block twice, learning it. The cooper's yard had a side gate, latched but not locked, opening onto the alley that ran behind Binder's Lane. The alley gave onto the back of the row of shops, and the back of her shop — she remembered with a clarity that felt like a gift from her father — had a cellar window with a broken latch that her father had never fixed because, he said, a man ought to be able to get into his own cellar, even if it was only himself getting in.

There would be guards inside. There might be a dozen men sleeping among the type cases. The thought should have stopped her.

Instead she found herself doing arithmetic. Vail ran his operation from the front rooms — lamplight, sentry, clerks. Cellars were for coal, for rats, for things important men didn't think about. And the proofs were beneath the floor of the composing room, which was above the cellar, wrapped in oilcloth, under a loose board her father had installed years ago for exactly this purpose, though he'd never imagined quite this purpose. To reach them she'd have to go up into the shop itself.

Or — no. Wait. She closed her eyes and saw the layout, saw it truly, the way she hadn't let herself see it since the night she'd fled. Beneath the composing room floor, yes. But the old coal chute. The chute ran from the alley down into the cellar, and at the top of the chute, inside the cellar wall, her father had built a little brick cupboard for the lamp oil — and the cupboard backed directly onto the underside of the composing room floor. She'd crawled in there as a child to hide from customers. She'd fit then. She'd eaten less since.

Her heart was hammering, but it was hammering forward.

*Tomorrow,* she decided. Tomorrow night, when the patrols changed and the city was at its deepest sleep. Tonight she needed a place to lie low, and she knew exactly one person in Vellmark whose door she could knock on without bringing soldiers down on them both.

* * *

The midwife's house was dark, and for one bad moment Coral feared the worst — that Sera's cousin had fled too, or worse. Then she saw the signal: a clay pot of rosemary on the windowsill. In summer it had meant *come around the back*. In winter, Sera had explained, it meant the same thing, but slower, because everything in winter meant the same thing but slower.

Marta Sera-Ondel — Sera's cousin, same build, same forearms, same way of speaking as though the world were a slightly slow apprentice — opened the back door before Coral had finished knocking, looked at her for exactly one second, and hauled her inside.

"You're either Coral Dravenmoor or you're the ghost of her," Marta whispered, "and ghosts don't carry turnips."

"It seemed safer."

"Nothing about you is safer. Sit down. Eat. Don't tell me anything, I don't want to know anything, knowing things is how my cousin ended up hiding babies." She set bread and broth in front of Coral and stood over her with crossed arms until she ate. "But I will say this, because Sera's last letter asked me to say it, and I quote: 'Tell her if she dies I will never forgive her, and also the churn still squeaks.'"

Coral laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the first since Greywether — and had to smother it in the bread.

"He's well?" she asked. "The baby?"

"Grows like a weed with opinions. Eats everything. Slept through the church bells last Sunday, which the priest says shows excellent character." Marta's voice softened despite herself. "Sera says he laughs like a goose."

"He does. It's appalling. I love it."

Marta looked at her for a moment, and whatever she saw made her sigh and fetch a blanket. "You're going somewhere tomorrow night. Don't tell me where. But listen — the Intelligence Service changed the watch schedules two days ago. My neighbor's boy is a clerk in their barracks, poor fool, and he grumbles. The Binder's Lane patrol turns over at the third bell now, not midnight. There's a gap of near a quarter hour while they walk the long way round by the customs house, because the direct street flooded when a water main burst and nobody's fixed it, because fixing things is apparently beneath the dignity of the state."

Coral stared at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because my cousin told me to keep you alive, and because I watched you eat that bread like a woman with a purpose, and because—" Marta shrugged, a great shrug, a family shrug, "—somebody in this city ought to be on your side. Now sleep. You snore, I hear, so I've put you by the stove where it won't matter."

* * *

The third bell rang, and Coral was already in the cooper's yard, shawl over her head, breath steaming, listening to the boots of the departing patrol fade toward the customs house. The alley was black and quiet. Somewhere a dog complained about the century in general.

She went over the fence, down the alley, to the coal chute.

The hatch cover was stiff with frost and came free with a crack that sounded, in the silence, like a gunshot. She froze, counted to sixty, heard nothing but her own blood. Then she swung her legs into the dark and lowered herself down the iron slide, hands burning on the cold metal, until her boots met the cellar floor.

Coal dust everywhere. She could taste her childhood in it. And there — her hand found it by memory alone, as she'd known it would — the little brick cupboard, the door swinging open at her touch, the smell of old lamp oil and dust and, faintly, impossibly, *ink*, soaked into these walls for thirty years.

She squeezed herself inside, pressed her cheek to the boards overhead, and listened.

Above her: footsteps. Slow, deliberate, pacing. One man, awake, walking the length of the composing room and back. Walking, pausing. Walking, pausing. A rhythm she recognized with a lurch — it was the pace of a man reading type cases, drawer by drawer, running his fingers along the sorts the way she did, the way her father had taught her, the way—

No. No, it couldn't be.

The footsteps stopped, directly overhead. And then, clear through the floorboards, calm as a man commenting on the weather, a voice she knew from nightmares said:

"You may as well come up, Mistress Dravenmoor. I heard the coal hatch five minutes ago. Do hurry — the tea's going cold, and I've been *so* looking forward to this conversation."
