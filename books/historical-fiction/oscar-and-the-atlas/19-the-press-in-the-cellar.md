## 19. The Press in the Cellar

The snow had stopped an hour before midnight, and Vellmark lay under it like a page waiting to be set — blank, patient, full of terrible possibility.

Coral stood in the alley behind her own print shop and could not make herself move.

It was absurd, she thought. She had crossed a frozen river in a coffin cart. She had talked her way past a checkpoint commander with a dead man's silver punch and a widow's borrowed grief. She had walked out of the mountains with a baby under her coat and a lie in her mouth so worn it fit like her own teeth. And now she stood thirty feet from a door she had opened ten thousand times, paralyzed by the sight of a *new lock*.

Brass. Shiny. Installed by someone who had never once had to coax the old one awake on a cold morning by breathing into the keyhole like a sick lamb.

"That's the part that offends you," she whispered to herself. "Not the soldiers. Not the spymaster. The lock."

But it was true, in its small way. The lock was hers. The door was hers. The building behind it — with its smell of ink and iron and lamp oil, with her father's type cases ranked along the walls like a choir — had been the only fixed point in a life that had otherwise been mostly weather. And Herron Vail had hung his hat there as though he owned it, which, in the way of governments everywhere, meant he soon would.

She pulled Sera's gray shawl tighter and counted windows. Two lit upstairs: the office that had been Quillon's, and the long room where the apprentices used to sleep. Downstairs, the shop itself was dark. Of course it was. Even spymasters slept, presumably, though Coral suspected Vail did it standing up, like a horse.

The coal chute was still where it had always been, because masonry is loyal even when men are not. It opened into the cellar, three feet above the floor, behind the paper stores. Her father had bricked a little ledge inside it years ago, after young Tam Fell got stuck halfway and had to be buttered out. Coral had spent half her childhood sliding down it when the front door was locked against late customers.

She went over the wall into the yard, boots silent in fresh snow, and lowered herself into the dark of the chute.

The smell hit her before her feet touched the floor. Ink, yes. Iron, yes. But underneath both, something wrong — lamp oil burned too long, unwashed wool, and the faint sweet-rot smell of a man who has been lying in one place for days.

She froze at the bottom of the chute, listening.

The cellar was empty. The press stood where it always stood, a great black whale of oak and iron, its counterweight chain hanging slack. Someone had moved the paper stock — she could tell by the bare patch on the floor — but they had not touched the press itself. Perhaps they thought it beneath them. Perhaps they thought it broken. Herron Vail, she was learning, understood people very well and machines not at all, and this gave her a flicker of comfort no larger than a candle flame but real.

Above her, through the floorboards, came the sound of a chair creaking. A guard, dozing at the top of the stairs. Then nothing.

And then, from the corner behind the type cases, a sound that stopped her heart and restarted it backwards:

A cough. Wet, rattling, human.

* * *

She found him by feel, not wanting a light. Her hands knew the geography of that room better than her eyes did — the composing stone, the galley rack, the bank of cases where the 14-point Caslon lived — and so she moved through the dark the way other people move through memory.

He was wedged between the jobbing case and the wall, wrapped in what had once been a good coat, and he was so much smaller than she remembered that for one horrible moment she thought it was someone else entirely. Then he coughed again and said her name, and there was no mistaking that voice, cracked down the middle like a dropped plate.

"Miss Dravenmoor." A wheeze that might have been a laugh. "You always did come in the back way."

"Master Quillon." She knelt. Her hand found his wrist; the pulse there fluttered like a moth in a jar. "God. What did they do to you?"

"What didn't they?" He tried to sit up and failed, and settled for turning his face toward her, though she doubted he could see more than her outline. His eyes had gone cloudy, unfocused, the eyes of a man already spending most of his time elsewhere. "I told them half. I want you to know that. Half. I held back the mountain pass. I held back the coffin trick — that was yours alone, that was beautiful, I'd have paid money to see Vail's face—"

"Hush."

"—but I gave them the river gate, and I gave them Ivor Kade's name, and I gave them—" the cough took him, long and awful, "—I gave them your father's name, Coral. Told them old Dravenmoor taught me everything. Thought it might buy you time, make them think you were just carrying on family business. Foolish. Sentimental. They laughed at me."

"They would," Coral said quietly. "They don't know anything about family business."

"I know." He sagged back. In the thin light from the high window, snow-glow, blue and cold, she could see how bad it was. Three ribs taped wrong. Fingers on his right hand swollen to sausages — the fingers of a compositor, ruined. Whatever else Herron Vail had done to him, he had done that first, and deliberately, and Coral felt something in her chest go hard and bright as type metal.

"You should go," Quillon said. "There's a man upstairs. He sleeps, but he sleeps badly. And Vail comes back at dawn. He's expecting — hah — he's expecting to find you here eventually. Said you were the sentimental sort. Said you'd come back for the proofs."

"The proofs," Coral said. "Where are they?"

"Where do you think?" Another wheeze-laugh, ending in a wince. "In the case. Where you left them. Locked in the job case with the display wood type, third row, behind the great big letters nobody ever uses. He's searched this cellar twice and never once looked in a type case. Doesn't know what they are. Thinks they're drawers full of little metal worms."

Despite everything — the dying man, the sleeping guard, the whole rotten architecture of the last month — Coral felt her mouth twitch. "Little metal worms."

"He called them that. I swear it on my mother's grave. A man who has never once in his life made a letter." Quillon's breath caught. "Coral. Listen to me. I haven't much time and less pride, and I'm going to spend both badly, so bear with me."

"Don't."

"I sold you." The words came out flat and fast, like type spilled from a broken stick. "I sat across from Herron Vail with tea in my hand and I sold you, and I told myself it was arithmetic — one girl against my skin, one girl against the whole network — and then I drank the tea and couldn't look at myself in the glass, and I have been lying on this floor for six days doing the sums over and over and they don't come out. They don't come out, Coral. There's no number of skins that makes it balance."

"Master Quillon—"

"Let me finish. I'm a printer. I only get one edition." He gripped her sleeve with his ruined hand, gentle as a bird. "I took you in when your father died because I owed him, and I let you believe it was charity, and it wasn't, it was debt, and debt is a coward's love. And then I asked you to carry the packet because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up as trust, and you carried it, God help you, you carried it like it was yours. Like it had always been yours." His voice frayed. "Your father set truth in metal every day of his life and they killed him for it, and I printed lies next door for forty years and died of nothing, and somehow that was supposed to be the sensible arrangement."

Coral found she was crying without any drama about it — just tears, running quietly, the way water finds cracks.

"I don't forgive you yet," she said. "Maybe I will. Maybe I won't. But I'm not leaving you here."

"Yes, you are." He smiled, and it was the old smile, dry as a well-set page. "Because you're going to do something better than forgiveness. Look at you. You've been crying for five seconds and already you're reaching for your apron string. I can hear it. Compositors, all alike."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "What are you talking about?"

"Set my words." His grip tightened. "That's what I'm asking. Not absolution — I wouldn't insult you by asking for that. Set them. My last words. In type. In your father's cases, on your father's stone, in this cellar where I did the worst thing of my life. Lock them up in a forme and keep them somewhere Vail will never find, and someday — after — pull one proof. Just one. So that somewhere in the world there exists a piece of type that says I was sorry, set by the person I wronged, in metal that tells the truth. That's the only confession a printer gets, Coral. We don't get priests. We get letters."

The candle of her anger guttered. What remained was something older and stranger — the feeling of standing at the case as a girl, her father's hand over hers, *this letter goes here, always here, the type doesn't care how you feel, that's why we trust it.*

"All right," she said.

Quillon dictated, slowly, stopping to cough, and Coral worked by touch in the dark, her fingers reading the case like scripture. She took her time. She chose the 12-point, his favorite size, and she did not rush the spacing, because a dying man's words deserve proper leading.

*I, Aldous Quillon, compositor, confess that I traded a life for my own and found the exchange worthless. Coral Dravenmoor carried the truth when I would not. If these letters survive me, let them say that courage ran in the shop after all, and it was never mine until I saw it in her.*

She locked the forme, wrapped it in a rag, and slid it deep behind the paper stores where the damp had ruined everything else years ago. No one would look there. No one ever had.

When she turned back, Quillon's eyes were closed, but he was smiling.

"Well?" he murmured.

"Twelve-point," she said. "Proper leading."

His breath came out long and slow. "Good girl. Your father would—" The sentence never found its end. The smile stayed. The moth in the jar went still.

Coral knelt beside him for a while in the cold and the ink-smell, holding a dead man's hand among the living letters, and she did not pray, because printers don't, quite — but she thought very hard at the darkness, *he balanced it, in the end, or near enough*, and hoped that wherever accounts were kept, somebody was keeping them properly.

Then she heard the stairs.

* * *

The tread was unhurried. That was the worst of it — the absolute lack of hurry, the soft measured fall of boots belonging to a man who had never once needed to run. The sleeping guard upstairs woke with a grunt, a scrape of the chair, and then went silent again, which meant he knew the step too.

Coral pressed herself into the shadow between the press and the wall, heart slamming, and did the only thing her body knew how to do under threat: she became furniture. Shoulders rounded. Breath shallow. A woman-shaped absence in a room full of machines.

Herron Vail descended the last steps and stood in the cellar doorway with a shaded lantern, and the light swung slowly across the room like a lighthouse that had learned cruelty.

"Coral Dravenmoor," he said, conversationally, to the dark. "Or should I say the Widow Marsh? Or the sister of Mrs. Halloran of Thornbeck? You've had more names this month than a fugitive has blisters."

She said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill, and she had been practicing since December.

"I know you're here." The lantern light crawled along the type cases, row by row. "The chute grate was re-seated. Freshly. Snow melted around the lip. Also, you left footprints in the yard that a child could follow, which disappoints me — I'd credited you with more craft." He stepped fully into the room. Up close, without lantern-shadow to help him, he looked tired. That was new. The hunt had been eating him too, apparently; it simply showed differently, in gray at the temples and a certain papery quality around the eyes. "I also know why you're here. You've come for the proofs. Corvane's signature, in his own hand, ordering Hallow Bridge. The one piece of paper in Vellmark that matters."

The light reached the press and stopped.

"And here is the thing I cannot decide," Vail went on, drifting toward the type cases with the dreamy air of a man browsing a bookshop, "whether you are a courier, or something worse. Couriers deliver. They hand the parcel over and walk away lighter. But you — you burned nothing, sold nothing, delivered nothing. You carry it all still, papers and child both, like a woman who can't decide which drawer to put things in." He paused at the job case. Third row. Coral's blood turned to ice water. "My mother was a seamstress. She said the difference between a tidy house and a ruin is whether a woman knows where things belong. Do you know where things belong, Miss Dravenmoor?"

His hand came to rest on the job case handle.

And Coral — exhausted, grieving, terrified, and quite out of patience — discovered that she had stopped being afraid, in the way that a rope stops being taut when it finally snaps.

"No," she said, stepping out of the shadow. "But I know where *yours* does."

Vail turned. For one instant, one clean unrepeatable instant, his face held pure surprise — the spymaster, seen through, out-waited, found standing in another man's cellar at the wrong hour — and Coral threw her whole weight against the release lever of the press.

The counterweight woke.

Four hundred pounds of iron, asleep for weeks, dropped singing down its chain, and the great frame of the press swung its arm across the cellar like a church bell deciding to answer for itself. Vail had time to turn his head. He had time to see it coming, which was perhaps the cruelest gift she could give him — time enough to understand exactly what weight was about to land where. The frame caught him across the chest and drove him into the stairwell, and the mechanism, true to her father's engineering, jammed fast, pinning him upright against the stairs with the whole patient mass of the machine leaning on him like a creditor.

He did not scream. She had known he wouldn't. He made one sound — a short, astonished grunt, almost scholarly — and then began, with terrible calm, to work himself free, inch by inch, his boots scrabbling on the stone.

It would hold him for minutes. Not hours. Her father built honest machines; they did not pretend to be prisons.

Coral was already at the job case, third row, behind the great useless display letters. The packet was there — oilcloth, tied, exactly as she'd left it, Corvane's signature sleeping inside like a snake in winter. She stuffed it inside her coat against her heart, where it beat double-time alongside her own.

At the bottom of the coal chute she paused, one knee up, and looked back once. Vail's face was gray above the black frame of the press. His eyes found hers across the cellar, and there was no hatred in them, which frightened her more than hatred would have. Only interest. The interest of a man adding a fact to a very long ledger.

"The century turns tonight," he said, winded, almost pleasant. "So does every lock in this city. Doors open both ways, Miss Dravenmoor."

"Not this one," Coral said, and dropped into the dark.

Behind her, overhead, the first bell of New Year's Eve began to ring.
