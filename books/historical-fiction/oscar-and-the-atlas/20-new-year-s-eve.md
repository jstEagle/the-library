## 20. New Year's Eve

The proofs were exactly where her father had always hidden everything worth hiding: inside the hellbox.

Every print shop had one — a wooden bin where spoiled type went to be melted down and cast again. Coral had been six years old when she first noticed her father never actually melted anything. The box sat beneath his composing stone, heavy as sin, and when she'd once pried up its false bottom she'd found a cache of ginger biscuits and a pamphlet about the rights of man, both equally contraband in Governor Corvane's Vellmark. She'd eaten three biscuits before he caught her, and instead of scolding her he'd sat down on the floor beside her and said, "Good. Now you know where we keep the truth. Mind you don't tell your mother about the biscuits."

She was twenty-six now, kneeling on the cold cellar flags of her own shop with a lantern turned low, prying up the false bottom of the hellbox while somewhere above her head Herron Vail's men moved through her father's type cases like beetles through flour.

The proofs were there. Of course they were. Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with printer's twine, dry as scripture. Her father had pulled them himself, eleven years ago — the original press sheets bearing the orders for Hallow Bridge, signed *Atlas Corvane* in a hand so confident it barely bothered to look like handwriting. He had hidden them under a false bottom because he was a printer, and printers understood better than any governor alive that the most dangerous thing in the world was not a weapon but a well-made copy.

Coral held them against her chest for a long moment, feeling the paper breathe.

"Hello, Papa," she whispered.

Above her, a floorboard groaned. Then another. Not the random music of men searching — searching was scattered, curious. This was purposeful. Someone was walking toward the cellar stairs with intent, and Coral knew only two people left alive who walked that way in this shop: herself, and the man who had spent the last week learning its every board.

She snuffed the lantern.

The darkness came down like a lid. She felt her way by memory alone — three steps past the stone, hand on the composing stone's edge, duck under the shelf of quoins — moving through her father's cellar the way other people move through their own names. The stair door opened above her just as she slid behind the stack of dampened paper bales, and lamplight spilled down the steps in a long yellow tongue.

Herron Vail descended slowly, which was somehow worse than descending fast. A hurried man could be surprised. A slow one had already decided how everything would go.

He carried no weapon that she could see. He didn't need one; he had six upstairs and the whole city besides. He wore a gray coat the color of dishwater and an expression of mild, patient interest, like a scholar reading a difficult page. In his gloved hand he held something small, and it took Coral's eyes a moment to adjust enough to identify it, and when she did her stomach dropped through the floorboards into the river mud below.

A piece of type. A single letter, brass-bright in the lamplight.

He stopped halfway down the stairs and simply looked at the cellar for a while. At the press. At the paper bales. At the composing stone where her father had set forty years of other people's words.

"They told me," he said conversationally, to the darkness in general, "that the old man kept his best work down here. Quillon, I mean." A pause. "Though I confess I've begun to wonder whether Quillon ever did anything in this shop but sweep."

Coral pressed herself against the paper bales and breathed through her mouth, shallow and silent. The proofs were a hard rectangle against her ribs. She had no plan beyond *out*, and *out* currently had a man standing in front of it.

Vail reached the bottom step. He crossed to the press — her press, her father's press, the great iron beast that had eaten half the cellar since before she was born — and ran one glove along its frame the way a man might stroke a horse he intended to buy.

"Do you know what I admire about a press, Miss Dravenmoor?"

Her blood went cold. He hadn't seen her. He couldn't have — but he said it anyway, easily, the way he'd said everything all week, a man laying cards face-up on a table to watch what flinched.

"It is perfectly honest," he went on. "It does exactly what it is built to do, every time, without opinion. No pamphlet ever printed itself out of passion. Somewhere there is always a hand. A compositor. Someone who chooses which words the world will wake up to." He lifted the single piece of type to the light and read its face. "*E*," he said. "How ordinary. And yet." He slipped it into his coat pocket, souvenir-like, proprietary, and Coral felt the theft like a slap. That was her father's type. That was from her father's case.

"You are not here," Vail said mildly, still addressing the room, "and yet you are here. The papers are gone from the hellbox — yes, we found the false bottom, it took us four minutes, printers are sentimental creatures — but the papers themselves walked out of this city a week ago and yet here you are, come back for something. People only return for things they cannot live without." He turned, slowly, surveying the dark corners. "I do hope it isn't sentiment. Sentiment is so heavy to carry across a border."

Coral's mind, working cold and quick behind the paper bales, made its inventory. Cellar. One stair door, occupied. Coal chute, far wall, half-blocked with ash, barely wide enough for her shoulders — she'd gone up and down it twice as a girl on dares and once, memorably, to avoid her mother's questions about a broken window. The counterweight.

Every press had one. The great iron frame of her father's press stood on a timber bed, and above it hung a counterweight the size of a coffin, stone-cored and iron-shod, riding in its channel to balance the platen. It was meant to rise and fall a few inches, smooth as breath. It was also, if you knew the machine, if you had grown up underneath it drawing pictures in the ink while your father pretended not to see —

It was also roughly nine hundred pounds of iron riding on a pin no thicker than her wrist.

And the pin's retaining catch was on the near side of the frame. Reachable. If one were lying flat on the floor beneath the press, say, looking up into the machinery's black underside, one could reach it. Her father had shown her when she was nine, in the spirit of thorough paternal education: *"And this, little wren, is why you never play under the press."*

She had never forgotten. Fathers rarely teach lessons knowing which ones will matter.

Vail drifted along the row of type cases, trailing one glove across the brass. "Quillon lasted four days," he remarked. "I want you to understand that I don't say this cruelly. Four days is respectable. Some men last an afternoon." A soft sound, almost a sigh. "He gave me the route. Half of it. He gave me the tanner's quarter and the river gate and the coffin — oh yes, we opened the poor man up again, I'm afraid, though he'd already been delivered, so no harm done except to dignity — but the pass, Miss Dravenmoor. The mountain tollhouse. On that, his nerve failed him entirely, and what his nerve wouldn't give me, his body couldn't." He paused by the hellbox, nudged it with his boot. "So you see my difficulty. I have the shape of your journey and none of its heart. And you, I think, are mostly heart."

He turned toward the paper bales.

Coral moved.

Not toward the stairs — toward the floor, down and forward, sliding out from behind the bales on her belly into the black gap beneath the press frame, the space her father had called *the cathedral*, close and iron-smelling and safe as a grave. Vail's boots scraped stone three feet away. Above her head, inches above, hung the counterweight channel, and her fingers found the retaining catch in the dark — cold, familiar, exactly where it had always been.

"And there you are," Vail said softly.

Lamplight swung. She saw his boots turn, saw the hem of his gray coat, saw him crouch — unhurried, interested, a man leaning over a interesting insect — and in that moment, looking up past the boots and the coat into the pale upside-down oval of his face, Coral Dravenmoor discovered something about herself that surprised her more than anything else that terrible winter:

She wasn't afraid.

She was angry. Clean, ringing anger, the kind that clarifies rather than fogs. This man had hunted a baby. Had unmade Quillon a joint at a time. Had pocketed her father's type like a coin off a dead man's eyes. And now he was crouching over her in her own cellar, in her father's cathedral, saying *there you are* as though she were the intruder.

"The child," Vail said, conversational to the last. "Where is it? Tell me that one thing and the rest becomes paperwork."

"He's not an *it*," Coral said. "And he's not mine to give you. That's rather the whole point."

Something flickered across the upside-down face — surprise, genuine and brief, the first honest expression she'd ever seen him wear. "You gave it away."

"I placed him," Coral said. "There's a difference. You wouldn't understand it. It involves loving something more than owning it."

His hand came down toward her collar, patient, inevitable — and Coral pulled the catch.

For one heartbeat nothing happened. Machinery has its own opinions about timing. Then the counterweight woke with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and nine hundred pounds of iron came down its channel with a shriek of rust and grievance, and the great frame above her shuddered as the whole balance of the beast shifted — and Vail, mid-lunge, had time for exactly one look of scholarly surprise before the descending weight took his shoulder and drove him sideways into the frame's leg.

The crash was biblical. Coral scrambled backward out of the cathedral on her elbows, ears roaring, and got her feet under her in time to see the aftermath: the counterweight jammed in its channel at a drunken angle, the frame listing, timbers groaning, and Herron Vail pinned from shoulder to hip beneath the press's fallen iron arm — alive, cursing with quiet venom, his lantern rolling on the flags and filling the cellar with swinging shadows.

He was not crushed. She checked, quickly, coldly, the way you check a wound. His legs were free. His breathing worked. But the iron lay across him like the arm of a very large, very tired God, and no amount of pulling would lift it, and the pin would need tools and hours to reset.

Vail stopped struggling. He lay back against the stone, gray coat black with dust, and began, incredibly, to laugh — a thin, dry sound with no pleasure in it whatsoever.

"Well," he said. "The machine of state, brought down by its own weight. There's a sermon in that, if either of us attended church." He turned his head, tracked her across the cellar. "You'll hang for this."

"I've been hanging since December," Coral said. "I'm getting used to the view." She scooped up the proofs, kicked the rolling lantern toward him — courtesy, of a sort; he'd want light where he was going — and ran for the coal chute.

Behind her, overhead, the first bell of New Year's Eve began to ring.

* * *

The chute was everything childhood memory promised and less: narrow, greasy with fifty years of coal dust, angled steeply enough that going down was less climbing than surrendering. Coral wedged the oilcloth packet inside her coat, crossed her arms over it, and let gravity have its way with her, coughing, eyes streaming, tasting the whole industrial history of Vellmark on her tongue.

At the bottom she paused, one knee up, and looked back once. Far above, a gray smudge of lamplight and a grayer smudge of man. His voice floated down the shaft, winded, almost pleasant, echoing off the brick:

"The century turns tonight. So does every lock in this city. Doors open both ways, Miss Dravenmoor."

"Not this one," Coral said, and dropped into the dark.

The alley received her the way alleys do — abruptly, and into something unspeakable. She lay in frozen slush for a moment, cataloguing limbs, finding them all present and merely insulted, then rolled upright and listened. The bell overhead was ringing and ringing, deep bronze strokes rolling out across the rooftops, and beneath it she could hear the city itself: doors, voices, the far-off crack of someone's holiday fireworks already going off in the harbor district, illegal and joyful. Vellmark on New Year's Eve always sounded like this — like a city holding its breath and giggling at the same time.

Somewhere behind her, in the cellar under her shop, Herron Vail was shouting for his men. Their boots thundered on the floors above. But a press falls loudly, and a counterweight jams thoroughly, and by the time they pried their master free — if the timbers allowed it tonight at all — she intended to be smoke.

She moved. Tanner's quarter instincts had soaked into her bones by now: hood up, hands in sleeves, walk like you're owed money, never run unless running is faster than thinking. Down the alley, left at the cooper's yard, through the gap in the fence that Sera Ondel had shown her with the words *"a midwife learns every back door in a city, dear, it's how we stay employed."*

The snow had started again, fat lazy flakes that fell straight down in the windless cold, erasing footprints behind her as fast as she made them. For that much, at least, she was prepared to thank whatever governed weather.

At the corner of Rope Walk she stopped, once, and looked back at the shop.

From outside it looked as it had always looked — the crooked sign, the shuttered windows, her father's initials cut into the doorpost where a boy with a knife had gotten bored a hundred years before either of them was born. Smoke curled from its chimney, lit and innocent. Men moved behind the frosted glass. By morning it would be Vail's headquarters in name as it already was in fact, and the type cases would hold soldiers' helmets, and the press would stand silent or be broken for scrap, and the smell of ink and iron — the smell that was the entire architecture of her life — would fade from the rooms until some future occupant wondered vaguely why the place smelled of nothing at all.

She had known, coming back, that she might lose it. Knowing and doing are different animals. Standing in the falling snow with the proofs against her heart, Coral let the loss arrive properly, all of it at once, and found — to her mild astonishment — that it fit. It hurt the way a tooth stops hurting when it finally comes free. Her father had kept the truth under a false bottom in a hellbox because the world wasn't ready for it. Maybe the shop had been a false bottom too. Maybe she'd been living in the lining of something larger all along.

The bells changed.

Anyone in Vellmark knew the pattern: the quarter-hour peals rang in fours, brisk and businesslike, but at midnight the great bourdon bell — old Boanerges, the Sons of Thunder, hanging in the cathedral tower since before the Corvanes had ever learned to spell *governor* — took over alone. Twelve strokes, each one a full second apart, each one rolling out over the river and the tanneries and the governor's palace and every checkpoint and every locked door, and everyone in the city, guilty and innocent alike, counted them without meaning to.

One. Two. Three.

Coral counted too, standing in the snow at the corner of Rope Walk, a fugitive with stolen evidence and no faction, no employer, no shop, no plan beyond a barn across the border and ten thousand copies waiting to exist.

Four. Five. Six.

The century was turning without her permission, the way centuries do. Somewhere in a warm house across the river a baby named — privately, bitterly, temporarily — Atlas was asleep and didn't care what year it was, which struck her as the single sanest position available to anyone in Vellmark.

Seven. Eight.

Boots behind her, distant, searching the wrong street. Good. Sera had taught her that too: *they always search the wrong street first, love, because wrong streets are easier.*

Nine. Ten.

She thought of Quillon among the type cases, dying or dead, and of the words he'd begged her to carry, and of the small leaden letters she'd set for him instead — his last sentence locked in a chase, waiting in a drawer for a press that might never run again. Somebody would find it someday. Somebody always finds things printers hide. It was practically the trade's founding principle.

Eleven.

She pulled her hood lower, turned her back on the shop, on the city, on the whole exhausted century, and stepped into the snow.

Twelve.

The bell rolled out its final note and let it go, and the year 1792 died quietly in the falling white, owing nothing to anyone, and Coral Dravenmoor walked north along the river road with the truth buttoned inside her coat, delivering it at last to the only party she trusted.

Herself.

By dawn she would be past the tanneries. By dusk, the border woods. And somewhere ahead of her, in a cold barn with a stubborn old press and a crate of good paper, waited the only New Year's resolution anyone had ever made in Vellmark that winter worth keeping:

Ten thousand copies. And not one of them for sale.
