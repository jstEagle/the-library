## 21. Ten Thousand Copies

The barn belonged to a widow named Marta Voss, who asked no questions because, as she put it, questions were like cats: once you let one in, it brought all its friends.

Coral had been at the farm three weeks when Ivor Kade arrived with a cart that smelled of turnips and gunpowder, and beneath the turnip sacks, the old press from the Hallow Bridge safe house — smaller than her father's, but honest. Its iron was pitted with rust. Its screw needed grease. It sang slightly flat when you pulled the bar, and Coral found she loved it immediately for that imperfection, the way one loves a voice cracked by weather.

"You said you wanted to work," Ivor said, watching her run her palm along the platen the way other people touch a sleeping child's head.

"I said I wanted to *print*."

"There's a difference?"

"Work is what you do to survive," Coral said. "Printing is what survives you."

Ivor looked at her for a long moment with those bookseller's eyes that read spines and people in the same glance. "Your father used to say something like that."

"My father said a great many things. Most of them while drunk on cheap cider."

"And yet they were true." He set down his lantern. "The proofs came through. All of them. Corvane's signature on the orders — Hallow Bridge, the Salt Market, the winter of '89. Originals."

Coral felt the word land somewhere under her ribs. *Originals.* For weeks those papers had been the thing itself — the truth, singular, precious, dangerous enough to kill for. Quillon had died half-broken over them. Vail had nearly died beneath a press frame for them. And here they were, wrapped in oilcloth in a turnip cart, smelling faintly of coal smoke and her own cellar.

"Where do you want me to start?" she asked.

* * *

The first copy took an hour. The type was old and some of the letters had worn soft, so that Corvane's name printed with a slight blur, as though history itself could not look directly at him. She set the massacre order line by line — dates, numbers of dead, the cold administrative language of butchery: *seventeen households, no survivors recorded, action authorized by the Governor's hand.* Her hands shook only once, when she reached the entry dated the eleventh of March, 1789, and saw her father's street listed among the searched quarters.

Marta brought her bread and salted fish and stood watching the press work with the frank interest of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived every authority sent to tax her.

"It prints lies too?" she asked.

"Anything. That's the trouble with presses."

"Then print well," Marta said, and went back to her geese.

By the fifth day, Coral had stopped shaking. By the tenth, she worked until her fingers were black to the wrist and her shoulders burned, and she slept on straw beside the press like a dog beside its master. The barn filled with drying sheets hung from rafters on twine, hundreds of them, thousands, fluttering in the draft like strange pale birds. At night the wind moved through them and made a sound like whispering, like ten thousand small voices rehearsing.

Ivor's network came and went in ones and twos — a drover, a nun, a boy who claimed to be fourteen and was clearly eleven. Each left with bundles sewn into grain sacks, hidden in false bottoms of milk carts, rolled inside the hollow legs of a table being "delivered" to Vellmark. Nobody told anybody anything except where and when. It was the same discipline her father had practiced and never explained, and understanding it now, Coral felt grief arrive without its usual weight — more like recognition than loss.

"They'll hang you if they catch you," she told the eleven-year-old, who was loading pamphlets into a fish barrel.

"No they won't," he said cheerfully. "I'm too quick."

He was not too quick. But he was lucky, which is nearly the same thing, and he lived to be sixteen, though that is another story.

* * *

The news from Vellmark arrived the way news does in winter — slowly, then all at once, carried by travelers who spoke in fragments and lowered their voices as if the words themselves might be taxed.

The pamphlets had surfaced at the Salt Market first. Then in the cathedral pews, tucked into hymnals. Then nailed to the doors of the Governor's Hall overnight — five hundred copies, so many that the soldiers ran out of nails. People who had spent years not-knowing suddenly knew, and knowing spread faster than any army could march.

Corvane declared martial law. Then he declared the pamphlets forged. Then he arrested the man assigned to prove they were forged, because the man kept hesitating. The hesitating turned out to be contagious. A garrison refused to fire on a crowd at Tanner's Bridge. Another simply went home. By the third week of January, the governor's own household guards had developed the peculiar deafness of servants whose master has begun to shout at empty rooms.

"He's still in the Hall," Ivor reported, standing in the barn doorway with snow in his hair. "But the Hall is emptying around him like water from a cracked jug. His ministers resign by letter now — they don't dare come. Vail hasn't been seen since New Year's Eve."

"Dead?"

"Buried alive is my guess, or limping somewhere with a grudge the size of a province. Either way, nobody's following him anymore." Ivor smiled thinly. "You know what did this? Not the resistance. We've been passing pamphlets for six years and mostly we passed them to each other. It was ink. Ten thousand copies, ordinary type, ordinary paper. Any fool can burn a resistance cell. No one can burn a fact."

Coral wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the press, tired and true, singing its flat note.

"Not armies," she said. "Ink."

* * *

They came for the originals in February — not Ivor's people, but men from across the mountains, representatives of the provisional council forming in Corvane's wake. They wore plain coats and spoke of archives, of accountability, of the historical record. They spoke, Coral noticed, very much the way Vail had spoken: patiently, reasonably, about ownership.

"The documents should be held in trust," said the elder of the two, a thin man with careful eyes. "Under guard. For the tribunal."

"Of course," Coral agreed pleasantly. "Let me fetch them."

She fetched them. She also fetched the iron kettle from Marta's kitchen, and while the careful-eyed man was explaining the importance of controlled access to the historical record, she fed the proofs into the stove page by page, holding each one up long enough for him to see the signature before it curled and went orange and became nothing.

"What are you *doing*?" he demanded.

"Burning the only originals in existence," Coral said. "There are ten thousand copies in Vellmark alone. There will be more. Anyone who wants the truth can have it free, from anyone, forever. What exactly would your archive add?"

"That these documents be *controlled*—"

"Yes. That's rather the point of the exercise, isn't it? Truth belongs to everyone or it belongs to whoever holds the last original, and I've met the people who want to hold it." She stirred the ashes with the poker. "You'll find I'm consistent."

The careful-eyed man stared at her the way Quillon once had, and Herron Vail, and every person who had ever believed that valuable things must be owned to matter. Then he and his colleague left without the documents and with a story they would tell carefully for years.

Marta, who had watched from the doorway with her arms folded, waited until the riders were gone and said, "That kettle's seen worse."

"I know," said Coral. "So have I."

That night she slept better than she had since a bundle had appeared at her door in December, and dreamed of nothing at all — just white space, like a fresh sheet waiting.

* * *

Spring came late to the valley, and with it a letter from Sera Ondel in a hand grown steadier than Coral remembered: the boy was well, he was fat, he had opinions, and the family across the river adored him beyond reason. Coral read it twice by the barn door and folded it away without tears, which surprised her, and didn't.

Ivor found her setting new type — not evidence now, just a small almanac, planting dates and tide tables, the humble literature of people planning to live another year.

"The council wants to give you a medal," he said. "Or a printing monopoly. Possibly both. You're famous in a way that requires paperwork."

"Tell them I died."

"That seems wasteful. You're very good."

"So are tides," said Coral, "and they don't take medals." She slid the last letter into its case and locked the composing stick. "Tell them the printer was a woman who burned everything and left no name. Let them make a myth of it. Myths don't need pensions."

Ivor laughed — really laughed, for the first time since she'd known him — and sat down on a feed barrel to watch her work, and outside the geese complained about spring, and somewhere south across the river a city learned to breathe again.

Coral pulled the bar. The press sang its flat note. The sheet came out clean.

And far off, past the hills, past the border stones, a woman named Sera was lifting a fat, opinionated boy onto her hip and calling him by a name no one in Vellmark would ever think to look for —

Oscar.
