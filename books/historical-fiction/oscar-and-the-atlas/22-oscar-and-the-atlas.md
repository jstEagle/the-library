## 22. Oscar and the Atlas

Spring came late to the border country that year, and when it came it came all at once, as if the hills had been holding their breath since December and had finally, collectively, decided to exhale.

Coral Dravenmoor — though no one in Hessel Fen called her Coral Dravenmoor, because no one in Hessel Fen called her anything but Marta, Marta the printer's widow, which was a fiction she had stitched together out of two truths and a convenient dead husband who existed only in her telling — stood at the window of her rented room above the chandlery and watched the thaw water run silver down the lane. The village smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and, faintly, of tallow from the shop below, a smell she had decided after three weeks was not unpleasant so much as honest. Everything in Hessel Fen was honest. That was its great virtue and its great limitation, and she had come to love it for both.

She had been there four months. Long enough for the winter to break. Long enough to learn which of the two taverns watered its beer (the one with the painted sign, not the one with the carved one) and which of the farmers' wives would trade gossip for mended bookbindings and which would trade gossip for nothing at all, being constitutionally incapable of keeping a thing to herself even when the thing was worthless. Long enough to set type again.

That last one had taken the longest. Ivor had smuggled a small job lot of type across the river for her in February — fourteen cases, a composing stick, a hand press old enough to have opinions — and when the crate arrived and she pried off the lid and saw the matrices gleaming in their beds like seeds waiting for spring, she had sat down on the floor of the chandlery's back room and cried for the better part of an hour. Not grief exactly. Something adjacent to grief. The feeling of picking up a tool your father put into your hands and finding that your hands still remembered him.

She printed wedding invitations now. Bills of sale. A small hymnal for the village chapel that the chapel's elderly pastor insisted on proofreading twice, line by line, with a magnifying glass he wore on a ribbon like a medal. It was not Vellmark. It was not ten thousand copies of a massacre scattered through a city like snowfall. It was, she found to her continuing surprise, enough.

Today she meant to walk to the Aldous farm.

She had been meaning to walk to the Aldous farm for six weeks. Each week she dressed for it, and each week some small emergency intervened — a jammed press, a misdelivered shipment of ink, an afternoon of rain that she told herself was too heavy for walking though she had walked in far worse — until she began to suspect that the emergencies were not entirely accidental, that some part of her was manufacturing them the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Because the Aldous farm was where Oscar lived now.

* * *

She took the north road out of the village, past the millpond where ducks were conducting their loud annual negotiations, past the first plowed fields lying open and dark as fresh-set type. The air had that washed quality of early spring, everything rinsed and beginning again. Her boots squelched. She did not mind. She had crossed the Tanner's Quarter in December with frozen mud sucking at her ankles and a baby against her chest and soldiers sweeping the streets behind her; a little honest squelching was nothing.

The Aldous farm sat in a shallow fold of land half a mile from the village: a low stone house, a barn with one wall newer than the rest, a dooryard full of chickens who regarded her approach with the flat suspicion of creatures who have been lied to about grain before. A woman was hanging washing between two posts — broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair escaping its kerchief, moving with the economy of someone who has never once in her life wasted a motion. She saw Coral coming and did not stop working, only lifted her chin in greeting.

"You'd be the printer," she said, by way of hello. "Marta."

"I would. Thank you for seeing me."

"Didn't see you yet. Saw a person walking up my lane is what I saw." But there was no edge in it. Grethe Aldous, Sera had warned her, took half an hour to decide whether she liked you and then never revisited the decision. Coral had four months of letters behind her — she had written to Sera, Sera had written to the family through the border network, the network had vouched — and apparently the decision had already been made somewhere in that chain of paper without her knowledge. It was a strange feeling, being pre-approved. She wasn't sure she trusted it.

"The boy," Coral said. "I understand he's well?"

"He's into everything." Grethe pegged a shirt to the line with a decisive snap. "Into the flour bin. Into the horse trough. Into the church, twice, which Pastor Ebel says is fine because God appreciates the company. He'll be two in the autumn." She paused, clothespin in hand, and looked at Coral directly for the first time. "You're the one carried him over the pass."

It was not a question, but it wanted an answer, or something like one.

"I carried him partway," Coral said. "Sera Ondel carried him further than I did. And a coffin carried him furthest of all." She hesitated. "I never learned how the last leg went. After the tollhouse. I crossed the mountains and handed him to the network and I never asked what happened next. I think I was afraid to ask."

Grethe studied her for a long moment. Somewhere behind the house a dog barked twice at nothing and then thought better of it.

"He cried for three days when we got him," Grethe said. "Not loud. Quiet crying, like he'd already worked out that loud didn't help. My husband Tomas walked him up and down this dooryard every night till the moon went down. Then one morning he stopped crying and started laughing, and he hasn't stopped since, except when the goose takes his bread." She turned back to the washing. "You want to see him."

"Yes," Coral said. And then, because it seemed important to be exact: "Once. I only want to see him once."

"Mm," said Grethe, in the tone of a woman who had heard a statement and filed it under things that were not her business, which was a very short file, because almost everything on a farm was her business.

* * *

He was in the barn.

Of course he was in the barn. Grethe had said *into everything*, and Coral should have known that a child who had begun his life wrapped in sedition and smuggled across three checkpoints would grow into a boy who could not be contained by a dooryard fence. She heard him before she saw him — a running commentary of nonsense syllables delivered with tremendous authority to an audience of hens — and then she rounded the corner of the stall and there he was.

Dark-haired. Round-faced. Sitting in the straw with his boots off and one sock missing, holding court. Before him, arranged in a careful row, stood six eggs, a horseshoe, a wooden spoon, and a turnip. He was pointing at the turnip and explaining something to the eggs with the grave intensity of a man delivering a eulogy.

Coral stopped walking.

Four months. Four months since she had left him with Sera at the safe house and slipped back across the river alone, and she had prepared herself for this moment the way you prepare for any wound you've decided is worth taking: carefully, thoroughly, and with the private knowledge that preparation helps not at all. He was bigger. Of course he was bigger. He had been seven months old when she'd pulled him from the type chest, small enough to fit inside a galley tray, heavy as a loaf of good bread and twice as warm. Now he was a person. A whole entire person, with opinions about turnips.

"Tomas!" he shouted suddenly, having noticed her, with the unembarrassed volume of the very young. "*Tomas!* Person!"

"That's right," Coral said, crouching down to his level, because it seemed the thing to do and also because her knees had gone unreliable. "A person."

He considered her with a frown of deep administrative seriousness, the same frown she had seen on customs officers and bishops, and then held up the turnip.

"For you," he said.

"Oh — I couldn't—"

"*For. You.*"

There was no arguing with it. She accepted the turnip. It was damp and slightly gnawed and the finest gift she had received in years, including the shop she'd once been offered and the revolution she'd accidentally helped deliver. She turned it over in her hands while he watched her with an expression of satisfaction, as though a transaction of great importance had been properly concluded.

"What's your name?" he demanded.

And here it was. Here was the moment she had rehearsed in a hundred variations on a hundred sleepless nights. *My name is Coral,* she might say. *I knew you when you were small. I kept you warm in a box of metal letters. Your mother loved you so much she gave you away to save you, and the man whose blood is in you built his throne out of other people's graves, and none of that matters now except that you are here, in a barn, with eggs.*

"My name is Marta," she said instead.

"Marta," he repeated solemnly, and then pointed at himself. "Osk."

"Osk," she agreed.

Grethe had told her, in the dooryard, in the flat voice of a woman reporting weather: *We couldn't keep the name they gave us on the papers — too foreign-sounding, would raise questions — so we called him Oskar. After Tomas's grandfather.* And Coral had nodded and said *that's a good name*, and meant it, and had gone back to her room and lain awake until dawn understanding that she would never tell them. Never tell anyone. That Lysandra Corvane's son would grow up as Oskar Aldous, grandson of a farmer's grandfather, heir to a barn and a horse trough and a goose with strong opinions about bread, and that this — this, precisely this — was what his mother had died hoping for. Not a figurehead. Not a banner. Not a name that made men kneel or flinch. A turnip. Eggs for an audience. Someone to shout *Tomas! Person!* when a stranger came up the lane.

The dynasty ended not with a knife or a decree but with a christening record in a border parish, one line among hundreds, and Coral was the only living soul who knew it, and she would carry that knowledge the way she'd once carried pamphlets under floorboards — quietly, and all the way to the grave.

Oskar — Osk — had lost interest in her. He was repossessing his horseshoe, apparently having concluded the audience was unworthy. Then, abruptly, he changed his mind, dropped the horseshoe, stood up with the wobbling determination of the newly steady-on-his-feet, and walked straight into her arms.

It was not graceful. It was mostly collision. He smelled of straw and milk and horse, and his fist closed in her collar with the grip of someone who has decided you are furniture now, and Coral held him and felt the whole of the winter pass through her and out — the checkpoints, the cellar, the counterweight falling, Vail's face in the lamplight, Quillon dying among the galley trays with her father's name in his mouth, the press hammering out ten thousand truths in a freezing barn while she burned the originals page by page and watched the only copies of Corvane's signature curl to ash — all of it passing through her like thaw water down a lane, going wherever such things go, leaving her standing in a barn holding a child who did not know her and never would, and finding that this, too, was bearable.

More than bearable. She closed her eyes and rested her cheek on his hair.

"There now," said Grethe from the barn door, in the voice of a woman who has seen everything twice and been unsurprised both times. "He doesn't do that for everyone. Mostly he does that for people holding food."

Coral laughed. It came out cracked, halfway to something else, and she let it be whatever it was.

After a while — she could not have said how long — Oskar wriggled free with the sudden ruthlessness of the very young, retrieved his turnip back from her hands (*mine*, he explained), and returned to his lecture. Coral got to her feet. Her knees worked again. Mostly.

"He needs his dinner," Grethe said. "So do you, I expect. Come in. Tomas will want to meet the woman who sent us our boy."

"I only came to—"

"To see him once. Yes." Grethe untied her apron with a snap. "Well. You've seen him. Now you'll eat. Those are different things, and around here we don't confuse them."

* * *

Tomas Aldous turned out to be a long, slow, deliberate man who spoke perhaps forty words an hour and made each one count, like a compositor setting display type. Over supper — ham, brown bread, pickled cabbage fierce enough to bring tears to the eyes of the unprepared — he studied Coral across the table and finally said:

"Printer, are you."

"I am."

"Good work's hard to find." He tore his bread with great care. "Our Bible's falling apart. The big one. Pastor Ebel's been after me two years."

"I could rebind it," Coral said. "Or print a new one, if the congregation could bear the cost. Paper's dearer here than it was in Vellmark, but not ruinously."

"Hm," said Tomas, which Grethe later informed Coral was his highest form of enthusiasm.

They talked a little — about paper sources, about the price of ink, about whether the chapel roof leaked (it did). Nobody asked her where she'd come from. Nobody asked why a printer's widow traveled alone with a crate of type and a face that went still and watchful whenever hoofbeats passed the window. In Hessel Fen, Coral had discovered, people extended you the courtesy of assuming your history was your own business, an arrangement she found so civilized it nearly moved her to tears the first month, and which she suspected was less philosophy than exhaustion — everyone in the valley had left somewhere, and asking questions meant answering them.

When the plates were cleared, Oskar climbed into Coral's lap without ceremony, demanded the spoon, and fell asleep against her shoulder mid-chew, in the manner of children everywhere, abandoning his meal and his dignity at once.

Grethe raised an eyebrow. Coral said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie or a confession, and she had spent a lifetime learning the difference between the two, and tonight neither was required.

Outside, the light went long and gold across the fields. A cart rattled past on the road, and the driver raised a hand to the house, and no one in the cart was a soldier, and no one in the house was hiding, and the boy breathed slow and warm against her collarbone, and Coral thought: *this is what it was for.* Not the fall of Corvane — though that had come, in April, when the pamphlets finally did their work and the city rose and the Governor's Guard, finding that power's servants doubt after all, declined to fire on the crowds. Not the burning of the proofs, or the emptying of Vellmark from her life like ink washed from a press bed. This. A kitchen. Cabbage. A sleeping weight on her shoulder that she would carry to the door, hand to his foster mother, and walk away from down a darkening lane with her heart full and her hands empty, and it would be worth it, it had been worth it, it would always have been worth it.

She stayed until he woke. Then she gave him back.

* * *

The walk home was dark and smelled of rain coming. She didn't hurry.

At the chandlery, she climbed the stairs to her room, lit the lamp, and sat down at the case. Not because there was work — the hymnal was done, the wedding invitations were done, tomorrow's bill of sale could wait. She sat down because her hands wanted to, and she had spent enough years ignoring what her hands wanted to know better by now.

She set a line of type. Just one. Her fingers found the letters without her looking, the way they always had, the way her father had taught them to before he was her father's ghost and simply her father, before Hallow Bridge and Herron Vail and the whole atlas of trouble that had fallen on her one December night wrapped in a blanket at her door.

She set: *OSCAR AND THE ATLAS.*

Then she looked at it a long time in the lamplight — the boy's true name and the tyrant's, side by side in the stick, locked tight together the way they always had been, blood calling to blood across the wreckage of a dynasty — and she distributed it, letter by letter, back into the case. Every piece to its home. The o and the s and the c and the a and the r. The capital A that had ruled a city and now weighed exactly as much as any other piece of lead.

Empty of everything but the work, her hands reached for tomorrow's type, and outside, soft on the roof, the spring rain began.
