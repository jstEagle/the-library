## 1. The Summons

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Lior Winterbourne would remember afterward as the last ordinary day of her life.

It arrived the way all bad news arrived in Vellamarch — by ferry, then by the postman's boots on the frozen cobbles of Thimble Lane, each footfall crisp enough to count. She heard him coming from the workbench, where she was fitting a mainspring into a lady's carriage watch so small it could hide in a walnut shell. Her hands did not stop. Ten years at that bench had taught her that nothing good ever walked up Thimble Lane with such deliberate steps. Bills came running. Grief came stumbling. But official paper — paper with seals and folded corners and other people's intentions pressed flat inside it — that always arrived at a walk.

The knock came twice. She set down her tweezers, wiped her fingers on the leather apron she had inherited along with everything else in this room, and opened the door to a gust of river cold and the postman's red-chapped face.

"Sign here, miss." He held out the board without ceremony, already looking past her to the next house. "From the observatory, by the look of it. Heavy paper."

Heavy paper. She signed, took the letter, and stood in the doorway until his footsteps faded toward the chandler's, because some part of her had learned — the way one learns the weight of a balance wheel, without being taught — that a letter like this should not be opened standing where the whole lane could see her face change.

She closed the door. She turned the bolt, though no one in Vellamarch had ever bothered robbing a clockmaker's shop, since the only things of value were too small to fence and too honest to steal. Then she carried the letter to the bench, set it down beside the walnut-shell watch, and looked at it for a long moment before touching it again.

The paper was cream-colored, thick as good vellum, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. The observatory's crest was pressed into it: a star over a gear. She knew that crest. Every soul in Vellamarch knew it, though few had seen it closer than the weathervane on the observatory dome, visible from every street in town like an eye that never blinked. For ten years that eye had been blind. For ten years the master clock inside it — the great regulator, the heart by which the town's church bells, the ferry schedules, the assizes calendar, and every shopkeeper's ledger were supposed to keep their time — had stood stopped at eleven minutes past four, frozen on some afternoon a decade gone when whatever broke inside it finally broke for good.

Her master had been summoned to repair it once. That had been the first year of her apprenticeship. He had come back from the observatory with his coat smelling of cold stone and dust, sat down at this very bench, and said nothing for three days. When she asked — fourteen years old, fearless in the way only the ignorant are fearless — he had told her: *Some clocks you don't fix, Lior. Some clocks you leave alone and thank God they stay broken.*

She had never understood it. She had written it off as one of Master Hollis's riddles, and there had been so many riddles in those years. Which customers to serve last. Which debts to let lie. Why certain men who came into the shop asking questions about the observatory were answered with blank politeness and shown the door. Why he never, in ten years, spoke the name Aldery above a mutter.

Master Hollis had been dead eleven weeks now. Long enough for the frost to take the geraniums in the window box. Not long enough for the creditors to lose interest.

That was the arithmetic she lived by now, and it was brutal in its simplicity. The workshop on Thimble Lane belonged, in law, to the estate of Edmund Hollis — and the estate owed money. Owed it to suppliers in Marrowfield, to the rent-holder, and most of all to Mr. Julius Aldery, who held notes against the business going back years, notes signed in her master's careful hand, and Mr. Aldery was not a patient man when it came to collecting. The lawyers had already come once, two gentlemen in gray with measuring eyes, walking the length of the shop and pricing the lathes, the stock, the unfinished commissions, even the stool she sat on. Twenty-eight days, they said. Twenty-eight days for the estate to satisfy its debts or the contents would be sold at auction on the market square, lot by lot, and Lior Winterbourne would be out on the street with her toolbox and nothing else.

Twenty-six days remained. She had spent the last two weeks doing sums she could not make come right. The carriage watches, the regulator clocks, the tower work she might bid on in spring — none of it would raise half the sum. She had begun, quietly and with a sickness in her stomach she refused to name, to decide which tools she would keep and which she would let go to strangers.

And now this letter. From the observatory. On heavy paper.

She broke the seal.

*To Miss Lior Winterbourne, clockmaker, of Thimble Lane, Vellamarch.*

*Madam,*

*It has pleased the Trustees of the Vellamarch Observatory to commission the repair of the Observatory Regulator, commonly called the master clock, which has stood out of service these ten years past. You are named herein as sole craftsman for this work. No other hand is to touch the mechanism. The commission carries payment of forty pounds upon completion, and lodging at the observatory for the duration, the work being of a nature requiring both secrecy and sustained attention.*

*You will present yourself at the observatory door on Thursday next, at dusk. Bring your own tools. Bring nothing else.*

*By order of the Trustees,*

*(signed)* *T. Merrick, Keeper*

Lior read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she would examine a movement whose ticking sounded wrong — not listening to what it said but to how it said it, hunting the flaw.

Forty pounds. Forty pounds was more than the shop cleared in a year. Forty pounds, paid to the estate, would push the creditors back past the auction date, buy time through the winter, maybe — maybe — buy enough time to find a way through. And lodging at the observatory meant coal she wasn't buying and bread she wasn't baking, which stretched the twenty-six days further still.

It was salvation. It was salvation arriving exactly, precisely, at the hour she needed it, dressed in heavy paper and sealed wax, and that was the first thing that frightened her.

Because she had grown up in this town, and she had grown up under Edmund Hollis, and one of the many things he had taught her without meaning to teach it was that in Vellamarch, nothing arrived on time by accident. The ferry ran late. The postman ran late. The magistrate's rulings ran very late indeed, unless you were rich, in which case they ran early. But this letter had found her in the exact week of her desperation, naming her — *sole craftsman*, *no other hand*, *you alone* — with a specificity that felt less like an honor than a hook baited with exactly the fish it wanted.

Why her? She was nobody. A journeywoman-apprentice of twenty-four with no guild standing, no name, no family anyone could name. There were clockmakers in Marrowfield with reputations; there was the guild chapter upriver that certified tower work. If the trustees wanted the great regulator repaired, they could have summoned anyone. Instead they had summoned a dead man's apprentice, alone, in secret, at dusk.

*Sole craftsman.* As if the clock were not merely broken but dangerous. As if whoever wrote this wanted the work done quietly, by someone with no one to report to, no one to ask questions, no one to notice if things went strangely up on that hill.

Or — and here was the thought she kept turning over like a coin worn smooth — as if someone wanted the work done by the one person in Vellamarch who had a reason to say yes to anything, on any terms, and ask nothing.

They knew about the debts. Of course they knew. Everyone knew. The gray gentlemen with the measuring eyes had made sure of that by walking the shop aloud, pricing her life piece by piece while she stood with her hands folded and her face arranged into nothing.

So: honor or trap. She set the letter down on the bench and looked at it beside the open walnut-shell watch, and understood that the question was wrong. It was both. In Vellamarch it was always both. The trap and the gift shared a single hinge, like the striking train of a clock — the same wheel that marked the hours delivered the blows.

What she could not see — yet — was which side of the hinge she was standing on.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon because her hands demanded occupation and because the carriage watch was due Saturday and because if she sat still she would begin to hope, and hope in her experience was the most expensive commodity a person in debt could purchase. She fitted the mainspring, cased the movement, regulated it against the shop's own regulator clock — a small, faithful thing that had ticked on this wall through her entire apprenticeship, through her master's illness, through the eleven weeks since, keeping its modest perfect time as if nothing in the world had ended.

At dusk the light went blue in the window and she lit the lamp and allowed herself, finally, to think.

The observatory sat on the hill above the town, a round stone drum crowned with its copper dome, built forty years ago by old Josiah Crane — the founder, the benefactor, the man whose statue in the market square pointed north with one brass finger worn bright by generations of schoolboys. Crane had been dead fifteen years. His endowment, everyone said, had kept the observatory running long after his death: the keeper's salary, the instruments, the great clock itself, which Crane had commissioned in his last years and which had run — so the story went — for barely five years before it failed, and had never run since.

Ten years stopped. But the clock was older than ten years. It had failed, been repaired, failed again. And her master — she turned the memory now with new care, the way you turn a screw you suspect has been cross-threaded — her master had been summoned up that hill in her first year, had spent three days silent afterward, and had never spoken of the place again except in those riddles. *Which questions not to ask.* One of the rules, unspoken but absolute, had concerned the observatory: she was never to solicit work there, never to remark on the stopped clock, never to repeat what gossips said about it. And the gossips said plenty. That the clock was cursed. That old Crane had died in the room with it. That the trustees quarreled over the estate. That the heir — the nephew, Aldery — had let the place go dark rather than spend a shilling on it.

Aldery. The name surfaced in her mind the way bodies surface in the river in spring, unwelcome and inevitable. Julius Aldery held the notes against her master's estate. Julius Aldery was the founder's nephew and the principal trustee of the observatory. Julius Aldery, if the talk was true, had bought the magistrate the way other men bought coats.

And now a commission from the observatory, in her master's death-week season, offering exactly the sum that would save her master's shop.

She got up and crossed to the back of the shop, to the door she rarely opened, and stood a moment with her hand on the latch before letting herself into the room beyond. His room. The master's bench, still bearing the ghost-rings of fifty years of work, the tools laid out in their racks in the order his hands had taught them, the good loupe in its velvet case, the drawer of templates cut from tin in his own patterns. Eleven weeks, and she had touched almost nothing in here. Dust lay on everything like a second, paler skin.

She did not know why she had come in. Except that the letter upstairs in her mind kept saying *no other hand is to touch the mechanism*, and something in her wanted to stand in the presence of the one other pair of hands that had ever mattered.

"Tell me," she said to the empty room, and her voice sounded foolish and young. "Tell me whether to go."

The room said nothing. The regulator clock in the shop ticked on, steady as a pulse. Outside, far off, she could hear the river — the ice shifting, groaning, settling its enormous weight against the piers, the sound it made all winter, the sound the town slept inside of like a heartbeat under the floor of the world.

She went back to the shop and did what she should have done at the start: she worked the problem like a problem.

Terms: forty pounds, payable on completion. Lodging provided. Secrecy required. Duration unspecified. Named her alone. Summoned her at dusk, not day. Signed by the keeper, T. Merrick — a name she half knew, a quiet man, a shadow figure, seen at funerals and little else. Ordered by the trustees — and the trustees were, as far as anyone knew, Aldery and whoever Aldery permitted to agree with him.

If it was a trap, what kind? They could seize the shop outright with less theater; the law was already moving. If they wanted her compromised, they had easier means than employment. If they wanted silence about something in that clock, why summon a clockmaker at all?

Unless the clock genuinely needed repairing, and repairing it would expose something, and whoever ordered the repair wanted it exposed — wanted it exposed by precisely one person, someone small enough to be disowned if it went wrong and honest enough, perhaps, to do it right.

Or someone wanted it covered, by precisely one person, someone desperate enough to sign anything and curious enough, obedient enough, skilled enough to find whatever was hidden and never speak of it.

Both readings fit the letter like a key fits a lock. She could not tell them apart. That, more than anything, convinced her she would go. Because there was one fact underneath all the speculation, one load-bearing truth the whole structure rested on, and it was this: in twenty-six days she would have nothing left of Edmund Hollis but her hands and his lessons, and this letter was the only door that had opened in eleven weeks.

Wary hope. She tested the phrase in her mind and found it accurate. Hope, sharpened by fear into something with an edge — a tool, not a feeling. You could work with a tool.

* * *

Wednesday passed in preparation. She serviced every tool she would take: cleaned the pliers' jaws, honed the screwdrivers to their proper edges, oiled the lathe one last time and left it — left it, and hated leaving it, the way you hate leaving a dog at a strange door. She packed the box her master had built her at sixteen, pine and brass corners, fitted trays, everything in its place. She wound the shop clocks and set the regulator true against her own pulse, which after ten years of training agreed with Greenwich to within a whisper.

In the evening she walked down to the river and stood on the pier in the cold and watched the ice.

It was moving. Not breaking — not yet — but moving, the vast white sheet groaning and creeping downstream in slow convulsions, humping itself against the bridge pilings, shedding shards that rang against each other like struck glass. The old men on the pier said the break would come within the fortnight, maybe sooner; the moon was wrong for a late thaw, and the water beneath was restless. When the ice went, it went all at once, with a roar the town heard in its teeth, and the ferries would stop, and the roads would flood low lanes, and Vellamarch would hold its breath until the river remembered its manners.

She stood there until her feet went numb, thinking: *Thursday dusk. I will climb the hill and knock on the door of the place my master feared, and I will open the heart of the clock that killed the peace of his last thirty years, and I will take the money because I need it, and I will keep my eyes open because I am not fourteen anymore.*

Below her, caught between two grinding floes, a single shard of ice spun slowly, catching the last light, flashing like a signal she couldn't read.

She turned home. Halfway up Thimble Lane she stopped, because the shop window was dark and in the dark glass she saw reflected the hill behind her, and on the hill the observatory dome, black against the deepening sky, with one light burning in it — a small yellow point, high up, where no light had burned in ten years.

Someone was up there tonight. Someone was awake in the blind eye of Vellamarch, waiting for Thursday, waiting for her.

She stood in the cold street with her breath smoking and her heart going hard, and she understood that whatever waited in that clock — honor, trap, or both hinged together — she had already, somewhere between the seal and this moment, decided.

Tomorrow at dusk she would carry her master's teachings up the hill in a pine box with brass corners, and she would find out what it was that Edmund Hollis had spent ten years refusing to say.

The light in the dome went out. The dark took the hill. Behind her, faint and faithful, the shop regulator ticked on in the empty room, keeping honest time over a shop that might not survive the month — and Lior Winterbourne climbed the rest of the lane with her collar up and her jaw set, counting the hours like a woman counting the teeth of a wheel she had not yet dared to turn.
