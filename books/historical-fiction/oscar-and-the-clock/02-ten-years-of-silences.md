## 2. Ten Years of Silences

The letter stayed in Lior's pocket all night, and the night was long enough to hold ten years.

She did not sleep in the workshop — she never slept there since the funeral — but she sat at her master's bench with a single lamp burning and let the dark come up against the windows the way the river came up against the ice: patiently, from underneath. The regulator on the wall ticked. The pine box waited by the door with its brass corners catching lamplight. And somewhere between one hour and the next, without deciding to, she began to count the rules Edmund Hollis had taught her, because that was what she did when she was afraid. She took things apart to see how they were made.

There had been more rules than gears, she thought now. There had been more rules than there were words for them, some spoken and some only demonstrated, and she had learned them the way water learns the shape of its channel — without ever once being told that it was being shaped.

* * *

The first rule had come when she was fourteen, three days after she was bound to him, before she could even file a flat face true. A woman had come into the shop with a mantel clock wrapped in a shawl, weeping, saying her late husband's mother had left it to her and would he look at it, please, please, would he tell her it could be saved. Master Hollis had taken the case, turned it over, opened the back, looked for perhaps four seconds, and closed it again.

"It cannot be saved," he said gently. "I'm sorry."

The woman left. When the bell above the door had finished swinging, Lior said, "But you didn't even look at the movement."

"I looked," he said. "It can be saved. It will cost four pounds and six weeks, and that woman has two shillings and a grief, and if I take her four pence down to nothing she will hate the clock, and then she will hate her husband, and then she will hate herself, and all for a clock that tells no better time than the sun." He set his palm flat on the bench. "Rule the first, girl. Some debts you ignore. Not because they are not owed — because collecting them costs more than they are worth. Learn which is which, and you may be an honest man in this town instead of merely a rich one."

He had smiled when he said it. She remembered that. She remembered everything about him now with the terrible fidelity of grief, every gesture catalogued and kept, and so she remembered the smile, and remembered thinking it was kindness.

* * *

Rule the second concerned questions. It came to her less as a lesson than as a slow accretion of small refusals, and it took her years to understand that the refusals themselves were the teaching.

There were customers whose names he wrote in the ledger but never spoke aloud in the shop. If Lior asked who Mr. A— was, who paid quarterly and always in banknotes fresh enough to crackle, Master Hollis would say, "A man who pays well and wants nothing but discretion," and turn the page, and that was the end of it. If she asked why certain letters went out sealed with wax rather than posted plain, he said, "Because some paper is worth more than what's written on it." Once — only once, and she had been nineteen and foolish with her own cleverness — she had asked him directly what he knew about Aldery, the nephew who had come up from the coast after old Josiah Merridew stopped leaving the observatory, the young man with the good boots and the bad eyes who stood too long in front of the shop window on market days like a man pricing something that wasn't for sale.

Master Hollis had put down his graver.

He hadn't shouted. That was the frightening part; she had expected shouting, and prepared herself for shouting, and what she got instead was silence — a full minute of it, while he looked at her with an expression she had never been able to name until this very night, sitting alone with a letter in her pocket, when she understood at last that it had been fear. Not for himself. For her.

"Lior," he had said finally, quietly, "in this trade there are wheels that drive other wheels. You can see the escapement wheel turning and you want to know what drives it, and that is a fine quality in an apprentice and a fatal one in a friend. Some trains are hidden. Some are hidden deliberately. When I tell you a question is not yours to ask, I am not insulting your intelligence. I am telling you where the teeth are." He picked up his graver again. "Now hand me the pivot file and ask me about the deadbeat escapement instead, because that is a mechanism you can survive understanding."

And she had asked about the deadbeat escapement, because she was nineteen and wanted to live, and because she loved him — she saw that now, plainly, the way you see the whole shape of a thing only when it has been taken off the shelf and held to the light. She had loved him the way an apprentice loves: entirely, uncritically, mistaking protection for truth.

Which questions not to ask. She could still list them. Not what the observatory paid for. Not why the quarterly notes came. Not why, in eleven years, Master Hollis had never once climbed the hill to Vellamarch Observatory except at night, though its founder had been, by everyone's account, the closest friend of his youth. Not why, when Josiah Merridew died the previous autumn, her master had locked the shop for three days and come out grey as pewter and said only, "The watch is ended," and would not explain what watch, or whose.

* * *

Rule the third: which customers to serve last.

This one he taught openly, almost cheerfully, because it was the rule that looked most like pettiness and was actually arithmetic. The assizes clerk's chronometer could wait a fortnight; the magistrate's could wait a month — men whose time was made of meetings could afford to lose some of it. But the ferryman's watch must be fixed same-day, because a ferryman who misses the tide loses his living. The miller's clock must run through harvest. The widow's mantel piece, whatever else was on the bench, came out of the case within the hour, because a widow counting the days since a death does not need a clock that lies to her.

"The order of the bench is the order of the world," he used to say, tapping the slate where he chalked the queue. "Great men believe their business runs on their own importance. It doesn't. It runs on ferry tides and flour and grief. Serve those, and the great men will wait, and be glad of the waiting, because it makes them feel served."

She had thought it philosophy. Sitting in the dark now, she wondered whether it had also been camouflage — whether a man who loudly ordered his bench around the humblest customers was also, quietly, making sure that nobody ever noticed the order in which certain other jobs were done. Whether the ferryman's same-day repair was partly a screen behind which a sealed letter could travel to the observatory unremarked.

She hated herself a little for the thought, and could not stop having it. That was the trouble with taking a man apart after he was dead: you found springs in him you had never seen while he was alive, and some of them were ugly, and you could not unknow them, and you could not ask him.

* * *

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick, and the flame stood up straight again, and the shadows on the tool wall leaned and settled like a congregation.

Ten years. Ten years of silences, itemized. The silences about money — where the comfortable surplus in the till had come from in the lean years, the years when half of Vellamarch owed the other half and nobody paid anybody. The silences about the letters — the thick ones that arrived monthly from the hill and were burned unread in the grate, or so he claimed, though she had once seen him read one twice before feeding it to the fire, and seen his face do something complicated and private, like a man swallowing a stone. The silences about the past — Josiah Merridew's name spoken exactly four times in ten years, she had counted, and always in the same tone, the tone of a man setting down something heavy very carefully so it will not ring against the floor.

And beneath all of those, the great silence, the load-bearing one, the one the whole structure rested on: whatever it was that Edmund Hollis had agreed not to know.

Because that was the thing she had finally understood tonight, holding the letter that named her and only her, reading between its courteous lines the shape of a debt: her master had not simply been discreet. He had been *paid*. Paid in quarterly banknotes, paid in the comfortable years, paid — she suspected, with a coldness in her stomach that felt like the first frost — for a silence so complete that he had built his entire character around maintaining it. The gentleness was real. The kindness was real. She would testify to both before any court in the county. But kindness had been the finish applied over the joinery, and somewhere under the veneer of that good man was a joint that had been glued shut with somebody's inheritance.

Or — no. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Or it was worse than that, and simpler, and more human: maybe he had been paid to stay silent about something he believed was wrong, and had stayed silent anyway, because the payment kept the shop alive and kept a fatherless girl fed and apprenticed, and he had spent ten years doing small arithmetic with that bargain the way he taught her to do arithmetic with clocks — *some debts you ignore, not because they are not owed.* Maybe she had been the debt he decided was worth ignoring everything else for.

That thought was unbearable, so she did what he had trained her to do with unbearable thoughts. She gave it a place in the train, and she let it turn.

* * *

Gratitude and grief braided together make a strange cordage. Strong, but it pulls in two directions at once, and by the small hours she was tired of being pulled.

She got up and walked the shop, touching things. His loupe, still hanging on its nail, the leather strap worn glossy by his neck. The slate with the last week's queue still chalked on it, half-erased where he had wiped his sleeve across it on the morning he died. The drawer of odd movements, orphaned watches waiting for parts that might never come, each one tagged in his neat hand with the customer's name and the phrase he always used, *patience required*, as though patience were a component he stocked.

He had given her everything, she thought. Trade, roof, name — the town called her Winterbourne now and mostly forgot it had been anything else. He had given her a way to be useful in the world, which is the only gift that keeps giving after it's opened. And he had given her the silences too, packed in among the lessons like ballast in a hull, and she had carried them ten years without knowing their weight because she'd never tried to set them down.

Tomorrow she would carry his teachings up the hill in a pine box, and the letter in her pocket said the observatory's master clock was failing, and said it in language too careful to be innocent — *a matter of some delicacy, best entrusted to one pair of hands* — and every rule he ever taught her told her that a commission like that was not a commission. It was a continuation. Whatever bargain Edmund Hollis had struck, someone intended it to pass to her like a tool passed handle-first, and the workshop would live or die depending on whether she gripped it.

She could refuse. She had rehearsed the refusal on the walk home: write back, decline, sell the shop honestly, keep her head down the way he'd taught her, live out her life in the channel he'd carved for her. Safe. Silent. The word *silent* snagged in her mind like a broken tooth, and she worried at it, and understood that the snags were the point. Ten years of silences had kept her safe the way the ice keeps the river safe — perfectly, and only until spring.

The regulator struck three. She counted the strokes out of habit, and heard, under them, the smaller sounds the shop made at night — the settling wood, the wind testing the shutters, the faint metallic breathing of forty mechanisms all keeping their different times in the dark. Her master's voice came back to her unbidden, from some ordinary afternoon, apropos of nothing: *"A clock doesn't hide what it is, girl. Every wheel shows. That's why men trust them and don't trust each other."*

Had he meant it as confession? Had he been trying, all along, in the only language he trusted, to show her every wheel?

She blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark the letter felt warm against her ribs, and the pine box stood by the door like a patient animal, and tomorrow was already leaning against the present moment, pressing.

One pair of hands, the letter said. Hers.

She slept two hours in the chair by the cold hearth, and dreamed of ice groaning on the river, and woke before dawn with the odd certainty — unreasonable, unwinnable, already true — that before the week was out she would find something on that hill that no rule of his had prepared her for.

She packed the tools. She latched the shop. And as she stepped out into the blue early cold with the box in her arms, the regulator behind her, faithful to the last, ticked on in the empty room — keeping honest time, keeping honest time, keeping honest time — over a silence she was finally, at long last, climbing uphill to break.
