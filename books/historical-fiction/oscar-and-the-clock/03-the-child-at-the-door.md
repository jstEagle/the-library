## 3. The Child at the Door

The observatory had a smell Lior decided she could live inside: cold stone, lamp oil, and beneath it all the faint iron sweetness of old clockwork, like blood that had learned to keep time. She spent her first afternoon cataloguing it the way her master had taught her to catalogue a mechanism — part by part, without flinching, so that nothing could surprise you later.

The master clock stood in the round chamber beneath the dome, a black walnut case eleven feet tall, its brass face gone green at the edges where a decade of breath and damp had worked into the metal. The pendulum hung dead. The weights sat slack on their cables. Ten years of silence, and the silence had a texture — thick, felted, the kind of quiet that presses on the ears.

She lit three lamps, set her tools out on a wool blanket in the exact order she'd used them since she was twelve — files left, pliers center, the fine screwdrivers in their rolled leather house right of the lamp — and began with what every clockmaker begins with: looking.

* * *

By dusk she had mapped the failure well enough to know it would take weeks, not days. The going train was seized with corrosion; the escapement was the real wound, though, and she hadn't yet opened that throat of the machine. She wrote in her log by lamplight:

*Case sound. Dial sound. Train fouled. Escapement not yet inspected. Note: whoever maintained this last did so with hatred or ignorance. Possibly both.*

She allowed herself one small vanity before bed: she ate the supper she'd carried up from town — bread, hard cheese, an apple going soft — sitting on the observatory steps with her coat around her shoulders, watching the river. The ice had begun to talk in its sleep. All winter it had lain there white and dumb as a closed ledger, but now, in the last week of February, it muttered along its edges, long groaning sighs that traveled through the frozen ground and up through the stone steps and into the soles of her feet. *Soon,* the river said. *Soon I'll move.*

Lior knew what moved when the river moved. Papers. Ferries. Legal terms. Creditors' clerks with their ink-stained cuffs. Every day the ice held was a day the workshop stayed hers to protect; every crack was the hourglass running.

She went back inside and barred the door, because the commission letter had said *you alone*, and *alone* meant she slept in a building full of brass and silver instruments in a town that knew exactly why the workshop needed saving. She took the cot in the keeper's room off the instrument gallery, put her father's knife under the pillow out of habit rather than expectation, and lay listening to the building settle.

That was when she heard the knocking.

Not knocking, exactly. A scraping, irregular, low down — the sound a small fist makes when the arm making it is already tired of trying.

* * *

She told herself it was a branch. She told herself it was the ice, transmitting itself through the hill. She lay in the dark for the length of forty ticks of her pocket watch — she counted, because counting was what her hands did instead of praying — and then the sound came again, and with it a voice, thin as wire:

"Please. It's very cold."

There are moments in a life when you can feel your own character being weighed, and this was one of them, and Lior knew it even as she resented it. Because the safe thing — the self-preserving thing, the thing ten years of her master's rules had been built to teach her — was to stay on the cot. To let the dark be dark. Whoever was on those steps at nine o'clock at night in late February had been sent by someone, and people who sent children to doors at night were people with purposes, and purposes were questions, and questions were the one currency her master had taught her never to carry.

But the voice had said *please* the way children say it — without any belief that please will work, saying it only because an adult once instructed them that it was required.

Lior got up. She took the knife from under the pillow and put it in her pocket anyway, because character and stupidity were different things. She unbarred the door.

The girl was six years old, perhaps, small for six, wearing a man's greatcoat buttoned wrong so that it hung off one shoulder like a shed skin. Her hair was dark and cut badly, hacked short with shears, someone's hasty mercy against lice or pursuit. She had a bruise blooming yellow-green along her jaw, old enough to be fading. And she was holding, in both mittened hands, a sealed letter pressed flat against her chest the way other children hold a doll.

Behind her, down the hill, the road was empty. No carriage. No lantern retreating. Nothing but the frost smoking in the light of the doorway lamp, and far below, the river groaning.

"Were you followed?" Lior asked. It was not the question a kind person asks first. She heard that herself, heard how her master's training came out of her mouth before her heart could edit it.

The girl shook her head, then stopped, considering, and said, "I don't know. The driver said run up the steps and don't look back, and I didn't look back, but I don't know if that means no."

Something in Lior's chest turned over — some gear whose existence she had not previously suspected. She crouched, which brought them nearly level, and held out her hand.

"The letter," she said. "Is that for me?"

The girl looked down at it as if seeing it for the first time. Then she pressed it into Lior's palm and said, with the gravity of a child reciting something she'd been made to memorize until it wore a groove in her:

"He said to tell you: forget the face. Finish the work."

* * *

Inside, Lior lit the lamp in the keeper's room and broke the seal with her thumbnail. The paper was good stock, expensive, unmarked by any name. One line, in a hand she did not recognize, written fast, the ink blotted where the writer had hurried:

*Forget the face. Finish the work. Trust no one who comes asking about papers. She is not yours to keep — only to keep alive.*

She read it four times. Then she burned it in the stove and watched the ash curl, because that was rule two of her master's rules: *paper is testimony, and testimony hangs people.*

Then she looked at the child.

The girl had not cried. Lior noticed that the way she noticed a flaw in a wheel — professionally, with a rising respect. The child stood where she'd been placed, beside the stove, and watched Lior with eyes that were doing arithmetic: measuring the room, the exits, the size of the woman, deciding something. Children who had been handled gently all their lives did not do that arithmetic. Lior had done that arithmetic herself, once, standing in a doorway at fourteen with a single carpet bag and a dead father's debt.

"What's your name?" she asked.

A pause. Long enough that Lior saw the answer arrive and be swallowed.

"Oscar," the girl said.

"That's a boy's name."

"It's the clock's name," the girl said, and pointed through the wall, toward the chamber below, with the certainty of a child pointing at a thing everyone else has forgotten is obvious. "Grandfather called it Oscar. He talked to it. He said it was the only one in the house that never lied."

Lior kept her face still. Inside, something was falling — slowly, the way the weights fall in a tall case, steadily, with terrible patience. *Grandfather.* The founder had been dead eight years. This child spoke of him the way you speak of someone you ate breakfast with.

"Are you hungry?"

"Yes," said Oscar, with equal honesty.

Lior fed her bread and cheese and the soft apple cut into slices, and watched the child eat the way her master had taught her to watch a movement run after repair — not staring, just attending, catching the small irregularities. The girl ate neatly. Too neatly. Hungry children who have learned to eat neatly are children who have been punished for the alternative.

"You'll sleep here," Lior said. "On the cot. I'll take the chair."

"I can sleep on the floor."

"You'll sleep on the cot."

Oscar considered this negotiation, found it concluded in her favor, and climbed onto the cot still wearing the greatcoat. Within four minutes she was asleep — dropped straight down, the way children fall when exhaustion finally outbids fear — and Lior sat in the keeper's chair with her knife in her lap and her thoughts moving like the river ice.

Someone had dressed this child in a man's coat, cut her hair with shears, coached her on one sentence, and delivered her to the one address in Vellamarch where a clockmaker was known to be sleeping alone. Someone knew the commission letter had named Lior Winterbourne and no one else. Someone knew enough about the observatory's affairs to send a child who called the founder *grandfather*, and knew enough about Lior to believe she would not turn the child back out into the frost.

Which meant someone knew her better than she had believed herself known. That was the thought that kept her awake. Not the danger — danger she had lived with for ten years, worn close as a thimble. The *being known*. Her master had spent a decade teaching her to be unreadable, and within one week of his death, strangers were writing her letters in hands she didn't recognize, delivering children to her door with instructions in their mouths, as though she were not a person at all but a compartment in some larger machine, waiting to be loaded.

And yet.

And yet the child had said *the only one in the house that never lied*, and Lior had felt that land somewhere below argument.

She sat until the lamps burned low, listening to the small ragged breathing from the cot and the vast slow breathing of the ice on the river, two sounds of unequal scale keeping, between them, an accidental time. Then she banked the stove, and in the grey hour before dawn she finally slept in the chair, her hand still in her pocket, closed around the handle of her father's knife, having already understood — without admitting it, the way a clockmaker understands a fault before she will write it in the log — that whatever the note said about the girl not being hers to keep, she was going to keep her anyway. Some things arrive at your door too cold to turn away, and no rule ever written by any master survives them.

She woke at first light to find Oscar standing at the top of the stair to the clock chamber, watching the dead machine with an expression Lior recognized from mirrors: the look of someone greeting a sick friend.

"Can you fix him?" the girl asked.

"I'm going to open his escapement today," Lior said. "We'll see what he's been hiding."

It was only later, tools laid out, the case panels coming away under her careful hands, that she remembered the note's warning — *trust no one who comes asking about papers* — and wondered, with the first true chill of the season, why a stranger's letter should fear papers she had never gone looking for.
