## 4. The Note in the Case

The girl slept in the astronomer's chair.

It was a great leather thing on iron casters, built for a man who spent his nights bent over eyepieces, and she had dragged it to the corner nearest the stove and curled into it like a cat claiming a hearth that had never belonged to anyone. She had refused the cot in the caretaker's room. She had refused the blanket Lior offered twice. But when Lior finally draped it over her anyway — pretending, both of them pretending, that this was not what it was — the girl had let it stay, and had watched her with those grave dark eyes until sleep took her mid-watch, her chin dropping onto her chest, one hand still closed around the stub of biscuit she'd been saving.

Lior stood a long while looking at her.

*Forget the face, finish the work.*

She turned away before the thought could finish forming — the thought that had been forming since the steps, since the note, since she'd carried the child inside out of an instinct she hadn't known she possessed: *no face is ever forgotten by accident.* Her master had taught her that much about mechanisms, if nothing else. A part left in a machine is left there for a reason. Someone wanted it found, or someone wanted it hidden, or someone died too soon to do either. Nothing simply *stayed*.

The stove ticked as its iron cooled. Above her, through the open dome slit, the February stars wheeled in their slow indifferent order, and below them all hung the master clock.

She had been avoiding it.

Three days she had worked around the great case rather than in it — cleaning the exterior, oiling the door hinges, cataloguing tools, establishing herself in the observatory's good graces with small visible labors while her mind circled the mechanism the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. It was not fear exactly. Or it was, but not of gears. The master clock was the largest machine she had ever been asked to serve alone, without her master standing at her shoulder saying *now* or *not yet* or *you'll strip the thread, girl, patience*. Ten years of apprenticeship and still, at the threshold of every important work, she heard his voice — and these days the hearing of it was a kind of grief that sat in the chest like a swallowed stone.

And beneath that, quieter, colder: the letter naming her. Not the workshop. Not Master Vell's name posthumously invoked, not the guild, not some senior journeyman. *Lior Winterbourne, alone.* She had read that word *alone* forty times since Vellamarch, and each reading it looked less like honor and more like a finger pointed.

But the commission would not wait, and neither would the creditors' clerk, whose fortnightly letters arrived smelling faintly of ink and satisfaction. If the observatory clock ran true by spring, the founder's bequest held, and the bequest held, the workshop breathed another year. That was the bargain she had made in the dark of her own head: go alone, work well, ask nothing, return.

So on the third night, with the child asleep and the stove down to embers, Lior lit two lamps, set her tools out on the canvas in the order her hands knew without consulting her, and opened the master clock.

***

The case was walnut, older than the town's church, and it opened in three places. The lower doors gave onto the weight train — two great iron masses hanging silent in their shaft, waiting for the winding crank. The waist panel concealed the pendulum's crutch and the suspension. And the hood above swung forward on hidden pivots to expose the movement itself: the going train, the striking train, the escapement, the whole bronze-and-steel heart of the thing, tall as a man's arm is long, every wheel cut before she was born.

Dust lay over everything in a grey felt, thick as snowfall on a branch. Somewhere in that dust, ten years deep, the failure waited.

She began where her master would have begun: at the top, with light and eyes before hands. She lifted each wheel with a fingertip under the arbor and felt for play. She checked the pallets. She examined the suspension spring with a loupe, hunting hairline fractures. Hours went by the way hours do in good work — uncounted, unmissed. The stove settled. The child murmured once in her sleep and was still. And Lior forgot, entirely and blessedly, to be afraid.

It was near dawn, working down through the striking train, that she found the false plate.

A brass plate should have covered the count-wheel hub — a plain disc, screwed fast, decorative in the way clockmakers make things decorative so that future strangers will leave them alone. This plate was there, screwed fast, plain. But the screw heads were bright. Everything else in the housing wore its decade of dust; these four screws had been turned within living memory. Within recent memory. She touched one with a gloved fingertip and felt her pulse move up into her throat.

*Nothing stays by accident.*

She fetched her screwdriver and stood a moment with it in her hand, listening. The building ticked and settled around her, full of its own small sounds — cooling iron, shifting stone, wind worrying the dome shutter. In the corner the girl breathed slow and even. No footsteps on the stair, no lantern-glow under the door.

She turned the screws.

The plate came away lighter than it should have. That was her first wrongness — a brass plate that size has honest heft, and this one came off the threads like a leaf off a stem. Her second wrongness followed at once: behind it there should have been the count-wheel, teeth and hub, and instead the space was shallow. A box. A false back, soldered neat as a lie, lined in green baize gone brown with age — and lying flat upon the baize, folded twice with its wax seal broken long ago, a document.

Paper, in a clock case. Every apprentice learns what paper does to a movement. It sheds fibers into the oil. It drinks damp and swells and binds the works. No clockmaker puts paper inside a clock unless the paper matters more than the clock — unless the clock exists, in part, to keep the paper.

Her hands were very steady. She noticed that about them, distantly, the way you notice weather in a window.

She lifted the document out and carried it, not unfolded, to the lamp.

The seal was black wax, cracked across its old fold, stamped with a device she did not know — a starburst, or a wheel with many spokes. The paper was good rag stock, heavy, the kind bought by men who expect what they write to outlive them. She turned it over. Turned it back. Then made herself unfold it, crease by crease, as though opening something that might strike.

It was a will. Or the beginning of one.

*I, Edmund Halloway, founder of this observatory, being of sound mind and failing body—*

She knew the name. Every soul in Vellamarch knew the name; the founder had been dead eleven years, and his marble likeness presided over the market square with a sextant under its arm, gazing eternally at a sky he no longer troubled. His death was why the clock had failed — no. His death was when the clock had *stopped*, which was not necessarily the same thing, and Lior filed that distinction away with the care of a woman who had learned from a great teacher that in clocks, as in towns, the visible cause is rarely the true one.

The hand was firm but tired, the letters of an educated man written quickly, and it proceeded in the way wills proceed — legacies itemized, instruments described, sums assigned — until it reached the passage that stopped her breath.

*—and to my heir I leave the whole of my estate entire: the observatory with its lands, its instruments, and its endowment, together with all rents and revenues issuing therefrom, to hold absolutely and without condition, my said heir being—*

There it ended. Mid-word, almost — the pen had been set down between *being* and the name, and had never been taken up again. Below that final stroke, blankness. Below the blankness, at the foot of the page, a line for signature, unsigned. A place for a date, undated.

An unfinished will, naming an heir who was never named.

She read it four times. The lamp burned steady. The stars moved. Somewhere below in the frozen town a dog barked itself out against nothing. And Lior Winterbourne stood in the observatory of Edmund Halloway holding the man's last intention in her ink-stained fingers, and understood several things in the order they chose to arrive.

First: this was why the clock mattered. Not timekeeping — the assizes and the creditors cared nothing for whether noon struck true. The founder's bequest, the endowment that kept the observatory alive and kept the workshop breathing by extension, was tied to this institution, and this instrument, in ways she did not yet understand. Whoever wrote the letter summoning her had not merely needed a repair.

Second: someone had hidden this here deliberately. The false plate was craftsman's work, and the screws were recently turned — turned, perhaps, by whoever had arranged for the summons. Perhaps by her own dead master, in some visit she had never known of. The thought arrived with a strange doubling, grief and suspicion wearing one another's faces.

Third — and this came last, and coldly, like water finding the crack in a floor — *heir*. An heir no one in Vellamarch had ever heard named. The founder's estate had been in dispute since his death; she knew the shape of it the way the whole town knew, in the way of things known and not discussed: a nephew, Mr. Aldery, who administered the estate's debts with great diligence and greater profit; a magistrate who saw no reason to hasten any resolution; a bequest hanging unresolved year upon year like a sword everyone had grown used to walking beneath. If there were another heir — a lawful one, named in the founder's own hand —

Then the nephew was not administrator. He was interloper. And everything done in those eleven years in the estate's name had been done by a man with no right to do it.

She folded the will along its old creases, precisely, the way she would close the back of a watch.

***

She did not hide it at once. That surprised her later — how long she stood there, document in hand, feeling the full weight of the choice arrange itself around her like the teeth of a wheel coming into mesh.

Because there were only two things to do with such a paper, and she knew them both already, and one of them was nothing.

Give it up. Carry it to the magistrate, to the assizes clerk, to the guild hall — put it in daylight where papers like it lived, and let the law do what the law was for. And then? Then the summons would be traced. Then questions would come to the workshop, to Widow Kessel, to the dead man's bench and the dead man's books, and the creditors — patient as rust — would descend on whatever the questions disturbed. Then Mr. Aldery, whoever and whatever he truly was, would learn that a clockmaker's apprentice had found what he had presumably spent eleven years ensuring stayed unfound. She thought of the bright screw heads. Someone had opened that plate recently. Someone might come back.

Or: nothing. Fold it, replace it, screw the plate home, finish the repair, take the fee, go home, and live. Forget the face. Finish the work. The words of the sealed note rose in her memory with the smoothness of something rehearsed, and for the first time she heard their shape clearly — not an instruction to ignore a child, but a philosophy. A rule. The same rule her master had taught her for ten years in a hundred gentler forms. *Which debts to ignore. Which questions not to ask. Which customers to serve last.* Silence as craft. Silence as survival. Silence as the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing, and her standing safe inside it.

She looked at the sleeping child.

The girl had slid sideways in the great chair in the night, blanket tangled, one bare foot hooked over the armrest, mouth open, utterly surrendered to sleep in the way only children and the just are. On the table beside her lay the remains of the biscuit, saved still, guarded even in dreams. She had eaten half the first night and hidden the rest, and when Lior had pretended not to see, the girl had known she was pretending, and something had passed between them that had no words and needed none: *I know how scarcity teaches. So do you.*

An heir. Six years old, abandoned on the steps with a note, and somewhere in the founder's unfinished sentence, in the space between *being* and the name that never came, there was a place that might be hers.

Or might not. Lior pressed hard against the thought, testing it as she tested metal, for flaws. She was a clockmaker's apprentice, not a magistrate. Children were abandoned in river towns every winter; grief and poverty did it daily. A note and a coincidence proved nothing. Wanting it to prove something — wanting the child to matter, wanting the foundling to be a key — that was not evidence. Her master had beaten sentimentality out of her work if out of nothing else. *The gear doesn't care what you hope, girl. Cut it true or don't touch it.*

But the paper was true. The paper was the truest thing in the room.

And silence — she felt it then, the exact moment, the way one feels a pallet stone seat itself — silence was no longer the thing that had kept her safe. Standing there with the founder's unfinished will in her hand, she understood that the silence she had kept for ten years had not been only hers. It had been purchased, maintained, handed down to her like the tools, like the trade, like the fear itself. And every day she kept it now, knowing what she knew, it would stop being protection and start being payment — her contribution, rendered willingly or not, to keeping the unnamed heir unnamed.

Complicity. The word arrived without drama, flat and technical, like a measurement. She had expected shame to feel like fire. It felt like arithmetic.

*You are now part of the mechanism,* she thought. *Choose what kind of part.*

***

She hid it well, because anything worth doing is worth doing as her master would have inspected it.

Not back in the clock — the clock was the first place any searcher would look, now that she knew to look at all. Instead she took the will down to the workshop corner she'd established among the crates of the caretaker's stores, and she did with it what clockmakers do with everything valuable: she gave it a job. Into a broken longcase movement awaiting salvage she fitted a false canister, soldered from sheet brass scrap, sized to pass for a weight shell. The will, wrapped in oilcloth with a desiccant packet of charcoal, went inside, and the canister went into the weight shaft among nine genuine shells, identical to the casual eye, one of ten. Anyone searching would find old brass and older dust. Only a clockmaker counting shells, weighing each in the hand, would find the difference — and only a clockmaker would think to.

She finished at gray dawn, hands grimy, mind running ahead of her the whole time like a train with the governor off.

Because the questions were multiplying, and each one had teeth. Who had summoned her — truly? Who had turned those bright screws? Was her master's death last autumn the quiet ending it had seemed, or had the quiet been arranged? What had he known, and when, and what exactly had Widow Kessel's tight mouth been holding shut these eleven years? And the child — the child whose arrival had been timed, surely, to the summons, delivered to the one person in Vellamarch with the skill to find what the clock held and the obscurity to be blamed for it —

*Forget the face.*

No. She was done being instructed by notes. Whatever game moved pieces across this town, she would play it with her eyes open or not at all.

Behind her, small feet on stone. She turned. The girl stood in the doorway wrapped in the blanket like a shawl, hair wild, watching her with that unnervingly adult gravity — watching the soldering iron still warm on its stand, the scattered brass, the longcase gutted open, reading the room the way hungry children read rooms.

"Breakfast?" Lior said.

The girl considered her. Considered the room. Then nodded once, a small precise nod, and came to sit on the crate nearest the stove, and Lior went to heat water and bread and tried not to notice that she had already begun arranging her day around the child — that the arrangement had happened somewhere below thought, the way a pendulum finds its beat.

It was while the kettle muttered that the knock came at the observatory's outer door. Three knocks, unhurried, confident. The knock of a man who has never in his life waited to see whether he is welcome.

Through the frost-furred pane, in the blue early light, she could make out a figure below: broad, well-coated, hatted, attended by a second thinner shape at his shoulder. Not the caretaker. Not the guild. Not anyone who climbed this hill before noon without purpose.

The girl went still beside the stove, and her stillness had a quality Lior recognized from her own childhood — the stillness of someone who has learned that certain visitors are weather to be waited out.

Lior wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, buying three seconds of thought, and found in those three seconds that her heart was behaving strangely: not afraid, exactly. Braced. Like a movement with a new escapement in it, waiting for the first tooth to drop.

She went to answer the door.
