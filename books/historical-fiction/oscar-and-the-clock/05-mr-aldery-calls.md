## 5. Mr. Aldery Calls

He came on the third morning, and he came early — earlier than any honest man in Vellamarch had business being awake.

Lior heard the horse before she saw it: a slow, deliberate clop up the observatory road, the sound of someone who wanted to be heard coming. She was at the bench by then, sleeves rolled, a lamp still burning against the grey light, because she had not slept. The girl had not slept either. The child had cried herself quiet sometime past two o'clock, hiccupping down into a sleep so shallow that Lior could hear her breathing change whenever Lior's file touched metal.

Now the breathing stopped. The girl was awake. And there was a knock at the door.

Not a knock — a tap. Two knuckles, twice, polite as an invitation card. Men who pounded were men who wanted something from you. Men who tapped believed you would already want to open.

Lior set down her file. She looked at the little bed behind the partition curtain — a pallet of blankets she'd dragged from the keeper's old quarters, arranged with no skill at all — and then at the narrow cupboard beside it, deep enough for brooms and lamp oil and, apparently, for a six-year-old who understood, with the terrible instinct of children raised among secrets, that when footsteps came she was supposed to disappear.

The girl was already moving. That frightened Lior more than the knock did. The child folded herself into the cupboard without being told, pulling the blanket after her, leaving a gap of perhaps a finger's width through which one dark eye gleamed.

*Someone has taught you this*, Lior thought. *Someone taught you to hide before you taught you your letters.*

She smoothed her apron, checked that her hands were clean, and opened the door.

---

Mr. Aldery was not what his reputation promised. Vellamarch spoke of him as a man made of iron filings and ledger ink, and Lior had imagined someone heavy, loud, dressed in the aggressive black of a creditor at a funeral. Instead he was slight, silvering at the temples, wearing a coat of good grey wool cut close to the body, and his face was the kind of face that made you feel you had met before — at church, perhaps, or across a shop counter, somewhere pleasant and unremarkable. His eyes were pale brown and very patient. He smiled the way a well-oiled mechanism moved: smoothly, without heat, without friction, and entirely under control.

"Miss Winterbourne." He said her name correctly, including the *Miss*, which meant he had asked about her. "I hope I'm not too early. I find the observatory is best seen in morning light. Everything shows itself honestly then."

"Mr. Aldery." She stepped back to admit him. "You're welcome, though the commission came from the estate's trustees, not—"

"Not from me, no." He walked in as if the room remembered him, which perhaps it did; the founder had been his uncle, and boys grow up in their uncles' houses. He removed his gloves finger by finger, unhurried. "Though I confess an interest. My uncle built this place. It grieves me to hear the great clock failed on his watch — and stranger still, on mine, the same season the estate comes to term. Coincidences make me curious. Curiosity is my only vice, Miss Winterbourne. I indulge it thoroughly."

There was nowhere to put him, so she didn't try. He drifted where he pleased, along the wall of the great stair, up toward the clock chamber with its arched windows going from grey to pewter to the first thin gold of morning. Lior followed half a step behind, the way an apprentice follows — she caught herself doing it and hated it. Ten years out of apprenticeship and her body still knew how to be small in a room with money.

The clock towered over them both, its case dark oak gone nearly black with age, its brass pendulum hanging dead. Silence lay over the whole building like snowfall. A master clock is never truly silent even when stopped — it holds its breath — but this one had been stopped so long the silence had become architecture.

"A magnificent thing," Aldery said softly. He laid one gloved hand flat against the case, almost tenderly. "My uncle loved it more than any living creature. More than my aunt. Certainly more than—" He stopped himself with a small laugh, self-deprecating, charming. "Forgive me. Dead men and family grievances. You'll have heard gossip in the town. Pay none of it attention."

"I don't listen to gossip," Lior said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You listen to clocks."

It was said lightly, and it was not said lightly at all. She felt the words go in like a probe finding the seam between plates of armor.

---

He inspected her work the way he did everything: courteously, completely.

He asked to see the movement drawings and she gave him the ones that were safe — the wheel trains, the strike train, her notes on wear in the third wheel's teeth. He read them standing, holding each sheet at arm's length, and nodded at intervals like a man following music.

"You were Ambrose Fenn's last apprentice." Not a question. "Ten years in his workshop. He taught you everything, they say. The escapements especially."

"He taught me the trade."

"And what else do tradesmen teach?" Aldery set down the drawing and turned to her with genuine, terrible interest. "My experience, Miss Winterbourne, is that every master teaches three curricula. There's the trade, which is written. There's the craft, which is shown. And there's the business, which is learned by watching what the master does with his ledgers closed." He smiled. "Which did he teach you best?"

"The escapements," Lior said.

For the first time something flickered behind the patience — not anger; disappointment, maybe, the way a man is disappointed by a lock that won't pick. Then it was gone, filed away, and he moved on.

He examined the weights. He examined the crutches and the suspension. He stood beneath the dead pendulum for a long moment, hands clasped behind his back, looking up the shaft toward the bell chamber as if listening for a sound ten years old.

"They tell me it struck wrong the night my uncle died," he said conversationally. "Struck thirteen, some say. Struck and kept striking. The whole town heard it. And then it never spoke again." He looked at her. "Do you believe a machine can grieve, Miss Winterbourne?"

"I believe a machine can be damaged."

"Yes. Yes, of course. Damaged." He tasted the word. "Or maintained into damage. There are ways to let a clock die that look exactly like neglect. Worn parts left unworn-out. Adjustments made slightly wrong. A good clockmaker can murder a movement so gently that the coroner calls it old age." He turned from the pendulum and regarded her with those patient eyes. "But you wouldn't know anything about that. Your master's work was always honest. Everyone says so."

*Everyone says so.* Twice now. Lior understood that she was being shown the shape of a trap without yet seeing its jaws: whatever everyone said about Ambrose Fenn, Mr. Aldery intended to make it true or make it damning, whichever served.

"My master has been dead four years," she said. "Whatever he did or didn't do, I can only speak to the state of the mechanism as I find it. Which is worn. Badly, in places. But repairable."

"Repairable." He said it like a price. "And the papers?"

---

There it was. He asked it the way he'd asked about the weather, sliding it into the pause between two remarks, so casually that a careless person might have answered before realizing an answer had been requested.

"Papers?" Lior kept her voice flat and busy, turning toward the bench as if the question were beneath her full attention.

"The commission documents. The trustees' warrant. My uncle's specifications, if he left any — he drew everything, you know, he trusted nothing to memory. And the maintenance records, of course." Aldery wandered toward the bench, trailing a gloved finger along its edge, examining the dust he collected. "An instrument of public time keeps a public history. Charters, registers, certificates of inspection. When the estate is settled, all of it will need to come together in one place. For the lawyers." He glanced at her sideways, mild as milk. "One place, Miss Winterbourne. Not scattered about in drawers. Papers have a way of becoming complicated when they're scattered."

"There are the trustees' letters," Lior said. "You're welcome to read them. The rest is in the town archive, where such things belong."

"Mm." He picked up a scrap of paper from the bench — a blank offcut, nothing — turned it over, set it down. "And in the case? Clockmakers hide things in cases. It's a tradition older than most guilds. Love letters, debts, spare keys." He laughed. "Once, as a boy, I found a will hidden in my uncle's tall-case clock in the hall. A real will! My aunt's first husband's, folded up behind the chime barrel. My uncle burned it, of course — it was superseded — but I remember thinking what a strange, secret heart a clock has. All those wheels and springs, and inside, a paper whisper."

Lior's hands, sorting tools that needed no sorting, did not stop moving. She was proud of them. Her mouth said, dryly: "Then you know better than anyone that what's inside a clock belongs to the clockmaker who opens it."

Something changed in the temperature of the room.

Aldery set down the blank paper with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the bench edge, and when he straightened, the charm was still there but it had thinned enough to see through, the way ice thins over running water — same surface, different color underneath.

"You mistake me," he said quietly. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm offering you a future. This commission ends when the clock runs or when the estate settles, whichever comes first — and the estate settles at the assizes, when the ferry runs. After that, the workshop on Candle Lane goes to the creditors. Your master's creditors, Miss Winterbourne, of whom I am the principal. I hold his paper. Four years of interest. The widow cannot pay; we both know it." He said it without cruelty, which was the worst part — the way one reports weather, or the price of coal. "Unless, of course, the estate's new obligations are discharged generously. Estates with clear titles settle quickly. Estates with complications settle slowly, and slowly is expensive, and expense falls on whoever owns the paper."

He let that sit. Outside, the river ice groaned once, long and low, like something enormous shifting in its sleep, and both of them paused to hear it.

"Bring me the clock's papers," Aldery said, "and I will show the trustees the commission is in good order, and I will look kindly on the Candle Lane debt. You keep your master's benches. The widow keeps her roof. A modest outcome, orderly, everyone content. Or—" he took up his gloves, working his fingers into them one at a time, unhurried as ever, "—you keep whatever it is you imagine you're keeping, and I discover it anyway, later, less conveniently, and Candle Lane goes under the hammer in April. I am told the widow Kessel has cousins near Harwich who would take her in. Cold country, the coast. Damp. Old women don't last in damp."

"You've thought about this," Lior said. Her voice sounded far away to herself, steady, someone else's voice.

"I think about everything. It's cheaper than regretting." At the door he paused, and turned back, and the smile returned full strength, warm, almost kind. "You have your master's hands, they say. Careful hands. Honest hands." He tapped the doorframe twice with one gloved knuckle — the same polite double-tap he'd used to knock. "See that they stay honest, Miss Winterbourne. Vellamarch remembers its craftsmen fondly. It forgets them entirely."

The door closed. Boots on gravel. Hooves on frost. Gone.

---

Lior stood in the middle of the clock chamber until the horse's sound faded entirely, and then some while longer, because her legs had opinions about holding her weight and she preferred not to consult them.

She had lied. She catalogued it, appalled at how easily the machinery had run: *there are the trustees' letters* — true, and irrelevant; *the rest is in the town archive* — false, and she had said it while looking him in the eye, in the flat tone her master had taught her for quoting prices to customers who haggled. Ten years she had kept silence by saying nothing. This morning she had kept silence by saying something, and the second skill sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

From the cupboard, a small sound. Not a cry. A sneeze — tiny, stifled, catastrophic with dust.

Lior crossed the room and opened the cupboard door.

The girl blinked up at her from the dark, blanket-wrapped, one cheek printed with the weave of the oilcloth lining, her eyes red-rimmed and ferociously dry. Children who cried in front of strangers got found out; this one had learned the arithmetic already.

"He's gone," Lior said.

The girl nodded. Then, in a voice sanded down to a whisper: "He talked to you like the man in the carriage."

Lior went cold from the feet up. "What man in the carriage?"

But the child's face closed like a shutter, and she shook her head, and pressed her small fist hard against her mouth, and Lior understood that she had just watched a six-year-old decide, consciously, deliberately, that some knowledge was safer unspoken — the oldest lesson in Vellamarch, taught young here, taught well.

"It's all right," Lior said, and knelt, and hated that she didn't fully mean it, because nothing was all right and the lying had started and the stone in her chest was getting heavier. "No one will talk to you like that. Not while you're mine to mind."

The girl considered this offer with grave, unsleeping eyes. Then she reached into the cupboard's corner and brought out something she had been holding in the dark all this time, through the knocking and the questions: a splinter of wood she'd pried from the crate she'd arrived on, whittled raggedly into the shape of a wheel. Six spokes. Lopsided. A cog made by someone who had watched cogs her whole short life without ever being given one.

"For the clock," the girl whispered. "So it can start again."

Lior took it in her palm. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. Somewhere below them the river groaned again, longer this time, and out on the ice the first hairline cracks were writing their slow blue sentences toward spring — toward the ferry, toward the assizes, toward whatever Mr. Aldery kept folded in the secret heart of his own accounts.

She closed her hand around the little wooden wheel.

"Come and eat," she said. "Then we have work to do. Both of us."

Because she had lied to Aldery about papers that were not in the archive — and the papers that were, the ones folded in oilcloth at the bottom of her tool chest since the night the child arrived, had just become the most dangerous objects in Vellamarch.
