## 6. What the Girl Remembers

The tick was the first thing she loved.

Before she had a name for it — before she knew that the great dark room at the top of the tower had a name, or that the woman who worked in it had one either — there was the sound. Tick. And then, after a pause so long she sometimes thought it had stopped forever: tock. She lay on the cot behind the workbench and counted the pauses. One, two, three, four. Then tick again. It never hurried. It never forgot. Everything else in the world forgot.

Her mother had hummed like that once. Not ticking, of course. Mothers did not tick. But the same patience underneath, the same *I am still here, count on me, I will come again.* The woman's humming had gone up and down and up again, round and round, and if you held your ear against her ribs you could feel it as well as hear it, a song with a body inside it. The clock was like that too. If you pressed your palm flat against its tall wooden case, the tick came up through your bones, and it felt the way being carried felt.

She did not remember her mother's face. This frightened her more than anything, though she could not have said why. She remembered the humming perfectly, note for note, the little rise at the end where the song turned over like a page. She remembered that the humming stopped when the carriage wheels were loud, and started again when they were soft. But the face above the humming had gone the way of dreams you try to keep hold of past breakfast. There had been brown hair. She was nearly sure about the brown hair. After that, only the song.

The man in the carriage had said *not yet.*

She remembered this the way you remember a stone in your shoe: small, hard, always there when you walked. They had been going somewhere — somewhere with a door that opened onto cold air and a smell like iron and old paper — and she had asked, or tried to ask, *are we almost there,* because children ask that, and the man had looked down at her from under his hat brim and said, quietly, *not yet.* Not unkindly. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over at night like a coin she couldn't spend. He hadn't been cruel. His voice had been warm as bread. *Not yet,* he'd said, as if they were both waiting for something good, as if the whole world were a loaf still in the oven. And then the carriage had stopped, and the cold air had come in, and someone had set her down on steps made of stone, and the humming was not there anymore, and neither was the man, and the doors did not open for a very long time.

She had knocked. She remembered knocking. Little fists, because big ones were all she had. The stone steps were freezing through her shoes. Somewhere above her, muffled through walls as thick as her arm was long, something went *tick... tock,* slow and enormous, like a heart sleeping underground.

She had sat down to wait, because the man had said *not yet*, and she had understood — in the simple, absolute way children understand the words grown-ups give them — that waiting was the job. Waiting was what she was for. So she waited, and the sky went from grey to blue to orange to black, and her toes went from cold to numb to a burning sort of nothing, and the tick went on above her like a patient friend calling down through the floor: *still here. Still here. Still here.*

Then the door opened, and there was light, and a woman stood in it holding a lamp, and the woman's face was tired and sharp and afraid all at once, and the woman said something she didn't understand, and then the woman saw her properly, and the fear changed shape into something softer and sadder, and the woman crouched down and said, "Where is whoever brought you?"

And she had answered honestly, because no one had ever taught her to do otherwise: "He said not yet."

* * *

The days at the top of the tower arranged themselves around the tick the way beads arrange themselves on a string.

Morning: the woman — whose name was Lior, which was a funny name, a name like a bell rung once — boiled water on a small stove and gave her bread with honey if there was honey, and bread alone if there wasn't. Midday: the woman worked at the bench with tiny shining pieces, and the girl watched, and the woman let her watch, which surprised her, because in her remembering of Before, grown-ups mostly said *go along now* and *not yet* and *mind yourself.* Evening: the cot, and the blanket that smelled faintly of oil and metal and, underneath that, of somebody's long-ago life, and the tick, and sleep.

She learned things without meaning to. She learned that the brass wheel on the bench turned easiest leftward. She learned that the woman swore under her breath in another language when a spring slipped her pliers, and that the swear-words sounded like sneezes, and that the woman always looked ashamed afterward and gave her an extra crust of bread, as if the swearing cost money. She learned that the tower had rooms below rooms, and stairs that went down further than up, and one door at the bottom that was locked and that the woman touched once, briefly, with two fingers, the way you'd touch a scar.

But most of all she learned the clock.

It lived in the middle of everything, taller than two of her, a dark wooden giant with a white face and hands that had stopped — the woman told her this on the second day, in the flat voice people use for bad news — years ago. Ten years, the woman said. Stopped ten years. The girl did not know how long ten years was, except that it sounded like a punishment, and she decided privately that a clock that had been stopped ten years must have done something terrible, or had something terrible done to it, and that either way it deserved kindness.

So she talked to it.

"You're very big," she told it, standing with her nose an inch from the case. "Bigger than the horse-man outside the church." The horse-man outside the church was the largest thing she knew. The clock won easily.

The woman looked up from her bench with an expression the girl couldn't read — something between laughing and crying, folded shut. "What are you doing?"

"Keeping him company." Obviously. "He's been alone ten years."

The woman put down her pliers. For a moment she just looked, and the girl felt herself being looked at the way the woman looked at the small brass pieces — carefully, turning her over, checking for flaws. Then the woman said, "His name might be Oscar."

"Oscar," the girl repeated, trying it against the tick. It fit. Names should fit like that, she thought, like a lid on a box. She wondered if hers would ever be like that. She had a name — she was almost sure she did, she could feel the shape of it somewhere behind her teeth, a short name, a name with a hard beginning — but when she reached for it, it slid away, the way the face had slid away, and she was left holding only the feeling of having had one. Someone had called her by it every night before the humming. She could almost hear the tune of it, the two notes it rose through. Almost.

"Did you lose your name too?" she asked the woman, because it seemed polite to offer.

Something crossed the woman's face then, quick as a fish under ice. "No," the woman said. "Mine's been sitting in a drawer for ten years. Different problem entirely." And she went back to her work, and the girl understood that this was one of the conversations that closed like a door, and that there would be others, and that this was simply what grown-ups were.

* * *

At night the memories came, and she had learned not to fight them, because fighting them was like grabbing at minnows — you got splashed and empty-handed. Better to lie still and let them swim past and look at each one as it went.

A carriage. Blue, she thought, or green — dark anyway, dark as bottle glass, with a crest on the door she used to trace with one finger, a shape like a bird or a leaf. Wheels that went *clack-clack* over the joins in the road, regular as anything, and her counting them out of boredom until the counting turned into the humming turned into sleep.

A kitchen that smelled of oranges. A table with a cloth on it, white with red stitching, and a hand — not her mother's, bigger, older, knuckly — setting down a plate of sliced apple cut into shapes. Birds, maybe. Or leaves. The same shape as the crest. She hadn't known, then, that shapes could mean things. She'd just eaten the birds.

The man saying *not yet.* Warm voice. Hat brim. And once — this fragment was newer, sharper, it had edges — the man saying something else, low, to someone outside the frame of the memory, someone she couldn't see: *"—before the thaw, or none of this holds."* She didn't know what it meant. Words like *thaw* belonged to other people's lives. But she kept it anyway, the way you keep a key you find on a road, without knowing its lock.

And the humming. Always last, always longest, the way the tick came last before sleep. Her mother's voice rising and turning over, and the feeling of being carried, hip-boned and swaying, and the smell — this she would trade every other memory to keep — the smell of lavender and candle smoke and wool.

She never cried about them. Crying, she had discovered, made the fragments scatter like startled birds, and she could not afford to lose any. Instead she lay still and recited them in order, carriage, apples, not-yet, humming, the way the woman laid out her tools each morning in an exact row: pliers, files, screwdriver, oil. Order kept things safe. Order kept things *hers.* Nobody could take a memory you'd put away properly.

One night — she judged it night by the tick's loudness, which grew in the dark the way stars did — she heard the woman moving at the bench, though the lamp was out. Metal sounds. Small, precise ones. The woman was working blind, or near it, and the girl sat up in the cot and whispered into the dark:

"Are you fixing Oscar?"

A pause. Then the woman's voice, quiet, roughened: "Go back to sleep."

"I can't. My head's too loud." This was true. The fragments swam worst after sundown.

Another pause, longer. Then: "Come here, then. Quietly. Bring your blanket."

The girl crossed the cold floorboards and stood beside the bench, and the woman lifted her — effortlessly, absently, the way you pick up a tool you've picked up a thousand times — and set her on the high stool, and wrapped the blanket around them both, and went back to the small blind work. And the girl watched the woman's hands move in the dark, sure of themselves, unhurried, and listened to the tick overhead, and felt the strangest thing happen, which was that her loud head went quiet. Not empty. Quiet. The fragments stopped swimming and settled, like sediment in a jar, and beneath them she could feel something steady, something that was not memory at all but was somehow related to it, some deep layer of *this is how the world goes* — hands, and patience, and tick, and tock.

"The clock sounds like home," she said. She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way the truth always seemed to come out of her, sideways, unasked-for.

The woman's hands stilled. In the dark the girl heard her breathe once, slowly, in and out.

"Yes," the woman said at last. "It does, doesn't it." And there was something in her voice that the girl was too young to name but stored away anyway, the way she stored everything: a cracked place, a place where the voice went thin, as though the word *home* had caught on something buried in the woman too.

They sat together a while, the woman and the girl and the stopped clock, three keepers of time in various states of repair.

* * *

In the morning the woman was different. Not worse. Differenter — more decided, the way weather looks decided before rain. She gave the girl bread and honey, even though yesterday there had been no honey, which meant the woman had gone down the stairs in the dark and come back with honey, and the girl understood that this was an apology for something, though for what she couldn't say.

"I'm going to open the case today," the woman said. "Oscar's case. All the way. You can watch, but you mustn't touch, and you mustn't breathe on the works. Can you manage that?"

Breathing was the hard part. Breathing was most of what she did. But she nodded solemnly, because this — being trusted to stand close, being allowed to witness — was the most important thing that had happened to her since the humming.

The woman worked the fastenings one by one, and the front of the great case swung open like a door, and the girl looked inside the body of the stopped giant, expecting gears, expecting the brass forest she had imagined, and instead saw the woman go very still, and very pale, and reach into the dark with two fingers, and draw out something flat and folded and old.

Paper. Folded paper, hidden in the clock, tucked into the works like a letter into a coat pocket.

"What is it?" the girl whispered, forgetting absolutely not to breathe.

The woman stared at the paper the way the girl stared at the locked door downstairs. Then she folded it smaller, smaller, and pushed it deep into her apron pocket, and covered the pocket with her hand as though keeping something alive in there.

"It's a question," the woman said, which was not an answer, and they both knew it, and the tick went on above them, patient as ever, keeping count of everything that was coming next.
