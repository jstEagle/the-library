## 7. The Escapement

The lamp burned low by the time Lior had the great case open to its heart.

She had worked since dawn, and the observatory had moved through its slow daylight around her — the girl asleep again on the cot in the corner, wrapped in Lior's spare coat, breathing with the small, even rhythm of a child who had decided, provisionally and without ceremony, to trust her. Outside, the light had climbed the dome's ribs and slid down again; the river had muttered all day under its lid of ice. Now the dark came back through the slit windows, and the only sound in the stone room was the tick of Lior's own traveling watch on the bench, patient as a conscience.

The master clock's movement filled the iron frame before her like the skeleton of some enormous, patient animal. Wheels the size of cartwheels. A weight train that ran through three floors of the tower, down into a shaft cut into the bedrock itself. Her master had brought her here once — once, ten years ago, at night, with his collar up and his eyes moving along the walls — and she had never understood until now why he had not let her touch anything. He had shown her the escapement from behind a rope of velvet cordage, the way one shows a child a relic.

*This is the part that decides,* he had said. *Everything else in a clock is muscle. This is the mind.*

She untied the last of her tools from their roll and set them out in order: pliers, pin-vice, the brass-handled screwdrivers her master had ground down himself from broken files because, he said, no tool should be bigger than the work it did. She lit a second lamp and hung it inside the frame so the light fell upward through the wheelwork, and for a long moment she simply looked.

The clock was dying. That much was true, and true in the ordinary way — dirt in the oil, wear in the pivots, a mainspring barrel gone brittle with age. Ten years of neglect would do this to any machine. But she had spent the day cleaning and measuring, and the neglect sat on the mechanism like dust on furniture: it explained the surface, not the sickness. A clock this well built did not stop every fourth hour. It did not lose nine minutes and then find seven of them again, like a man who stumbles and pretends he didn't.

Something was wrong underneath.

She began at the top and worked down, the way her master had taught her — *diagnose before you prescribe; a clockmaker who guesses is a butcher* — checking each wheel against the arithmetic of the train. The great wheel: sound. The center wheel: sound, its pivot polished by someone's hand within living memory, the oil still faintly amber where it hadn't dried. The third wheel carried a tooth chipped at its tip, but old chips round themselves off with wear, and this one was rounded, ancient, harmless.

Then she came to the escape wheel.

It was a beautiful thing even now, forty teeth of hardened steel, cut with a fineness that made her fingertips ache with envy. Her master had spoken of the founder — old Aldery, the dead one, the astronomer whose name the nephew wore like a borrowed coat — as a man who machined the way monks illuminated. *He cut teeth you could read scripture off.* She bent close, angled the lamp, and began to run her loupe along each tooth in turn, counting, because counting was prayer and prayer kept the hands steady.

Thirty-eight teeth were worn honestly. Two decades of use had burnished them soft and grey, edges gone slightly blind, the way any steel goes after years of catching and releasing the pallets sixty times a minute, a million times a month, a billion times in ten years —

Ten years.

The clock had been failing for ten years. She had heard the story from Widow Kessel often enough, told with a shrug that didn't quite convince: *the founder died, the money stopped, nobody wound it properly, machines are like marriages, they die of inattention.* Ten years of stopping, starting, drifting, striking wrong hours, until the town stopped setting its days by the observatory and the magistrates started writing about the estate instead.

But these two teeth —

Lior went very still.

Two teeth, near the bottom of the arc where the pallets bit deepest, were not worn at all. They were bright. Bright as if they had been cut yesterday — and not cut, exactly. Not filed down by time. They had been *reshaped*. Someone had taken a graver to them, recently, within months, perhaps within weeks, and altered the profile: shaved the locking face back by half a hair's width, eased the impulse face so that the pallets caught shallow. To any inspector who opened the case, it would look like simple wear — uneven, yes, but wear concentrated where wear concentrates, at the points of greatest stress. An honest assessor would shake his head over it and write *escapement worn beyond economic repair*, and an honest assessor would be wrong.

Her mouth had gone dry. She made herself keep looking.

Wear forgives. That was the first law her master had ever taught her about reading damage. Wear follows physics; it falls hardest where the load falls hardest, and it rounds everything, softens everything, takes the sharp corners off the truth. Deliberate alteration does the opposite. It has intent in it. It puts sharpness where smoothness should be, and it leaves tool marks too fine and too regular for accident — the marks of a craftsman, not of time.

There. Under the second altered tooth, along the root of the steel, a single score line, straight as a ruled edge. No file wears a root like that. Files wander. Hands wander. This line did not wander.

And below the escape wheel, where the pallet arms met the crutch that carried motion down to the seconds hand, she found the rest of it. A small brass wedge, no larger than a thumbnail, fitted snugly between the crutch and its arbor — pinned in place with a taper pin driven home so neatly that only someone who knew what the assembly should look like would ever think to question it. The wedge introduced a fractional hesitation at each swing. A heartbeat of lost impulse, twice a minute, forever. Nine minutes lost, seven found again, depending on temperature, depending on how the brass expanded in the cold of the tower and contracted in the warm of the afternoon. A fault no one could reproduce on demand. A fault that existed precisely so that it could not be diagnosed.

Someone had engineered this clock to fail in a way that looked like age.

Lior lowered the loupe and stood there a long time in the lamplight, hearing her own pulse in her ears, hearing beneath it the silence where the clock's pulse should have been.

*Designed to look like neglect.*

Neglect voided things. That was how bequests worked — she was no lawyer, but she had sat in Widow Kessel's kitchen enough evenings to know how lawyers talked when they thought the help wasn't listening. A founder leaves his instruments, his endowment, his observatory, held in trust, maintained in working order, *kept in honest time* — the phrase was probably written into the deed somewhere in Aldery's strongbox, inked by the dead man's own pen. And if the instrument failed — if it failed visibly, publicly, year after year, until the whole town could say the observatory's great clock had not kept honest time in a decade — then the condition of the bequest had lapsed. Then the estate could be declared forfeit. Then it passed, not to whatever heir the founder might have named in some paper nobody had seen, but to whoever the courts said it passed to. To whoever had bought the courts.

To Mr. Aldery.

She thought of the letter that had summoned her. *You alone.* Not the guild, not the widow, not any of the journeymen who had scattered after her master's death. Her. A twenty-four-year-old woman with no name worth buying and no standing worth bribing, whose continued possession of her dead master's workshop hung on this one commission like a coat hook on a plaster wall. She had read the letter as an honor. She had read it as a trap. She had lain awake turning both readings over, unable to choose between them.

Now she saw they were the same reading.

She had been hired not to repair the clock but to *certify* its ruin. To open the case, examine the escapement, and write — in her own apprentice's hand, on her own authority, such as it was — the verdict that made everything legal: *worn beyond repair; failure consistent with prolonged neglect; condition of bequest unfulfilled.* Her signature would be the final tooth in the mechanism. Aldery had not needed to buy her. He had only needed to employ her, because a craftsman's honest report carries more weight than a bought magistrate's decree. You cannot forge a clockmaker's finding. So you arrange for the clockmaker to find exactly what you have hidden for her to find.

Except she had found something else.

Lior set down the loupe very carefully, as though it might go off. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady — steadier than they had been in ten days, steadier than they had been at her master's funeral, where she had wept into her sleeve and told herself the grief was simple. There is a particular coldness that comes over a craftsman when she understands that she has been made part of someone else's machine. It is not fear. Fear comes later, when the hands remember they are attached to a person. For now there was only the clarity, hard and clean as freshly cut steel: *this was never about repair.*

She took out the wedge first. It resisted — the taper pin had been seated with real skill — and she worked it loose with the pin-vice, easing, easing, refusing to hurry, because a hurried hand mars the evidence and a marred piece tells no tales. When it came free she held it in her palm: brass, good brass, milled square, its faces bearing the fine parallel striations of a jeweler's saw. Not workshop scrap. Not a field expedient. This had been made at a bench, deliberately, by someone who owned good tools.

She wrapped it in a scrap of chamois and placed it in the inner pocket of her waistcoat, against her ribs, where the letter had lived for a week. Then she turned back to the escape wheel and did not touch it. The altered teeth would stay exactly as they were — photographed in her memory, measurable with her gauges, deniable by everyone except the man who had ordered them cut. Evidence, her master used to say, is like a fusee: it only works if you don't wind it past its strength. Touch it too much and you strip it.

The lamp fluttered. Somewhere above her, wind moved across the dome, and the building groaned in a register she felt through the soles of her boots.

From the cot in the corner came a small sound — not waking, just the shifting of a child rearranging herself around a dream — and Lior glanced over, and the glance changed something.

Because the thought arrived then, unbidden and complete, the way conclusions arrive when the hands have been busy and the mind has been left alone too long: *the note.* *Forget the face, finish the work.* She had read those words a dozen times since the night on the steps, and every reading had assumed the sentence was addressed to her — forget the child's face, the child's history, the child's claim; finish the commission, collect your fee, save your shop. A warning. A bribe. A kindness, even: *don't get entangled, clockmaker; entanglement is how small people die.*

But suppose the sentence had another reader.

Suppose it had been written by the same hand that had cut those two teeth — or by a different hand serving the same purpose — and suppose its purpose was not to warn her but to *frame* her. A sealed note, found in her possession, instructing her to abandon a child: what did that look like to a magistrate who had already drafted his sympathies? It looked like conspiracy. It looked like proof that the observatory's caretaker had known of the child and conspired to conceal her. Or worse — reverse the pronouns, and it became a confession planted where she would inevitably find it: *the clockmaker was paid to forget the face and finish the work.* Either way, the paper was not a message. It was a component. Fitted into the mechanism of the case the way the wedge was fitted into the crutch — small, precise, designed to throw the whole train out of true at the critical moment.

She had taken the note from the child's blanket and put it in her pocket, and she had been carrying a loaded spring against her chest for six days without knowing it.

Lior crossed the room quietly and stood over the sleeping girl. In the lamplight the child's face was slack with the total trust of deep sleep — mouth open, one fist curled under her chin, hair stuck to her forehead. Six years old. Left on the steps of an observatory in February with a note for a stranger. Whoever had arranged that delivery had calculated, to the hair, how much a lone woman could be relied upon to do the decent thing — had counted on Lior taking the girl in, and had prepared, with equal care, for the possibility that she talked. The note covered both outcomes. If Lior stayed silent, the child vanished and no one connected her to the estate. If Lior spoke, the note damned her. Every path led through the same works, and every path ended with the bequest voided and the nephew holding the keys.

*They built the trap around a child,* Lior thought, and the coldness in her cracked, and something hotter came up through it.

Not rage. Not yet. What she felt was more dangerous than rage: it was the flat, workmanlike anger of a craftsman examining another craftsman's shoddy joinery and finding it *good* — finding that her enemy worked cleanly, planned patiently, wasted nothing. Respect, almost. And under the respect, the resolve that respect produces in anyone who has sworn an oath over a bench: *then I will work more cleanly than you.*

She went back to the frame and did the only sane thing left, the thing her master would have done, the thing the oath demanded: she worked. She cleaned the pallets, dressed the honest wear, measured the altered teeth with her depth gauge and wrote the figures in her logbook — not the report Aldery expected, but her own private record, dated, signed, specific, the numbers a witness needs. She removed the chipped-and-brightened mystery of the escape wheel from speculation and fixed it in ink. Whatever came next, the sabotage was now a documented fact with her name attached to it, and facts, unlike clocks, cannot be unwound.

By the time she finished, the candle stubs had drowned in their own wax and the windows had gone from black to the bruised grey that precedes a river dawn. Her back ached; her eyes burned. On the bench lay the disassembled remains of a fault that was not a fault, catalogued and bagged in oiled paper like surgical specimens.

She was closing the roll of tools when she heard it — faintly, from the direction of the stairs, the outer door's iron latch lifting and settling, and then footsteps on the lower landing. Unhurried. Familiar with the building. Coming up.

Lior put out the second lamp, slipped the chamois-wrapped wedge deeper into her pocket, and stood between the open clock case and the sleeping child as the footsteps reached the top of the stair.

* * *

The knock, when it came, was polite — three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has never in his life had to break a door. Lior crossed to the cot and laid her hand briefly on the blanket, feeling the small warmth rise and fall beneath it, and the child did not wake, and something in Lior's chest both broke and hardened over the breaking, the way water freezes into sharper shapes.

She opened the door.

Mr. Aldery stood on the landing in a coat better than the hour deserved, hat in hand, smiling the smile of a creditor at a debtor's funeral. Behind him, at the turn of the stair, waited a servant she had not seen before, broad through the shoulders, hands folded, watching her with the flat professional attention of a man counting exits.

"Miss Winterbourne." His breath smoked in the cold of the stairwell. "Forgive the hour. I'm told the great patient has been opened at last, and I confess I couldn't sleep for wanting to hear the diagnosis." He peered past her shoulder into the lamplit dark of the clock room, mild and curious as a parishioner at a christening. "I do hope it's nothing... hopeless."

Lior stepped aside, blocking the view of the bench and its oiled-paper parcels, and smiled back, and heard herself say — smoothly, steadily, in the voice of a tired tradeswoman with nothing to hide —

"Neglect, sir. Nothing that money and patience can't answer."

His smile widened by a degree, and his eyes — quick, pale, and entirely unkind — went from her face to the pocket of her waistcoat, where a brass wedge the size of a thumbnail pressed cold against her ribs, and stayed there a half-second too long.
