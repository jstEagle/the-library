## 9. Ice on the River

The river spoke first.

Lior heard it before she saw it — a long groan rolling up through the observatory's stone foundations and into the clock case itself, as if the great mechanism had shivered in its sleep. She was kneeling at the master clock's base with a lamp between her knees and a wrench in her hand, three hours past midnight, when the sound came: not the crack of a branch or the pop of a settling house, but something older, vaster, like the earth clearing its throat beneath a mile of frozen water.

She climbed the spiral stair to the lantern room with her coat over her nightdress, breath smoking, and looked down at the Vellamarch.

From that height the river was a white road laid from hill to hill, moonlit and still. But along its southern edge, where the current ran fastest under the willow bank, the whiteness had begun to fray. Dark seams opened in the ice like hairline fractures in porcelain — thin black threads of open water, stitching themselves longer as she watched. As she watched, one of them sighed and widened by a hand's breadth, and the whole sheet shuddered, and the sound it made was the sound she'd been hearing all her life every late February without ever once climbing to see where it came from.

*It begins at the edges,* her master had told her once, years ago, when they'd walked down to watch the break together and he'd kept her well back from the bank. *Ice fails the way any structure fails, girl — never in the middle, always at the joints. Remember that.*

She hadn't known then what he meant her to remember it *for*. Now, standing above the darkening river with a child asleep two floors below and a forged future sealed inside a clock case, she thought she was beginning to understand him better than she had ever wanted to.

The girl — Oscar, though no one had yet spoken the name aloud to her; Lior only knew it now from the register, and carried it like a coal in her pocket — slept badly on cold nights. Lior had taken to leaving the little parlor stove fed until dawn. Tonight the girl had fallen asleep sitting up against the clock case itself, cheek pressed to the wood, one small hand curled around the pendulum door's brass handle, and when Lior came back down from the lantern room she found her still there, breathing slow, the ticking rising and falling around her like water around a moored boat.

Lior crouched beside her and did not wake her. There were things a body knew in sleep that a waking mind would only argue about. The girl had been left here in the dead of winter with nothing but a note and someone else's name stitched into her collar, and she had chosen — six years old, alone, unasked — to fall asleep against the loudest thing in the building. As if the ticking sounded like home.

Lior covered her with the good blanket and went back to work.

***

By morning the town had noticed too.

Vellamarch counted its year by the river the way other towns counted by church bells. The ice-out was a festival, an economy, an argument — ferrymen betting on the date, merchants praying for early water so their spring freight could move, children dared each other onto the weakening shelf glass every year and every year somebody's brother went in. From the first seam to the full break could be four days or fourteen, and nobody pretended to know which, and the whole town lived in the uncertainty like fish in a net, feeling it draw.

Lior felt it drawing tighter than anyone.

She went down for salt pork and lamp oil at Cobbett's general store, Oscar's mittened hand locked in hers, and found the store already full of men arguing over a chalked board behind the counter where old Cobbett ran his annual pool: a copper penny a guess, closest date wins the pot. The board read **ICE-OUT — 22 FEB** in one corner and **14 MAR** in the other, and between them the guesses clustered thick as flies.

"Clockmaker!" hailed Dan Ferris, who ran the rope ferry and therefore had more money riding on the ice than any man in town. "You're the one keeps the observatory clock. Tell them — does the river keep schedule like your works?"

"The river keeps no schedule," Lior said. "That's rather the point of a river."

"Ha! Hear that? The lady hedges." Ferris grinned and turned back to the board, but the grin sat oddly on him this year, and when Lior passed close she heard him mutter to Cobbett, low: "...ferry can't run till she's clear, and if the hearing's set for term day and the ferry's shut—" He stopped when he saw her listening. He stopped very completely, the way men do when they've said one word too many.

*Term day.* The legal term. The assizes sat in Vellamarch twice a year, and the spring session convened on the first day the ferry crossed — because the circuit judge came by water, there being no road worth the name up the eastern bluff. Everyone in the store understood what Ferris had almost said aloud: whoever controlled the ferry controlled the calendar, and whoever controlled the calendar controlled the court.

Lior bought her salt pork and did not let her face do anything at all.

Outside, the morning was hard and bright, the kind of light that makes frost smoke rise off rooftops. Along the millrace the ice had gone gray and rotten-sounding overnight. Two boys were throwing stones at it to hear the change in tone — the dull wet *whump* instead of January's bright ring — and their mothers called them off from doorways with the particular sharpness of women who had lost someone to that water before.

Oscar tugged her sleeve. "Why do they hit the ice?"

"To listen to it."

"Why?"

"Because it tells you things." Lior looked down the long white reach of the river, at the black threads widening along the southern shore, and heard her own voice come out quieter than she intended. "Everything under strain talks, if you know how to listen. Clocks. Ice. People."

The girl considered this with the terrible seriousness she brought to everything. "What am I saying?"

Lior's chest tightened. "Nothing," she said, and immediately hated herself for the smoothness of it, the practiced ease, ten years of *nothing, nothing, nothing* worn into her voice like a groove worn into a pivot. "You're saying you're cold. Come along."

But the girl looked back at the river the whole way home, and Lior, watching her look, felt the deadline coming down the valley toward them both, patient and enormous, wearing the face of ordinary weather.

***

Aldery posted the notice on Thursday.

He did it properly — broadsheet, printed, wax-sealed copies nailed to the assizes board outside the courthouse, the market cross, and the ferry landing, with a courtesy copy delivered to the observatory by a hired clerk who would not meet Lior's eye. She read it standing in the observatory doorway with the wind pulling at the paper's corner, and the clerk stood shifting his weight and saying nothing, and the words arranged themselves into exactly the shape she had feared:

> **NOTICE OF ESTATE HEARING**
>
> Whereas the estate of the late EDMUND VALE, founder of the Vellamarch Observatory, deceased, stands in dispute among claimants; and whereas certain instruments and bequests of said estate remain unsettled;
>
> NOTICE IS GIVEN that the matter shall come before the Assizes at Vellamarch at the opening of the spring term, being the first lawful crossing of the ferry following the breaking of the river ice.
>
> All persons holding papers, instruments, registers, or effects belonging to said estate are required to produce the same at hearing, or show cause.
>
> — J. ALDERY, Administrator

*All persons holding papers.* It was addressed to the whole town and it was addressed to her and only to her, and they both knew it. *Produce the same, or show cause.* Show cause meant explain why you were withholding. Explain meant testify. Testify meant Aldery's counsel asking, gently, before the assembled town, how exactly an apprentice clockmaker came to hold documents of the Vale estate at all — and whether she'd had them since before the founder died, and whose instruction she'd been following, and wasn't it strange how her dead master's debts all seemed to run through Mr. Aldery's hands.

Or it meant handing the papers over and letting them vanish into an administrator's strongbox, from which nothing emerged except signatures.

She thanked the clerk. She closed the door. Her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have; steadiness was something her master had drilled into her across a thousand hours at the bench, and she caught herself wondering, with the ledger's numbers still fresh in memory, how much of what he'd taught her had been craft and how much had been training her to be useful to a silence.

Inside, Oscar was feeding crumbs of yesterday's bread to the observatory cat through the bars of the stair rail, an arrangement the cat tolerated with imperial disdain. "Was that a letter?"

"A notice."

"What's it notice?"

"That a thing is going to happen," Lior said, "on a day that hasn't decided to happen yet."

Oscar frowned at the paper upside down. "That's a bad way to write. You should say the day."

"The day isn't ours to say." Lior folded the notice and put it in the drawer with the register and the half-will, the drawer she now checked each morning and each night, and had begun checking also at odd hours, waking from dreams in which the drawer hung open and the pages lay blank. "Some days are set by rivers."

***

Friday the wind turned south, and the river began talking in earnest.

All day the sounds carried up the bluff to the observatory — cracks like pistol shots out along the eastern channel, deep booming reports from midstream where the ice field shifted and ground against itself, and underneath it all a continuous low grinding, like a millstone turning somewhere below the world. The horses in the livery yard below the bluff had to be blanketed and walked, they were so wild with it. Dogs howled at intervals. And the town, instead of hiding indoors, poured out onto the banks to stand in the cold and listen, because everyone in Vellamarch knew these sounds the way farmers know the smell of rain: the river was telling them its decision, and the decision was *soon*.

Lior worked with the windows rattling. The replacement escapement was finished — cut, jeweled, poised, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden in the false bottom of the tool chest, in the place only she and two others knew — but the escapement was the last easy part. What remained was the strike train, frozen these ten years, its lift wires seized with corrosion, and the chime barrel with its pins, and whatever secret her master had built into the release mechanism that she had not yet found and did not dare force. A clockmaker learns early that the difference between restoration and destruction is patience. She worked slowly. The river worked fast.

Toward dusk she went down to the bank alone, leaving Oscar with Mrs. Prue the observatory keeper's widow next door, and joined the crowd at the ferry landing.

The Vellamarch ice was going out ugly this year. Where in gentle springs it rotted in place and drifted off in plates, this year's cold had built it thick and hungry, and now the south wind and the swollen upstream melt were shoving it whole. The channel below the landing had gone black and wide, but a hundred yards out the main floe still held, groaning, buckling, ridging up along pressure lines like skin over a healing wound. Men stood at the rail measuring it with their eyes. Dan Ferris stood among them, arms crossed, and he was not looking at the ice. He was looking at his ferry — moored double, tarped, its cable paid out and anchored — with the expression of a man watching a debt come due.

"Ferris," said one of the grain merchants, jerking his chin at the black water. "Could put her across tomorrow, near enough. Channel's open past the point."

"Channel's open," Ferris agreed. "And the middle's a moving mountain. I don't cross against that with the judge aboard, nor with anybody." He spat over the rail. "Besides. Crossing's not mine to call till the ice is fully out. That's the law of the landing, older than me, older than my father. No ferry runs while the river runs ice. Ask any waterman."

"And if some gentleman asked different?" the merchant said, delicately. "If some gentleman with interests in the term date offered good money for an early crossing?"

The landing went quiet the way a courtroom goes quiet.

Ferris looked at the merchant a long moment. Then he laughed, short and hard, with nothing in it. "Then some gentleman," he said, "would be asking me to hang my license and maybe my boy on a floe the size of the churchyard. And I'll tell you another thing for free." He lowered his voice, but the wind carried it anyway, carried it straight up the steps to where Lior stood. "Gentleman already asked. Tuesday. Sent a clerk with a figure wrote on paper. I sent the paper back."

"You sent back—"

"I sent it back with a hole punched through the figure, is what I sent." Ferris turned away, done. But Lior had seen his face in the instant before he turned, and it was not the face of a man pleased with his own virtue. It was the face of a man who had counted what refusing might cost him, and had refused anyway, and was now waiting to find out what that refusal would buy him in return.

She walked home in the blue dark with the river thundering behind her, and she understood that the board had been laid out plainly now, for anyone willing to read it. The ice would break within days — the whole town knew it, felt it in their teeth. The hearing would convene on the first lawful crossing after the break. Aldery had tried to buy the crossing and failed, or appeared to fail. Which meant either that the ferryman was braver than his purse, or that Aldery had already bought something else — the judge's schedule, the clerk's pen, the magistrate's ruling (that one, surely; that one was bought and dry) — and the ferry was only the piece he didn't need.

At the observatory door she stopped. The wind had dropped with the dark, and in the sudden stillness the river's voice came up the bluff enormous and unhurried, and beneath it, fainter, from inside the stone walls, came the other sound — the master clock, stopped these ten years, silent, holding its secret in its belly.

Two clocks now, she thought. One counting down openly for the whole town to hear. And one that hadn't yet struck, that everyone believed broken beyond mending, that Aldery's own sabotage had been meant to bury — and that she had promised, in whatever wordless way apprentices promise, to bring back to life.

She went in. She checked the drawer. She wound nothing, because there was nothing yet to wind, but she laid her palm flat against the cold oak of the case, the way she had seen Oscar do in her sleep, and stood there a long time in the lamplight, listening to a river tear itself apart and a clock hold its peace.

In the morning, the first floe went through the bridge arches with a sound like the end of the world, and Vellamarch woke to water.

In the morning, too, there was a second paper nailed to the observatory door — smaller than the notice, unsealed, written in a clerk's hurried hand. Lior read it standing on the step with ice dust blowing past her ears, and read it again, and her blood went slow and cold as the river under the ice:

*They mean to move the hearing to Millford, where the roads run and the ferry doesn't matter. Term opens there the same week. Your papers won't travel and neither need you. If you want them heard in this town, you have until the ice goes out to find a way that isn't the assizes.*

No signature. But the ink was fresh, and the paper smelled of the guildhall's cheap tallow, and Lior stood holding it and understood that somewhere in Vellamarch, in the last forty-eight hours, someone had decided she was worth gambling on — and that whatever door they were offering her, she would have to walk through it knowing it might be a trap.

Behind her, inside, the child was singing to the stopped clock, some half-remembered tune, soft and off-key and entirely unafraid.

Lior folded the note into her pocket, next to the register's page, and went in to find out what her oath was going to cost her.
