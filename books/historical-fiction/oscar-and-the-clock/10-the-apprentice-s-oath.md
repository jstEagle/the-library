## 10. The Apprentice's Oath

The child slept in the observatory's back room, in a bed that smelled of dust and camphor and the particular loneliness of furniture no one had wanted for years. Lior sat outside her door on the landing with a lamp turned low and the master clock's tick coming up through the floorboards like a second heartbeat, slower than her own, steadier than her own, patient as something that had outlived everyone who wound it.

She could not sleep. She had not properly slept in nine days, since the girl arrived, since the note, since the word *finish* had begun to work its way through her thoughts like water through plaster — invisible until the surface gave.

So she did what she always did when she could not sleep. She went down to the clock.

Not to work. There was nothing left to adjust tonight; she had checked the escapement twice already and found it behaving exactly as an escapement should, releasing time one tooth at a time with the indifference of a good servant. She simply stood beneath the great case with her lamp raised and let the sound of it fill her the way it had filled her when she was small enough to be frightened of thunder.

It was standing under this same clock — or one very much like it, in the workshop on Tanner's Row — that she had sworn the oath.

* * *

She had been fourteen. She remembered that because her master, Aldous Wrenn, had made her wait until she was fourteen to swear it, and she had spent the better part of two years asking why, and he had spent those two years declining to answer, which was his way of teaching patience before he taught anything else.

"You want to know what the words are," he'd said, on the evening he finally set down his file and looked at her across the bench. "You've wanted to know since you were twelve."

"Yes, Master."

"And now you're old enough to be trusted with them." He wiped his hands on the leather apron he wore even when he wasn't working, a habit she never understood and later adopted without noticing. "Come here. Stand where you can see the train."

The workshop clock — the little brother of the observatory's monster, built by the same hands, keeping the same stubborn time — hung on the east wall above the window that looked out on Tanner's Row. Through its glass face you could see the escapement working, that small tireless motion, catch and release, catch and release. He made her stand so close she could hear every tooth.

"Repeat after me," he said. "And understand that I will only say these words with you once. After tonight they live in you or they don't."

"I swear by the work of my hands—"

"I swear by the work of my hands—"

"—to protect the work of my master above all things—"

She repeated it. She could still feel the cold of the shop on her bare feet, because it was March and the fire had burned low and Aldous would not stoke it while there was daylight left to work by.

"Above profit, above comfort, above reputation—"

Above reputation. Even then, fourteen years old and hungry, she had felt the strangeness of that clause. What apprentice swears away her own name?

"—above all questions asked and all answers owed—"

That one too. Above all answers owed. She had repeated it faithfully because repeating was easier than understanding, and understanding, she suspected, was not actually required. The oath was a shape. You stepped into it. Later, if you lived long enough, it closed around you.

"Last part," Aldous said, and here his voice changed — dropped lower, gentler, the way it did when he spoke about the dead. "The work outlasts the maker. The maker serves the work. And the keeper of the work—" he paused, watching her, and she saw something in his face she had never seen before and would spend ten years trying to name: fear, maybe, or the memory of fear, worn smooth from handling — "the keeper of the work keeps faith with whoever comes after."

"The keeper of the work keeps faith with whoever comes after," she said.

"So help me every gear I ever cut," he finished, and she finished, and he shook her hand formally, once, like a man concluding a contract, and then put an arm around her shoulders and walked her to the stove and fed the fire at last, because the day was over and oaths, he said, deserved warmth afterward.

She had thought it was about clocks. That was the joke of it — the terrible, beautiful joke of it — that she had stood there at fourteen with her hand in her master's hand and believed the oath was about clocks.

* * *

Now, under the observatory's greater clock, holding her lamp up into the case where the founder's initials were cut deep into the crossbeam — *E.V.M.*, Edmund Vellamarch himself, dead eight years and still ticking — Lior finally understood what the oath had been shaped around.

Not the clocks. The papers.

She thought of the half-written will sewn inside the case lining, hidden where only someone who knew movements the way she knew movements would ever think to look. She thought of the sealed note pinned to a child's coat: *forget the face, finish the work.* She thought of Aldous Wrenn, ten years dead, who had served the Vellamarch family quietly and completely, and who had taught her which customers to serve last, which debts to ignore, which questions not to ask — and she understood, with the slow sick certainty of ice shifting underfoot, that everything he had taught her had been a wall built around a secret. The rules weren't prudence. They were camouflage.

And the oath — *protect the work of my master above all things* —

Whose work?

Because here was the knot she had been worrying for three days without being able to loosen: the will in the case was not Aldous Wrenn's work. It was Edmund Vellamarch's, or nearly so, abandoned mid-sentence by a dying hand. But Aldous had hidden it. Aldous had carried it, kept it, stitched it into the lining of a clock case with the neat surgical stitches she knew better than her own handwriting. In protecting the hiding place, was she protecting her master's work — his silence, his complicity, whatever bargain had bought him twenty years of Vellamarch patronage? Or was she protecting the founder's work — the true will, the interrupted intention, the granddaughter sleeping upstairs whose name had been cut off before it could be written?

*Above all questions asked and all answers owed.*

The oath forbade her to ask. The oath forbade her to answer. And yet here she stood, having asked every question the night allowed, holding a lamp up to the answer itself.

* * *

She climbed down from the ladder and sat on the observatory steps with the lamp between her feet and the March wind coming off the river, and she tried to remember what else Aldous had said that night, because memory was the only witness she had left.

There had been something else. Something after the warmth, after the stew she couldn't afford and he pretended he could, when she was pulling on her coat to walk home through the dark streets of Vellamarch and he stopped her at the door with his hand on her shoulder.

"Lior." He almost never used her name. Apprentices got surnames, or nothing. "Do you know why I made you wait?"

"Because I was too young before."

"No." His thumb pressed briefly against her shoulder blade, hard, the way he tested metal. "Because I needed to know whether you'd ask me again. Two years, and you asked every week. Never angrily. Never sulkily. You just asked." He had smiled then, but it was the tired kind of smile, the kind that costs something. "A person who stops asking is easy to use. A person who never stops asking is hard to keep quiet. I need both, do you see? I need someone who asks, and someone who can bear not knowing the answer."

"I don't understand, Master."

"No," he said. "But you will. Probably sooner than I'd like."

He had ruffled her hair — undignified, unmasterlike — and sent her out into the dark, and she had walked home along the frozen river feeling chosen, feeling armored, feeling that she had been given a sword when really she had been handed a lock and told to guard the door without looking at what was behind it.

He had known. Standing there in the doorway of his own shop, smelling of solder and pipe smoke, he had known that someday the oath would split down the middle — that protecting his work and protecting the truth would turn out to be different directions, opposite directions — and he had armed her anyway, because he was a fair man, or a cowardly one, or both, and he wanted at least one honest pair of eyes in the room when it happened.

*Probably sooner than I'd like.* Ten years, Aldous. You got ten years more than you deserved.

* * *

The wind shifted and brought the smell of the river — thaw underneath the frost, mud and old weed and the green ghost of spring. Down the bank, the ice groaned. It was doing that more often now, long shuddering complaints that traveled the whole width of the Vella like the river clearing its throat. The older townsfolk said you could count the break from the first groan: a fortnight, sometimes less.

Behind her, upstairs, the child cried out in her sleep — a small sharp sound, quickly muffled, the cry of someone learning early that crying loud gets you moved along. Lior was on her feet before she'd decided to stand. She waited. The sound didn't come again. Just dreams, then. A carriage, perhaps. A woman singing. Whatever the girl carried that she hadn't yet spoken of, and that Lior hadn't yet dared to open the way she'd opened the clock case.

She thought: I swore to protect the work of my master above all things. And the work of my master is silence. And the price of that silence is standing in a borrowed nightgown in a borrowed bed, dreaming of a mother nobody in this town will admit existed.

She thought: If Aldous were alive, what would he tell me to do?

And then she caught herself, because that was the wrong question, the coward's question, and worse — she realized she already knew the answer he would give, the answer he'd spent ten years teaching her without saying it. *Keep the work. Keep the silence. Keep safe.* He would say it kindly. He would say it with his arm around her shoulders. And he would say it because he had taken Aldery's money for years and called it retainer, because he had buried a will rather than bury himself, because kindness and cowardice in the same man wear the same coat and you cannot always tell which sleeve your arm is in.

No. Not quite that either. Because he had also hidden the will instead of burning it. A man fully bought burns the paper. A man fully loyal delivers it. Aldous Wrenn had done neither — had stitched it into a clock like a man sewing a confession into the hem of a coat he meant someone else to wear. He had wanted it found. He had wanted it found by someone brave enough to act on it and careful enough to survive acting on it, and he had spent ten years of her apprenticeship building exactly that person, tooth by tooth, catch and release.

The realization landed in her chest like a weight dropping onto a pallet: he hadn't trained an apprentice. He'd trained an heir. To the silence, yes — but to the breaking of it, too.

*The keeper of the work keeps faith with whoever comes after.*

Whoever came after was six years old and asleep above her head.

Lior picked up the lamp and stood a moment longer in the wind, listening to the ice complain and the clock tick, the two sounds running together now, the river and the mechanism keeping the same stubborn appointment with the future. Then she went inside and barred the door and did not go back to the landing, because a decision had been made somewhere below language, the way a decision is made in a train of wheels — silently, mechanically, irreversibly — and all that remained was for the striking train to catch up.

She would need proof. Not feelings, not fragments of a dream, not a half-written page in a dead man's stitching. Proof that would hold in front of a magistrate, in front of Aldery's counsel, in front of the whole smug purchased face of Vellamarch. And she knew where such proof might live, because observatories keep records the way churches keep souls — registers, visitor books, ledgers of comings and goings going back to the founder's own hand.

Tomorrow, she told the dark. Tomorrow I stop guarding the door and start reading what's behind it.

Upstairs, the clock struck two, clear and unhurried, and did not care at all what she had decided. That was the thing about honest machines. They just kept the time until you were ready to meet it.
