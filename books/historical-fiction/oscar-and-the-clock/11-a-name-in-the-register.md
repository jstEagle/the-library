## 11. A Name in the Register

The visitor book lived on a shelf behind the observatory's north window, wedged between a broken barometer and a stack of star charts gone soft at the edges with damp. I had noticed it my first week and ignored it — old houses are full of old paper, and a clockmaker learns to leave other people's records alone.

But that was before the will. Before the half-written document folded inside the clock's case like a confession tucked into a breast pocket. Before I understood that every object in this building might be a sentence waiting to be read aloud.

I took the book down on the ninth morning of my commission, while the girl slept in the cot I had made up for her in the astronomer's old dressing room. She slept badly still — she woke at every sound, her small body going rigid as a mainspring wound past its limit — but she had begun to sleep, and that was something. That was a mechanism slowly settling into true.

The book was leather-bound, its spine cracked like river ice. *Visitors to Meridian Observatory*, in faded ink across the first page, followed by a date from thirty years back. The founder — Aldous Merrivale, though no one in Vellamarch spoke the name without lowering their voice, as if he were a saint or a scandal — had kept it himself, by the look of the early entries. A precise hand. An engineer's hand. Every visitor logged with name, purpose, and time of arrival, down to the minute. Even his family, apparently, were admitted to him on the record.

I turned pages by the gray light of the window. The entries marched backward through the years: aldermen, astronomers passing through, a bishop whose signature was so ornate it looked like a spider drowning. Guild men. Guild women. My own master's name, appearing again and again through the decades — *Josiah Kessel, Vellamarch Clockworks, called regarding the master escapement* — sometimes twice in a single month. Ten years ago the entries stopped. The last line was dated eleven days after the founder's death, written by a different hand, shakier, and then nothing. The book had been closed like an eye.

I should have stopped there. The record was dead; whatever it held belonged to a decade that had already buried itself. But my fingers kept turning, because there was one thing I had not let myself think about directly since the night the child arrived, and the book offered a place where thinking about it could look like mere curiosity.

The girl had been left on my steps wearing a coat too fine for any foundling. Good wool, mended carefully, cut for a child of means. Whoever dressed her that morning expected her to be cold and had planned against it. Foundlings do not get planned for. Foundlings get wrapped in sacking and prayed over.

And the note — *forget the face, finish the work* — had not been written in haste. It was written in a hand I now recognized, because I had spent three nights with it pressed against lamplight: the same hand as the half-will in the clock case. Aldous Merrivale's hand. Which meant either a very clever forger, or a dead man arranging things after death, which is exactly what clocks are for.

So I turned pages. I told myself I was looking for anything — a pattern, a name, some thread connecting the observatory's last living months to the child who had appeared on its steps as neatly as a key sliding into a lock. I went forward instead of backward, into the final year of entries, when the writing had grown cramped and the ink blotted, as though the founder's hands had begun to fail before the rest of him did.

Mostly it was tradesmen. Coal, lamp oil, a glazier twice in one winter. Then, near the end, an entry that stopped my thumb flat against the page.

*Marta Merrivale — daughter — arrived 4th hour past noon, 12 October. Purpose: private. Duration: open.*

Marta Merrivale. Daughter.

I sat down on the window bench without deciding to. The word seemed to move on the page, the way letters do when you have been reading too long by bad light, except this was not fatigue. This was the particular vertigo of a thing clicking into place — the sensation I knew best in the world, the feeling of a train of wheels engaging all at once, each tooth finding its socket, so that motion that had seemed random resolves into intention.

A daughter. The founder had a daughter, and no one in Vellamarch had ever spoken of a daughter. They spoke of the nephew, Mr. Aldery, often enough, and always with that careful smoothness people use around money. But a daughter — a daughter would have been the heir. Unless she had been cut out. Unless she had gone away. Unless —

Unless she was dead, and her child was alive, and someone had carried that child through the October dark and set her on the steps of the one building in town where a sealed note could be trusted to find honest hands.

My own hands were shaking. I laughed at them, quietly, the way you laugh at a tool that betrays you at a delicate moment. Steady, Lior. Proof is not inference. Inference is what gets apprentices dismissed and witnesses hanged.

I read the entry again. *Purpose: private.* The only such entry in the whole book — every other visit declared itself, coal is coal and bishops are bishops, but the daughter came under seal of privacy, in the founder's last autumn, when his handwriting was failing and his mind, by every account the town gave, was fixed entirely on his clocks.

I turned the page. There was one more entry, four days later.

*Marta Merrivale — daughter — departed 9th hour morning, 16 October. Accompanied by infant.*

An infant. Twelve Octobers ago? No — the dates in the register ran to the founder's death, ten years back. Ten years ago this month, a woman had come to this observatory carrying a baby, and stayed four days, and left with the baby, and within weeks the founder was dead and his will had vanished and his nephew had begun his long patient work of buying the town.

Ten years. The girl was six.

The arithmetic refused to close. Six does not fit ten. I checked the dates twice, three times, running my finger under the columns like a boy checking sums. Either the register lied, or the child was younger than she seemed — malnourished children run small, the workhouse children in Vellamarch all looked two years young — or the infant of ten years ago and the girl on my steps were not the same child.

Or the mother had come back. Later. Another visit, unrecorded, because the register had already been closed by then.

I sat with that a long time. Through the north window the river lay white and ridged to the far bank, and somewhere under the ice the water moved the way it always moves, patient, keeping its own kind of time. The fire ticked in the grate. Above me, faint through two floors of stone, the dead master clock hung in its housing like a heart awaiting surgery, and I thought — not for the first time — how strange it was that a machine built to measure hours had become the place where everyone's secrets were stored, as if time itself were the only vault they trusted.

The girl woke mid-morning. I heard her on the stairs before I saw her — she had learned the third step creaked and stepped over it now, a small courtesy that undid me more than tears would have. She came into the room with her hair sleep-flattened on one side and stood looking at me with that grave, assessing stare she used on everything, as if the world were a mechanism she intended eventually to take apart.

"You're reading," she said.

"I'm reading."

"Reading isn't work."

"It is if you're doing it right." I closed the register and set my palm flat on its cover, and felt, absurdly, as though I were taking its pulse. "Did you sleep?"

"A bit. There was a dream about singing."

Something went through me, low and cold, like the first crack traveling along a frozen river. Singing. The ferryman's wife had said — no, I hadn't asked anyone yet, I had been careful, so careful, asking no one anything. But the note in my pocket, the one that had come wrapped around the girl's blanket that first night along with the instruction to forget her face — I had never shown it to her, never mentioned it, and yet here was singing arriving out of her sleep as if it had been waiting for permission.

"What did the singing sound like?" I made my voice idle. Curious, not hungry. Children can taste hunger in a question; they flinch from it.

She considered, head tipped. "Like the clock. But softer. And it stopped in the middle, like someone shut a door on it." She climbed onto the bench beside me, uninvited, the way she did everything — as though the world owed her a seat and she was collecting. She looked at the closed register. "Whose book is that?"

"The man who built this house."

"Is he the one who died?"

"He is."

"Was he your friend?"

"No," I said. And then, because lying to her had begun to feel like a splinter working deeper the longer it stayed in: "I never met him. But I owe him. The way you can owe someone you've never met — if they built the road you're walking on."

She nodded seriously, accepting this, and reached out and touched the cover of the book with one finger, the way she touched the clock cases — lightly, respectfully, the way you touch something that might be listening.

"Was I born here?" she asked.

The room went very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls the pipes settled with the morning frost.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because I dreamed the singing here. Dreams don't lie about places. Only about people." She said it simply, without drama, a fact filed among facts, and then she slid off the bench and went to see whether the kettle had boiled, because she was six, and the enormous questions and the kettle existed for her on the same plane, both requiring attention, neither requiring weeping.

I remained on the bench with my hand on the register, and I let myself do the thing I had forbidden myself to do. I assembled the whole mechanism in my mind and watched it turn.

A founder with a daughter he loved enough to log privately, even family, even her. A daughter who arrives in his dying autumn with an infant and leaves four days later — leaving, or being made to leave. A founder who dies within the month. A will that vanishes — or rather, a will that hides, folded into the great clock, in a hand identical to the note on my steps. A nephew who inherits by default and spends ten years buying magistrates and letting the observatory rot, because a rotting observatory is an argument no one has to answer. And now, ten years on, a child set on those same steps, wearing good wool, carrying a note that says *finish the work* — because the work was never the clock. The work was the will. The clock was only its hiding place, and the repair commission was only the key, posted to the one apprentice in Vellamarch too stubborn and too indebted to refuse.

And the mother. Where was the mother? Dead, said the shape of it — the note had the urgency of a last arrangement, the coat had the care of a woman dressing a child for a journey she would not be making herself. Dead the previous autumn, perhaps. Dead recently. Dead soon enough that whoever placed the girl had done it in grief and hurry both.

If I was right, then the sleeping child in the next room, the one who stepped over the creaking stair and touched old books gently, was not a foundling at all. She was Aldous Merrivale's granddaughter. She was the heir Mr. Aldery had spent ten years and a fortune ensuring did not exist — and the moment anyone with standing looked at her and believed it, every pound he had invested in silence would begin to burn.

I understood, sitting there, why the note had commanded me to forget her face. Faces are evidence. A face that favors a dead man can be read like a ledger.

Because — yes. Now that I let myself look, now that I had a name to hold the looking steady, I saw it. The set of the jaw, the pale level eyes, the way her brow came down when she concentrated, exactly the expression carved into the marble bust of the founder that stood dust-sheeted in the hall. I had walked past that bust twenty times and thought it merely a fine piece of work. It had been a portrait, waiting. She was his face returned, ten years younger and six sizes smaller, asking me if the kettle had boiled.

The vertigo came fully then, the true vertigo, the floor-of-the-world kind. Not doubt — worse than doubt. Certainty. Certainty with nothing underneath it yet, certainty balanced on inference and a child's dream of singing, certainty that would need paper and witnesses and law to stand on, and every one of those things in Vellamarch belonged, in one degree or another, to Mr. Aldery.

I had wanted, ten days ago, for this commission to be simple. Repair the clock, collect the fee, keep the creditors off the workshop another season, go home. I had even, in my weakest hours, wanted it to be a trap I could understand — a test of loyalty, a debt called in — because traps at least have shapes.

This was not a trap. This was a door, and I had opened it, and through it I could see the whole machinery of the town laid bare: who paid whom, who looked away, how many silent men it takes to keep one child nameless. My master's silences. The widow's warnings. The magistrate's convenient rulings. All of it one train of wheels, and Mr. Aldery's hand on the winding crank, and every tooth of it turning on the premise that a six-year-old girl would never be permitted to say her own name.

Well. Names could be found. Registers could be read. Dead men, it turned out, wrote excellent letters.

The girl came back with two cups of tea, both hers, one offered to me as a loan. She sat beside me on the bench and drank and watched the river ice with the patience I was only beginning to learn she owned outright.

"Lior," she said, using my name the way she'd begun to a few days ago, tasting it, owning it. "If I did live here, when I was a baby —" she paused, choosing words with visible care, a watchmaker of sentences — "then the singing lady might be my mama. And if my mama brought me here, it would be because this was the safest place she knew." She looked up at me. "It's still the safest place I know. So whoever she was, she chose right."

I could not speak for a moment. When I could, all I said was, "Yes. She chose right." And I put my arm around the shoulders of the heiress of Vellamarch's greatest fortune, drinking tea from a chipped cup in a house everyone else had agreed to abandon, and I began — quietly, wheel by wheel, tooth by tooth — to plan how to prove a dead man's daughter had ever lived, and where she had died, and what name she had given the child the world was determined she never had.

The register had given me the beginning: *Marta.* One name, four days, ten years gone. It would have to be enough to start with. There would be parish books, a burial record, someone — some nurse, some neighbor from another town, some woman who had sung — who remembered her.

And there would be Mr. Aldery, who had bought a magistrate and half a town to make sure no one ever went looking.

That evening, an invitation arrived at the observatory, delivered by a boy in livery too fine for the errand. The magistrate requested the pleasure of my company at dinner — a gathering of the town's principal men, he wrote, to celebrate the approaching season and the *orderly settlement of local affairs*. Mr. Aldery would be present.

The girl watched me read it. "Are you going?"

"I am," I said, folding the card. It was, I understood, the next wheel in the train — and for once, it was turning toward me.

* * *

They seat me below the salt, of course, among the attorneys' clerks and the widow of a customs officer — close enough to hear everything, unimportant enough to be spoken over. The candles are beeswax, expensive, and the wine goes round freely, and by the second course the talk has found its favorite channel: the observatory, and what a shame it is, and how fortunate the town is that Mr. Aldery has taken such personal interest in seeing the estate resolved *properly*.

The magistrate rises to toast him. I keep my hands still in my lap, and my face pleasant, and I listen the way I listen to a clock that has begun, very softly, to tick wrong.
