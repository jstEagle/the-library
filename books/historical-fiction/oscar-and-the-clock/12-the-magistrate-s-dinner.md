## 12. The Magistrate's Dinner

The invitation came by hand, not post — a cream-colored card edged in gilt, delivered by a boy who waited on the observatory step for an answer as though the answer mattered. *Mr. Justice Corvane requests the pleasure of Miss Lior Winterbourne's company at dinner, Thursday next, in celebration of the town's continued prosperity and order.*

Lior read it twice, standing in the doorway with the February wind pushing at her back and the girl — Oscar, she had begun to think of her, though she hadn't yet earned the right to use any name at all — peering around her skirts.

"It's a summons," Lior said.

"It's a card," the girl said.

"It's a summons wearing good clothes."

She had half a mind to decline. Half a mind, and then the other half reminded her what the workshop's creditors would do if the town's justice took her refusal as insolence, and what Aldery would make of a clockmaker too proud to sit at his table. She wrote her acceptance in her smallest, most obedient hand.

*Miss Winterbourne gratefully accepts.*

Then she sat down at the workbench and looked at the word *gratefully* for a long time before blotting it.

* * *

The dinner was held at the magistrate's house on Cordwainer Hill, the finest private residence in Vellamarch after the founder's own — a tall brick face with tall windows, every one of them lit against the early dark, so that the whole building glowed like a lantern set out to advertise its owner's warmth. Lior walked up from the river through streets still glazed with ice, past shopfronts shuttered against the cold, and felt the house watching her the way the master clock watched a room: patiently, with an attention that never blinked.

Inside, the warmth hit her like water. Candles everywhere — dozens, hundreds, it seemed — and the smell of roast meat and beeswax and the particular sweetness of hothouse flowers bought in winter at prices that could have fed a family for a month. She counted the candles without meaning to. Ten years of ledgers will teach you to count things.

A servant took her cloak. Another announced her. *"Miss Winterbourne, apprentice clockmaker,"* in a voice pitched exactly between respect and amusement, and she crossed the threshold into a room where twenty faces turned toward her at once, performed their small calculations, and turned away again.

She knew some of them. The apothecary Grell, who had once tried to buy her master's lathe at auction price while the man was still warm in his grave. The Harrow twins, whose shipping interests depended on the ferry running and the river staying docile. Mrs. Quennel, who organized the charity basket drives and whose husband owned the paper the town notices were printed on. And across the room, near the fire, in a coat of dark green wool that must have cost more than a year of the workshop's rent —

Aldery.

He was laughing at something. He laughed easily; she had noticed that from the first day he called at the observatory, hat in hand, all courtesy and casual questions about papers. His laugh was a tool like any other, well-oiled, kept clean. When he saw her he raised his glass a fraction — acknowledgment, not greeting — and let his eyes rest on her just long enough to say: *I know why you're here, and you know why I'm here, and neither of us will be so vulgar as to say it.*

Lior bowed her head slightly and went to stand near the window, where the cold crept through the panes and kept her thoughts honest.

"Miss Winterbourne."

The magistrate's wife, Mrs. Corvane, a broad comfortable woman with rings on every finger. "How brave of you to come. After everything. It can't be easy, keeping that old place running all alone." Her voice was kind the way a padded cell is kind.

"The observatory keeps me well enough, madam."

"And the little foundling? We hear you've taken in a child." A pause, delicately measured, into which Lior could have poured anything. "That was charitable of you. Though some have wondered whether it's quite proper, a young woman alone up there with no one but a child for company and all those valuable instruments."

"I keep the founder's bequest, Mrs. Corvane. The terms are in the town record."

"Oh, records." Mrs. Corvane waved a ringed hand as though brushing away a moth. "Records are so dreary. More wine?"

There was a lesson in that exchange, and Lior took it the way she'd take a measurement off a worn gear: this was not a room where records carried weight. This was a room where weight was assigned, by people, over wine.

Dinner was called. Twenty covers laid in the long room under a portrait of some Corvane ancestor in a judge's wig. Lior found herself seated far down the table, below the salt and below notice, between the Harrow twins' factor and a tax assessor who talked at length about drainage rates without ever once requiring her to speak. That suited her. From the lower end of the table she could see the whole length of it, the way one sees the whole train of a clock from behind the dial — and she watched.

She watched the magistrate, Josiah Corvane, a narrow man with a narrow face and hands that folded themselves neatly on the tablecloth whenever he wasn't using them, fold themselves now. She watched him listen to Aldery, leaning a degree or two toward him, no more — the angle was subtle, the angle of a man who did not wish to appear to lean. She watched Aldery pour the magistrate's wine himself when the servant was slow, and watched Corvane accept it, and watched the two men share a look over the rim of the glass that had nothing to do with wine.

And she listened. Listening was the first thing her master had taught her — *the movement tells you where it's wrong, girl, but only if your ear is quieter than the mechanism.* She had spent ten years listening to clocks. Men were easier. They had more parts, and none of them were made to last.

* * *

After the plates were cleared and the port went round, the magistrate rose to speak.

He was not a gifted speaker, but he didn't need to be; he needed only to be understood. He spoke of Vellamarch's prosperity, of the river's bounty, of the coming spring and the trade it would bring. He spoke of law and order as though they were crops he had personally planted. And then, lifting his glass toward the head of the table where Aldery sat:

"—and to that end, we owe a debt to Mr. Edmund Aldery, whose stewardship of the observatory estate promises to bring that unfortunate entanglement to an orderly settlement. Orderly settlement!" He said the phrase again, tasting it. "Gentlemen, ladies — there is nothing our town requires more than orderly settlement. The dead leave matters tangled; it falls to the living to untangle them, quickly, cleanly, and for the common good. To Mr. Aldery."

"To Mr. Aldery," said the room.

Twenty glasses rose. Lior's stayed where it was, her fingers loose around the stem, resting on the cloth.

*Orderly settlement.* She turned the phrase over as the toasts went round. It was a good phrase. It had been machined, she thought, the way a dishonest movement is machined — every surface polished, every term fitted to another term, so that the whole assembly ran smooth and told you exactly the time its maker wished you to believe. *Orderly.* As though the founder's death had been orderly. As though a six-year-old child left on frozen steps in the night had been orderly. As though a sabotaged escapement buried in the heart of a public clock —

Her hand wanted to tighten. She felt the want travel down her forearm like a current looking for ground, and she let it pass through her and into the table, harmlessly, and kept her fingers soft.

Across the room, Aldery caught her eye over his glass. He did not smile. What he did was worse: he inclined his head a quarter-inch, a craftsman acknowledging a colleague, one professional recognizing another's discipline. *Well held,* the nod said. *You'll do.*

Lior smiled back — a small, correct, social smile, the kind that costs nothing and means nothing — and picked up her glass at last, and drank to Mr. Aldery with everyone else.

It was the first lie of the evening that required skill. The others were merely arithmetic.

* * *

Coffee was served in the drawing room, and with coffee came freedom of movement, and with freedom of movement came the real business of such evenings, conducted in murmurs at the edges of rooms. Lior stationed herself near the mantelpiece, beneath a marble clock — a French piece, ormolu and porcelain, pretty and dead, its pendulum hanging motionless because no one in that house had wound anything in years — and let the room come to her.

It came in pieces.

"—heard the estate papers are being drawn up already," the tax assessor murmured to the factor. "Corvane's clerk has had them in hand since January. Since *January*, do you mark me. Before there was even a hearing date."

"Efficient," said the factor.

"Purchased," said the assessor, and laughed, and both men glanced around to see who might have heard, and neither looked at the woman by the fireplace, because women who repaired clocks for a living were furniture, and one does not check whether the furniture is listening.

Near the window, Mrs. Quennel said to Mrs. Corvane, in a voice tuned to carry precisely four feet: "Of course the child complicates matters. Foundlings always do. Such sad creatures. Still, Mr. Aldery has been very generous about it — says the parish should take charge of the poor mite directly, for her own sake. The parish, or some respectable institution. Anything better than that cold observatory."

"How generous," said Mrs. Corvane.

"He does have a feeling for order."

*A feeling for order.* Lior turned her wine glass slowly on the mantel and thought about the word *order* the way she thought about a stripped thread — something that had once meant holding together, now worn smooth enough to mean whatever pressure was applied to it. Aldery had a feeling for order. The magistrate had drafted his rulings in advance and called it efficiency. The town's law was a clock with a false pendulum: it swung beautifully and kept whatever time its owner preferred.

And here was the thing she had come to understand tonight, standing in borrowed warmth among candles worth a winter's bread: she had walked in believing she was attending a dinner. She was attending an auction. Everything in this room was for sale — the hearing, the child's future, the meaning of words — and the bidding had opened months ago and closed before she ever knew it existed. The magistrate's toast was not a promise. It was a receipt.

Justice, if it came, would not come from this table. It would have to come from somewhere else entirely — from paper hidden in wood, from guild charters older than the magistrate's office, from the testimony of small people the great ones had forgotten to buy. Or it would not come at all.

"You've gone quiet, Miss Winterbourne."

Aldery. Of course. He materialized at her elbow the way cold comes through a cracked pane, holding two cups of coffee, offering her one. She took it because refusing would be a statement, and statements were not yet what she could afford.

"I'm admiring your clock," she said, nodding at the dead French piece above the mantel.

"Ah." He glanced up at it with mild distaste. "My late aunt's taste. It never worked properly, I'm told. Decorative objects rarely do."

"Everything works properly," Lior said, "if someone tends it."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; calculation, quick and clean, a jeweler assessing a stone. "Just so. Which brings me to a small matter." He lowered his voice, pitched it beneath the room's murmur, a private frequency. "When the estate is settled — and it will be settled soon, the magistrate is eager to have it behind us before the assizes calendar fills — there will be questions about certain documents. Papers belonging to my uncle. Correspondence, memoranda, that sort of thing. Nothing of value to a working clockmaker, but sentimental to a family." He sipped his coffee. "You've been keeping the observatory. You'd know if anything of that nature had... surfaced."

"The observatory contains a great many gears," Lior said, "and very little correspondence."

"Mm. But if something should surface. A drawer stuck, a case opened, an old housing come apart in your hands." His eyes went, briefly and without emphasis, to the window — north, toward the river, toward the observatory's dark bulk on the hill. "I hope you'd bring it to me first. Rather than letting it fall into careless hands. There are people in this town, Miss Winterbourne, who collect old papers and build trouble out of them. I'd hate to see your name attached to trouble. You've worked so hard to stay unattached to everything."

The words were courteous. The words were almost gentle. And underneath them, precise as a mainspring under tension, lay the threat entire: *I know you've found something. Give it to me, and remain unattached — which is to say, safe, silent, and alone, as you have been for ten years.*

Lior looked at him and thought of the girl asleep upstairs at the observatory, curled around a stolen cog the way other children curl around dolls. She thought of her master's bench, kept exactly as he'd left it, dust settling into the grooves of tools she still could not bear to move. She thought: *you mistake me for my silence, sir, and my silence is not me. It is only the shape I was made to fit.*

"I'll certainly keep it in mind, Mr. Aldery," she said, and smiled her small correct smile, and drank her coffee, and kept her hands perfectly still.

* * *

She left early, pleading the walk and the ice. At the door, wrapping her cloak while the servant fetched nothing fast enough, she heard behind her the sound of the party reconstituting itself around her absence — laughter resuming, glasses refilled, the evening closing over the space she'd made like water over a dropped coin.

Outside, the cold took her breath and gave her back her own thoughts. She stood a moment on Cordwainer Hill looking down at the town: the dark lanes, the few lit windows, the black line of the river beyond, its ice lying flat and white under the stars, patient as a filed document. Somewhere under that ice the water was moving, had been moving all winter, working at the underside of things. No one above it believed the break would come early. Everyone had scheduled the season to suit themselves.

Let them, she thought. She had spent the evening among people who believed the world was a machine they had already assembled, sealed, and regulated — a machine in which the magistrate's ruling was cut before the hearing was held and the toast was simply the chime announcing a hour already agreed upon.

But she was a clockmaker. And she knew what every clockmaker knows, what the whole trade exists because of: no assembly is perfect. Every sealed case opens. Every false pendulum can be replaced with a true one, given tools, given time, given hands steady enough to hold the truth of a thing against the weight of it.

She began the long walk down through the frozen streets, her boots ringing on the glazed cobbles, and behind her the magistrate's windows blazed on, advertising warmth to a town that couldn't afford candles.

At the foot of the hill, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step beside her — and Lior's heart went hard and quick, until the lamplight found a shawl and a gray braid, and the bent shoulders of Widow Kessel, waiting for her in the cold with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth held tight against her chest.

"Lior," the old woman said, and her voice was not the voice she used for customers. "Walk with me. There's something you need to see before you trust anyone else in this town — and I'm afraid, child, that includes the dead."
