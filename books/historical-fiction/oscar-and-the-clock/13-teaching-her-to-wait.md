## 13. Teaching Her to Wait

The girl had a way of standing at the bench that made Lior's skin prickle — not with annoyance, but with recognition. It was the stance Lior herself had worn at nine years old, chin just clearing the edge of her master's workbench, watching his hands the way other children watched fireworks.

"You're breathing on it again," Lior said, without looking up from the wheel she was polishing.

"I'm not."

"Your breath moves the dust. The dust scratches the brass." She turned the wheel in the light. "Everything you do to a clock, you do forever. There's no taking back a scratch."

The girl — who had been nameless for three weeks now, called only *the little one* or *child* by everyone including, guiltily, Lior herself — leaned back on her heels and exhaled at the ceiling with theatrical care, as if her breath might travel up through the rafters and bother the stars instead.

Lior bit down on a smile. It wouldn't do to reward impertinence, even the kind that was secretly correct.

It had been six weeks since the child arrived on the observatory steps, wrapped against the February cold in a blanket that smelled of another household's soap. Six weeks of porridge and questions, of small feet on observatory stairs, of a girl who slept curled against the clock's case because — she said — it sounded like someone talking in their sleep. Six weeks during which Lior had told herself every morning that this arrangement was temporary, that somewhere there must be family, that the note pinned to the blanket — *forget the face, finish the work* — meant nothing good, and that a clockmaker with creditors circling her dead master's workshop could not afford entanglements.

And six weeks during which Lior had, without deciding to, begun teaching.

It happened the way water finds a channel. The girl wanted to help; Lior needed hands that didn't tremble; and so the lessons began — how to hold a burnisher, how to read the shadow of a bent tooth, how to listen to an escapement the way you'd listen to a sick person's chest.

Today's lesson was patience, which was to say today's lesson was failure.

"Again," Lior said, setting the pallet knife in the girl's small hand.

They were fitting a new click spring to the winding ratchet, a job Lior could have done alone in four minutes. With the child, it had already consumed half the afternoon. The spring kept leaping free of the tweezers and vanishing into the sawdust on the floor, where it lay invisible as a hair.

"Why does it matter," the girl said — not whining, exactly, though close — "if we finish it today? Nobody winds this clock anyway."

"The master clock keeps the town's time—"

"It doesn't run."

"It will." Lior heard her own voice go flat and certain, and something in the child's face changed — she straightened, the way a plant straightens toward light. "When it runs, everything under it answers to it. Every clock in Vellamarch that wants to be right will set itself by what this one says. So the work matters today, and it matters tomorrow, and it matters whether anyone is watching or no one is."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she knelt on the floorboards and began hunting the lost spring among the shavings, patient as a heron.

That was the thing about her, Lior thought. She fought instruction like any child fights it — with sighs and sidelong mutiny — but once she accepted a task, she gave it everything. No hedging. No keeping a portion of herself in reserve. When she listened, she listened with her whole spine.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. As if a face like that could be forgotten. As if a whole spine could be overlooked.

The girl found the spring and held it up between finger and thumb, triumphant, her cheeks flushed from kneeling. "Got it."

"So you did. Now—" Lior guided her fingers to the ratchet wheel, positioned the spring's tail in its seat. "Don't push. Hold. Let the metal decide when it's home. You can't hurry a spring. It goes in when it agrees to go in."

"That's stupid."

"That's clockwork."

The girl pressed her lips together, held, waited — three seconds, five — and the spring seated itself with a click so soft Lior nearly missed it. The child's eyes went wide.

"It agreed," she whispered, as if the mechanism were a creature that had chosen to be tamed.

"It always does," Lior said. "Eventually. That's the second rule."

"What's the first?"

"Never breathe on the work."

* * *

That evening they ate in the observatory's ground room: bread and cheese and the last of the pickled onions, the lamp turned low because oil cost money and the workshop's accounts were a slow hemorrhage Lior checked nightly like a pulse. Outside, the river ice boomed and settled, a sound like a giant turning over in bed. The break couldn't be far off now. Ten days, perhaps less.

The girl chewed, watched Lior over her bread, and said, "You talk funny."

"I beg your pardon."

"When you're scared. You say things twice. Or you say them sideways." The girl took another bite, chewing with the frankness of the very young. "Like when Mr. Aldery came. He asked about papers, and you said, 'I'm sure I wouldn't know anything about that,' which means you know something about that."

Lior set down her knife very carefully.

"And when Widow Kessel told you to burn things, you said 'perhaps' and 'I'll think on it,' and you meant *no*. I could tell. Your mouth says maybe and your hands say no." The girl pointed with her fork, merciless. "Your mouth does one thing and your hands do another. Clocks don't do that. A clock that said one time with its hands and another with its chime would be broken."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, in the tower, the dead master clock stood in its silence, holding its secrets in its belly.

"Is that so," Lior managed.

"You should say what you mean," the girl said, with the terrible simplicity of a six-year-old handing down judgment. "Then people would know where they are. Like knowing where the hands are. If the hands lie, the clock's no good to anybody, is it?"

"No," Lior said quietly. "It isn't."

She thought of ten years of *perhaps* and *I'll think on it*. Ten years of serving difficult customers last, as her master had taught her; of never asking why Aldery's name appeared so often in the ledger; of saying *I'm sure I wouldn't know* with a straight face while her heart hammered. Silence had been her craft as much as clockwork — learned early, practiced daily, worn smooth as a polished arbor.

A six-year-old had just told her, over pickled onions, that silence was a broken clock.

"I'll consider it," Lior said.

The girl frowned. "That's a hedge."

Lior laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, startled out of her, the first in weeks. The girl grinned around her bread, pleased with herself beyond all measure, and the sound of the two of them filled the cold room better than the stove did.

* * *

Nights were harder. The girl still woke sometimes, crying out from dreams she couldn't describe — a carriage, a woman singing, a man's voice saying *not yet*, fragments that surfaced and sank again like things beneath river ice. On those nights Lior sat on the edge of the child's cot in the little room off the clock chamber, and they listened together to the great clock ticking its slow, patient tick — the only part of it still alive, the pendulum swinging in the dark like a metronome counting down to something.

"Tell me about the singing woman," Lior said once, gently.

"She sang a song about a boat. And then the man said not yet, and then—" the girl's face screwed up with effort, "—then it's fog. Fog after that."

"Do you remember her face?"

"In the fog I do." The girl's hand found Lior's sleeve and gripped it. "In the day I don't. Isn't that strange?"

"It's not strange. Night remembers differently." Lior smoothed the blanket. "Go back to sleep. The clock's watching. Nothing gets past it."

"Does it watch all night?"

"All night."

"Even the bad parts?"

"*Especially* the bad parts," Lior said. "That's when clocks are most useful. Anyone can keep time in daylight."

The girl's grip loosened. Within minutes she was breathing slow and deep, her cheek slack against the pillow, and Lior sat a moment longer in the dark, listening to the pendulum, thinking about hands and mouths saying different things, about springs that went in only when they agreed, about how long a thing could wait in the dark before it came out.

Her master had waited ten years — hiding whatever he hid inside that clock case, taking Aldery's money, keeping his silences polished. And now the waiting was ending, whether Lior willed it or not. The ice would break. Aldery had posted notice; the hearing would come with the ferry; and everything hidden — the papers in the case, the girl's identity, Lior's own decade of convenient blindness — would be dragged into the light and laid on some magistrate's table.

*Say what you mean,* the child had ordered her. *If the hands lie, the clock's no good to anybody.*

* * *

Three mornings later, the lesson reversed itself.

Lior was struggling with the strike train's locking plate — a piece of the chime mechanism that had defeated her twice already — when the girl climbed onto the stool beside her and watched her fumble the same measurement for the third time.

"You're doing it wrong," the girl said.

"I'm aware."

"You're doing it wrong *because* you're being careful. You keep measuring it halfway and then stopping, like you don't want to know the real number." The girl pointed a jam-sticky finger at the calipers. "Just measure it all the way. Say the number out loud. Numbers don't get hurt."

Lior stared at her.

Because the child was right, and not merely about the locking plate. Lior had been measuring everything halfway for ten years — her master's debts, his silences, the shape of the danger around the observatory. She took readings she could live with and stopped short of the ones that would ruin her. She knew the founder's will existed inside the clock; she refused to ask herself what it would cost to bring it out. She knew the girl was more than a foundling; she refused to follow the thought to its end.

"Just measure it," the girl said again, softer. "Numbers don't get hurt. People do, but only when they don't know the number."

So Lior closed the calipers fully, all the way, and read the figure aloud in a clear voice — 3.72 millimeters — and the locking plate fit on the first try, and the strike train turned in her hands like something waking up.

"There," the girl said, satisfied. "You see?"

"I see." And Lior did see — further than the girl knew. That evening she took the founder's register down from its shelf, the old visitor book she'd been circling for weeks the way one circles a sore tooth, and set it under the lamp, and opened it, and this time she did not stop at the entries she could bear.

She measured all the way.

Page by page, year by year, the founder's guests marched past in faded ink — aldermen and astronomers, guild men and river captains — until she reached an entry from eleven years past, written in a slanting feminine hand:

*Mrs. Elara Vane and daughter, expected fortnightly. The founder's own request.*

Daughter. *Expected fortnightly* — regular visits, a family's rhythm, hidden in plain sight in a book anyone could have read.

And below it, in the founder's own cramped script, a single line that stopped Lior's breath:

*The child is to be raised knowing the clock. Time is the only inheritance that cannot be stolen.*

Lior looked up the stairs toward the little room where the girl slept, toward the pendulum's slow swing in the dark. Her hands, resting on the open register, had gone perfectly still — steadier than they'd been in weeks, as if they too had finally been given a true reading and knew what to do with it.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would follow the name Elara Vane through the parish records, all the way, no halfway measures.

Tonight she climbed the stairs and stood a long time in the doorway, watching the sleeping child, memorizing the face she had been instructed to forget.

Outside, far down the valley, the river gave a long groan — the ice shifting, the deadline moving closer, the world preparing to break open.
