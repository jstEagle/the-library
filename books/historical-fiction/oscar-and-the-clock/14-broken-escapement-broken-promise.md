## 14. Broken Escapement, Broken Promise

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Lior had learned, in ten years of mending other people's time, that Vellamarch kept its hours honestly. Bakers knocked before dawn. Doctors knocked before midnight. Nobody knocked at half past two in the morning except trouble, or grief, and she could not have said which one she expected when she lifted the latch of the observatory's side door with the poker already in her hand.

It was the wind. Only the wind, worrying a loose shutter on the east face, knocking it against its frame in a rhythm too regular to be innocent. She stood in the doorway in her nightdress with her feet going numb on the stone and listened until the rhythm broke — three knocks, then four, then nothing — because honest things broke their patterns and guilty ones kept them.

She was about to close the door when she saw that the lamp in the instrument room was burning.

She had banked it herself at eleven. She remembered the particular economy of it, the wick turned low, the flame no taller than a thumbnail, because oil cost money she did not have and the observatory's accounts were now, by some legal alchemy she did not fully understand, partly Aldery's. The lamp she was looking at through the doorway of the transit room burned high and yellow, and beneath it, on the long bench where the master clock's dismantled heart lay under linen, something moved.

Not a person. Not quite. A shape crouched low, broad-shouldered, working at the bench with its back to her, and there was a sound coming from it — a small, rhythmic, terrible sound, like someone eating something delicate.

She knew before she raised the poker what the sound was. She had made that sound herself, forty times, with tweezers in her mouth while she worked. It was the click of steel teeth against teeth. Someone was handling her escapement.

"Step away from that."

Her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened by sleep, and it was enough. The shape straightened, turned — not quickly, which frightened her more than speed would have — and she saw it was a man in a plain dark coat with his collar up and a scarf across the lower half of his face, so that what remained visible was only his eyes, and his eyes were calm. That was the detail her mind seized and would not release afterward: he was not startled. He had heard her come down the stairs; he had let her find him; he stood in the lamplight as if he had been posed there.

"Good evening, miss," he said through the scarf. His voice was educated and unhurried. "You should go back to bed. This is above your head."

"This is my work." She tightened her grip on the poker and hated how young her knuckles looked, white and thin. "Put it down."

He looked down at the bench, and she followed his gaze, and that was when the bottom of the night opened under her.

The replacement escapement lay in pieces on the linen — not disassembled, which is a word with order in it, but *broken*. The pallets she had filed over six days to tolerances finer than a hair were snapped at their roots. The anchor bar was bent into an angle no force of falling could account for; it had been bent deliberately, slowly, by someone who knew exactly where steel gives up. Her escape wheel — the wheel she had cut and crossed and jeweled, the finest thing she had ever made, the thing that was supposed to give the old clock back its voice — had been gripped in something heavy and twisted, and its teeth were sheared off in a crescent of bright wreckage, and the teeth lay scattered across the linen like spilled grain.

And the man was picking up one of those teeth between finger and thumb, examining it against the light, the way a buyer examines goods.

"You cut this yourself?" he asked. There was something almost respectful in it, and that respect was the cruelest thing in the room. "That's good work. Guild-standard, near enough. Waste of good work, this." He set the tooth down precisely where it had lain, aligned with the others, tidying.

"I'll call the watch."

"The watch," he said, tasting the word, "is asleep. And when it wakes, it will find nothing but a broken clock and a nervous apprentice who has been telling stories about gentlemen's papers." He took a step toward her. He did not hurry. "Here is what I'm instructed to tell you. The clock is dead. It has been dead for ten years, and dead things stay buried unless somebody digs them up. You've been paid honest money to do honest repair work, and honest repair work means you make the clock *go*, miss — not make it speak. There's a difference, and I'm told you're clever enough to know it."

"There's nothing in that clock but wheels," Lior said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "There isn't. Keep it so." He pulled the scarf down from his face as he passed her — deliberately, letting her see him, a heavy jaw, a scar through one eyebrow, a face she did not know and understood instantly that she was meant to remember — and went out the side door into the dark, unhurried as a man leaving church.

She locked the door behind him with hands that would not obey her. Then she stood a long time with her forehead against the cold wood, listening to the wind worry the shutter, and only when she was sure her legs would hold did she turn around and look at what was left of her six days' work.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the bench, cataloguing the ruin the way she catalogued everything, because method was the only prayer she trusted. Broken pallets: unfixable, the grain of the steel fractured past any filing. Bent anchor: scrap. Escape wheel: she turned it over twice, looking for one sound tooth, and found none. Fourteen hours of file-work and a fortnight of thought, destroyed in perhaps five minutes by someone who had known exactly where to reach in the dark.

And that was the arithmetic that would not close.

The escapement had not been on the main bench. She had hidden it — hidden it well, she had believed — wrapped in oilcloth inside the case of the dead regulator clock in the north alcove, the one nobody used, behind the false panel her master had built into every cabinet he ever made. Three living souls knew that hiding place existed. Herself. The girl, who could not open the false panel alone and who had been asleep in the loft above with the ladder drawn up, and who had slept through the whole affair with the dreamless honesty of a six-year-old. And one other.

Widow Kessel.

Lior sat very still while the light came gray through the east windows and lit the wreckage gold at its edges, and made herself think it through like a train of gears, tooth by tooth, because thinking was better than feeling and she had a fortnight of practice at substituting one for the other.

She had gone to the widow's house nine days ago. She had gone because the escapement needed a lathe better than hers and the widow's back room still held her husband's tools, and because part of her — the part that still grieved like a limb that wouldn't stop aching — had wanted simply to sit in a room that smelled of him. She had cut the pallet blanks there. She had finished them there. And at some hour past midnight, drinking the widow's bitter chicory, she had said — why had she said it? — *I'll keep the finished piece at the observatory, behind the panel in the old regulator; no one thinks to look in a dead clock.*

The widow had nodded. Had said, *Aye, clever,* in that flat way of hers, and had turned to the fire, and had said nothing more about anything at all.

Three people. And the man last night had walked to the north alcove without lighting a second lamp, without searching, without opening a single drawer. He had gone to the false panel the way a man goes to a cupboard in his own kitchen.

Unless. Unless he hadn't needed telling. Unless Aldery's people had watched the observatory — they surely watched it — and seen *her* habits, seen her slip out to the alcove at odd hours, seen enough to guess. That was possible. She held on to possible the way a drowning woman holds on to a spar, and examined it, and found it waterlogged. Possible, yes. But the panel was a quarter inch proud of its frame and painted the same color as the wall, and she had once watched a magistrate's clerk stand three feet from it for half an hour and never mark it. Strangers didn't see it. People who had grown up around her master's cabinets saw it the moment they walked in, because they knew the trick of the eye — the seam that caught shadow differently.

Who had grown up around her master's cabinets? She had. The widow had. And —

And the girl.

Lior closed her eyes and hated herself for even holding the thought. The girl had been asleep. The girl was six. The girl had cried for an hour over a cracked marble and would cry again if the marble turned out to be beyond mending. Whatever else was true in the world, that small fierce honest creature was not standing in a dark room twisting steel.

But the circle was three, and one of the three was a widow who had told her, twice, in words that had seemed like love, to burn the papers and take the child to the coast and let the dead keep their secrets. And one of the three was a child who talked to strangers in shops, who repeated overheard sentences with a parrot's fidelity, who had been seen — Lior had seen it herself — chattering cheerfully to the coal man about *the pretty broken clock and how Lior was making it a new heart and hiding it where Grandfather's other clocks slept.*

Hiding it where Grandfather's other clocks slept.

Lior put both hands flat on the bench to stop them shaking.

The coal man delivered to half of Vellamarch. The coal man carried gossip the way his sacks carried dust. If the girl had said those words to the coal man, she had said them to the fishwife and the baker's boy and anyone else who smiled at her, and any one of those mouths might have fed Aldery for a coin, or fed him for free, for the pleasure of mattering.

So it might be no one she loved. It might be everyone. It might be that in a river town there were no circles at all, only ripples, and she had spent a fortnight dropping stones and calling the spreading rings a secret.

She got up and swept the broken teeth into an envelope, all of them, counting each one onto the linen first, because a clockmaker counts. Forty-eight teeth, and she found forty-seven, and spent twenty minutes on her knees on the cold flags searching for the forty-eighth before she found it wedged in the seam of the floorboards, and dropped it into the envelope with the others, and sealed it, and wrote on the outside, in the small hand her master had drilled into her: *Escapement no. 2. Destroyed. 14 March.* As though it were a patient she had lost. As though the ledger of her life were kept somewhere and must be honest even here.

Then she went up to the loft, where the girl slept in a nest of blankets with the stolen brass cog — the little one she'd fished from the scrap drawer weeks ago and refused to surrender — clutched in one fist like a talisman, and Lior stood over her a long time, watching her breathe, doing the terrible arithmetic of trust.

* * *

She did not sleep. When the sky went from black to iron she dressed, woke the girl gently, fed her bread and dripping at the workbench because the stove in the kitchen smoked and the workbench was warmest, and told her, in the tone she used for instructions that admitted no discussion:

"We're going out this morning. Both of us. And today, when people ask you questions, you answer them with weather."

The girl chewed and considered this. "Weather?"

"Rain. Cold. The river." Lior crouched to the child's level and took her small sticky hands. "Listen to me. Last night a man came into this house and broke something I made, and I don't know yet how he knew where to look, and until I know, I want you to talk about nothing but the weather to anyone who isn't me. Can you do that?"

The girl looked at her with those grave dark eyes that sometimes seemed far older than six, and Lior braced for the question — *why? who? what did he break?* — and the girl said only:

"Was it the man with the scarf? Because I heard the door."

The world went very quiet and very large. "What did you say?"

"I heard the side door." The girl said it patiently, the way one explains rain to the slow. "In the night. It does a sound — the hinge goes *ee* at the top. I was awake because I'd been counting the big clock's silence. It's easier to count when it's silent. And the door went *ee*, and then later it went *ee* again, and I thought it was you checking the lamps, but your boots go *tick-tick* on the stairs and these boots didn't. So I stayed under the blankets, because you said if I ever hear boots that aren't yours I should stay under the blankets and count."

Lior's mouth had gone dry. "You counted. How long?"

"Till the door went *ee* again." The girl shrugged. "Four hundred and twelve. Then I slept." She picked up her bread. "Is the new heart very broken?"

"It's broken."

"Will you make another one?"

"Yes."

"Better than the last one?"

Lior looked at her — at this small person who had lain awake in the dark, heard a stranger enter and leave, done exactly what she'd been taught, and slept soundly after — and felt something in her chest crack along a line that had nothing to do with steel. "Better," she said. "Harder to find, too. Now eat, and then we're going to town, and you're going to talk about the river being loud and the sky being gray, and nothing else. Nothing else at all."

"Even to Mrs. Kessel?"

The bread stopped halfway to the girl's mouth. Lior realized the child had asked the one question she herself had been circling all night, the way children walk straight through walls adults spend years building.

"Why do you say Mrs. Kessel?" Lior asked carefully.

"Because she asked me where we keep things." The girl said it lightly, licking butter from her thumb. "Weeks ago. When she came to bring the jam. She asked where we keep the good tools and where we keep papers and whether Grandfather's clocks have secret drawers, and I showed her the regulator's drawer because she's your friend, and she said *clever girl.*" The girl frowned slightly, sensing at last the change in the temperature of the room. "Was that wrong?"

Down in the instrument room the morning light was climbing the wall, touching the north alcove, touching the dead regulator with its false panel hanging open as she had left it since dawn, gaping like a small mouth that had finally finished saying what it had to say.

"No," Lior heard herself say. "No, sweetheart. It wasn't wrong. Eat your bread."

But her mind was already moving, fast and cold, down a corridor she did not want to walk. The widow had asked the child. The widow had known before Lior ever confided — or the widow had confirmed what the child showed her, or — no. Simpler. Simpler and worse: the widow had asked a six-year-old where the secrets slept, and the six-year-old had answered honestly, because that is what children do, and Lior had then handed the same secret to the same widow a week later like a gift, trusting her with it twice over.

Why would the widow need to ask the child, if Lior was going to tell her anyway?

Unless the widow had needed to know *before* — before some visit, some message, some accounting with men like the one with the scarred eyebrow. Unless the flat voice and the turned shoulder and the advice to burn everything had not been fear for Lior at all, but bookkeeping. Unless the widow Kessel had spent the winter balancing her own accounts, and the entries ran both directions.

There was a letter in Lior's coat pocket. It had come two days ago, and she had not opened it, because the handwriting was the widow's and the seal was unbroken and some animal part of her had known, the way one knows weather coming, that whatever was inside would change the shape of the winter. She had told herself she'd read it when there was time. There would never be time. Time was the one thing the man with the calm eyes had taken from her last night along with the steel.

She took the letter out and set it on the bench between the bread plate and the envelope of broken teeth.

The girl looked at it, then at her. "Are you going to open it?"

Lior picked up her knife and slid it under the flap, and outside, far downriver, the ice gave a long groan like something turning over in its sleep.
