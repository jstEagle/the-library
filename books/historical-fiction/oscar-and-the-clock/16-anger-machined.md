## 16. Anger, Machined

The ledger lay on the workbench where the widow's messenger had left it, and for a long time I could not touch it.

I had read it once already — twice, if you counted the reading I did with my eyes shut, the numbers still burning there in the dark behind my eyelids. Payments received from Mr. E. Aldery, quarterly, for eleven years. Small sums at first. Larger ones later. And beside each entry, in my master's neat, upright hand — the same hand that had taught me to cut a tooth, to true an arbor, to hold a file so it sang instead of scraped — the same word, over and over: *For the keeping of the clock.*

Not the repair of it. Not the maintenance of it.

The *keeping* of it.

I stood in the workshop with the dawn coming grey through the east window and the child asleep upstairs on the cot, and I understood that everything I had built my life upon was a machine with a sabotaged escapement of its own. It had run. It had kept time. But something inside it had been broken on purpose, by the one person I had trusted above all others, and every honest tick it had ever made was a lie told beautifully.

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake, the way I'd watch a balance wheel flutter when it was out of poise, and I thought: *this will not do.* A clockmaker whose hands tremble is no clockmaker at all. My master had said that to me when I was twelve and grieving my mother, standing at his bench while my fingers fumbled every tool they touched. *Grief is not a fault in the mechanism, Lior. But it is a drag on the train. You must find where it enters the works, and you must machine around it.*

So I did what he had taught me, though he was six months dead and had betrayed me besides, and I have never been sure whether that made it more bitter or less.

I put on my apron. I lit the bench lamps against the grey morning. I took the ledger off the workbench and set it in the drawer where I kept the founder's half-written will and the register page, because those papers were evidence now, and this was too — evidence against him, evidence against myself and ten years of easy sleep — and then I turned to the lathe, and I began to work.

* * *

The escapement is the heart of a clock. Everything else in the mechanism merely passes time along; the escapement alone decides how time shall be measured. Each swing of the pendulum releases one tooth of the escape wheel, and each release is a small, precise permission — *yes, now, this much, no more* — and the whole honesty of the instrument depends on the shape of that permission being perfect.

The original escapement of the observatory's master clock had been destroyed. First by Aldery's saboteur, who had filed the pallets wrong so the failure would look like neglect; then, after I discovered the sabotage, by whoever had come through the observatory window three nights ago and hammered the replacement I had spent nine days machining into a scrap of twisted brass.

Three people knew where that replacement was kept. The thought had been sitting in my chest like a stone ever since, but this morning I lifted it out and looked at it properly, the way you look at a wheel under the loupe. The widow Kessel, who had sent me the ledger. The ferryman's boy, Tam, who had carried papers for me by night. And myself.

Or — no. That wasn't quite true, was it. The guildmaster knew I was cutting a new one. He had asked me directly, in his dry way, whether I meant to certify the clock with a sound escapement or only with a story about one. So four people, then. Perhaps five. Perhaps the number would keep growing the way cracks grow across river ice, silently and all at once.

It didn't matter. That was the decision I made at the lathe, and I made it with the same finality as a punch mark on brass: it didn't matter who had broken the first escapement or the second. What mattered was that I could cut a third, and that no one — not Aldery, not his hired file-men, not the ghost of my own beloved master — could stop me from cutting it well.

Anger, I discovered over the next fourteen hours, machines very much like grief does. You cannot make it go away. You can only find where it enters the works, and machine around it.

* * *

The stock came from the drawer of oddments my master had kept against just such days — a bar of hard bell-brass, older than me, bought I don't know when from some foundry that probably no longer existed. He had always said good escapements were not designed but *recognized*, that the metal itself told you what it wanted to become if you listened long enough with your fingertips. I had thought it mysticism when I was young. Standing at the lathe that morning, I understood it exactly. You cannot force brass into honesty. You can only remove everything that isn't honest, and see what remains.

I faced off the stock first, taking light cuts, letting the tool do its work without hurry. The shavings came off in fine bright curls that dropped onto the bench like curls of hair, and I found a rhythm, and the rhythm found me.

Somewhere in the second hour the shaking left my hands.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had that day, and I hoarded even my own memories of it. It did not leave gradually. It went the way a knot goes out of a wire — one turn past the point of resistance, and suddenly straight. One moment I was filing the blank round and hating a dead man with every stroke; the next, I was simply filing, and the hate had gone *into* the filing, dissolved into it like salt into water, invisible and everywhere and doing useful work.

I measured. I marked out. I scribed the pallet faces with the sharp-pointed scriber my master had given me at fourteen, on the day I swore the oath — *to protect the work above all things* — and I wondered, not for the first time and not gently, what he had thought I was swearing to protect. The clocks? Or the silence?

The pallets were the soul of the job. An anchor escapement's pallets must engage the escape teeth at exactly the right depth, at exactly the right angles, or the clock will either lose time like sand through open fingers or beat itself to death against its own constraints. I had cut two sets already in my life. This third set I cut slower than either, checking each face against my angle-plate a dozen times, stoning each surface until it reflected the lamp flame as a single unbroken line.

And all the while I talked to him. In my head. Silently, viciously, exhaustively.

*You took his money,* I told him, as the graver peeled a curl of brass off the pallet blank. *Eleven years of it. You sat at this bench and you taught me never to take a dishonest commission, never to pad a bill, never to let a clock leave this shop telling anything but the truth — and every quarter, some messenger brought you Aldery's coin for the keeping of the clock, and you banked it, and you said nothing.*

The lamp flame wavered in the draft from the door. I steadied it with my hand cupped around the glass, and went on cutting.

*You hid the will inside the case,* I told him, turning the work, inspecting, turning again. *Why? If you meant to be silent forever, why hide it where an apprentice might find it? Unless you hoped. Unless some part of you — the part that wrote the teaching notes, the part that made me swear the oath — was waiting all those years for someone braver than yourself to come along and finish what you couldn't.*

That was the thought that had come to me in the night, rereading his notes, finding the coded apology folded between the lines of instruction like a letter hidden in a book. He had hidden the will hoping. And he had taken Aldery's money *because* he had lost hope. Both things at once. A man is not one escapement; he is a whole train of wheels, some of them turning forward, some backward, and the truth of him is only the sum.

It did not absolve him. I want to be clear about that too, since clarity was my religion that day. Forgiveness without absolution: that was the settlement I reached somewhere around the ninth hour, with brass dust in my hair and my shoulders screaming, and it held, and holds yet.

* * *

She came down sometime past noon. I heard her on the stairs before I saw her — she had learned to move quietly, but not quietly enough for a man who had grown up in a shop full of ticking, and certainly not quietly enough for me. I heard her pause at the bottom step, and I felt her watching me the way children watch fire: fascinated, respectful, keeping a safe distance.

"You're angry," she said at last.

"I'm working," I said, and did not look up from the stoning of the second pallet face.

"It looks like angry."

"Then it should teach you something," I said, before I could stop myself, "which is that anger and work can wear the same clothes."

I expected her to retreat. Six-year-olds retreat from adults who snap. Instead she crossed the floor — I heard the small determined footsteps — and climbed onto the stool at the far end of the bench, the one I kept for customers' children, and folded her hands, and watched.

I worked another quarter hour under that gaze. Then, because the pallet needed oiling for the final polish and my hands wanted a rest, I set down the stone and looked at her. She had Oscar's eyes — the founder's, her grandfather's — that pale watchful grey, and the stubborn line of mouth that I had seen in the portrait miniature hidden in the register. She was wearing the coat I had cut down for her from one of the observatory's old store-room curtains, green wool gone thin at the cuffs, and her hands were folded on the bench edge as neatly as any journeyman awaiting inspection.

"What does it do?" she asked. "The thing you're making."

"It decides," I said. "When the clock may move, and how much."

"Who tells it?"

"No one. It decides by its shape." I picked up the rough pallet casting and held it out to her. She took it with both hands, solemnly, the way she took everything — bread, letters, the stolen cog she had pressed against the workhouse bars — as though objects might be trusted or might not, and required weighing. "Feel the edges. They're rough now. When I'm finished they'll be smooth, and smoothness isn't prettiness, it's *truth*. A rough edge lies about where it begins and ends. A smooth one doesn't."

"Are you smoothing the anger?"

I looked at her. Children ask their questions sideways and hit the mark dead center; it is one of God's better jokes, and rarely a comfortable one.

"Yes," I said.

She nodded, satisfied, and gave the brass back, and settled in to watch, and did not speak again for a long time. But she stayed. Through the afternoon, through the grinding middle hours when the light went yellow and then copper in the west windows, she stayed on her stool, sometimes watching my hands, sometimes watching the unfinished clock case across the room where her grandfather's will waited inside the chime mechanism like a heart waiting to beat. Once she got up and fetched me the loupe without being asked, having noticed me reaching for it. Once she fetched nothing at all but stood close beside me for the space of ten breaths, looking up at the work, and then went back to her stool. That was all the conversation we needed. We had learned each other's language by then, hers and mine: the language of waiting done well.

* * *

By the eighth hour I had both pallets roughed, and began the fitting, which is where escapement work separates craftsmen from operators. You fit an escapement by trial, drop by drop — setting the pallets in the frame, offering the escape wheel, counting how many degrees of swing the tooth gives before release, adjusting a hair's width, trying again. A hair's width in brass is a lifetime in patience. I must have offered and withdrawn the wheel forty times before the drop came right: that soft, distinct *tick* with no slap behind it, no double-strike, the sound of a permission granted cleanly.

Tick.

Something in my chest released with it. I hadn't known I was holding anything until I heard the sound and felt it let go.

The child's head came up sharply at the noise. "It spoke."

"One tooth of it. There are thirty in the wheel, and each must speak the same way, and none may speak out of turn." I wiped my palms on my apron. "That's the whole secret of a clock, if you want it. Everyone thinks a clock is about moving. It isn't. It's about agreeing — every wheel with every other wheel — on exactly how much to move, and when, and stopping precisely there."

"Even when it's angry?"

"*Especially* when it's angry." I heard my own voice come out harder than I intended, and softened it. "An angry clock that rushes beats badly. An angry clock that stops has failed entirely. The only thing an angry clock may do is keep perfect time anyway, and let the anger drive the winding, and wait for the hour to strike."

She considered this with the gravity she brought to all matters mechanical. "Whose hour?"

Ah. Out of the mouths of six-year-olds.

"The town's," I said. "Everyone's. And one person's in particular." I met her eyes. "Yours."

She looked at the great dark case across the room — at the silent clock that had not struck in ten years, that would strike again when I finished with it, and when it struck, its chime mechanism would release the compartment my master had built, and the founder's sealed final will would come into the light of the world. She didn't know the details. She knew, in the way she knew the ticking sounded like home, that the big clock and her own fate were wound on the same arbor.

"When?" she said.

"Soon," I said, and it was not quite a hedge, and not quite a promise, and I hated myself a little for the space between the two words.

* * *

Hours eleven through fourteen belong to the polishing and the final assembly, and I will not pretend I remember them clearly, because by then I was running on craft alone, the fuel of conscious thought burned away hours since. What remains in memory is sensation: the stone whispering over brass, the smell of oil and hot metal, the ache spreading up from the base of my thumbs, the lamplight lying along the finished pallet faces like water lying along ice. Somewhere in there the child fell asleep on her stool, chin on her stacked fists, and I draped my coat over her shoulders without stopping work, and she slept through the clatter and scrape as she never slept through thunderstorms — because, I think, the sounds of the shop were the sounds of safety, whatever else in her short life had failed to be.

At the fourteenth hour, near midnight, I assembled the escapement complete in its frame, hung the test weight, and set the pendulum swinging.

It took. Of course it took; I had cut it true. The beat came steady and low, tick-tock, tick-tock, filling the workshop the way a heartbeat fills a chest, and I stood in front of it with my ruined hands hanging at my sides and my eyes burning, and let it count for me — sixty seconds, one hundred and twenty — before I trusted my voice.

"Well?" I said to the sleeping child, softly, absurdly, wanting her witness. "There's your answer. Fury can be machined. It cuts cleaner than almost anything, if you hold it in a vice and feed it slowly and never let it touch the measuring tools."

She didn't wake. The clock ticked. Outside, far off toward the river, I heard the ice groan — a long, deep, shifting complaint, the sound of a season straining against its own end — and I thought of the notice Aldery had posted: the hearing at the assizes when the ferry runs, and the ferry held in his pocket, and the legal term closing like a trap the day after the last safe crossing. He owned the law and the landing and most of the town's paper. But he did not own this bench. He did not own these fourteen hours. He did not own the small brass permission-giver now beating steadily in its frame, ready to be installed in the master clock, ready to give the great wheels back their license to move.

I cleaned the tools. Every one, back to its place in the rack, edges oiled, points capped — the discipline drilled into me by a man who sold his silence by the quarter-year, and I performed it anyway, because the discipline was mine now, not his, and I would not let his betrayal steal that too.

Then I opened the drawer and took out the ledger one last time, and set it beside the new escapement on the bench, side by side under the lamp: the record of the bribe and the answer to it. Eleven years of payments, and fourteen hours of brass.

Let him send his men again. Let them break what I build as fast as I build it. I would build it again, and again, and each time faster, because I had finally learned what my master tried to teach me at fourteen and I had understood only tonight:

A clock does not defeat the darkness. It simply refuses to stop, and the darkness, being nothing, cannot argue.

I blew out the lamps. And in the sudden black, listening to the test escapement tick and the river groan and the child breathe, I heard — faint, from the direction of the road — the creak of a carriage slowing, and stopping, outside the observatory gate.
