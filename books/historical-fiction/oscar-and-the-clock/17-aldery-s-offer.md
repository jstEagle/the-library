## 17. Aldery's Offer

He came on a Tuesday, in the gray hour between the lamplighters' rounds and the market bells, when the observatory was quietest and the town least likely to be watching.

Lior knew the knock before she heard it — not because it was distinctive, but because it was too careful. The workmen who came to the observatory knocked the way they did everything, heavily and without apology. The widow Kessel rapped twice and entered. But this knock was measured, three soft impacts spaced like the ticks of a well-regulated escapement, and it told her before she opened the door that the man on the other side had thought about how he wanted to be heard.

Mr. Aldery stood on the steps with his hat in his hands.

She had seen him at a distance a dozen times since her arrival — across the market square, at the head of a procession of creditors, once through the observatory's long window as he stood on the far bank looking up at the dome like a man appraising a horse he intended to buy. Up close he was smaller than she expected, and older, with gray coming in at his temples in a way that seemed deliberate, arranged, as though even his aging had been managed by an accountant. His coat was good wool, dark, cut for a man who did not want to look rich so much as solvent. Everything about him said: I am reasonable. Everything about him said: you may trust me, which is precisely why you should not.

"Miss Winterbourne." He inclined his head. "I hope the hour isn't inconvenient. I find that honest conversations go better before the town is awake to complicate them."

"The clock isn't running," Lior said. "That's the only inconvenience here."

"So I'm told." He smiled, and the smile was warm in the way a stove is warm — useful, controlled, dangerous if you leaned against it carelessly. "May I come in? I promise I won't touch anything."

He kept the promise, mostly. He walked through the lower hall of the observatory with his hands clasped behind his back like a man in a museum, pausing at the cases of instruments, reading the brass plaques with the air of someone who already knew what they said. Lior followed him, and hated herself a little for following him in her own building — except it wasn't her building, and they both knew it, and that was the whole reason he had come.

"You've made remarkable progress," he said, nodding toward the stair that led down to the clock chamber. "The guild men said the great wheel alone would take a month. You did it in nine days."

"I didn't do it in nine days," Lior said. "I did it in fourteen-hour days."

"Which is a kind of nine days." He turned and looked at her directly then, and she saw that the warmth in his face could be banked like a fire — not extinguished, but set aside when there was work to do. "You have your master's reputation for exactly that. I confess I've been curious about you for some time, Miss Winterbourne. Ten years in Vellamarch and we never once met properly. A man begins to wonder what a person is doing all that while, working so hard and being seen so little."

"Making clocks."

"Yes. Making clocks." He let the words sit. "And staying out of other matters entirely. It's one of the things your master was most admired for, you know. Discretion. Half the merchants in this town trusted him more than they trusted their own clerks, because he never repeated what he saw. A workshop full of secrets and not one of them ever leaked." He smiled again. "I always assumed it was a quality that couldn't be taught. And then here you are."

The observation was a blade wrapped in flannel. Lior recognized the technique because her master had taught it to her — though never for use, only for recognition: *when a man praises your silence, he has already priced it.* She folded her hands and waited.

Aldery took the hint, or appeared to. "I'll be direct, then. Directness saves us both the trouble of pretending afterward." He gestured toward a bench near the window, and when she didn't move, he sat anyway, unhurried, arranging himself as though the room had been prepared for him. "The estate will be heard at the assizes when the ferry runs. That's perhaps three weeks off, four if the ice is stubborn. When it is heard, certain documents will be produced, certain claims will be weighed, and the magistrate — who is, whatever else he may be, a careful man — will rule. The founder's bequest will either stand or fall. If it stands, the estate passes intact to whoever can prove blood. If it falls, the debts attach, and the property is sold to satisfy them. I hold most of those debts, Miss Winterbourne. I would hold all of them within the week."

"I know," Lior said.

"Of course you know. You're not a fool; no apprentice of his could be." He studied her with something that looked almost like pleasure. "Then you also know that the child complicates everything. A foundling left on the steps of a public institution is a charity case. A foundling with a name is a claimant. And a claimant, however small, however young, gives the magistrate a thread to pull, and careful men love pulling threads. So the question before us — before everyone, really, though few have the clarity to see it plainly — is whether that child is ever given a name."

"She has a name," Lior said quietly.

"Every child has a name," Aldery agreed. "Not every name survives scrutiny. There are registers, witnesses, proofs. A name is not a fact until the law says it is, and the law says what the evidence permits it to say. Which brings me to my offer."

He reached inside his coat and drew out a single sheet of paper, folded once, and laid it on the bench beside him without unfolding it — a gesture of such studied casualness that Lior understood instantly it had been rehearsed. He was letting her look at it, letting her wonder, letting the paper do its work before any words did.

"Your master's workshop on Cooper's Lane," Aldery said. "Freehold, clear title, every debt discharged as of the day you sign. The tools stay. The stock stays. The lease converts to ownership — I hold the mortgage myself, as it happens, one of the quieter acquisitions of the last year, and I am prepared to burn it. You would walk out of that hearing with the only thing in this town you have ever loved, owned outright, forever, and no one able to touch it again. Not the creditors. Not me. Not the flood, when it comes, though I'd advise insurance regardless." He unfolded the paper and smoothed it flat on his knee, but still did not offer it. "All it requires is a declaration. One line, witnessed, filed with the assizes clerk. That the child abandoned at the observatory is a foundling of no known parentage and no account to the estate. Nothing cruel. Nothing false, even — how could you prove otherwise? You'd simply decline to assert what you cannot honestly know. A statement of ignorance, Miss Winterbourne. The most respectable document in the world. Courts run on them."

The room was very quiet. Above them, through two floors of stone and timber, Lior could hear the faint tick of the temporary regulator she'd set running in the transit room — the only voice in the building besides their own. She thought of the girl upstairs, asleep in the cot she'd moved into the keeper's old dressing room, one arm flung over her eyes, the stolen brass cog from the clock case tucked under her pillow where Lior was strictly not supposed to have noticed it.

"You've thought about this," Lior said.

"I think about everything. It's my profession." Aldery's tone didn't change, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly mastered, of what might have been genuine feeling. "You believe I'm the villain of this piece. I'd expect nothing less; your master certainly did, God rest him, and he taught you to read the world the way he read movements — find the wheel that drives the rest. But I'll tell you something true, since I'm asking you to trade in truth. I don't want the girl harmed. I have no use for harming children, and less taste for it than my reputation suggests. What I want is order. My uncle built this observatory out of vanity and mathematics and left it to rot in a will nobody can find, and now every schemer in the valley is circling it like gulls after a fish cart. The girl is a hook for every one of those schemes. Whoever holds her name holds the estate, and whoever holds the estate holds this town's time itself — the almanac, the ferry schedules, the calibration every shipping firm on the river depends on. Do you imagine I'm the only one who understands that? I am merely the one who understands it first."

"And so the child should grow up nameless for the good of navigation."

"And so the child should grow up safe," Aldery said, and for a moment the banker's polish thinned and she glimpsed something underneath it — tiredness, maybe, or the ghost of a boy who had grown up in that same house on the hill watching an uncle love a clock more than any living thing. "Nameless children survive, Miss Winterbourne. I was nearly one myself. Named children with claims die of fevers, of accidents on stairs, of grief-stricken aunts who cannot bear the responsibility. You've been in this town ten years. You know what happens to inconvenient inheritances. I am offering her obscurity, which is the only protection that has never yet failed. And I am offering you the workshop, which is the only thing I have ever seen you look at the way my uncle looked at that clock."

He rose, finally, and held out the paper.

Lior did not take it.

"Read it tonight," Aldery said. "There's no deadline written on it, because deadlines insult serious people. But the ice is going out, the ferry is coming, and the clerk's office opens early. I'll call tomorrow at this hour for an answer — yes or no, nothing in between. In between is how towns like this one eat people alive." He put his hat back on, settling it with two fingers, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "One more thing, and I say it as a courtesy between people who understand value. Whatever you found in that clock — papers, letters, half-finished intentions — your master put it there to be found by someone with your choices ahead of you, not mine behind me. He spent years being paid to keep his mouth shut, and I paid him, and I liked him, and I will say at his grave that he was the best man whose silence money ever bought. Don't mistake that for a compliment. It's a warning about what silence costs the person holding it." He tipped the hat an inch. "Good morning, Miss Winterbourne. Do give the child my regards. Or don't. As suits you."

The door closed behind him with a click as precise as anything in the clock chamber.

* * *

Lior stood in the empty hall for a long moment, listening to the regulator tick above her, and then she went up the stairs on legs that felt borrowed.

The paper lay on the bench below, but she couldn't bring herself to fetch it yet. First she climbed to the dressing room and opened the door just wide enough to see. The girl was asleep exactly as she'd pictured, sprawled and boneless the way only children sleep, her breath slow and even, the lump under her pillow shaped unmistakably like a cog. On the wall above the cot Lior had pinned a scrap of paper with the first four letters of the alphabet copied out in her own careful drafting hand, because the girl had asked — *not for my name,* she'd said, *for names in general, in case I need mine later.* Lior had laughed at the time. Now the laugh came back up her throat sour.

*A foundling of no account.*

She tried to weigh it the way Aldery had presented it — as arithmetic. One signature, and the workshop survived: the benches, the lathes, the smell of oil and boxwood shavings, the last physical body of everything her master had been. Ten years of her life had been organized around keeping that room alive. She had eaten bread and dripping for a decade so the rent could be paid. She had watched the creditors circle after his death and understood, with the cold clarity of the apprentice sweeping the floor, that everything he built would be sold by the pound unless something intervened. And now the intervention stood in front of her wearing a good wool coat, offering exactly what she wanted, asking only that she unname a six-year-old child back into the fog she'd been carried out of.

It would even be kind, in its way. Obscurity as shelter. The girl would grow up somewhere fed and unremarkable, and no one would ever come up the river with questions, the way — the way no one had ever come up the river for Lior, either. She thought suddenly, violently, of her own childhood: the orphanage register, the blank where a father's name should have gone, the master choosing her at eleven not despite the blank but because of it. *A girl nobody owns can belong to the work,* he'd said. She had built her entire self on that foundation. She knew precisely what it cost, because she was standing in the ruins of it at forty-one minutes past seven in the morning.

And underneath the arithmetic, colder and clearer, sat the thing she had found screwed behind the striking-work plate. The half-written will. The heir named in a hand that trembled with illness or haste or both. Her master had hidden it where only a repairer's hands would reach — hidden it, or buried it, or planted it, she still didn't know which — and every day she kept it secret was another day the gears of Aldery's machine turned freely. He had called her master the best man whose silence money ever bought. He'd meant it as a warning. It landed like a diagnosis.

She went down, took up the paper, and read it by the window light.

It was exactly what he'd said: a declaration, one clause, two witness lines, drafted in fair clerk's hand. At the bottom, where her signature would go, someone had already ruled a neat guide-line in pencil — a kindness, a courtesy, the small mechanical aid offered to anyone about to sign away a child's name. She stared at that pencil line longer than at any clause. It was such a considerate detail. That was the horror of him. Not cruelty — accommodation. He had built a machine for destroying the girl and oiled every joint of it so it would run without friction, and the pencil line was the finest oil of all.

Lior carried the paper to the hearth in the keeper's office, held it over the coals, and stopped.

Not yet. Burning it now was a gesture, and gestures were for people who could afford them. If Aldery came tomorrow and she refused him with nothing but refusal in her hands, he would simply proceed — the hearing, the magistrate, the purchased verdict. No. If she was going to refuse the only safety she would ever be offered, she needed to understand what her master had actually left her. He had spent years taking Aldery's coin and saying nothing, and then, at the end, he had hidden a will inside a clock like a man swallowing a confession. There had to be a reason. There had to be instructions, or an apology, or at minimum an explanation of why he'd waited so long that waiting had become its own crime. His teaching notes were still in the drawer of the observatory desk where she'd brought them — twenty years of marginalia, exercises, corrections in his cramped left-slanting hand.

She put Aldery's unsigned offer in the drawer beneath them, face down, and locked the drawer, and stood with the key hot in her fist.

Upstairs, the girl slept on. Below, the regulator ticked. Somewhere out on the river, under the gray skin of ice, something shifted and groaned — the first deep note of the breakup, weeks early, the water underneath deciding it had waited long enough.

Lior unlocked the drawer again, took out her master's teaching notes, and began, page by page, to read them as she had never read anything in her life — not for the lessons on their faces, but for whatever he had hidden between the lines.
