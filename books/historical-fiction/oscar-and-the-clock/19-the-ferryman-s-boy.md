## 19. The Ferryman's Boy

The boy's name was Tam Osker, and he had been keeping the river's secrets since before his voice broke.

He was fourteen, the same age Lior Winterbourne had been when she'd sworn her apprentice's oath, though no one in Vellamarch would have drawn the comparison. Tam didn't swear oaths. He made bargains. It was how a ferryman's son survived — you learned what the water gave and what it took, and you learned that everything else in town ran on the same current. Money moved like the river. Favors moved like the river. Truth, most of all, moved like the river: it went where the channel cut deepest, and it drowned whatever stood in its way.

Tam kept the skiff that crossed the winter ice-gap at Miller's Bend, where the current ran fast enough to hold a channel open even in the cruelest February. It was dangerous work and illegal besides — the magistrate had posted notices against night crossings years ago, after a drover drowned with two lambs tied to his back — but the drover had been poor, and poor men's deaths changed nothing in Vellamarch. The crossing stayed open because people needed it, and people paid for what they needed, and Tam's father took the coin while Tam held the lantern.

That was the arrangement. His father ferried; Tam watched.

And on the night of the ninth of February, watching from the bend with the lantern shuttered to a slit, Tam saw something he would spend the rest of the chapter of his short life deciding whether to sell.

* * *

It had been a hard freeze that week. The ice on the main channel had gone black and thick, groaning all through the dark hours like an old man turning over in bed, but at Miller's Bend the water still showed itself — a seam of open current maybe eight feet across, steaming faintly in the cold, black as spilled ink. Tam knew the sound of that seam better than he knew his own breathing. He could stand on the bank and tell by the note of it whether the crossing would hold or whether the ice was working loose downstream.

His father had gone up to the Anchor & Chain for the evening, which meant Tam was alone with the skiff and the lantern and the long habit of watching.

He heard the carriage first.

That was strange in itself. No carriage came to Miller's Bend. There was no road worth the name down there — just the towpath and the shingle and the lean-to where they kept tar and rope. Carriages belonged up on Vellamarch Hill, where the observatory stood white under the moon like a skull wearing a crown, and along the merchant row where Aldery's counting house glowed yellow behind its shutters. Down at the bend there was nothing anyone wanted.

But he heard it — wheels on frozen mud, slow, careful, coming down the switchback path that wasn't meant for wheels at all — and then the lantern-light of it swinging through the bare alders, and Tam did the thing his father had taught him before anything else: he put out his own light and stepped back into the shadow of the tar-shed, and he watched.

The carriage stopped twenty feet from the water. It was a good carriage, closed, the kind with a crest on the door, and even in the dark Tam caught the shape of the crest — he'd seen it often enough nailed to crates on the merchant dock. A stag courant above crossed keys. The Aldery arms.

A woman got down.

She was dressed for travel, hooded, and she carried a bundle, and Tam's first thought — the honest, terrible first thought of a boy who had grown up on this river — was that she had come to drown it. Things were drowned at Miller's Bend. He had seen sacks go into the open water that had moved wrong inside the sacking. He had never told anyone about those either. The river kept what it took, and Tam kept what the river showed him, and between them they had never yet been caught.

But the woman didn't go to the water. She stood beside the carriage door a moment, and then she crouched, and set the bundle down on the shingle, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping cat, and Tam heard — thin across the frozen air — the unmistakable sound of a child crying.

Not loud. That was the worst of it. Not screaming, not wailing. Just the small cracked hiccup of a child who has cried herself empty and is doing it again on almost nothing, because there is nothing left to do with fear except leak it out.

The woman spoke to her. Tam couldn't catch the words — the wind off the channel chewed them up — but he caught the tone, and the tone was not unkind. Weary, hurried, but not cruel. Then the woman straightened, looked up at the observatory hill for a long moment — Tam followed her look, and saw the one lit window high in the tower, the window of the clock room, burning steady above the dark town like a star that had fallen upward instead of down — and then she did something Tam never managed to explain to himself afterward.

She hesitated.

She stood over the bundle on the freezing shingle with her hands half-lifted, and she hesitated, and in the light spilling from the carriage door Tam saw her face for three full seconds, and it was not a hard face. It was a tired one. Younger than he'd expected. And there was something in it that looked less like a servant abandoning a child than like a woman setting down a weight so heavy her arms shook with the relief of it — and hating herself for the relief.

Then she got back in the carriage, and it turned, careful as a funeral, and ground away up the switchback, and the child was alone on the bank of the black water, and Tam Osker stood in the tar-smell behind the shed and did nothing at all.

That was the part that sat in him afterward. Not the seeing — seeing was his trade. The *doing nothing*. He could have called out. He could have run up the towpath to the clockmaker's door, which was closer than anyone guessed, a quarter mile along the frozen path. He could have wrapped the girl in sacking and put her by his own stove. Instead he stood in the dark like his father had taught him, holding a secret the way you hold a netted fish — feeling it thrash, waiting to see what it would be worth.

By the time the clockmaker found the girl — and Tam learned within two days that she'd been taken in at the observatory, that the Winterbourne woman had carried her up through the snow — the story in town had already settled into the shape towns give such stories. A foundling. Left at the observatory steps, poor mite, God help it. The kind of story people told twice and then forgot, because foundlings happened everywhere and belonged to no one, and no one's coin depended on them.

Except someone's coin did depend on it. Tam understood that much without being told. Because a week later, a man came down to the bend.

* * *

He came on a Sunday afternoon, which Tam recognized immediately as theater. Nobody came to Miller's Bend on Sunday unless they wanted to be seen coming somewhere they shouldn't be seen — the whole town was at church or pretending to be, and the riverbank was as private as a confessional.

He was one of Aldery's men. Tam knew him by sight — Mr. Quill, the counting house under-clerk, a soft-bodied man with a clerk's pale hands and a walker's hard boots, the boots being the only honest thing about him. He walked down the towpath like a man inspecting property, stopped at the skiff, and looked at Tam for a while without speaking.

Tam looked back. This too was trade craft. First one to talk pays more.

"Your father crosses people," Quill said finally. "Night work. Winter rates."

"Father's at church," Tam said.

"I know where your father is." Quill smiled a little, showing he did, showing that he'd checked, showing that everything about this conversation had been checked. "I'm asking about you."

"What about me?"

"You keep the lantern. You watch the bend." Quill crouched down, forearms on knees, bringing his face level with Tam's, and Tam smelled ink and wet wool and, underneath it, the ghost of brandy taken early. "Something came across the water lately, boy. Or near it. Something small."

"There's ice coming down from the shallows every night," Tam said. "I see a great deal of something small."

Quill studied him. Tam had the peculiar sensation of being weighed, like scrap metal — not disliked, just valued, and the valuation happening behind eyes that had done this before, possibly many times.

"Aldery keeps this town," Quill said. "You'll notice I'm not whispering it. Everyone knows it; only the church pretends otherwise. And Aldery pays for information the way the mill pays for grain — fair rates, prompt settlement, no questions asked twice." He straightened, brushed his knees, and produced a coin — a crown piece, silver, bright enough to have been polished for the occasion. He set it on the thwart of the skiff, deliberately, where Tam would have to pick it up or leave it lying. "If you remember anything — a carriage, a woman, a bundle — you come to the counting house and ask for me. If you remember nothing, the crown buys your silence anyway. Aldery considers silence a purchase like any other."

He walked back up the towpath. Tam waited until the alders swallowed him, then picked up the crown, bit it out of pure ritual, and put it in his pocket.

Then he sat on the cold thwart for a long time, listening to the ice groan, and thought about the difference between selling a thing and being bought.

Because here was what Tam Osker knew, and had known since the night itself: he had seen the livery. Not just the crest on the door — the woman's coat, when she leaned into the carriage light, had been Aldery blue with the grey braid of the house servants, and the braid had caught the lantern-glow plain as print. He had seen her hesitate over the child like a woman leaving something at a foundling wheel with her heart still attached. And he had seen, above it all, the one lit window of the clock room, and understood — with the simple, total clarity available only to boys and rivers — that the child hadn't come across the water at all. The child had come *from* the hill. From the top of the town, down the switchback, to be planted on the bank below it like evidence, like a seed, like something meant to be found growing exactly there.

Foundlings were found at doors. This one had been found at the bottom of the founder's own hill, delivered by Aldery's own household, on the coldest night of the year.

Tam didn't have words for what that meant. But he had a feeling for it, the way he had a feeling for ice — a sense of the exact depth at which it would bear a man and the exact depth at which it would fold around him and close.

* * *

He didn't go to the counting house. That surprised him a little; he'd expected himself to go. A second crown would follow the first, and a third after that, and Aldery paid promptly and asked nothing twice, and Tam's father drank a fair portion of what Tam earned and beat a smaller portion out of him on the Saturdays it suited him, and a boy in Tam's position did not generally trouble himself over whose coin warmed his pocket.

But he kept not going. Days passed. The crown sat in a crack in the tar-shed wall where Tam hid his real money, and it began to feel less like wages than like a hook.

What held him — he examined it honestly, because Tam was honest with himself the way he was honest with no one else — was the hesitation. The woman's face in the carriage light. He had seen men abandon things at that bend before: sacks, dogs, once a whole chest of someone's papers, dropped through the ice-hole weighted with chain. They always did it quickly. Guilt makes people fast. But she had stood there with her hands half-lifted like a woman setting down a child at a nurse's door, and that meant the child was *known*, meant the child was somebody, meant the whole arrangement was somebody's design —

— and if it was somebody's design, then the design had a shape, and if it had a shape, then someday the shape would matter to somebody, and the person who had seen the shape being made would be worth something more than crowns. Worth, perhaps, exactly as much as the truth was worth to whoever needed it most.

Tam was fourteen, and cynical, and greedy in the modest way of the very poor, but underneath all of it he was his father's son and his river's son, and both of those parents taught the same lesson: don't sell at the first offer. Wait for the flood.

So he waited, and he watched, and the river counted the days for him.

He watched the observatory smoke rise each morning, one thread, regular as a pulse — the clockmaker and the child, living up there together now. He watched Aldery's men walk the hill road twice, then four times, then daily, taking the air with the studied casualness of cats outside a mousehole. He watched the Widow Kessel's boy carry a covered basket up the path one grey afternoon and come down lighter. Small traffic. Meaningful traffic. Tam read the hill the way he read the water, and the water was saying: something is gathering up there, and the man with the stag-and-keys knows it, and is frightened of it, and frightened men spend money.

Then, on a Thursday raw with sleet, the clockmaker came down to the bend herself.

* * *

She came on foot, hooded, alone, and Tam knew her by her hands before her face — the burn-spotted, nick-scared, precise hands of a craftsman hanging loose at her sides like tools returned to their hooks. She stopped at the edge of the shingle and looked at the open seam of the channel for a while, and Tam, mending net in the lean-to doorway, let her look. People came to the river to practice saying things.

"You're Osker's boy," she said at last.

"Tam."

"The ferryman." She turned. Her face was thinner than the town gossip made it, and had the particular set of someone carrying more than her arms could manage. "They say you watch the bend at night."

"They say right."

"I need to ask you about a night. The ninth of February."

"And why would the ninth of February interest a clockmaker?"

"Because I've been thinking," she said, "about how the girl came to my door. Foundlings are left at thresholds. Churches. Workhouse gates. Not the bottom of a hill road, a quarter mile from anywhere, on the coldest night we've had in six years." She came closer, boots crunching the frozen shingle, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at him with the flat directness of someone reading a gauge. "Someone brought her down that switchback. Someone with a carriage. You were here. You're always here. So either you saw it and sold the telling already, or you saw it and are waiting."

Tam felt the hook shift in his pocket — the crown, cold, patient, Aldery-polished. He looked at this woman who had spent ten years keeping silent in a dead man's workshop and who had, everyone said, taken in a stranger's child because no one else would. He thought about the difference between selling a thing and being bought, and he thought about floods.

"Say your price," he said.

"I don't bargain with children."

"I bargain with everyone. It's the only trade I have." He jerked his chin toward the lean-to. "Sit, if you're sitting. My price isn't coin."

"No?"

"Coin I can get from the other side. They've offered. Crown piece, polished special, sitting in that wall crack right now." He enjoyed the flicker that went through her face — not greed, he noted, but the quick arithmetic of a woman recalculating a whole town. "My price is standing. When this breaks — and it will break, everything on this river breaks eventually — I want it known that I told the truth before I was made to. Freely. There's a circuit judge coming on the first ferry when the ice goes. I want to say my piece in front of him, on the record, with my name spelled right. O-S-K-E-R. And I want protection after. Because the last boy who talked against the Aldery house left town with a broken arm and no references, and I've got a father who'd sell me for the fare."

Lior Winterbourne was quiet for a moment. Out on the channel the ice boomed, long and deep, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep, and both of them glanced at the water without meaning to.

"You want to testify," she said.

"I want to *matter*," Tam said. "There's a difference. Testifying is what they let you do. Mattering is what you take." He held out his hand, palm up, the way his father did over settled fares. "Do we cross palms or don't we? I'll tell you what I saw either way — I decided that before you came down the hill, which is why I'm still poor. But the terms change what happens after."

She looked at his hand. Then she took it, and her grip was dry and strong and callused in the patterns he would later recognize as the grip of every honest tradesman in Vellamarch, and she said, "Terms agreed. Tell me."

So Tam told her.

He told it well — he'd had days to lay it out in his head like cargo, everything squared and stowed — the carriage on the switchback, the stag-and-keys crest, the woman in the blue coat with the grey braid, the bundle set down gentle as a sleeping cat, the crying that wasn't loud, and above all the hesitation, the lifted hands, the face in the carriage light that was tired rather than cruel.

"She looked at the clock room window," he said. "Three seconds. Maybe four. Like she was checking the job was done. Or like she was sorry. Both, maybe. I've seen both on the same face." He shrugged. "And then she went, and the child cried, and I stood behind my shed like a coward with a good memory."

"You weren't a coward."

"I was fourteen and cold and I didn't know what the cargo was." He met her eyes. "Now I do, roughly. And here's the part you didn't ask for, clockmaker, free of charge, because it changes your arithmetic." He leaned forward. "That child wasn't abandoned at your door by accident of weather. She was *delivered*. Down the hill, past the town, to the one spot where a carriage can't be seen from the road and a body can't be traced to any doorstep in Vellamarch. Planted at the foot of the founder's own hill, in the middle of the night, ten days after old Master Hollis died. Somebody wanted a foundling at the observatory. Somebody wanted it *found*."

Lior had gone very still. Stillness, Tam noticed, was her native language — she had the whole vocabulary of it, the stillness of a balance wheel, of a held breath, of a trap about to spring. But this stillness was different from the others. This one had cracks running through it.

"The woman in the blue coat," Tam said. "Grey braid. House livery, upper grade. There's only one household in Vellamarch dresses its women like that."

"I know whose household," Lior said quietly.

"Then you know what I know. And now we both know what the other knows." Tam picked up his net-mending and threaded the needle, hands steady, heart going like a hammer in a loose box. "I'll be at the assizes when the ferry runs. Find me there, or send for me at the Anchor & Chain — my father drinks there; everyone knows where everyone drinks in this town. But hear me, clockmaker." He looked up, and for a moment the market-stall sharpness fell off his face and something younger showed underneath, something that had stood behind a tar-shed on a freezing night and listened to a child cry and done nothing. "When I tell it in front of the judge, I'll tell all of it. Including the part where I stood in the dark and let her lie there. I'm not selling you a clean version. The great ones buy clean versions. What us small people have is the truth with the mud still on it, and the mud's the proof."

Lior Winterbourne looked at him for a long moment, and then she said something he did not expect.

"You saw her face. Could you know it again? Among faces?"

"Ma'am, I know every face that comes to this bend, and I forget none of them. It's the only inheritance my father ever gave me that's worth anything."

"Then stay findable," she said. "And stay alive until the ferry runs. Can you do that?"

"I can hide," Tam said. "Hiding's the family business."

She went back up the towpath, and Tam watched until the sleet took her, and then he sat alone in the lean-to with the net in his lap, listening to the river boom and grind and count the days, and he took out the crown piece at last and turned it over in his fingers, Aldery's polish dull under the grey light.

Then he walked to the open seam of black water, and weighed it in his palm one final time — the whole worth of his silence, priced by the richest man in Vellamarch — and threw it in.

The river took it without a sound.

That same evening, Tam Osker packed a spare shirt, his father's second-best knife, and the lantern, and slipped out of the lean-to before dark, heading not away from the town but around it — toward the guildhall on Cooper's Lane, where lights burned late these days, and where, three streets from the counting house, a boy with a memory and a grudge might just find the second door the law kept for people like him.

Behind him the ice groaned, louder than it had any night that winter, as if the river too had begun counting down.
