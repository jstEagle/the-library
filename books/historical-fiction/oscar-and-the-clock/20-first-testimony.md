## 20. First Testimony

The assizes clerk kept his office above the chandler's on Rope Walk, in a room that smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of river rot rising through the floorboards. Lior climbed the outside stair with her collar up against the wind off the water, and at the top she paused to compose herself — a habit her master had drilled into her before any difficult customer. *You are not asking for anything,* she told herself. *You are gathering information. Information is only inventory by another name.*

She did not quite believe it. Her hands were cold in a way that had nothing to do with March.

The girl was not with her. She had left Isolde at the observatory with strict instructions: wind nothing, touch nothing, count the seconds aloud if she grew frightened, because counting made the fear keep time instead of running wild. The girl had taken this seriously as scripture, and had asked whether she might count out loud even if she wasn't frightened. Lior had said yes, thinking of the empty building full of ticking, and how a child's voice carrying through it might be the least lonely sound in Vellamarch.

Now she knocked.

"Come, come." The clerk's name was Mr. Tallowby — no relation to the chandler below, though he enjoyed the coincidence — and he was a narrow man of about sixty who sat behind a desk stacked with folios like a man besieged by his own diligence. He looked up over half-moon spectacles and his expression went through three changes in the space of a breath: recognition, calculation, and something that might once have been sympathy, long rusted into professional blankness.

"Miss Winterbourne," he said. "The clockmaker."

"The apprentice," Lior said, before she could stop herself. "Clockmaker's apprentice."

He smiled without warmth. "Of course. What can I do for you?"

"I've come about the hearing." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice one used across a counter. "The notice says the observatory estate will be heard at the assizes when the ferry runs. I'm named in some of the papers. I wanted to understand what evidence will be heard, and in what order, so I can prepare."

Tallowby took off his spectacles and began polishing them with a square of chamois, which gave him somewhere to look that wasn't her face. "That's an unusual request."

"It shouldn't be. A witness who doesn't know the questions is a witness wasting the court's time. I'd have thought you'd welcome it."

"Oh, I welcome it. I welcome efficiency in all its forms." He set the spectacles back on his nose and folded his hands. "But you should understand how these matters proceed. The estate of the late Josiah Merrow is in dispute between creditors holding instruments of debt — principally Mr. Aldery, acting for himself and several others — and any claimant asserting an interest by blood or bequest. The magistrate will hear representations from both sides. Evidence may be submitted. Witnesses may speak. All quite ordinary."

"And my testimony? If I have documents relevant to the estate?"

His hands stilled on the desk. It was a small motion, but Lior had spent ten years reading machines, and men were machines whose gears showed at the wrists. Something turned behind his eyes and caught.

"What documents?" he asked.

"That depends on what the court wants to hear."

"It depends," Tallowby said carefully, "on what the court considers *relevant*. And relevance, Miss Winterbourne, is determined by the magistrate. Who has already given the matter considerable attention." He rose, crossed to a shelf of ledgers, and ran a finger down the spines with the air of a man deciding how much rope to pay out. "I'll tell you what I tell everyone who asks. Come to the hearing prepared. Bring your papers. Speak when called upon. Don't interrupt. And don't expect—" he paused, choosing, "—don't expect the outcome to surprise anyone who knows this town."

Lior felt the words land somewhere below her ribs. *Don't expect the outcome to surprise anyone.* It was almost honest, for a clerk. Almost kind, in the way a weather report is kind when it tells you the bridge will flood.

"I see," she said. "May I ask one more thing?"

"You may ask. I can't promise answers."

"When the ferry runs. That's the condition in the notice. Why the ferry?"

Tallowby's mouth twitched. "Because the assizes travel with the court circuit, and the circuit travels by water until the roads dry. And because—" here he glanced toward the window, where the river showed grey and locked between its banks, the ice still whole but groaning at night now, audibly, like a large animal turning in its sleep, "—because Mr. Aldery owns the ferry, and Mr. Aldery wished the hearing scheduled with precision. Precision being, I'm told, a virtue among mechanical persons."

The insult was gentle enough to pass for small talk. Lior let it pass. She had learned long ago that a craftsman who answered every jab was a craftsman with no time for work.

"Thank you, Mr. Tallowby. You've been more forthcoming than I expected."

"Yes," he said, returning to his chair. "I occasionally regret it."

* * *

She meant to leave then. She was halfway down the outside stair when she heard, through the chandler's window below, two voices she recognized: Tallowby's, thin and dry, and another, rounder, carrying the particular confidence of a man who has never been interrupted in his life. The second voice belonged to Aldery's solicitor, a soft-bellied man named Prosser whom Lior had seen twice at the workshop door, always smiling, always leaving with something.

She stopped on the stair. The window was frosted at the edges but clear at the center, and the light inside threw their shadows onto the glass — two dark shapes bent over the counter, and between them a folio opened flat.

She told herself to keep walking. She was, after all, a woman who had just been reminded that outcomes in Vellamarch surprised no one; there was no reason to confirm the reminder at personal risk. But her boots stayed where they were, and she found herself reasoning the way she reasoned about a mechanism that behaved wrongly: if the fault is visible, refusing to look does not make the machine run true. It only leaves you standing beside it when it fails.

Prosser's shadow gestured at the folio. Tallowby's shook its head. Then Tallowby's produced — she saw the shape of the motion clearly against the light — a key from his waistcoat, unlocked a drawer, and laid something flat on the counter. A document. Several pages. Prosser's shadow leaned over it, read for a long while, and then nodded, twice, in the manner of a man approving a bill of sale.

They spoke too low for her to catch the words. It didn't matter. She knew what she had seen. A ruling does not get drafted, reviewed, and approved by the opposing counsel's solicitor in a chandler's back room unless it has already been written — unless the ink was dry before the witnesses were ever summoned.

The ice on the river groaned, long and low, and Lior walked home with her hands in her pockets so no one would see them shaking.

* * *

The observatory at dusk was a cathedral of small sounds. She let herself in and stood a moment in the dome room's cold blue light, listening: the dead master clock, silent as a held breath; the regulator on the wall keeping faithful seconds; and beneath it all, threading through everything, Isolde's voice, high and steady, counting.

"—forty-one, forty-two, forty-three—"

The girl sat on the floor beneath the master clock's case, cross-legged, a candle stub beside her, sorting a tray of screws into rows by size with the grave concentration of a general arranging troops. When she saw Lior she didn't stop counting — she had been told to count — but she raised her eyebrows in the question she couldn't ask aloud without losing her place.

Lior knelt beside her and began sorting screws too, matching her rhythm to the girl's, and for a few minutes neither of them said anything beyond the numbers. It was the closest thing to prayer either of them practiced.

At sixty, Isolde finished her minute and looked up. "You came back early. You came back wrong."

"Wrong?"

"You walk different when something's wrong. You walk small. Like you're trying to fit through a gap that isn't there anymore." She said it without cruelty, the way children report weather. "Did the man in the office say no?"

"He said yes," Lior said slowly. "He said everything was ordinary. He said bring my papers and speak when called upon and don't expect surprises." She picked up a screw, turned it in her fingers. "And then I watched his visitor read a paper that I think was the verdict."

Isolde considered this. "So it's already decided."

"It looks decided."

"But it isn't really. Because decided things are finished, and the hearing hasn't happened yet. Master used to say—" the girl stopped, and Lior saw her reach for something and find the memory missing, the way she sometimes did, reaching for a father or mother or whoever *Master* had been and coming back with empty hands. She covered it smoothly, the way children do, better than adults do. "Things aren't done until they're done. That's what the waiting is *for*."

"Who taught you that?"

"A clock, probably." She shrugged. "Everything I know about waiting, I learned from clocks."

Lior looked at her — six years old, orphaned twice over by different kinds of silence, sitting under a broken clock in a stolen hour of peace — and felt the thing in her chest that had been tightening since the chandler's window pull taut past any comfortable point. Because the child was right. Things weren't done until they were done. But she also knew, with a mechanic's certainty, the difference between a mechanism that would run and one that had been deliberately jammed, and what she had seen in that window was not a court preparing to deliberate. It was a ratchet. A pawl dropped into a toothed wheel so it could turn only one direction — Aldery's direction — no matter how hard anyone cranked from the other side.

"Isolde," she said. "I need to think about something, and I want you to help me think, because you're better at it than most grown men I know."

The girl sat up straighter, visibly pleased.

"There's a wheel," Lior said, holding up an imaginary gear between thumb and forefinger. "It turns one way only. Everything we try pushes it the other way, and it won't go. Pushing harder breaks our hands. So what do we do?"

Isolde frowned at the invisible gear. "Find where it's held."

"Yes."

"And take the pawl away?"

"If we can reach it."

"And if we can't reach it?" The girl's eyes were very clear. "Then you don't use that wheel. You put your work on a different train. That's what you said when the third wheel was cracked — you said, we don't fight the wheel, we route around it. New path, same power."

Lior sat very still.

Route around it. Same power. The law of Vellamarch was Aldery's wheel, bought and pawled and pre-ruled, and pushing against it would break her hands and lose the child her name besides. But law was not the only machinery in town that moved under authority. There were charters older than the magistrate's commission. There were institutions with their own jurisdiction, their own seals, their own teeth. Her master had spoken of them with contempt, mostly — guildmen, he called them, men who mistook their rulebooks for the sky — but contempt from her master had always carried, underneath it, a certain wary respect for anything with real force.

"The Clockmakers' Guild," she said aloud.

Isolde blinked. "What about them?"

"They certify public clocks. Observatory instruments. Timekeeping standards for the whole valley." Lior was speaking faster now, thoughts arriving like parts tumbling into a tray. "Their charter predates the town's charter. They have standing over instruments of public time — and the master clock of an observatory is the definition of an instrument of public time. If the clock itself were certified unfit — if the sabotage were documented, formally, by guild authority — that wouldn't be a matter for the magistrate at all. That would be a guild finding. And a guild finding about the observatory's instrument touches the observatory's estate, because the bequest conditions the estate on the clock."

"You've gone fast," Isolde said. "Go back to the part where you explain why they'd listen to us."

"Because—" Lior stopped. And there it was, the honest answer, ugly as a cracked pivot: *because I don't know that they would.* Because the guildmaster was her master's old rival, a man named Corvus Hale who hadn't exchanged a civil word with Edmund Winterbourne in thirty years, and who had no reason whatsoever to lift a finger for his dead rival's apprentice. Because appealing to the guild meant putting herself, her oath, her craft, and the child into the hands of strangers with their own politics and prices. Because it meant going outside the law she'd spent her whole quiet life believing in — believing in the way you believe in a floor, without ever examining the joists.

Because the last time someone tried to route justice around Aldery, they'd hidden a will inside a clock and waited ten years for an honest apprentice, and the honest apprentice had nearly missed it.

"Never mind why they'd listen," Lior said finally. "First I need to know what they'd hear. Evidence first. Then the door."

"That's what you were doing today," Isolde said. "At the office. Gathering evidence."

"I was doing what the law told me to do." Lior looked at her hands — competent hands, honest hands, hands that had spent ten years doing exactly as they were bid and calling the bidding prudence. "And the law, it turns out, has already signed its verdict and gone to dinner. So tomorrow I stop asking permission from the man who sold the answer." She stood, and her knees cracked in the cold, and the sound seemed to settle something. "Tomorrow I go to the guildhall."

Isolde picked up her candle stub and held it up, lighting them both from below, so that Lior's shadow climbed the dead clock's case and lay across its frozen dial like a hand reaching for the hands it couldn't move.

"Good," the girl said simply. "Counting helps, you know. When you're scared. One number at a time, and the fear has to keep time with you instead of the other way around." She blew out the flame. In the sudden dark, the regulator's tick filled the dome, patient and impartial, indifferent to magistrates and guilds alike. "So start counting, Lior. We haven't got many days left before the ice goes."

Outside, far downriver, the ice answered her — a boom like a struck bell rolling up through the foundations of the observatory, deep enough to feel in the teeth. The break was coming sooner than anyone had reckoned. Whatever Lior meant to build, she would have to build it fast, and in the dark, and without asking anyone's leave.

She reached out in the blackness and found the girl's shoulder, and together they stood a moment listening to the river count down.
