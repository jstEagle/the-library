## 21. The Clockmaker's Guild

The guildhall stood at the top of Cooper's Stair, where the town ran out of patience for hills and the river below looked like a gray blade laid flat between the banks. Lior had climbed those stairs perhaps six times in her life — twice as a child trailing her master's coat hem, twice carrying tools he was too proud to let an apprentice shoulder alone, and once, the day of his funeral, when she had walked up and then, without quite deciding to, walked back down again without going in.

She had not gone in because the guild had not come to his funeral.

That memory sat in her chest like a cold rivet as she climbed now, her breath smoking in the March air, the register wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat and pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The ledger from Widow Kessel she had left behind, hidden in the observatory's coal cellar under a flagstone she'd loosened herself with a chisel at three in the morning, while the girl slept upstairs with her arms around the stolen cog. She had considered bringing everything. But everything carried together was a confession, and she was not yet ready to confess. She was ready only to ask.

The guild charter hung beside the door, behind glass gone cloudy with age, and she stopped to read it as she always did — not because she'd forgotten the words but because she needed to hear them in her own voice this time. *Instruments of public time are held in trust by the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers of Vellamarch, whose authority over such instruments is absolute and whose certification no temporal power may override.* Absolute. No temporal power. She had recited that clause at fourteen with her hand on her master's bench, understanding it about as well as she understood the words *in sickness and in health* — which is to say, not at all, until life made them plain.

She knocked. The knocker was a brass escapement wheel, worn smooth on one side by a century of hands, and it turned under her palm with a small mechanical click that steadied her more than any prayer could have.

* * *

The guildmaster was named Corvus Hane, and he had been her master's enemy for thirty years, if you could call it enmity between men who spoke to each other perhaps four times a decade and otherwise conducted their war through apprentices, price lists, and pointed silences at guild meetings. Master Aldous Winterbourne — her master, her name given to her in service, though the blood relation went back two generations to a quarrel no one alive remembered the start of — Master Aldous had called Hane "a man who measures twice and cuts nothing." Hane, she knew from overheard tavern talk, had called Aldous "a genius who files his tolerances with his temper."

Hane was seventy if he was a day, narrow as a pendulum rod, with white hair cut close as a brush and hands so steady they seemed almost unnatural, the way very still water seems unreal. He received her in a room lined floor to ceiling with regulator clocks, each one ticking, each one set to a slightly different standard, so that the room filled with a soft, arrhythmic shivering of sound — like rain on a roof, like a crowd murmuring just below the level of speech.

"Apprentice Winterbourne." He did not offer a chair. He stood at a bench with a loupe pushed up on his forehead and a disassembled anchor escapement laid out on baize before him, and he looked at her the way a man looks at a movement that has come into his shop running fast and nobody knows why. "I heard the observatory commission passed to you. I heard it and I said nothing, which my wife will tell you cost me something."

"The commission isn't why I've come."

"No," he said. "I didn't imagine it was."

He still didn't offer a chair. So she stood, and she told him.

She told him about the letter naming her alone, and the child on the steps with the sealed note. She told him about the half-written will inside the clock case, and the sabotage in the escapement — deliberate, recent, machined by someone who understood what they were doing — designed to make the founder's bequest fail as neglect. She told him about Mr. Aldery's visit, the too-casual questions about the clock's papers, the polished menace of a man who owned the magistrate the way other men owned a watch. She told him about the visitor register in her coat, and the mother listed in it, dead last autumn, and the granddaughter no law in Vellamarch currently acknowledged.

She did not tell him about her master's ledger, or the payments from Aldery, or the ten years of instructed silence. That wound was too fresh and too deep, and she found, standing there among all those ticking regulators, that she was not yet willing to hand her dead master's shame to his living enemy. Later, perhaps. If it came to testimony. But not as an opening gift.

Hane listened. That was the first surprise. She had prepared herself for interruption, dismissal, the particular contempt of an old craftsman for a young woman bringing him politics instead of work. Instead he stood motionless at his bench, and when she reached the part about the sabotaged escapement, he picked up the broken piece she'd brought wrapped in flannel — the original, pried from the mechanism before she replaced it — and turned it in his fingers without a word.

"The filing marks are fresh," he said at last. "Recent months." He tilted it toward the window light. "And the hand is good. Not workshop-good. *Guild*-good. This was done by someone trained to a high standard, and recently enough that the steel hasn't oxidized in the cuts." His eyes came up. "You understand what that means."

"That the man who owns the town also employs a clockmaker."

"It means," Hane said, setting the piece down with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate, "that somewhere in this town or near it is a journeyman who took guild money for training and used guild craft to break a public instrument. Which is a matter for this hall before it is ever a matter for the assizes."

Lior felt something loosen in her chest — cautiously, the way ice loosens at the river's edge, cracking before it moves. "Then you'll hear me."

"I haven't decided what I'll do," Hane said. "Sit down."

There was, she discovered, a stool tucked under the bench's shadow, and had been the whole time. She sat.

* * *

"You should know how your master and I ended," Hane said, when she had finished and the silence had gone on long enough to become its own kind of question. He wiped his hands on a rag, slowly, thoroughly, each finger separately, a ritual she recognized because her own master had performed the same one before difficult conversations — the craftsman's way of buying time. "It began over a turret clock in Marrowgate, thirty-one years ago. A church clock, badly built by a mason who fancied himself a mechanic. Your master rebuilt the train in nine days. Beautiful work. Everyone said so. What no one asked was whether the tower could carry the new weights. The south wall cracked within the year. The parish had no money left, because your master had been paid already, and I was sent to salvage what could be salvaged, and I charged the parish honestly and rebuilt what remained, and the two of us never exchanged a civil word again."

"He never spoke of it."

"No. He wouldn't. That was his gift and his disease, the same thing — he could keep a silence so complete that even the people damaged by it couldn't see inside it." Hane looked at her, and his eyes were pale and very direct. "You've inherited it, I think. You've walked into this hall carrying a scandal the size of a cathedral and told me perhaps a third of it. I'm not offended. I'm noting it."

Heat rose in her face. "What I've withheld isn't mine alone to give away."

"Nothing ever is," Hane said. "That's what makes giving it away either treachery or courage, and usually you can't tell which until afterward, and sometimes not even then." He pulled the loupe off his forehead and set it down. "Tell me precisely what you want from this guild. Not the noble version. The practical one."

She had rehearsed this on the stairs and rewritten it four times, and now she threw the rehearsal away and said the true thing. "The hearing is set for the assizes when the ferry runs. The magistrate has bought the ruling already — I've seen the clerk's face, I know how it will go. Aldery will claim the child is a foundling of no account, void the founder's bequest for neglect, take the observatory, and bury the will so deep no one ever finds it, because the only copy I know of is half-finished and unsigned and worth nothing in a civil court against a bought judge." Her voice stayed level; she was surprised by that. "But the master clock is an instrument of public time. It keeps the hour for the harbor, the tide tables, the ferry schedules, the whole town's working day. Under your charter, its condition is yours to certify. If the guild declares the sabotage and certifies the restoration — if the guild stands in that hearing as the authority on the instrument — then Aldery's story about neglect dies on the table, and the bequest stands, and then the question of the heir becomes a question someone has to actually answer instead of bury."

"And the child?"

"The child," Lior said, "is the answer."

For a long moment the only sound was the shivering chorus of the regulators, forty-odd clocks agreeing and disagreeing about the time, none of them quite wrong, none of them quite right.

"Do you know what you're asking me to spend?" Hane said finally. "Not money. Standing. This hall has survived two hundred years in a river town by staying out of exactly these fights. We certify instruments. We do not certify heirs. The moment we stand in an inheritance dispute, every rich man in Vellamarch starts wondering how to buy us, and every poor one starts hoping, and both of them stop trusting us, and a guild that is neither trusted nor feared is furniture." He tapped the broken escapement once with one steady finger. "Aldery holds paper on half the workshops in this town. Including, if I recall the creditors' notices correctly, the estate of your late master. You are asking me to make war on the man who could foreclose on a third of my membership in a season."

"Yes," Lior said.

"At least you don't pretend it's a small thing." He turned away from her and walked down the line of regulators, and she watched his reflection move along the glass case fronts, distorted, doubled. "Your master would have handled this differently. Your master would have found a way to fix the clock, protect the child, preserve the workshop, keep his name clean, and tell no one anything — and it would have worked beautifully right up until it destroyed everyone, including him, which is in fact what happened, if you follow the shape of it backward." He stopped walking. "Are you asking me to help you finish his design? Or to break it?"

The question landed harder than she expected, because it was the question she had been avoiding since she found the ledger. Ten years of silence, purchased and maintained. A will hidden in a clock, waiting — or abandoned. An apology written in code between the lines of teaching notes she hadn't fully decoded yet. She thought of the girl's hands around the stolen cog, and the note that had said *forget the face*, and the widow saying *burn it, take her to the coast*, and every exit offered to her shaped exactly like safety and costing exactly her conscience.

"Neither," she said. "I'm asking you to help me build something he never dared to. In the open. On the record."

Hane was quiet for so long that one of the regulators, a tall black case model near the window, struck the quarter-hour alone, a single bright chime that rang through the room and died.

"When your master died," he said, "I wrote him a letter. Finished it, sealed it, never sent it. It's in the drawer of that desk, and it has been for eleven years, because the man it was addressed to was past reading and my pride was past mending." He opened the drawer, took out an envelope yellowed at the edges, and held it without breaking the seal. "In it I conceded that his Marrowgate train was the finest piece of public horology this county had seen in fifty years, and that the wall cracked because the parish lied to him about the masonry, not because he lied to the parish. I conceded that he was better than me. And then I sealed it, because conceding it to a corpse costs nothing and I wanted to say it to his face." He set the envelope down on the bench between them. "You have his hands. I watched you stand there and hold your tongue through everything I said that stung, and you held it the way he used to — like a man winding a spring, storing the force rather than spending it. So I'll tell you what I'd have told him, to his face, if God had granted me the hour."

He straightened, and something in his bearing shifted — less the gatekeeper, more the guildmaster, the office settling onto him like a tool taken up deliberately.

"I'll listen longer than prudence recommends. I'll convene a quorum of the hall — three masters besides myself, men whose debts Aldery does not hold, and I can name them, because I make it my business to know who in this town owes whom, which is the only intelligence that matters anymore. And I will put to them a formal inquiry into the condition of the observatory's master clock as an instrument of public time, with full charter authority to inspect, certify, and — if need be — to sit in judgment wherever that instrument's fate is decided." He raised one finger before she could speak. "Conditions."

"Name them."

"First: the guild inspects the clock itself, in place, with my own eyes on the mechanism. I do not certify on another craftsman's word, even yours — *especially* not yours, because you are compromised and you know it. Second: whatever else exists — papers, ledgers, evidence beyond what you've shown me — comes to this hall before it goes anywhere else, or the alliance ends the moment I learn otherwise. I am too old to be anyone's second choice." His gaze was flint. "Third, and this is the one that matters: if the guild enters this fight and it goes wrong — if the magistrate strips our standing, if Aldery buries us in litigation, if the circuit judge sides with the civil courts and breaks the charter's teeth — then this hall falls, and every workshop behind it, and the child you're trying to save watches the last institution that might have protected her collapse on top of her. Do you accept that arithmetic?"

Lior sat with it. She thought about refusing to accept it, about the prideful answer, the answer that said the truth doesn't cost, the truth is free. But she had spent ten years learning from a man who taught that every mechanism trades force for force, nothing moves without something paying, and a clock that pretends otherwise is simply a clock that hasn't run long enough.

"I accept the arithmetic," she said. "And I'll add to it. Whatever happens to the guild, I'll stand between it and the worst of it. My name goes on the complaint before the guild's seal does. Let Aldery aim at the apprentice first."

Something moved at the corner of Hane's mouth — not quite a smile; the ghost of one, filed down to tolerance. "Offered unprompted. He never did that. He'd have negotiated until the guild bled first and apologized after." He extended his hand across the bench, and she took it, and his grip was dry and precise and surprisingly strong. "Come Thursday, then. Bring your tools and your broken parts and that register under your coat, which I could hear you guarding the entire time you stood there like a woman hiding bread in a famine. And Apprentice Winterbourne—" she paused at the door, "—whatever you're still holding back, decide quickly how much of it the truth requires. Courts eat partial truths and call the remainder perjury. I'd hate to watch them do it to you."

* * *

She came down Cooper's Stair with the light going amber behind the western hills and the river below running high and dark, its edges fringed white where the ice still held but the current underneath had begun, invisibly, to win. The town smelled of woodsmoke and thaw. Somewhere a dog was barking at the water.

At the bottom of the stair she stopped, because the plan that had carried her up the hill was already obsolete, remade in Hane's back room, and the new plan required things she didn't yet have in safe places. The register against her ribs. The ledger under the flagstone. The half-will still sewn inside the clock case, where any competent inspector — Hane himself, Thursday — would find it in the first hour of opening the housing. That was fine; that was intended; the will belonged in front of witnesses. But the register, and the ledger, and the original sabotaged escapement, and the widow's letters — those could not sit in a building Aldery's men watched, waiting for a hearing that Aldery had every reason to delay past the legal term, past the ice break, past her endurance.

They would have to move. Tonight, or soon. Quietly. Past watchers.

She was still turning the problem over — routes, hours, who could be trusted to walk beside her carrying contraband paper through a town that owed its soul to Mr. Aldery — when she noticed the boy.

He was sitting on the low wall by the ferry landing, mending a net with quick, bored hands, and he had been watching her come down the stair with the fixed, sideways attention of someone working up to something. She knew his face distantly — the ferryman's son, fourteen or so, all elbows and river-colored eyes, the kind of boy who saw everything on the water and was believed about none of it.

She might have walked past. Any sensible person would have.

But he stood up as she drew level, dropped the net, and said, loud enough to carry across the empty landing:

"You're the clockwoman off the observatory. I seen somethin' the night the little girl came." He glanced upriver, toward the Aldery warehouse, toward the windows, and lowered his voice. "And I think somebody's been paid to make sure nobody asks me what."
