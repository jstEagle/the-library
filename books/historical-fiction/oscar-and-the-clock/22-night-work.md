## 22. Night Work

The guildmaster's plan, when he finally said it aloud, sounded almost insultingly simple. *Paper is lighter than gold,* he had told me, tapping the guild charter with one blunt finger. *And heavier. No man ever drowned carrying a chest of coin into a court that wanted him hanged. But a will — a will can be carried in a coat pocket by anyone with clean hands and a reason.*

I had three documents and no clean reason left in this town. That was the whole of my fortune.

The ferryman's boy — Tam, though everyone in Vellamarch called him Ferry's Boy as if it were a trade, like cooper or tallow-chandler — came to the observatory after dark, slipping through the door I'd left unlatched the way his father slipped boats through the ice leads. He was fourteen and built like a whipstock, all tendon and nerve, and when he looked at me his eyes did arithmetic. I recognized the look. It was mine, ten years ago, standing in Master Hollis's workshop while he explained which debts we would honor and which we would pretend not to see.

"They're back," Tam said. "Two of them. The corner by the chandler's, and one walking the river path every quarter hour. He carries a lantern and he swings it like he's checking for ice, but he doesn't check for ice."

"Where does he turn around?"

"At the boathouse stair. Never past it. And there's a third man on the observatory lane itself, but he's the lazy one — he stands under the yew across from the gate and smokes, so you can smell him before you can see him."

I had spent two evenings confirming exactly this, watching from the cupola slit until my knees ached on cold stone. Aldery's men were not watching me. They were watching the building, because Aldery believed — correctly — that whatever could ruin him was inside it. A man who owns the magistrate, the ferry, half the mortgages in Vellamarch, and the silence of most of its respectable citizens does not send hired watchers out into a March night for anything small.

Which meant they would watch the observatory all night, patiently, professionally, while the papers walked out the other end of town in a fish basket.

* * *

We made our preparations the way I had been taught to make a difficult repair: never touch the piece twice; do the thinking before the hands start; assume the worst joint is the one you cannot see.

I took the founder's will from its hiding place behind the great weight trunk, where it lay wrapped in oilcloth between two dead pendulum bobs. I took the ledger the Widow Kessel had sent — her master's payments from Aldery, ten years of them, entered in her husband's careful upright hand beside sums for candles and clock oil, as if bribes were a household expense like any other. I took the visitor register with its fatal line: *Miss Elowen Vairre, accompanied by an infant.* Three objects, none heavier than a hymnal. All of them worth more than the observatory, more than my master's workshop, more than everything Aldery owned, if they reached the right table at the right hour.

I wrapped each separately. Will against my ribs, inside my stays, flat as a second heartbeat. Ledger in the false bottom of my tool basket, beneath the files and pin-wire, where any searcher's fingers would find only the honest clutter of a working craftswoman. Register — that was the problem child, broad and stiff-backed and impossible to disguise — went into the fish basket under a layer of straw and two genuinely foul herring, because Tam insisted with great seriousness that no watchman alive would dig past fresh herring after dark.

"You'll ruin the register," I said.

"It's already got blood on the corner," Tam said. "From when I found it. Books survive worse than fish. I've seen a Bible fished out of the shallows at Easter and it dried fine." He paused. "Mostly fine."

There was a theology in that I chose not to examine.

The last preparation was the decoy. If Aldery's men were watching the observatory, then the observatory should appear worth watching until the moment it didn't matter anymore. I lit every lamp on the ground floor as I always did, set my work lamp burning over the dismantled escapement, and — this was Tam's contribution, delivered with the shameless delight of a boy who has found his vocation — arranged my cloak and bonnet on the stand by the north window, angled so that from the lane it might read, in lamplight, as a woman bent over her bench. From thirty feet, in the dark, through glass fogged with river damp? Perhaps. Perhaps not. But a watcher who believes he sees a silhouette does not run to his master to report that he sees nothing. Fear makes men generous with what they think they know.

"And if they come to the door?" I asked.

Tam shrugged. "Then they knock, and nobody answers, and they stand in the cold deciding whether a locked door means the bird has flown or just gone to bed. That's their whole job, miss. Deciding. We want them deciding slowly."

He said it with such cheerful authority that I nearly laughed, and laughing felt dangerous, so I swallowed it, and the swallow hurt, and I understood that the hurt was fear finding its level. Good. Fear, properly machined, is just attention with teeth.

* * *

We went out at the change of the watch, or what passed for it in Vellamarch, which was simply the moment the taverns emptied enough that the watchmen drifted toward the warm corners they preferred. The sky was low and bruised, no moon, the kind of night the river made for itself in late winter — smelling of thaw and old wood and the mineral breath of ice about to give up. Under everything, faint but ceaseless, came the sound I had been hearing for days now: the groan of the ice field below the bend, shifting, testing its own strength, a giant turning over in sleep.

The river was counting down too. Everyone in town was. Aldery's notice named the assizes for the week the ferry ran; the ferry ran when the ice broke; and the ice would break when it pleased, which meant every scheme in Vellamarch — his and mine both — was now scheduled against a force no man could bribe.

Tam went first. I watched him melt down the observatory steps and along the wall, a shadow among shadows, pausing at the corner with the stillness of a heron before sliding across the open stretch of lane. The smoker under the yew was mid-pipe; I could smell the tobacco even from the doorway, cheap black twist, sour. Tam passed him at six feet, herring basket swinging, shoulders slumped in the universal posture of boys sent on errands they resent, and the man did not so much as lift his head.

I gave him a hundred heartbeats, then followed.

My route was different. There was no pretending to be a boy for me; instead I was a woman walking fast to catch someone, hood up, chin down, stride angry. Angry women are invisible in a way timid women are not. Nobody stops an angry woman; they step aside. I crossed the lane two doors up from the yew, close enough to hear the smoker mutter something to himself, close enough to have seen the wet shine of his boots, and kept walking, past the chandler's shuttered front, past the blind mouth of Cooper's Alley, and into the alley itself, where Tam stood waiting in the dark with his hand out.

"Ledger," he whispered.

I lifted the basket's false bottom and put the ledger into the straw atop the register, and he settled the herrings back over both with the tenderness of a nurse tucking in patients, and then we were two people again — a woman and a boy, a basket between us — walking the long way, the river way, toward the guildhall.

The river path was the dangerous part and the necessary part. The road looped wide through town past the magistrate's house, and I would not carry those papers past that house if I could help it; some instincts you don't argue with. So we took the shingle below the boathouse stair, where the stones were iced slick and the watcher's lantern sweep caught us twice — twice we froze, crouched behind a stranded skiff overturned on trestles, and twice the light passed over us and moved on, because a lantern swung by a bored man finds only what he expects to find, and he expected ice, gulls, and nothing.

Between the sweeps we moved. My hands were steady on the stones. I noticed that they were steady and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a train running true after you've filed every pallet by hand and cannot quite believe your own work.

Halfway along the shingle the ice spoke.

It was not the nightly groan this time. It was a crack — a single report like a musket, sharp and enormous, rolling up the valley, and every dog in Vellamarch began to bark at once. Tam grabbed my sleeve. Above us, the lantern stopped swinging. We heard boots on the stair above the boathouse, quick, uncertain, and a voice calling to another voice — *did you hear it, the river's going, go tell Mr. Aldery* — and then running feet, receding, and the lantern light bobbing away up the bank and off toward the lit windows of the better streets.

For a long moment neither of us moved. Then Tam started to shake. Not with fear — with laughter, silent, doubled-over laughter, his fist pressed to his mouth.

"The river," he gasped, "the river just did our diversion for us. Miss Lior, if I'd planned that, you'd have called it showing off."

I laughed then too, briefly, badly, the laugh of someone whose nerves have been stretched wire-tight for weeks and have just been plucked. Then I stood up, and the wind came off the ice field carrying the smell of open water somewhere far out in the dark, and the laugh died in my throat, because I understood what we had heard. The break was beginning. Not tonight, perhaps not tomorrow — but the deadline was no longer a date on a notice. It was a sound. It was coming for all of us at its own pace, indifferent to Aldery's money and my oath alike.

"Move," I said.

* * *

The guildhall sat on the square like a squat gray fact: no columns, no grandeur, just thick walls, iron-studded doors, and the arms of the chartered trades cut into the lintel — wheel, loom, anvil, and above them all, because the guildhall had been built by men who knew what order was worth, the balance wheel and escapement of the clockmakers' mark. Guild law was older than the town's incorporation. The magistrate's writ ran to the edges of the square and stopped, politely, at the gutter, the way it had for a hundred and forty years, because some ancestor of his had signed a charter he probably hadn't read and every magistrate since had chosen not to remember.

Aldery remembered. That was why he bought the magistrate instead of fighting the charter. But charters are stubborn instruments. They keep time longer than men do.

Tam knocked the pattern the guildmaster had given him — two, pause, three — and the wicket in the great door opened onto lamplight and the face of old Master Quillon, the hall's night porter, who looked at us, at the basket, at the street beyond us, and opened the door without one word, because in forty years of keeping this door he had evidently learned that questions were not part of his wages.

Inside, the hall smelled of tallow and stone cold and old paper — hundreds of years of paper, ledgers and rolls and indentures sleeping in the archive behind iron grating, the accumulated memory of every transaction Vellamarch had ever thought worth writing down. The guildmaster was waiting in the council chamber, dressed, though the hour was past midnight, as if he had never intended to sleep tonight at all. Old Corven Halsey, my master's rival these thirty years, sat behind the long table with the charter roll open before him and a single lamp turned low.

"You were seen?" he asked. Not *are you well*, not *did it go easily*. A craftsman's question. Was the work done without flaw.

"No," I said. "Twice nearly. The river cracked — loudly — near the boathouse, and drew the watchers off. We came in on the shingle."

Halsey nodded slowly. "It cracks loud three, four nights before it goes, usually. Sometimes five." He looked at the basket. "Open it."

I unwrapped the ledger first and laid it before him. Then the register, fishy of corner and straw-marked, open to the page with my mother's— with Elowen Vairre's name. Last, from against my ribs, the will, and I saw the old man's hands go still above the table when the oilcloth came away, because whatever he had expected, he had expected it larger, and here was the whole war in four pages of a dead founder's handwriting.

Halsey read for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere in the building a door settled. When he finished, he did not speak at once, and I stood there in the lamplight understanding suddenly how much rode on this man's opinion — this rival, this stranger, this keeper of the second door — and how little I could do about it now that the papers were out of my hands and on his table.

"Well," he said at last, and his voice was dry as the archive. "Your master sat on this for ten years. Sat on it, took Aldery's money for sitting on it, and taught you to sit quietly too." His eyes came up, sharp as a graver. "Why should I believe you're any different?"

It was the same question the assizes would ask, the same question Aldery's counsel would flay me with, and he was rehearsing it on me now, at midnight, with the herrings stinking on his council table. I made myself answer slowly, because hedging was the habit of ten years and this was no night for habits.

"You shouldn't believe me," I said. "Believe the papers. They don't need my character. The will is in the founder's own hand — you knew him, you can swear to the hand. The ledger is my master's own book, in his own ink, and the widow sent it knowing what it would cost his name. The register proves the child's mother. None of it needs me to be good. It only needs to be true, and it is true whether I'm a saint or a liar."

Halsey studied me for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then, unexpectedly, the corner of his mouth moved.

"Hollis always said you argued like filing — slow, even strokes, no wasted metal." He closed the ledger. "He also said you were the best apprentice he ever ruined by being soft with. I begin to see the shape of both remarks." He rose, stiff in the knees, and rang a small handbell once. Old Quillon appeared in the doorway as if he'd been standing there all along, which he probably had. "The archive. Iron grate, east wall, drawer marked *Charter Disputes — Standing*. Seal it tonight. Nothing goes in or comes out without my key and the wardens' countersign, both."

Quillon gathered the papers with ceremony, the will last, and I watched it disappear behind iron grating into the dark of the guild's memory, and something in my chest unlocked that had been clenched since the night the child appeared on the observatory steps. For ten years the truth had lived wherever danger lived — in my master's silences, in the widow's fear, in my own ribs. Tonight it slept behind iron, in a building Aldery's money could not buy and his magistrate's writ could not cross.

Tam was grinning at me by the door, herring basket empty at last of everything but its honest stink.

"Walk me home, miss?" he said. "Father'll want a report. And—" the grin flickered into something younger, "—it's a long dark walk otherwise."

* * *

We came up out of the riverside lanes together, boy and woman, past shuttered shops, past the chapel whose bell had not rung true since before I was born. At the corner by the cooper's yard Tam stopped and touched my sleeve.

"They'll figure it soon," he said. "That the observatory's been dark and empty all night. The one under the yew, he'll knock eventually, or his master will make him." He looked up at me, and in the dark his face was very serious for a boy who smelled of herring. "When they figure it out, they won't come for the papers. Papers are gone; even Aldery knows a chase to the guildhall is a fight he loses. They'll come for what's left. You. And the girl."

The cold went through me like water through a seam.

The girl. The girl was at the widow Kessel's — I had moved her two days ago for exactly this reason, or told myself I had — asleep upstairs in the widow's spare bed, behind a good lock, on a street Aldery considered beneath his attention. She was safe. Probably she was safe. Almost certainly—

"Go home, Tam," I said, already turning. "Quickly. Don't stop for anyone."

I ran. Through the lanes, hood torn back, breath burning, past the chandler's and the yew tree — where the smoker was no longer smoking, where the corner by the chandler's stood empty, where the watcher's post on the river path was abandoned and dark — every post empty, every lantern gone, and I understood with terrible clarity what empty posts meant. The watching was over. The watching had ended the moment it failed, and what replaced watching, in a town where a man owned the magistrate and the workhouse and the parish register besides, was administration.

The widow's house stood dark and shut like all the others. I hammered on the door until my fist sang, and after an age bolts rattled, and the widow Kessel stood in the gap with a candle, gray-faced, older than she had been two days ago.

"He's taken her," she said, before I could speak. "An hour past. Two men and the parish beadle, with a paper signed by Magistrate Crowe. Said she was a vagrant child lodged unlawfully, and they've placed her in the workhouse under the name *Martha Doe*." Her voice shook, and steadied itself with visible effort. "They wrote it in the parish book, Lior. In ink. As if a name in a book were the same thing as a name."

I stood on the doorstep with the river cracking somewhere out in the dark, louder now, closer, and felt the floor of the world drop — the papers safe behind iron, the truth sealed and witnessed and utterly useless, because Aldery had never needed to stop the papers. He had only needed to take the child, and file her, and let the law's own machinery grind her name smooth.

Behind the iron grate across town lay a will naming an heir. Behind the workhouse grate lay the heir herself, renamed and unfindable, unless—

"Get your cloak, Widow," I heard myself say. "And your strongest tea. We're going to need the whole night, and I need to think like a clockmaker."
