## 23. The Child Is Taken

The guildhall clock said a quarter past four when we came out by the coal-yard gate, and the sky over Vellamarch had gone the color of pewter — that thin, bruised grey that comes before dawn in the last weeks of February, when the river ice has begun to talk to itself all night and the town sleeps badly.

I carried the papers against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat: the half-written will from the clock case, my master's ledger with its columns of Aldery's payments, and the founder's visitor register with its single damning line. The ferryman's boy, Tam Osk, walked beside me with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, whistling nothing, the way boys whistle when they have been paid and are pretending they haven't. We had gone by way of Tanner's Row and the mill race, doubling back twice, because Aldery's men had been watching the observatory since Tuesday — two of them, taking turns at the corner by the chandler's shed, smoking in the lee of the wall where the wind couldn't carry their smoke to the road.

They hadn't followed us. I had counted on that. A man watches a door when he thinks what he wants is behind it.

"You did well," I told Tam at the mouth of Ferry Lane.

He shrugged, fourteen years old and already too shrewd for his own comfort. "You'll keep it about the woman in the livery? That you won't say where you heard it?"

"I keep what I'm given," I said. "That's the trade."

He nodded once and went down toward the water, where the ice groaned and cracked like something enormous turning over in its sleep. I watched him until the dark took him, then turned uphill alone, the papers heavy as a child against my ribs.

The observatory stood black against the lightening east, its dome rimmed with frost, its windows blind. From the outside it looked exactly as I had left it six hours before. It was only later — much later, standing in that empty room with the morning coming through the glass — that I understood how completely I had already lost everything, while I was still congratulating myself on the night's work. The papers were safe. The papers had never been what mattered.

* * *

The lock was untouched. I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left afterward: the lock was untouched, the key still sitting where I kept it under the third flagstone, and when I let myself into the instrument room the air smelled of lamp oil and chalk and cold brass, the smell I had come to think of as safety itself.

"Oscar," I said softly, to the dark. "It's me."

No small voice answered from the cot behind the partition.

She always answered. Even asleep, even half-dreaming, she would murmur something — *is it morning yet, is the river loud, can I hold the little screwdriver* — the way other children talk in their sleep about sweets or dogs. Six nights I had slept in the room next to hers, and six nights her breathing had been the first thing I heard when I woke: quick and light, like a fast train of gears running true.

I lit the lamp.

The cot was made. Made neatly, corners tucked, blanket folded back in a triangle the way she'd been taught at whatever place she'd lived before the steps of the observatory — some institution, some parish hand-me-down orderliness that had survived everything else. Her shoes were beneath it, side by side. Her one possession, the blue ribbon she wore braided into her hair, was gone.

On the folded blanket lay a paper. Official stock, watermarked, the magistrate's office seal in red wax.

I did not pick it up at once. I remember standing there with the lamp raised and thinking, with a strange floating calm, *he got past me while I was carrying his ruin across town.* Then the calm went out like a snuffed wick and I read it three times because the words would not stay still.

**NOTICE OF PARISH RECEIPT.** Dated the previous evening, nine o'clock. Received into the care of the parish workhouse, St. Ambrose Ward, one female child, apparent age five to seven years, found wandering near the Vellamarch road, name given as **MARTHA GREY**, parentage unknown, no claimant presenting.

Found wandering. Near the Vellamarch road. At nine o'clock, while I was walking through the coal yards with her history pressed to my heart, someone had carried her out of the room I had locked, driven her down to the parish house, and delivered her to the one place in Vellamarch that no one could legally take her out of without a magistrate's order.

Aldery had not stolen her. That was the genius of it, the thing I only understood hours later when my mind stopped circling and started working: he had *rescued* her. On paper. In the sight of God and the parish register, Mr. Josiah Aldery, solicitor and creditor, had done a charitable deed — had taken in an abandoned child of no account, name unknown, parentage unknown, and placed her where the law required abandoned children to be. If I produced the will, if I produced the register, if I stood up in any court in the county and declared that Martha Grey was in truth Elowen Marchmont, granddaughter of the founder — then every document I held became evidence that I, Lior Winterbourne, apprentice clockmaker, had conspired to conceal a child's identity, had harbored her under false pretenses, had perhaps invented the whole inheritance for purposes of my own.

My silence, ten years of it, the silence that had kept me safe and useless, was now the strongest weapon he owned.

* * *

I do not clearly remember leaving the observatory. I remember the papers — I had put them back in their oilcloth, though God knows why, since they were suddenly worth less than the brass scrap in my apron pocket. I remember walking down through the sleeping town with my fists closed so hard my nails cut my palms, and the river below the embankment roaring to itself, the ice lifting and settling, lifting and settling, like a great animal testing the bars of its pen.

St. Ambrose Ward occupied a converted granary off Cordwainer Street: a long brick building with high barred windows and a bell over the door that had been cast, I knew, by a founder my master had despised, because it ran flat in damp weather. Even in grief I noticed the bell. Grief does not stop the habit; that is either the mercy of craft or its curse, I have never decided which.

The wardress who answered my knock was a broad, competent woman with chilblained knuckles and eyes that had seen every kind of misery and priced them all at a penny. She had a ledger open on the desk behind her and a candle burning down to its last inch.

"We don't take callers before six," she said.

"I've come about a child received last night. Brought in around nine o'clock."

"Name?"

"That's rather the question," I said. "She was entered as Martha Grey."

The wardress looked at me with the particular patience of a woman who has heard a hundred stories begin exactly this way. "If you're a relative, you'll need to show it. If you're not a relative, you'll need the magistrate's order. Them's the rules."

"She isn't a Grey. She was left on the steps of the observatory six weeks ago with a note—"

"Ah." Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, and beneath it, faintly, pity. "You'd be the clockmaker." She sighed and stepped back from the door. "Come in out of the cold, miss. But I'll tell you straight, because I've no taste for cruelty dressed as kindness: there's nothing here for you to do."

The receiving room smelled of carbolic and boiled cabbage. A stove ticked in the corner. On the wall hung the parish rules, painted on board: SILENCE AT MEALS. NO PLAY BEFORE WORK IS DONE. CHILDREN TO BE INSPECTED WEEKLY BY THE GUARDIANS. And beneath those, smaller: **NO PERSON MAY REMOVE A WARD SAVE BY ORDER OF THE MAGISTRATE OR THE BOARD OF GUARDIANS.**

"The gentleman who brought her in," the wardress said, following my eyes to the rules. "Very proper. Papers all in order. Said the child had been seen wandering by the mill pond, near frozen, poor mite, and he couldn't in conscience leave her another night. Paid a quarter's maintenance on the spot, cash." She named the sum. It was generous. It was the sum a man pays when he wants a transaction witnessed and remembered favorably.

"What name did he give?" I asked.

"He didn't give none. Didn't need to. Everyone knows Mr. Aldery." She said it without irony, without malice — simply as fact, the way one says *the river freezes in winter*. "Now, miss, I'll say what I'm allowed to say and then no more. The child ate her supper. She didn't cry till the lamp went out, and then only a little. This morning she asked after 'the clock lady,' and I said I didn't know any clock lady, and she said—" the wardress hesitated, and for the first time her competence cracked enough to show the woman underneath, tired and honest — "she said, *that's all right, clocks wait.*"

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us a floorboard creaked, a child turning over in a crowded bed.

"Can I see her?"

"Not today. Visiting is Thursday, and not for you unless you've standing." The wardress folded her arms, and I saw that she was bracing herself, that she had decided something between the door and the stove. "And miss — I'll give you one piece more for free, because I liked the look of her and I don't like how she came. Mr. Aldery's man was back this morning before seven. Not to see the child. To see the book." She tapped the ledger on the desk. "He copied out the entry page. Both sides. And he left word that if anyone came asking after Martha Grey — anyone at all — I was to send to Cordwainer Street first, before admitting them Thursday or any other day."

"So if I come Thursday—"

"He'll know within the hour. Whatever you mean to do, miss, do it knowing that." She looked at me steadily. "I keep the rules. It's how I keep the roof on forty children's heads. But I count pages, same as I count spoons. And I'll tell you what I counted: that entry was wrote in a fair hand, but the ink of the date is darker than the ink of the body of it. Like somebody filled in the date after." She picked up her candle. "Which could mean nothing. Ink dries uneven. Good morning to you."

* * *

I stood in Cordwainer Street in the full grey of morning, and for a long time I could not make my feet go anywhere.

Here is what I understood, standing there, and it broke me open in a way I am still not certain has healed: for ten years I had believed my silence was a kind of armor. My master had taught me which questions not to ask, which customers to serve last, which debts to pretend not to see, and I had obeyed so thoroughly that obedience had become my character. When the widow Kessel warned me to burn the papers and run for the coast, I refused — refused *for the child*, I told myself, but also because silence had been safe so long I could no longer imagine any other posture in the world. When Aldery offered me the workshop free and clear if I called the girl a foundling of no account, I refused that too, and thought myself brave.

But I had refused everything *quietly*. I had hidden the will instead of publishing it. I had moved the papers by night instead of carrying them into the daylight of the guildhall and letting them fall where they fell. Every choice I had made since the note on the steps — *forget the face, finish the work* — had been a choice to keep my own hands clean while keeping the truth in the dark. And a truth kept in the dark belongs to whoever owns the dark.

Aldery owned the dark. He had bought it, parcel by parcel, for years: the magistrate, the parish guardians, the men at the chandler's shed, and — God forgive him, and God forgive me for loving him anyway — my own master, whose ledger I had carried across town in the belief that paper could outrun a man who had spent twenty years buying every hand in Vellamarch that might otherwise have signed against him.

He had not needed to break my lock. He had only needed to wait until I left the building, because he had known I would leave it. Perhaps he had even arranged the timing — a word to Tam Osk, a rumor, a nudge; I would learn later that the boy's payment had come from a stranger at the ferry stairs, and I would lie awake over that. It hardly mattered. What mattered was this: while I was winning the war for documents, he had taken the only territory that had ever actually been in dispute.

A child cannot be hidden in a clock case. But a child can be buried alive in a parish ledger, under a false name, behind rules that no decent person may break — and the burying is legal, witnessed, charitable, paid in advance. He had done it in the space of six hours, with a fair hand and darker ink, and he had done it *while I slept* — no. While I worked. While I did the very thing the sealed note had commanded: finished the work, forgot the face.

I had forgotten the face. I had walked out of that room and locked the door on her and gone off to move paper, and I had not even said goodbye properly, because I had promised her we would wind Oscar together in the morning.

* * *

The guildhall opened at eight. I was on its steps at ten minutes to, and Pellworth the clerk let me in early because I was shaking too visibly to be turned out into the cold.

Guildmaster Harneth was at his bench in the inner room, squinting through his eyeglass at a fusee chain, and when I put the oilcloth packet on his bench and told him what had happened, in as few words as I could manage, he set down his tools very slowly and carefully, the way a man sets things down when his hands want to do something else entirely.

"They took her into the ward under a false name," I said. "Legally she doesn't exist. Legally *Elowen Marchmont* doesn't exist until the hearing proves her — and if I prove her, I prove myself a kidnapper of record. That's the trap. He built it while I was carrying the evidence to safety."

Harneth took off his eyeglass and rubbed the bridge of his nose. He was an old rival of my master's, and he had listened longer than I expected once, and I had dared to hope. Now he looked at me across the bench with something worse than anger: with the weary clarity of a man watching a younger version of himself pay for an old mistake.

"You went at night," he said.

"There were watchers on the observatory. It was the only—"

"You went at *night*," he repeated. "Girl, sit down. No — stand, I don't care, but listen. Do you know what a guild charter is *for*? Not for sneaking. A charter is a public instrument. It exists so that when the magistrate is bought and the parish is bought and the assizes clerk has the ruling drafted in his drawer, there remains one door in this town that opens in daylight, in front of witnesses, with a bell on it." He pointed at the ceiling, toward the hall above, where the guild's charter hung framed in ironwork — instruments of public time, our jurisdiction, our standing, our one unbuyable thing. "Your master knew that. Why do you think he spent his life being hated at dinners? He hid the will inside a *guild-certified public clock*, girl. Not inside a drawer. Inside the one machine in Vellamarch that Aldery couldn't buy without everyone seeing the purchase."

"And it wasn't enough," I said. "They broke the escapement. They broke the second escapement. And now they've taken her, and Thursday they'll know I'm coming before I reach the grate—"

"Then we stop going quietly." Harneth put his eyeglass back on, and something in his face changed — the craftsman's look, the look I knew from my own mirror at 3 a.m., when a mechanism refuses and the mind goes cold and clear and begins, gear by gear, to find the fault. "Under the charter, the guild holds standing over any estate in dispute whose instruments fall under our certification. The observatory clock is such an instrument. An heir in dispute is a matter touching the integrity of the founder's bequest. A bequest touches the instrument's endowment. Therefore the guild may demand examination of persons materially affecting a certified instrument — including, if I frame the petition correctly, any ward entered into parish care *during the period of that dispute*, under circumstances the guild may lawfully require to be investigated." He smiled without warmth. "Aldery used the law's letter to bury her. Very good. But the law has two doors, Miss Winterbourne, and I told you once I'd show you the second. We're going to walk through it in broad daylight, with witnesses, and make him watch."

"When?"

Harneth glanced at the window, where the light had come up hard and white over the rooftops, and beyond the roofs, faint even here, we could both hear the river — not groaning now, but cracking, sharp reports like distant gunfire, the ice beginning to lift along the banks.

"Before the term closes," he said. "Before the ferry runs and Aldery's circuit judge arrives with his purchased ruling. Which means we file tomorrow." He looked at me a moment longer, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Go home, girl. Eat something. Sleep if your body will allow it. Tomorrow we do this in daylight, and daylight requires you upright."

* * *

I did not go home. Or rather — I had no home that was not the observatory, and I could not bear its emptiness, so I walked, and my feet chose the road without asking me, and I ended where I had known all along I would end: at the shop on Wheelwright Lane, at the widow Kessel's door, with the morning bell ringing flat across the wet slate roofs.

She opened before I knocked. She had been crying; I could see she had been crying for some time, days perhaps, and that she had made herself presentable over it the way she made bread — thoroughly, out of duty, with no expectation of pleasure.

"I heard," she said. "Osk's mother told me at the pump. The whole lane knows by now. Lior—" and then she stopped, because I suppose my face told her the rest, and she reached out and took my wrist with fingers that smelled of yeast and lampblack, and drew me inside.

The workshop was just as he had left it. His bench, his loupe, his tin of mixed screws with its worn label in his handwriting. Ten years of silences had been manufactured in this room, and his ghost sat in every corner of it, and I sat down on his stool without asking and put my head in my hands and wept like a child — not for the papers, not for the hearing, not even for the oath, but for a small voice saying *that's all right, clocks wait.*

The widow let me cry. Then she sat down across from me, folded her chapped hands, and said, in the voice she used for hard truths sold at cost:

"Now you understand what I tried to tell you. Burn the papers. Take the child. Go to the coast." She leaned forward. "But you won't. So listen to me instead, because I loved that wicked clever man thirty years, and I know how he thought, better than you ever did. Aldery has made one mistake, girl, and it's the mistake proud men always make: he's done it *legally*. Legal things leave records. Records can be read." Her eyes went to the ledger packet still under my arm, and then, unbearably, to her husband's empty bench. "And there is one more record he doesn't know exists. One more page in one more book. He bought my husband's silence — but he never thought to ask whether the silence was *only* his to sell."

I lifted my head.

"The night before your master died," the widow Kessel said, "he wrote a letter. Sealed it. Told me: *if ever the girl finds her spine, give her this.*" She rose, went to the cupboard above the bench — the cupboard I had dusted a thousand times as an apprentice and never once been permitted to open — and took down a small tin box, and set it in my hands.

"It's addressed to you," she said. "It always was."

Outside, the river boomed — a long, splitting crack, louder than any before it, and somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark, and the ice of ten silent years shifted all at once, everywhere, and began at last to move.
