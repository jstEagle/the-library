## 24. The Workhouse

The parish workhouse stood at the end of Tanner's Row like a tooth gone bad in an otherwise serviceable jaw — a long gray building of two stories, its windows soaped with lime to keep the poor from looking out and the respectable from looking in. Lior had walked past it perhaps a hundred times in her life without once considering what happened behind the glass. Now she stood before the iron gate with her hands curled into fists inside her mittens, and the gate looked back at her the way gates do: patiently, indifferently, as though it had outlasted better people than either of them.

The bell rope was stiff with frost. She pulled it three times before she heard footsteps within.

A pauper-guard opened the door on a chain — a broad woman with a face like a boiled ham and keys strung across her apron the way other women wore beads. Her eyes did the arithmetic of Lior's coat, boots, and bearing in under a second and arrived at a number she considered acceptable.

"Visiting is Thursdays," the woman said. "Between two and four."

"It's Thursday."

"Who is it you've come for?"

"A child brought in yesterday evening. A girl, about six. Brought by —" Lior made herself say it flatly, as if the words were ordinary goods on an ordinary invoice. "By persons unknown. She'd have been registered under some name that isn't hers."

The guard's mouth tightened with something that might have been sympathy or might have been indigestion; from this close they were hard to tell apart. "Foundlings go in the north ward. We've two girls of that age come in since the cold snap." She unhooked the chain and stepped back. "You can look through the grate. Five minutes. No gifts, no promises, no money passed. Them's the rules, and I don't make 'em, I only enforce 'em, so don't start."

"I wouldn't dream of starting," Lior said, and heard how much of Aldery's polished smoothness had crept into her own voice. It sat wrong in her mouth, like a coin of the wrong weight.

* * *

They took her down a corridor that smelled of lye and boiled cabbage and old wet wool, past a room where rows of women bent over laundry tubs without looking up, past a room where men mended sacks with the slow, defeated rhythm of hands that had forgotten every other motion. The plaster was stained green where the damp came through. Somewhere above them a baby was crying with the tireless, mechanical persistence of a thing wound up and left running.

Lior thought: *this is what he bought.* Not a lie, not even cruelty in any dramatic sense. This. Cabbage and lime and a crying baby and five minutes through a grate on Thursdays. He had done it all with paper, properly witnessed, entirely legal, and the town would call it orderly settlement.

The north ward was at the corridor's end. A door, and then another door — the second one barred with a grate of iron slats set at a hand's width apart, so that visitors could see the children and the children could see the visitors and nothing more substantial than light and voice could pass between. Beyond it lay a long low room with straw pallets ranked along the walls like bunks in a ship's hold. Two dozen children sat or knelt or lay in the afternoon dimness, quiet in the way of children who had learned that noise earned nothing here.

She found the girl at once. Of course she did. The girl was sitting cross-legged at the far end of the third pallet row, apart from the others, very straight, hands folded in her lap — sitting the way Lior had taught her to sit when waiting for glue to set, which was to say: still on the outside, counting on the inside.

Something went through Lior's chest like a punchwheel through brass plate. She gripped the grate.

"Maren," she said.

The girl's head came up. For one heartbeat she didn't move — the wariness of a child who had already learned, in one day, that hope in this place was a currency you were taxed for holding. Then she saw who stood behind the bars, and her whole body changed, the way a clock changes when the escapement catches after a stall: everything suddenly moving again, alive, ordered, going somewhere.

She crossed the floor fast and hit the grate with both palms, and her face pressed between the slats, and she said, in a fierce whisper pitched to carry exactly one yard and no further:

"I knew you'd come."

"I came as fast as I could." Lior got her fingers through the gaps and found the girl's hands. They were cold. That was the detail that undid her — not the straw, not the smell, not the lime-soaped windows, but the small cold fingers, and the fact that Maren did not cry, because Lior had taught her that too, or because the workhouse teaches it faster than any master ever could. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took a night. There were papers, there were —"

"I know." The girl's eyes flicked toward the pauper-guard standing well back down the corridor, then back up. "Don't talk loud. Miss Ham says talking gets you marked."

"Miss Ham."

"The lady with the keys. She's not so bad. She gave me extra bread because I fixed the door latch in the washroom and she didn't ask how." A ghost of the gap-toothed grin. "I used a spoon."

Lior laughed — a broken, wet sound, half sob, and she hated herself for both halves. "Of course you did. Of course you used a spoon."

"They put me under a name." Maren's voice dropped further, and now the grin was gone and something older was in its place, something Lior recognized with horror as her own expression — the look of someone doing sums about survival. "They wrote it in their book. *Martha Wick.* But that's not my name."

"No," Lior said. "It isn't."

"And I know what my name is."

The corridor seemed to tilt slightly. Lior kept hold of the small cold hands and kept her voice level, because if she let it shake the girl would hear it, and if the girl heard it the girl would carry it, and this child carried enough. "Do you. Tell me."

Maren glanced down the corridor again — the little sentry's check Lior had seen her perform a dozen times at the observatory window — and then leaned her forehead against the bars, close enough that Lior could see the chapped skin, the smudge of ash at her hairline, the place where a bruise was coming up yellow-green along her jaw from a fall or worse.

"Oscar used to sing it," she said. "When Mama wound him. He sang it in the bells. Grandpapa's name and Mama's name and mine, all together, every hour, and Mama said *listen, listen, that's your name in the big voice, no one can take that out of the air.*"

The world went very quiet and very far away. Lior heard the baby crying upstairs, heard the sack-menders' needles, heard her own blood. *Oscar.* The founder's nickname for the clock — she had read it scrawled in the margin of the maintenance ledger, *Oscar wants oiling,* in a hand gone shaky years before the founder died. The child knew the clock by his pet name. The child had been told, in whatever fragmentary way a mother tells a six-year-old the most important thing she'll ever tell her, exactly whose blood ran in her.

"He sang it last week too," Maren went on. "Before they came. Oscar sang my name and then he stopped, and Mama's friend said *not yet, not yet,* and put me in the basket, and then the river ice went *crack crack crack* all night like teeth." She pulled back just far enough to look Lior full in the face, and her eyes were dry and enormous and absolutely certain. "You fixed his singing. I heard it stop being broken. You're the one who makes Oscar honest."

"Yes," Lior managed. "That's me. That's — yes."

"Then you know." It wasn't a question. It was the girl pressing her whole small certainty against the bars the way she might press a palm against a warm stove: testing, trusting, burning if need be. "You know my name. Say it."

And Lior understood that this was the moment the chapter of her life turned on — that the child was not asking for comfort, was not asking for bread or rescue or even reassurance. She was asking for the one thing Aldery had spent a fortune to erase, the one thing no workhouse register could overwrite if Lior spoke it aloud and meant it. Names were the truest mechanism ever built. Wind them and they run.

"Elowen Vellamarch," Lior said quietly, clearly, letting each syllable seat itself like a jewel into its setting. "Daughter of Sera Vellamarch. Granddaughter of Josiah Vellamarch, who built this observatory, who built Oscar, who left you everything he had. That is your name. I will spend the rest of my life making the world say it back to you."

The girl closed her eyes. Her chin trembled once — a single fault-line — and then steadied, and when she opened her eyes they were dry, and Lior thought, with a pride so fierce it hurt worse than the grief: *there. There it is. That's not a foundling's face. That never was.*

"That's right," Elowen said, with the grave satisfaction of a journeyman checking her master's work and finding it true. "That's my name. Don't forget it."

"As if I could."

* * *

They had perhaps three of the five minutes left. Down the corridor, Miss Ham stood with her arms folded, watching the ceiling with the studied blindness of a woman who enforced rules she hadn't made and despised half of them. Lior lowered her voice to the register she used for explaining delicate work — the one that made listeners lean in rather than lean away.

"Listen to me carefully, because I can only stay a short time, and everything I say next matters. Do you understand? Everything matters."

Elowen nodded. Her hands came back to the bars and gripped Lior's fingers.

"I'm working to get you out. Properly out — not over a wall at night, but out through the front, with papers, with your name in the right books instead of the wrong ones. It will take days. Maybe a week. It feels like forever and it isn't. Can you be patient the way I taught you?"

"Counting-patient?"

"Counting-patient. Exactly. While you wait, I want you to do three things. First: eat everything they give you, even the cabbage. Especially the cabbage. Your body is a movement train and I won't have it running light."

The ghost of the grin again. "Even the cabbage."

"Even the cabbage. Second: fix things. Quietly. Latches, stools, the pump handle, anything that's broken and won't be missed being mended. Make yourself useful in ways nobody ordered. It keeps your hands remembering they're clever, and it makes Miss Ham owe you, and owed people are kind people."

"Like you and Master Corvane's widow."

Lior blinked. "Yes. Like that. You remember everything, don't you."

"Everything important." And then, softer, with the terrible directness of six-year-olds: "Do you remember everything?"

"No," Lior said honestly. "I forgot things for ten years because forgetting felt safe. It wasn't. I'm spending all of it now, every piece, to get you home. So learn from me the cheap way: remembering costs less."

Elowen considered this with the gravity she gave to gear trains. Then she said, "Third thing."

"Ah." Lior hesitated. The third instruction was the hardest one, and the truest, and she almost softened it — almost wrapped it in the cotton wool adults use when they mean *be afraid.* She thought of the observatory wrecked in the night, the escapement shattered, the circle of trust narrowed to a knife-edge. She thought of Aldery's men watching the doors. She thought of what she would want a fourteen-year-old apprentice to know, if she ever had one, in a building full of strangers.

"The third thing is this. Someone may come for you. A man, or a woman dressed finer than Miss Ham, saying they're taking you to family, to a new home, to your mama. If anyone comes who isn't me — anyone at all — you ask them to say your name. Just that. Ask them: *what's my name?* If they say Martha Wick, or any name but the true one, you scream. You scream until the roof rings and Miss Ham comes running, and you tell her a stranger tried to carry you off, and you keep screaming until strangers are the least of that person's troubles. Can you do that?"

"Scream my name?"

"Scream *until* they give you your name. It's the same thing, nearly."

Elowen nodded slowly, filing it among her permanent holdings. "And if they do know it?"

"Then they're on our side, and you'll know it, because they'll say it the way I just did — like it's worth something." Lior squeezed the small fingers. "No one who means you harm will ever bother to learn what you're called. Harm doesn't learn names. That's how you'll always know it."

The girl absorbed this in silence. Then, glancing once more down the corridor, she did something Lior did not expect and would remember for the rest of her life.

She let go of Lior's hands, reached into the front of her rough gray smock, and drew out something small that she had been keeping against her stomach, warmed by her own body through the long cold day and night of her first imprisonment. She pressed it through the bars into Lior's palm.

A cog.

Brass, forty-odd teeth, worn bright at the pivots — and Lior knew it instantly, the way a mother knows a child's cough in a crowded church. It was from the strike-train. One of the small intermediate wheels that carried the count from the going barrel up to the hammer lift, the ones she had cleaned and re-bushed herself, lying in her disassembly tray on the bench by the east window, catalogued, accounted for, hers until the repair was done. Stolen — no, *taken*, taken deliberately, taken the way a person takes a hostage — on the night they came for her, or in the chaos of the morning they led her out.

"You lost this," Elowen whispered. "When they broke your pretty wheel, pieces went everywhere, and I picked this one up off the floor and put it in my pocket because—" Her voice caught, the first crack in her composure, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything. "Because it was part of Oscar's song. And because if I kept a piece of him, then I wasn't — I wasn't nowhere. I was somewhere. I was *his.*"

Lior stood with the cog in her hand and could not speak.

It was warm. That was the unbearable part. It was warm from the child's body, this small machined tooth-wheel that had counted hours for thirty years before either of them was born, that had sung her name in bells every hour of her mother's winding — warm, alive-seeming, humming faintly with borrowed heat like a thing that remembered its purpose and refused, in captivity, to forget it.

A message, and she read it perfectly. *I know what I am. I know whose I am. I am not Martha Wick and I am not nowhere. I am the granddaughter of Josiah Vellamarch and I am a piece of Oscar and I have been keeping myself safe for you, the way you keep the works safe for me.*

Fierce pride rose in her like floodwater. Heartbreak rose beside it, and she let them stand together in her chest, twin weights on one beam, balanced, because neither alone was the truth.

"You shouldn't have kept it," Lior said gently. "If Miss Ham had searched your pockets—"

"She did search. First night." A flicker of the grin, quick and wicked as a spark off a grinding wheel. "But I'm not stupid. I didn't keep it in my pocket. I kept it in my cheek till lights-out, and then I hid it inside the seam of my mattress, in the straw, and I moved it every morning to a different pallet. Nobody counts straw." She held out her hand, palm up, imperious. "Give it back. It's safer with me till you come. You've got a hole in your tray now and everyone knows you're missing a piece — if it turns up in your coat they'll say you stole it, and I know how stories work in this town now. I've had a whole day of lessons."

Lior stared at her. Six years old. One night in a workhouse. Already thinking three moves ahead along the exact grooves Aldery's town had cut into everyone unlucky enough to live in it — and refusing, with every scrap of her small ferocious intelligence, to be cut to fit them.

She pressed the cog back into the girl's palm and closed the small fingers over it with both of her hands.

"Guard it," she said. "Guard it like a signature. When we walk out of here — and we will walk out, through the front door, in daylight, with your name spoken aloud in the register book and struck clean through in the right ink — you bring it home to Oscar yourself. You'll put it in his train with your own hands. That's a promise, and I keep them the way clocks do. Slowly. In order. All of them eventually."

"All of them eventually," Elowen repeated, sealing it, and Lior saw that the child understood the phrase completely — understood that it was not a hedge but the deepest kind of vow a clockmaker could make, the vow of the mechanism itself, which forgives nothing and forgets nothing and arrives.

Down the corridor, Miss Ham shifted her weight and cleared her throat with the delicacy of a landslide. "Time," she called, without malice. "Five minutes is five minutes, miss, or the superintendent has my hide."

"One moment more," Lior called back, and got, surprisingly, a grunt that might have been agreement.

She leaned her forehead against the grate, opposite the girl's, so that the iron bars made a small fence between two faces that had, in the space of three weeks, become the whole geography of each other's lives.

"Counting-patient," Lior whispered.

"Counting-patient," Elowen whispered back. "Go be clever. I'll be clever here."

Lior stepped back. The girl did not wave — waving was for children with nothing to guard — but lifted one hand and touched two fingers to her sternum, over the hidden cog, over the hidden name, a gesture Lior had never taught her and understood completely anyway. *Kept safe. Kept true. Wound and waiting.*

She walked up the corridor with her spine straight and her eyes dry and her heart in pieces, and behind her, fading, she could hear the baby start up again, and the needles in the sack-room, and beneath it all — so faint she might have imagined it, except that she chose, permanently, not to imagine it — a small clear voice counting. Steadily. Patiently. Keeping time.

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the wind off the river carried a sound underneath it that the whole town had begun to sleep badly to: the ice, shifting, muttering, counting too.

She had promised the girl all of it, eventually. Which meant the guildmaster's price was due tomorrow, and Aldery's watchers were on the observatory steps tonight, and somewhere between the two lay the only road out of Tanner's Row that ended anywhere good.

Lior pulled her collar up against the wind and turned toward the guildhall, walking fast, a clockmaker with an appointment to keep and nothing left in the world she wasn't prepared to spend.
