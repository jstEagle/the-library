## 25. The Guildmaster's Price

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and cold iron, the way it had smelled when I was fourteen and stood in this same room with my hands shaking behind my back while Master Hollis read out the terms of my apprenticeship to a panel of men who barely looked up from their tea. Ten years later I stood on the same flagstones, and the same iron smell was in the air, and my hands were shaking again — though this time I made sure no one could see it.

The guildmaster's name was Corvane. He had been an old rival of my master's even before Master Winterbourne died, and he had the particular patience of men who have spent forty years listening to arguments and finding every one of them insufficient. He sat behind a desk that had been carved sometime before the town had a name, with a lamp turned low and a fire burning too small for the size of the room, so that the hall's shadows gathered around him like unpaid debts.

He did not offer me a chair.

"You've been here twice already this week," he said. "The clerk tells me you waited four hours yesterday. In February."

"I did."

"Most people give up after two hours." He folded his hands on the ledger in front of him. "So either you are very stupid, or what you want is worth waiting for. Sit down anyway. Standing petitioners make me feel like a magistrate, and I have spent my life avoiding that profession."

I sat. The chair across from him was hard oak, worn smooth by generations of anxious bottoms, and I understood suddenly that it was worn smooth by design — by guildmasters who wanted petitioners uncomfortable, who wanted them honest.

"Tell me again," Corvane said. "Slowly. The first time you told it to my clerk, you told it like a frightened woman. Frightened women leave things out."

I took a breath. "The observatory master clock has failed. It has been dead eleven days. Under the founder's bequest — Ambrose Vellamarch's bequest, sealed into the guild charter itself — if the clock runs and is certified running at each quarter-day, the estate passes intact to his lawful heir. If the clock stands dead past the assizes, the estate reverts as an unsettled instrument, and the creditors may claim against it. Mr. Aldery holds most of those notes."

"I know what Aldery holds," Corvane said dryly. "Aldery holds half the mortgages between here and the coast road, including, I am given to understand, one against a clockmaker's workshop on Tanner's Lane."

The words went through me like a probe finding the wound. "You've looked into me."

"I look into everyone who walks through that door. Continue."

"The clock didn't fail of neglect. I opened the movement. Someone cut the escapement's pallets — deliberately, cleanly, with knowledge of the mechanism. It was done recently. Within the year. Possibly within the month." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the voice I used with difficult customers, and hated it. "And inside the case I found papers. A will, unfinished but witnessed. A register. Evidence that the founder named an heir — a granddaughter — whom the town has never been told about. That child is currently sitting in my workshop eating bread and honey and asking me questions I don't always know how to answer."

Corvane said nothing for a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, in the rafters, a pigeon shifted its weight, and the sound was so much like the settling of a great clock that my chest tightened.

"And the magistrate," he said at last.

"Has already drafted his ruling. I saw it in the clerk's office at the assizes. Dated ahead of the hearing. In Aldery's favor."

"Yes." Corvane reached out and closed the ledger in front of him with a small, deliberate sound. "Yes, I imagine he has."

* * *

Here is what I had not told the clerk, and what I told Corvane now, because he was right: frightened women leave things out, and I had left out the part that mattered most.

The guild charter, drawn up when Vellamarch was still a village with ambitions, gave the Clockmakers' Guild standing over instruments of public time. Not over private clocks, not over shop pieces, not over the little mantel engines that marked the hours in parlors along the river. But a public clock — a clock that governed the town's ferries, its market bells, its legal day — belonged to the guild's authority in a way that no creditor's lawyer could argue away, because the charter predated every debt Aldery held and would outlast them all. If the guild certified the master clock as repaired and running, then the condition of the founder's bequest was met, on the record, under seal, and the hearing could not simply be postponed into nothing. The estate would stand. The heir would have her claim.

That was the law's second door, and I had found it after three nights of reading the charter by candlelight with the girl asleep against my shoulder, and I had walked into the guildhall that morning believing — God help me, believing — that all I had to do was show them the sabotage and they would act.

Corvane listened to all of it without moving. When I finished, he rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the river. The ice lay white and seamless from bank to bank, but I had seen the edges darkening, seen the water weeping through, and I knew he saw it too. Everyone in Vellamarch was counting the days now. Even the pigeons seemed to count.

"Do you know what your master did," Corvane said, still facing the window, "the last time he stood in this room?"

"No."

"He came to ask the guild to certify the observatory clock after the founder died. Eleven years ago. He brought the same evidence you're describing — or some of it; he was never a man to show his whole hand. And the guild refused him." He turned back. "Do you know why?"

My throat had gone dry. "Because the magistrate sat on the panel in those days."

"He did. And because half the guild's operating funds came from Aldery's father before him, and the other half from fees on workshops like yours — workshops that could be called due whenever the guild displeased the wrong man. Your master understood that better than anyone. He stood where you're standing and watched us fold like wet paper, and he walked out of here, and he never once set foot in this hall again. Twenty-two years of membership. He paid his dues by leaving coin with the clerk rather than come himself." Corvane smiled, and there was no pleasure in it. "He thought we were cowards. He was right. He also thought we were finished. He was wrong about that, but only barely, and only because cowards keep accounts longer than brave men do."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you came in here with a speech prepared, and speeches assume an audience that wants to say yes. I need you to understand what you're actually asking." He returned to the desk but did not sit. "Certifying the clock means invoking the charter. Invoking the charter means the guild formally contests the settlement of the largest estate in this valley, against a man who owns our magistrate, our ferry, our timber contracts, and — as of last autumn — a substantial portion of this hall's roof repairs, which he donated, publicly, at the founding feast, with a speech about civic pride. Do you remember the speech?"

"I wasn't invited to the founding feast."

"No. You wouldn't have been." He sat down heavily. "If I do this, Aldery will not ruin the guild with lawyers. Lawyers cost money and take time. He will ruin us the way he ruins everything — quietly, through ledgers. Contracts withdrawn. Fees raised. Debts called. Within a year there will be no Clockmakers' Guild in Vellamarch. There will be six old men meeting in a tavern complaining about the price of brass, and within five years there won't even be that."

The fire ticked. My hands were steady now, which surprised me.

"So you'll refuse," I said.

"I didn't say that."

* * *

What he did instead was worse. What he did was lean forward, put both hands flat on the desk, and name his price.

"There is a way," he said, "to do this cleanly. One way. The charter allows the guild to certify an instrument when a member of good standing presents it for inspection and swears to the work. Member of good standing, Lior. Sworn member. Not an apprentice. An apprentice presenting a commission of this magnitude — a commission touching an inheritance dispute, a contested estate, a child of unknown parentage — an apprentice has no standing at all. The certification dies in committee before the ink dries. Aldery's counsel would laugh us out of our own hall."

"Then grant me my papers. Test me. I've been ready for two years—"

"No." He said it gently, which made it crueler. "Journeywoman's papers require examination before the full panel, notice posted, thirty days' wait. We have nine days until the ice breaks and perhaps twelve until the assizes term closes. There is no time for the ordinary road. But—" and here he paused, and I watched him choose his words the way a man chooses footing on rotten ice, "—there is an old provision. Rarely used. The surrender clause. A member may transfer his standing to a named apprentice by formal surrender — he gives up his place in the rolls, she takes it, whole, immediately, examination waived, effective upon signature. It was written for dying masters passing shops to sons. It has been invoked eleven times in the history of this town. Every one of them was a deathbed."

"Then it can't apply. My master's dead. He died ten years ago."

"Not his standing to give." Corvane's eyes held mine, and I felt the floor tilt beneath the chair, felt the shape of the trap assembling itself around me with the clean, patient geometry of a mechanism. "Mine. I am prepared to surrender my own seniority — my seat, my vote, my standing in the rolls — to you. Effective today. You would walk out of this hall a journeywoman of the Clockmakers' Guild, with full authority to present the observatory clock for certification. By tomorrow it would be done, sealed, beyond Aldery's reach. The girl gets her name. The estate stands. Everything you came here for, delivered inside a week."

My heart was pounding so hard I could hear it in my ears, hear it the way I heard a train ticking in a case that hadn't yet been regulated — fast, eager, untrustworthy.

"What does it cost?" I asked, because with Corvane everything cost, and because some instinct older than hope told me to ask before I said yes.

"Nothing," he said. "From you."

And then he let the silence sit, and the silence did his talking for him, and I understood.

Not from *you*, he had said. From her. From the woman who surrendered the standing. "If I give you my seat," Corvane said quietly, "I cease to be a member of this guild. No seat, no vote, no standing. And a non-member cannot hold the office of guildmaster. The provision is explicit — it was written to prevent exactly the maneuver I'm proposing from being used twice. I sign, you rise, and I step down into the ordinary world like any retired tradesman. The new guildmaster will be elected from the remaining members within the fortnight." He smiled again, thin as a pallet spring. "And I will tell you something else for free, since you've waited four hours twice: the man who will be elected is Osric Bell. Bell holds notes from Aldery. Bell has wanted my chair for six years. Bell will certify your clock — he'll have no choice, it'll be under seal before he takes office — but he will never lift a finger for the hearing itself, never push the charter's standing one inch further than the minimum, and when Aldery comes to collect, Bell will hand this guild to him with a ribbon on it. You'll have your certification and lose the war inside a season."

"Then don't sign it," I said. "Find another way."

"There is no other way. There is only this way, and its price, and the price isn't mine." He spread his hands. "It's yours. Think, girl. Who taught you the trade? Who kept you fed through the bad winters? Whose bench did you sleep beside when you had nowhere else? You are asking me to believe that Lior Winterbourne will take the guild's protection, take the standing that should have been hers by right of work and never was because men like me refused her master, take everything — and in exchange, the guild loses its one incorruptible officer at the precise moment corruption owns the town. You'd be winning the battle and dismantling the army. And you'd be doing it to the only institution in Vellamarch that ever told Aldery no."

The chair was very hard beneath me. The fire was very small. I thought of the widow Kessel saying *burn the papers, take the child to the coast* — the first offer of safety, wrapped in love, and how I had refused it because safety bought with silence was just silence with a nicer view. And here was the second offer, wrapped in law and seals and the guild's honor, and it asked a different silence of me: not the silence of keeping secrets, but the silence of stepping aside. Take your prize and go. Be safe. Be finished. Let the machine run on without you.

Ten years ago, a letter had arrived naming me, alone, to repair a clock, and I had read it as an honor and a trap, and I had been right about both, and I had gone anyway, because the alternative was watching the workshop die. Every good thing that had come since had come from walking toward the trap instead of around it. The girl at the door. The note in the case. All of it.

"Who elected you guildmaster?" I asked.

Corvane blinked. "The members. Nineteen years ago."

"Were you the only candidate?"

"I was the best candidate. Which is not the same thing."

"Was there anyone better?"

He was silent a moment. "There was a man," he said finally, "who understood mechanisms better than any of us. Who could see into a movement the way other men see into a room. Who argued with the panel for three hours about escapement theory and lost on points but won the room. He withdrew his name before the vote. Said the guild wasn't ready to be right." Something moved behind Corvane's face, some old account being opened. "Your master, Lior. I'm speaking about your master."

"I know."

"He never forgave us. And I have spent nineteen years wondering whether the town would be different — whether *we* would be different — if I had stepped aside then and let a better man hold this chair." He tapped the desk once, a sharp, final sound, like a detent engaging. "So understand what I'm offering. Not charity. Arithmetic. This guild has one honest gear left in its train, and it's me, and gears are worthless sitting in a drawer. Put me to use."

"No," I said.

* * *

He stared at me. Of everything in that long conversation, I think that was the word he expected least.

"Girl—"

"No." I stood up. My knees were steady. Ten years of hunching over a bench had given me steadier knees than I'd ever had at fourteen. "You're offering me a bargain where I win and the guild loses and the only person who pays is the man brave enough to make the offer. That's not arithmetic. That's a ransom, and I won't pay it, and more to the point — you shouldn't. Nineteen years you've wondered what would have happened if you'd stepped aside for a better man. Don't spend the rest of your life wondering it again in the other direction."

"The surrender clause is the only road that moves fast enough—"

"Then I'll build a new road." My voice came out lower than I intended, and harder. "You want a member of good standing to present the clock. Fine. Find me one. Not one you manufacture by tearing yourself out of the rolls — one who already exists. There are working clockmakers in this valley who still owe their tickets to the guild, who aren't in Aldery's ledger, who haven't been bought. Old Pellarin upriver. The Ferro sisters. Half the men on your own panel hate Aldery privately and have never had a reason to say so out loud. Give them a reason. Show them the sabotaged escapement. Let them hold the piece Aldery's man cut with his own hands and tell them what happens to their trade, to all of it, if the man who owns the magistrate owns time itself."

"That's a speech," Corvane said. "Speeches assume an audience that wants to say yes."

"Then stop giving them speeches. Give them the escapement. Men like you trust objects more than words anyway — I learned that in this room, watching you listen to my master lose on points and win the crowd." I leaned on the desk, and the old oak was cold under my palms, and I thought: he touched this wood, ten years ago, and walked out, and never came back, and I am going to come back. "But hear my price, guildmaster, since we're trading in prices tonight. If the guild acts — really acts, under seal, under charter, not a gesture but a fight — then I will stand before your full panel when this is over. After the ice breaks, after the hearing, whatever's left of me. Examine me. Fail me if I deserve it. I won't ask for the surrender clause and I won't accept stolen standing. I want the papers I earn. Journeywoman's tickets, granted by this guild, in daylight, on merit — mine, and no one else's."

For a moment Corvane said nothing at all. The pigeon shifted overhead. Down at the river, faint through the frost-furred glass, came a sound like a far-off shot — the ice talking to itself in the night, the way it had begun to talk every night now, counting down.

"You realize," he said at last, slowly, "that if the panel examines you honestly, half of them will fail you out of spite, and the other half will fail you because you're right, and being right is the one sin this hall has never learned to forgive."

"Then I'll fail," I said. "But I'll fail as a clockmaker. Not as a foundling someone handed a chair to. My master taught me that every wheel in a train has to carry its own load — borrow the load from another wheel and the whole mechanism strips itself. You taught him that, maybe. Or he taught you. Either way, guildmaster: no surrender clause. Earned papers, or none."

Corvane looked at me for a long time. Then he laughed — one short, rusty bark, like a clock striking after years of silence — and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him, and dipped his pen.

"Pellarin owes me a favor older than you are," he said, writing. "Ferro owes me two. And Osric Bell is dining with Aldery tonight, which means by morning every man on my panel will know exactly whose side the future intends to be on, and some of them will find their spines purely out of contrariness." He wrote three lines, quickly, in a hand like engraved brass, then looked up. "Come back at dawn. Bring the sabotaged escapement. And bring the girl's papers — whatever you have of her mother's, the register, all of it. If we're going to commit treason against the magistrate of Vellamarch, we will do it with the documents in order."

I was at the door when he spoke again, and I stopped, because his voice had changed — quieter, stripped of the guildmaster, just an old man by a small fire.

"Lior. Your master withdrew his name from this chair because he thought the guild wasn't ready to be right. He may have been correct. But he left something better than a successor. He left you." A pause. "Don't repeat his mistake. When they offer you this seat someday — and they will — take it."

Outside, the cold hit me like clear water. The sky over the river had gone the color of pewter, and along the banks the ice had begun to sing — that high, groaning note the whole town knew, the sound of a deadline approaching faster than any document could travel. Nine days, perhaps fewer.

I turned toward Tanner's Lane, toward the workshop and the girl and the candle I'd left burning in the window, and I began to run.
