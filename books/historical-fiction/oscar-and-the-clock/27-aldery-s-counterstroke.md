## 27. Aldery's Counterstroke

The ledger of a town is not so different from the works of a clock, Aldery had always believed. Every part bears weight. Every wheel turns another. And the man who understands which tooth engages which pinion can make the whole mechanism do what he wishes while it appears to be doing nothing at all.

He sat in his study above the counting-house with the ferry's deed before him, still warm from the clerk's pen, and allowed himself the small pleasure of straightening its corner against the blotter until it lay square to the edge of the desk.

Three hundred and forty pounds. The old ferryman, Hobb, had wept when he signed — wept, and then shaken Aldery's hand, because grief and gratitude are not strangers in men who have spent their lives at the mercy of weather. *You're saving her from the ice,* Hobb had said, as though being bought were a kindness. Perhaps it was. Aldery had never found that kindness and profit could not share a purse. They made comfortable companions, each covering for the other's excesses.

*Vellamarch Ferry, sole right of crossing.* He read the words twice. The ink was good ink. It would hold.

* * *

He had learned administration young, from a father who kept his accounts in a fair hand and his conscience in no hand at all. His father had taught him one thing worth carrying: never fight a battle you can convert into paperwork. A man struck down in anger leaves witnesses. A man erased by procedure leaves only a file, and files do not testify.

So when word came — through the assizes clerk's under-clerk, whose sister did laundry for Aldery's household, whose brother-in-law drove Aldery's coal cart; the town was a web and he had spent thirty years touching every strand — when word came that the clockmaker girl had been to the guildhall, that the guildmaster had listened to her longer than courtesy required, that papers had moved by night along the towpath — Aldery did not rage. Rage was for men who had run out of instruments.

He rang for Brase, his man of business, who arrived with the particular gait of a man who already knows he will be up all night.

"Sit down, Brase. We are going to buy a river."

Brase sat. "The river's not for sale, sir."

"No," Aldery agreed. "But everything on it is."

* * *

The arithmetic was simple once you held the right numbers. The hearing — such as the guild intended it to be — would stand or fall on attendance. The circuit judge, the one honest instrument left in the county's case, was due on the first ferry after the ice broke. The magistrate had promised the estate matter would be heard *when the ferry runs*, and the phrase had seemed safe enough in February, when the river was still a white road and everyone spoke of the break as a farmer speaks of rain: certain in season, uncertain in day.

But Aldery owned the ferry now. And a ferry that does not sail needs no reason beyond safety.

"Ice out on the channel still," he said, tapping the map of the reach below Vellamarch. "Insurance surveyors will want an inspection. Insurers are careful men. Careful men take days."

"And the guild?" Brase asked. "They've got charter authority. They don't need the ferry."

"The guild needs a judge the way a sermon needs a congregation. Without the circuit man, they can rule on their clock — let them. But the estate, the inheritance, the child—" he said the word *child* without inflection, the way one says *barley* or *freight*— "the child is a matter for the law of the land. And the law of the land arrives by water."

He rose and went to the window. Below, the high street ran wet and lamplit, and beyond the rooftops he could see the pale seam of the river between the warehouses — still frozen, but rotten now at the edges, dark water showing like rot in fruit. Any day. The whole town felt it, that coiled tension before the roar, the way a house feels the moment before a door slams.

Everyone in Vellamarch was waiting for the ice to break.

Aldery intended to be the only man who knew exactly what would happen when it did.

* * *

The next morning he called on Magistrate Corven at home, which was itself a message; official visits happened at the courthouse, and domestic ones meant the matter was either too delicate or too profitable for walls with ears.

Corven received him in a dressing gown, grey-faced over coffee, and Aldery noted with mild distaste how quickly the man had gone soft since the election. Power without work was a sweet poison. He preferred his own arrangement: power with all the work done elsewhere, by other hands, invisibly.

"They intend to hold it in the observatory," Corven said. "Under guild charter. I've told them it's irregular."

"You've told them. Have you stopped them?"

Corven's cup rattled against its saucer. "On what grounds?"

"On no grounds at all, if you do it properly." Aldery took the chair opposite and did not ask permission, because asking permission teaches people they may refuse. "Corven. Think like a clerk, just for once in your life. What does a hearing require?"

"Witnesses. Papers. An officer of the court."

"Precisely. Now — the papers in question. The will, the ledger, the register. Where are they?"

"The guildhall. Under seal."

"Seals can be examined. Documents entered as evidence must be catalogued, authenticated, verified as to provenance." He smiled without warmth. "Verification requires the court's expert. And the court's expert is in Marchester, and cannot travel while the crossing is closed for the ice. You see? I am not stopping anything. Nothing is stopped. Things are merely... pending. Pending is the most respectable state in the world. Half the empire runs on pending."

Corven set down his cup. "And the term? The legal term closes when?"

"Twelve days after the break. Which is why the break matters." Aldery leaned forward. "If the hearing happens within the term, the guild's ruling stands and the circuit judge confirms it and I lose everything — the observatory, the bequest, the name. If it happens after, it dies in committee, and committees bury faster than gravediggers. So my entire fortune, Corven, rests on one question." He paused, savoring it. "When does the ferry sail?"

"And you own the ferry."

"I own the ferry." He let the sentence sit there, gleaming. "I have not yet decided when the ice makes it unsafe to cross. That is a judgment requiring expertise. Surveys. Reports. Possibly several reports, each more cautious than the last. Safety, Corven, is the one virtue that can be stretched as far as a man requires."

* * *

He worked through the list that afternoon, ticking names like a man closing shutters before a storm.

The insurance office in Marchester: a letter already drafted, expressing grave concern regarding spring navigation, requesting formal survey before any crossing resumed. Signed by three concerned citizens, two of whom owed him money and the third of whom was his cousin.

The surveyor himself: a cautious little man named Pell who had once taken eleven days to approve a bridge that was already standing. Pell would need no persuading. Pell's nature was the persuasion.

The newspaper: nothing so crude as a bribe. A piece on the dangers of early navigation, supplied by an anonymous correspondent *with engineering knowledge*. The town would read that the river was deadly, and by the third reading believe it had always known.

And the guildmaster. That one required care.

Aldery had known Ossenbruck for thirty years — had watched him and his master circle each other across two decades of guild politics like dogs separated by a fence. There was no buying Ossenbruck. But there might be no need. Guildsmen loved procedure the way monks loved silence, and procedure had a thousand rooms in which a matter could be politely lost. Let them certify their clock. Let them rule their ruling. Without the circuit judge to confirm jurisdiction over the estate, their verdict would be a beautiful machine with nothing attached to its wheelwork. He almost admired the symmetry: the clockmaker had hidden her truth inside a clock, and he would answer with the one clock she could not repair — the law itself.

* * *

It was Brase, at dusk, who raised the flaw.

"The girl," he said. "The Winterbourne woman. She doesn't wait well. She went to the guild, she went to the clerk, she moved the papers. If the term starts slipping past her, she'll try something else."

Aldery stood at the window again. Down the hill, the observatory dome caught the last light, a dull bronze coin on the black ridge. Somewhere inside it, presumably, the clockmaker and the child sat among gears and candlelight, believing that truth was a weight that would sink through any current given time.

"She'll try something else," he agreed. "That's what makes her worth the trouble. Her master was the same — ten years of silence, bought and paid, and underneath it all the same stubborn turning." He touched the glass, lightly, as though the observatory were an instrument he could adjust with one finger. "But see it clearly, Brase. She has three assets: the papers, the guild, and the child. The papers are in the guildhall, sealed — sealed documents move slowly, and slow is dead. The guild needs a judge who cannot come. Which leaves the child."

"You wouldn't," Brase said, and there was something in his voice Aldery chose not to examine. "She's six."

"She is a ward of an estate in dispute, currently housed in the parish workhouse under a name that isn't hers, entered in a register I helped write." He said it evenly, cataloguing rather than confessing. "I haven't touched her. I have merely ensured that the law knows where she is and that her circumstances are — irregular. Irregular children stay where the law puts them. That is what the law is *for*."

He turned back to the desk and began, in his fair, unhurried hand, the letter to Pell the surveyor. *Regarding the state of the channel ice below Vellamarch, and the prudent interval required before resumption of crossing—*

Outside, far off, there came a sound beneath the wind — deep, structural, like a door settling in a very large house. Both men paused. The ice on the river, shifting in its sleep.

"Soon," Aldery said softly. "Not yet."

*Not yet* — the phrase sat strangely in his mouth, and he wondered, briefly, why it tasted familiar. Then he sanded the letter, sealed it, and set it with the others: a neat stack of paper, each sheet innocent alone, together a wall.

Let the girl have her truth. Truth was heavy, and heavy things needed roads, and he owned every road that led to Vellamarch — the ferry, the court calendar, the term itself. When the ice broke, the river would roar and the town would cheer, believing the world was opening.

By then the world would already have quietly closed.

* * *

He was wrong about one thing, though he would not learn it until later, and it would cost him everything: he believed the girl's strength lay in what she carried — the will, the ledger, the proof. He built his wall against paper.

He never thought to count the hours. He never thought to ask what happens to a town that has waited all winter for a sound — and hears, instead of the ferry bell, a clock strike ten across the frozen valley, loud enough to wake the dead.

The ice went out three days early, in the night, with a roaring like judgment.

And Oscar was ready.
