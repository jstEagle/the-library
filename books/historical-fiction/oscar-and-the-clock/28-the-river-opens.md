## 28. The River Opens

The ice went at three in the morning, and it did not go quietly.

I woke to a sound I had heard only once before in my life, as a child, when my father carried me to the window and told me to remember it: a low groan beneath the town, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Then a crack — not the small sharp cracks of the past week that had been creeping inward from the banks all through the last days of February, but a single detonation that rattled the observatory windows in their frames and sent a fine rain of plaster dust down onto the clock room floor.

I was out of bed before I had decided to be. The girl was already at the window, wrapped in her blanket, her breath fogging the glass.

"It's singing," she said.

It was not singing. It was screaming. Down on the river the ice sheet, which had lain over Vellamarch since the feast of Saint Andrew like a lid on a pot, was heaving itself apart. Even from the height of the observatory hill I could see the fracture lines running white and jagged across the dark water, could hear the grinding roar as whole fields of ice buckled and drove themselves up against one another, ridging into walls taller than a man. The river had been counting the days longer than any of us. Now it had finished its arithmetic.

"Come away from the glass," I said, and she came, because by now she knew I said such things for reasons.

We stood together at the open window instead, cold pouring over us, and watched the town wake below. Lamps bloomed in the lower streets. Men ran toward the ferry landing with lanterns, then stopped, because there was nothing to be done — no landing left to save, if the pilings went; no ferry that would run this side of a week, perhaps two. Somewhere a church bell began to ring the alarm, and was answered by another across the water, both of them ragged and urgent against the roar of the river.

The girl's hand found mine. Her fingers were freezing. Neither of us moved.

"Lior," she said, after a long time. "Is this good or bad?"

I thought of Aldery's counsel, waiting across the river at Marchmont with his documents in order, his affidavits sealed, his purchased magistrate holding a ruling already drafted in his own hand. I thought of the legal term closing when the ferry resumed — and of the guildmaster's letter, which had arrived two nights before and which I had read so many times I could recite it: *By authority of the Guild Charter, section eleven, instruments of public time falling within an estate in dispute may be examined and adjudged wherever the guild shall convene. Venue follows the instrument.*

Venue follows the instrument. And the instrument — Oscar, the master clock, the dead man's testimony sealed inside his own heart — stood fifty feet behind me.

"It's good," I said. "God forgive me, child. It's very good."

* * *

By seven the lower town was a procession of wet hemlines and wild rumors. The flood had taken the timber yards first; the river, released, had shoved a battering raft of broken ice straight through them, and men were standing on the high bank watching their winter's work float away in pieces. The ferry landing was gone entirely — not damaged, *gone*, the whole structure lifted and carried downstream like a toy left in a gutter. Two skiffs were missing. A horse had drowned in its paddock by the water gate. No lives lost yet, or none they knew of, which in a town like Vellamarch meant the count might still rise with the water.

I dressed for work the way I always did — apron, tools, hair pinned tight — and then I took the papers out of the false bottom of my tool chest where they had lived for three weeks, wrapped in oilcloth: the half-written will, the master's ledger, the page from the founder's register. I weighed them in my hands. Three sheets of paper and a bound ledger, lighter than the smallest gear in the clock above me, heavier than anything else in the building.

The girl watched me dress them into the oilcloth again.

"You're going to the guildhall."

"I'm going to see the guildmaster. You stay inside. Doors locked, shutters closed until I knock twice." I knelt to her level. "If anyone knocks otherwise, you don't exist. Do you understand? You are nobody's business today."

She nodded, solemn, and then spoiled it the way she always spoiled it, reaching up and straightening my collar with two precise tugs, the way I straightened hers every morning. "Twice," she said. "Slowly. So I know it's you and not your echo."

I kissed the top of her head and went out into the roaring morning.

* * *

Vellamarch smelled of mud and pine sap and the green, mineral breath of a river waking. The streets sloping down to the water were rivers themselves, snowmelt running brown between the cobbles. Everywhere the same conversation, shouted across fences and doorways: *when will the ferry run?* Ten days, said some. A fortnight, said others. The boatmen shrugged and spread their hands; the crossing depended on the current dropping, and the current answered to no alderman.

And everywhere, threaded under the talk about lumber and landings, the other question, quieter: *what happens to the assizes now?*

The term closed when the ferry ran. Everyone knew it. Aldery's petition — the hearing that would settle the observatory estate, the bequest, the child, everything — sat across the river with the rest of the circuit papers, sealed and stamped and useless until a boat could carry it back. I saw the understanding move through the town like a shiver. Mr. Aldery had bought the law entire, everyone said so behind their hands, but even Aldery could not buy the weather.

At the guildhall the doors were already open and the hall already full — not with guild members, there were scarcely thirty of those in Vellamarch, but with half the town come to gawk, the way people gather at a hanging. The guildmaster stood on the steps in his chain of office, an old man in a good coat getting soaked, and beside him the guild clerk with the charter book under his arm, protected from the rain by an umbrella held by a boy who was being paid, I suspected, in nothing but the importance of it.

I pushed through to the foot of the steps. "Master Hollis."

He looked down at me, and for a moment the old rivalry with my dead master flickered behind his eyes — ten years of it, cooled now into something almost like respect, or at least like use. "Apprentice Winterbourne." He still called me apprentice. He knew what that word cost me. He used it anyway, deliberately, the way a man taps a wheel to hear if it rings true.

"The river has settled our date for us," I said.

"So it seems." He turned his face to the water, gray and thundering below the town. "The ferry won't run inside the term. Every document Mr. Aldery's men prepared dies unopened on the far bank. His counsel cannot cross. His affidavits cannot cross. And—" he smiled without warmth, "—neither can his magistrate. The assizes are adjourned by act of God, and God, I find, keeps better hours than the circuit court."

"And the guild?"

"And the guild," Master Hollis said, "keeps the second-best hours in Vellamarch." He raised his voice so the crowd could hear, and the crowd hushed, because a crowd will always hush for a man in a chain of office. "By authority of the guild charter, section eleven! The instrument known as the observatory master clock, being an instrument of public time within an estate presently in dispute, shall be examined, certified, and adjudged by this guild — at the place where the instrument stands! Let the record show the venue: the Observatory of Vellamarch, upon the hill, tomorrow at the striking of nine!"

A sound went up from the crowd — half cheer, half gasp. Someone laughed outright. On the hill, in the observatory, with the clock itself presiding. I felt the rightness of it go through me like a struck bell. My master had hidden the founder's will inside the machine because paper can be burned and stolen and bought, but a clock is public; a clock belongs to everyone who ever set their day by it. He had known that whoever finally opened it would have to do it in front of the town.

"Master Hollis." I kept my voice low, just for him, under the noise. "Aldery will fight the venue. He'll say the guild has no standing outside its own hall."

"He'll say a great many things." The guildmaster's eyes were pale and steady. "Let him. Section eleven was written eighty years ago, girl, by men who remembered what it is to have a great house swallow a small one. The charter says the guild may convene wherever its instrument stands. It does not say the instrument must consent to travel." He paused. "There will be a cost to this. To you especially. You know that."

"I've stopped pricing things," I said. "Everything costs more than I have."

Something moved across his old face — pity, maybe, or recognition. "Tomorrow at nine," he said. "Bring your evidence. Bring the child. And bring, if you can find it, your nerve. Aldery will bring his counsel's tongue, and it is sharper than anything in your toolbox."

* * *

I spent the afternoon in the clock room, and I worked, because working was the only prayer I had ever learned.

Oscar stood open to the waist, his long case panels folded back, his movement exposed — the great going train, the weight lines taut, the escapement I had cut in fourteen furious hours sitting in its cradle like a new-set bone. Tomorrow he would strike for the first time in ten years. I had promised myself that much regardless of what the guild decided; a clockmaker does not present a silent clock, whatever else the day holds.

But today, checking him over one final time, I found my hands were not steady, and that frightened me worse than Aldery, worse than the river. My hands had never been unsteady. Ten years of silence, ten years of careful, invisible work, and my hands had never once betrayed me. I set down the burnisher and pressed my palms flat against the cold case wood and breathed until the shaking stopped.

*You taught me that too,* I told my dead master silently. *Steady hands, quiet mouth. You built me for exactly this day and then you died before you had to watch it.*

The knock came at dusk. Twice, slowly.

I let her in and locked the door behind her, and she stood dripping in the entry hall, having disobeyed me in the smallest possible way — gone no farther than the doorstep, close enough to watch the street, far enough to break my rule. Her chin was set at the angle I had learned, these six weeks, meant defiance rather than fear.

"There was a man," she said.

The cold went through me like river water. "Where?"

"Across the lane. Under the yew. He wasn't hiding. That's how I knew to watch him. Hiding men look at windows. He looked at the door." She unwound her scarf with careful fingers. "He had a ring on a ribbon. Green ribbon. He turned it around and around while he waited."

Aldery's livery colors. I had seen that ribbon once, on a servant at the magistrate's dinner, and had spent the rest of the evening keeping my hands still.

"What did he do when you looked at him?"

"He bowed." She demonstrated — a small, mocking, perfect bow. "Like I was a lady. Then he left. Lior, why did he bow to me?"

Because Aldery had seen the guild notice posted, and understood faster than I did what tomorrow meant, and wanted her counted, watched, measured — or wanted her frightened, or wanted me frightened through her. Because a man who owns the law learns quickly when the law has slipped his grip, and turns to older tools.

I crouched to her. "Listen to me. Tomorrow you will stand in front of a room full of strangers, and they will ask you questions, and some of them will try to make you feel small, and one of them may try to make you feel *lost*. They will say you are nobody. They will say nobody knows whose child you are." I took her cold hands in mine. "But you know. And I know. And tomorrow, if you are willing, we tell everyone together. Not because I make you. Because you choose it. Do you understand the difference?"

She thought about it the way she thought about everything — completely, seriously, her head tipped. Then: "The clock doesn't ask the weights whether they want to fall."

"No," I said, startled into a laugh. "It doesn't."

"But it decides *when*." She squeezed my hands. "That's what you're giving me. The when. I choose the falling."

Six years old, and she had understood in one sentence what I had needed chapters of a dead man's notes to learn. I pulled her against me and held her, and over her shoulder the tall case stood in the lamplight, patient as stone, and I swear the tick of it sounded louder than the river.

* * *

They came for the hearing an hour before dawn — not the crowd, the crowd came later — but Aldery's men, four of them, walking up the hill road with a lantern, and I watched them from the clock room window and understood at once that they had not come to argue.

They had come to measure.

Two walked the perimeter of the observatory grounds. One examined the door hinges, the shutter fastenings, the chimney. The fourth — the man with the green ribbon, I was certain of it, though the lantern light made his face a smear — climbed as far as the base of the tower and stood there a long time looking up at the clock dial, black against the graying sky, its hands motionless at twenty past four where they had frozen a decade ago.

Then they went away down the hill, unhurried, and I stood at the window with my heart hammering and made myself think it through the way my master would have: *they are not soldiers, they are clerks. Whatever Aldery intends, he intends it through paper and procedure, because force would confirm everything the guild claims. He will contest the venue. He will produce witnesses. He will attack me — my character, my silence, my ten careful years. He will not break the law in view of the whole town. He doesn't need to. He owns most of it.*

Most of it. But not the hill, and not the clock, and not, as of this morning, the river.

Below, the flood had crested in the night. Water stood in the market square knee-deep, and boats moved down streets where carts should be, and the church bell rang not alarm now but warning — the bridge at Mill Lane was groaning under the ice load. The town had its hands full simply saving itself. There would be no crowd small enough, no silence deep enough, for anything done quietly on the observatory hill today. Nature had stripped away every shadow Aldery liked to work in and flooded the whole town with one flat, honest daylight.

The deadline I had feared all winter — the ferry, the term, the closing of the legal door — lay shattered in the timber yards with everything else the ice had taken. What remained was the thing I had not dared hope for: a hearing held in the house of the dead man himself, before his own machine, on authority no one had bribed because no one had thought it worth buying until it was too late.

I lit the lamps. I wound the weight lines and seated the new escapement and set the striking mechanism, tooth by tooth, the way my master had taught me at fourteen, swearing me to protect his work above all things.

*I am protecting it,* I told him. *You just never said protecting it might mean opening it.*

At half past eight I braided the girl's hair, and pinned my own, and put the oilcloth packet inside my coat, against my heart, where the papers rode warm and light as a bird.

Far below, the river roared. On the hour, for the first time in ten years, Oscar was going to speak — and everything he said, he would say to the whole town at once.
