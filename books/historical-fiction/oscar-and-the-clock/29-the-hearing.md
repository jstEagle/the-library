## 29. The Hearing

They held it in the observatory itself, because there was nowhere else the guild's authority could reach and no road that was still a road.

The ice had broken at dawn — not the polite cracking of the past fortnight but a roar, a long white avalanche of it, the river shrugging off its winter coat all at once. Lior had heard it from her cot beneath the great clock and thought, for one vertiginous moment, that the world was ending. Then she understood what it meant: the ferry could not run. Aldery's documents could not run either. The circuit judge, the magistrate's clerks, the lawyers summoned from Vellamarch's sister towns — all of them were stranded on the far bank with their sealed briefcases and their purchased opinions, watching the brown water carry whole trees like matchwood.

The hearing would happen today or not at all.

By nine o'clock the observatory dome was full of people who should never have fit inside it. The guildmaster sat at the founder's old surveying table with two assessors flanking him, men from Guildhalls upriver who had come by cart before the roads closed, their chain-of-office dull with road dust. Aldery sat opposite with his counsel, Mr. Quill — a thin, grey man whose voice Lior already hated from the way he arranged his papers: softly, precisely, like someone laying out instruments before an operation. The magistrate came too, uninvited and unstripped of anything yet, and stood near the door wearing the expression of a man attending his own funeral out of curiosity about the eulogy. Behind them crowded whoever could get in: the Widow Kessel in her blackest wool, the ferryman's boy with his cap crushed in both fists, half the town's small people — the chandlers and coopers and washerwomen whose debts Aldery held and whose eyes, Lior noticed, did not meet hers.

The girl was not there. The guildmaster had ruled it so the night before — *a ward in dispute is not displayed at her own trial* — and Lior had spent the morning wanting to argue and knowing he was right. The child waited instead in the clockmaker's room below with Mrs. Kessel's hired woman, and every quarter hour Lior felt the pull of the stairs like a tide.

The guildmaster opened without ceremony. "This hearing is convened under the Charter of the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers, Article Nine, concerning instruments of public time and estates bound to them. The observatory master clock is such an instrument. Its failure, its repair, and the papers found within its case are matters of guild jurisdiction. Mr. Aldery, you have standing here as claimant to the estate. Mistress Winterbourne, you have standing as the last apprentice of the late Master Hollis and as the instrument's appointed mender." He looked at her over his spectacles. "You will speak when asked. You will answer truly. The guild does not require you to be eloquent. Only honest."

*Only honest.* As if it were a small thing.

Lior stood. Her hands were steady — fourteen hours at the lathe had taught them steadiness that no fear could shake loose now — but her mouth was dry as brass filings.

"I am ready," she said, which was true, and which was also the most frightening sentence she had ever spoken aloud.

* * *

Quill began gently, the way a man tests ice before putting his weight on it.

"Mistress Winterbourne. You were apprenticed to Master Edmund Hollis for how long?"

"Ten years."

"And during those ten years, you lived where?"

"In his workshop on Cordwainer Lane."

"Kept his books?"

"Yes."

"Served his customers, knew his debts, handled his correspondence?"

"Yes."

Quill nodded, satisfied, and Lior understood with a cold drop in her stomach that every yes was being written down somewhere to be used against her later. He was building her, piece by careful piece, into exactly the witness he needed.

"You were summoned to this observatory," he said, "by a letter. Who wrote that letter?"

"I don't know. It bore the observatory seal."

"A letter of unknown authorship summons you alone — not the widow, not the creditors' appointed valuer, not any journeyman of the town — and you simply came." He let that hang. "You took possession of the premises."

"I took a commission. The commission kept the workshop from seizure—"

"We'll come to your workshop." A soft smile. "Tell the court about the night the child arrived. In your own words. Take your time."

So she told it. The steps, the sleet, the bundle of coats, the note in a hand she hadn't recognized until much later. *Forget the face, finish the work.* She heard herself say it aloud for the first time and heard how it sounded — how it sounded like exactly what it was, an instruction to abandon a child — and watched the assessors write it down without expression.

"And you obeyed," Quill said mildly.

"No."

"You hesitated, though. How long did the child stand on those steps before you opened the door?"

The question landed like a splinter driven under a fingernail. Because she *had* hesitated. She had stood with her hand on the latch while the sleet came sideways, telling herself it wasn't her business, telling herself about the commission, the workshop, the creditors, and the whole time some colder voice whispering *not your name on this doorstep, not your problem, Winterbourne.*

"Long enough to be ashamed of," she said.

A murmur went through the room — not hostile, not kind. Just the sound of a town discovering that other people's doors have latches too.

* * *

Then the evidence, in the order she had rehearsed it a hundred times against the dark.

The will first — the half-written document she'd found wired behind the striking train, naming *Mirela, daughter of Anselm Voss, my granddaughter* — laid on the table under glass. Quill examined it the way a jeweler examines paste. "Unsigned. Unwitnessed. Undated save by inference. A fragment, Mistress Winterbourne, found by a person with every motive to find it. What is it worth?"

"It's in the founder's own hand. I've compared it to forty pages of his engineering notes. The slant, the ink, the way he crosses his sevens."

"Clever of you to know his sevens." Quill turned a page. "The ledger next, if you please."

She gave them the ledger — Master Hollis's private book, sent to her by the Widow Kessel in a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and she saw the widow's face as it crossed the room, that terrible composed face doing its work of not breaking. Payment after payment, dated across eleven years, from A. Aldery, each annotated in her master's cramped script: *for discretion. for continued silence re: family papers. Q — see me before speaking.* Eleven years. Her master had been bought the way a clock is bought, serviced regularly, kept running.

"The register," Quill said, and she gave him that too: the visitor book from the founder's study, the entry from eight years ago, *Annelise Voss, visiting her father's instrument,* and beneath it, in different ink added later, *with infant daughter.* And the parish record beside it — Annelise Voss dead the previous autumn of the lung fever, and no record anywhere of a surviving child, because a record could be made to vanish when a magistrate's palm was greased.

Last, the escapement. She carried it to the table herself — the ruined original, its pallet faces filed to a deliberate wrong angle, the wear marks faked with emery paper, the sabotage dressed up to look like thirty years of neglect rather than three weeks of malice.

"This," she said, "was done recently. By someone with access to the housing. Someone who wanted the clock's failure to void the founder's bequest — because the bequest required the clock to keep public time, and a dead clock releases the estate to the next claimant. Which is Mr. Aldery."

"An accusation," Quill observed. "From an apprentice. Against the estate's lawful claimant. Based on—" he lifted the escapement delicately between two fingers— "a broken part."

"Based on the file marks. They're fresh. They cross the older wear at the wrong angle. Anyone who has ever cut steel can read them. Can anyone in this room read them?" She looked around. Silence. "I can."

"Fascinating skill," said Quill. "We'll return to your skills."

* * *

They returned to her skills sooner than she liked.

It began after the ferryman's boy testified — Pip Hallow, fifteen, standing on a chair to be seen, saying in a voice that cracked twice that he'd been on the night ice in December when a closed carriage came down to the frozen crossing, and a woman in Aldery livery carried a bundle to the observatory steps, and the carriage turned back before dawn. He'd been paid two shillings to forget it. He was returning the shillings now; he set them on the table with the gravity of a man surrendering stolen goods.

Quill didn't touch the boy. He barely looked at him. Instead he folded his hands and turned back to Lior with the air of a man resuming a conversation they'd both been enjoying.

"Mistress Winterbourne. Let us speak plainly, since you value plainness. For ten years you served a master who took money to stay silent about this very estate. You kept his books. You entered these payments yourself — or do you claim otherwise?"

"I recorded figures. I didn't know what they were for."

"You didn't know. Ten years. Eleven years of payments marked *for discretion*, passing through your ledgers, and our clever reader of file marks never once wondered what was being discretely purchased?" He shook his head slowly, sorrowfully, a performance so good Lior almost admired it. "You found a forged-looking will. You found a convenient ledger. You found a register entry that happens to name a dead woman's unknown daughter. Every document this court has seen passed through the hands of a woman trained by a man paid to conceal documents. And we are asked to believe that in ten years of apprenticeship, she learned nothing, suspected nothing, asked nothing."

"I asked nothing," Lior said, "because I was taught not to. That's the truth you keep circling, Mr. Quill, so let me say it for you: I was a coward for ten years. My master taught me which questions not to ask, and I didn't ask them, and I slept well, and I told myself it was prudence. If you want witnesses without silences in their history, look around this room. You won't find one in Vellamarch. Everyone here was paid, or frightened, or both. The difference between me and most of them—" she gestured at the packed benches, at the downcast eyes— "is that mine are testifying today."

The room made a sound again, lower this time. Somewhere in the back, someone said *that's God's truth* and someone else hushed them.

But Quill only smiled, and Lior learned then that she had given him exactly what he wanted. Confession was just confession, neatly packaged.

"So you admit," he said, "a decade of complicity."

"Withheld knowledge, yes."

"Withheld knowledge while children were placed on doorsteps in sleet. While a magistrate sold a parish record. While your master banked blood money by the quarter." He spread his hands. "Members of the guild, I ask you: what is such a witness worth? A woman whose loyalty is for sale to whichever dead man speaks to her most recently? Yesterday she served Master Hollis's silence. Today she serves his ghost against the living. Tomorrow, who knows? This court cannot build an inheritance on the shifting conscience of an apprentice who tells us, proudly, that she was a coward."

"I didn't say proudly."

"You said it fluently. It amounts to the same thing." Quill gathered his papers, tapped them square. "I make no accusation of dishonesty. I merely observe that honesty arriving this suddenly, this conveniently, this *profitably*—"

"Profitably?" Lior heard her own voice change and couldn't stop it. "What have I profited? Name it. My workshop goes to the estate if the child wins — I lose everything I've spent ten years keeping alive. I've been offered freedom twice in three weeks, once by your client and once by the guildmaster himself, and refused both times, and the refusals are on record. What is my profit, Mr. Quill? Say it."

For the first time, something flickered behind Quill's grey composure — not defeat, but recalculation. He glanced once, briefly, at Aldery, and Lior followed the glance, and saw the thing she had been dreading and waiting for all day: Aldery was not looking at Quill. He was looking at her, and his face was not the polished mask of the dinner party or the creditor's office. It was tired. It was the face of a man watching the last exit close and finding, to his own surprise, part of him relieved.

He recovered it almost instantly. Almost.

"My client," Quill said smoothly, "has been more than patient. But patience is not evidence. I move that the documents be weighed as unproven: an unsigned will, a self-serving ledger, a register of unverifiable entries, and the word of a confessed coward and a bribed child—"

"He's fifteen," Lior said.

"—a bribed boy, regarding events on a night with no moon and no other witnesses." Quill sat. "The guild may weigh these papers as it likes. But weight requires a scale, and I submit this court has none."

The guildmaster was quiet a long moment. His fingers drummed once on the surveying table, and Lior, who had spent her life listening to mechanisms, heard in the drumming the same rhythm she heard in a train of gears hunting for its lock.

"There is a scale," he said at last.

Every head turned.

"The bequest itself names one," the guildmaster said. "Read it again, Mr. Quill — clause four. *The observatory master clock, being built to my design and bearing my private works, shall be the arbiter of this instrument's succession: while it keeps true time, the estate stands as willed; when it fails, the estate passes to the next claimant.* The founder put his machine in his own will, sir. Not sentiment. Engineering. He knew someday a man like you would stand in this room and call every paper a forgery — so he left a witness that can't be coached, can't be bribed, and can't be forged." He looked across the table at Lior, and his old rival's eyes were unreadable and very bright. "Mistress Winterbourne. Is the clock ready?"

Her heart slammed once, hard, against her sternum.

The escapement was in. The new one — the one she'd cut in fourteen hours of fury, the one nobody had known about, the one she'd installed at three o'clock yesterday morning with the girl holding the lamp — it was in, and the going train had run for twenty-two hours without losing four seconds, and the strike train was assembled, and the weights were wound. Everything was ready except the one thing she had not dared to finish, the thing her master had built into the case behind the chime mechanism, the locked compartment she'd found and studied and understood and not yet sprung, because springing it would mean the dead man spoke, and whatever he said, no one in this room could take it back.

"The clock is ready," she said.

"Then wind it," said the guildmaster, "and let us hear what time it keeps."

Lior turned toward the stair that climbed into the clock's case — and stopped, because Aldery had risen from his chair, and his counsel was whispering urgently at his elbow, and the magistrate by the door had gone the color of tallow. But Aldery ignored them all. He was looking up, past her, at the tall dark shape of the case against the dome's light, at the machine his uncle had built and his money had tried to kill, and his lips were moving slightly, silently, as if in prayer — or as if answering a question he'd been asked a long time ago.

Lior put her hand on the stair rail and began to climb.
