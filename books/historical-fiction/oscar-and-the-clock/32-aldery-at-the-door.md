## 32. Aldery at the Door

The knock came at half past ten, when the observatory had settled into its nighttime voice — the slow tick of Oscar's escapement, the settling creak of the dome, the wind worrying the weathervane. Lior knew every sound the building made now, the way she had once known her master's workshop, and this knock belonged to none of them.

She was at the bench, oiling the chime train for its second week of running, and she sat very still with the brush in her hand until the knock came again. Three blows, measured, unhurried. Not the fists of Aldery's men, who had pounded like men who wanted to be heard through walls. This was the knock of someone who expected to be admitted because he had always been admitted everywhere he went.

The girl — Elara, though the register still smelled of fresh ink where her name had been written three days before — looked up from her cot in the corner of the instrument room, where she had been reading by candlelight with the ferocious concentration of a child making up for lost years.

"Stay here," Lior said. "If I call out, run to the Widow Kessel's. You know the way."

"I know the way." The girl did not argue; she never argued anymore, not since the workhouse, but she watched Lior with those grave dark eyes that missed nothing, and she slid off the cot without a sound and stood behind the door of the clock housing, small hands folded, waiting.

Lior took the lamp from the bench and went down the stone stairs to the observatory door.

Through the iron grille in the door she saw him standing on the steps, hatless, coat collar turned up against the river damp. Without his retinue, without the magistrate at his shoulder, Mr. Corwin Aldery looked smaller than he did in the hearing room — a man of perhaps fifty-five, silvering at the temples, with the fine, tired face of someone who had spent thirty years being photographed by portraitists who flattered him. The flood had gone down two days ago, and the mud of the riverbank was still on everyone's boots in Vellamarch; there was mud drying on his good shoes too, which meant he had walked up the hill himself rather than send his carriage. That detail more than any other made her open the door.

"Miss Winterbourne." He inclined his head. "I won't keep you long."

"You'll keep me as long as you keep talking, Mr. Aldery. It's late."

"It is." He glanced past her into the stairwell, and something moved across his face that she might have mistaken, in a better man, for wistfulness. "May I come in? I'd rather not say what I've come to say on your doorstep, where the whole town can watch through its curtains. And it *is* watching. It has been watching since the ice broke."

Lior considered him. Three weeks ago she would have barred the door. Two weeks ago she would have called for the guildmaster. But she had testified against this man, had dismantled his inheritance before the guild's assessors, had heard her own ten years of silence read aloud as evidence of her character, and she had discovered that after such a thing there was no posture left worth keeping. Fear was a currency and she had spent all of hers.

"The girl is asleep," she said, which was true enough, or would be soon. "If you raise your voice, you leave."

"I won't raise my voice." And, stepping past her into the stairwell, quietly: "I never have. That's rather the whole history of me, Miss Winterbourne. I have never once raised my voice."

* * *

She led him up to the instrument room because she refused — she could not have said why, except that it felt like a kind of honesty — to let him think she had anything to hide. Let him see the bench, the tools laid out in their furred rows, the great brass case of Oscar standing open like a reliquary, the pendulum breathing behind its glass. Let him see the child's cot with its blanket folded back and the book of constellations lying face-down on the pillow.

Aldery stopped just inside the door and looked at the clock for a long time.

"He called it Oscar," he said. "My uncle. Did you know that?"

"I know it now. The girl told me. She remembers him calling it that."

"Yes." Aldery removed his hat entirely — he had been carrying it, she realized, rather than wearing it — and held it by the brim, turning it slowly, a man handling something fragile. "He used to let me wind it when I was nine years old. Ten. I would come up from the town on the ferry, and he would stand me on this stool — there was a stool, I suppose it's gone now —"

"There's a stool," Lior said. "In the corner. He kept it."

Aldery looked at the stool. It was an ordinary thing, oak, its seat worn pale in one oval patch by sixty years of sitting. He crossed the room and touched the back of it with one finger, the way a man touches a door he does not dare open, and then he took his hand away and did not sit.

"He let me wind it exactly twice," he said. "Both times he stood beside me with his hand under my elbow, and both times he said, 'Gently. A clock doesn't want force, it wants patience.' And then when I was done he would say 'Well done, Corwin,' and take the key away, and that would be the whole of my visit. Once a year. Twice a year if the roads were good." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and crooked and real. "Do you know what I did with that? Do you know what a boy makes of a man who gives him a clock and takes away the key?"

Lior thought of her master. Thought of the ledger with its columns of Aldery's payments, the neat figures in her dead master's hand, month upon month upon year, buying his silence about a will that would have cut this man out of everything. She thought of the coded apology hidden between the lines of the teaching notes — *an honest apprentice will find it, someday, and do what I could not* — and she thought of how little forgiveness actually weighed in the hand, how much lighter than anger.

"I imagine," she said carefully, "that a boy might make almost anything of it."

"I made an empire of it." Aldery set his hat down on the bench — she nearly objected, then didn't — and stood looking at the pendulum through the glass. "Every ledger I ever balanced, every debt I ever called in, every magistrate I ever bought — and I don't insult us both by pretending otherwise, not tonight — every one of them was that key. Something I was owed. Something someone was withholding. My uncle built the finest thing in this valley and gave it a name like a joke and showed it to me twice a year with his hand under my elbow saying *gently*. And when he died, the will — the real will, the last will — named some heir nobody had ever heard of, and I thought —" He stopped. Turned the hat brim in his fingers. "I thought, of course. Of course. Even from the grave. Even the clock keeps something from me."

The fire had burned low in the grate. Lior fed it a split of birch, more for something to do with her hands than for warmth, and waited. In fifteen years of apprenticeship she had learned that the most useful thing a craftsman can do with silence is refuse to fill it.

"My counsel tells me the circuit judge arrives Friday," Aldery said. "On the first ferry. There will be charges. Sabotage of a guild instrument, tampering with estate papers — they're drawing them up already, and they're accurate, which is the worst of it. I did all of it and worse and I did it neatly, and neatly is the only crime gentlemen are ever punished for." He turned to look at her directly for the first time, and up close his eyes were not the cold gray she remembered from the dinner, from the hearing. They were brown. Ordinary, tired, human brown eyes, red-rimmed at the lids. "I'm not here to bargain, Miss Winterbourne. I've lost the bargaining. I'm here to ask you something, and I find I need to ask it honestly, which is a sensation I recommend to no one."

"Ask it, then."

He drew a breath, and Lior watched a man assemble himself out of wreckage, brick by brick, the way she had once assembled a movement out of scattered parts on a bench at midnight.

"My uncle," Aldery said. "Ambrose Vellamarch. In all those years — and you were with him ten, weren't you, from fourteen to twenty-four — in all those years, did he ever speak of me?"

The question hung in the lamplight between them.

Not *did he speak well of me.* Not *did he curse my name.* Just: *did he speak of me.* As if the mere fact of having been mentioned, somewhere, sometime, in the privacy of a workshop, might count for something. Might be carried home like a coin.

And Lior understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with pity and everything to do with recognition, exactly what she was holding. Because she knew the answer. She had known it since she found the ledger, since she read the teaching notes line by line looking for the man inside the silence. Ambrose Vellamarch had spoken of his nephew. Many times. Over many years. Usually late at night, over the tea the widow Kessel sent up, usually after a payment had arrived, usually with an expression Lior had been too young to read and had filed, in her ten years of trained silence, under *do not ask.*

What had he said? She cast backward through the years, through the fog of rules — which debts to ignore, which questions not to ask, which customers to serve last — and found the fragments the way the girl found her memories: a carriage, a woman singing, *not yet.*

*The boy wanted the key,* her master said once, watching snow come down over the river. *I gave him the winding and kept the key. That was cowardice dressed as pedagogy, Lior. Remember that, when you have apprentices. Whatever you withhold from a child, you withhold forever.*

Another night, older, sicker, the tremor in his hands beginning: *Corwin writes to me every Christmas. Thirty years of letters. I answer perhaps one in five. A man tells himself the boy is hard, that the boy is grasping. But I know what I made. I wound him twice a year and hid the key, and I am surprised the clock ran at all.*

And once — she had never forgotten this one, had carried it unexamined for a decade like a splinter worked too deep to dig out — once he had laughed. Actually laughed, wiping his eye with a knuckle stained with bluing: *Corwin has bought the ferry. Bought it outright! The absurd creature. When I was his age I was building clocks, and he's buying rivers.* And then the laugh had died in his face, abruptly, the way a flame dies when a draft finds it, and he had said nothing more, and Lior had asked nothing, because asking was forbidden, because silence was safety, because —

Because.

She looked at Aldery standing in the lamplight with his ruined empire and his ordinary brown eyes, waiting for the answer that would either confirm him as the villain of his uncle's story or — worse, far worse — fail to.

She could lie. It would be easy. It would even be kind, by one accounting. *Yes, he spoke of you often, and warmly.* One sentence, and this man might walk down the hill tomorrow a little less damned, might meet the circuit judge with something other than a lifetime of withheld keys rattling in him.

But kindness that is a lie is just another locked case, her master's voice said in her memory, in the tone he saved for the finest work. And the whole theme of her year — of her decade — had been that silence stops being safety and becomes complicity. She had testified at the cost of everything she owned precisely so she would never again tell a comfortable falsehood to a comfortable man.

"No," Lior said.

Aldery's shoulders did not move, but something behind them moved. She saw it the way she saw a hairline crack in a case — not the fracture itself, only the light changing along it.

"He spoke of you," she went on, "but I won't dress it for you, because you came here asking honestly, and you deserve the same coin. He spoke of you often, and mostly late at night, and mostly after your payments came. Yes — I know about the payments. I've read the ledger. Your money bought his silence about the will, and he took it, and I loved him anyway, and loving him anyway is the hardest work I have ever done, harder than any escapement I ever cut. So hear the rest the same way."

Aldery said nothing. His hand had gone white on the hat brim.

"He said you wrote to him every Christmas. Thirty years of letters. He answered perhaps one in five, and he was ashamed of it, and shame made him crueler in his letters than he meant to be — I read the drafts he threw away; I swept them out of the grate for ten years. He said —" Lior paused, steadied herself. "*I wound him twice a year and hid the key, and I am surprised the clock ran at all.* Those were his words, Mr. Aldery. His own words, about you. He knew what he had done. He knew the boy he made. And near the end — the very end — he hid the true will inside this clock, inside a compartment that only opens when the chime mechanism runs, because he was hoping — I believe with everything left in him — that some honest apprentice would someday wind the thing properly and let it speak, where he himself had never managed to say a single honest word aloud to your face."

The fire ticked. The pendulum breathed. Somewhere below, the river moved against its banks, free of ice now, running loud and dark toward the sea.

"And affection?" Aldery asked. His voice had gone quiet and stripped, the polish finally worn through to whatever was underneath. "You've given me everything but that. Did he love me? Ever? At any point? Yes or no, Miss Winterbourne. I have sat through hearings and indictments and the reading of my ruin, and I find I cannot leave this room without that one word."

Lior set down the lamp.

Here was the truth, and she examined it one last time before speaking, the way she would examine a finished wheel under the loupe, checking for flaws, for burrs, for anything false.

Her master had been a coward about love. He had paid for silence instead of paying for courage, all his life, in every direction — toward his nephew, toward his apprentice, toward the granddaughter he'd hidden and the daughter he'd failed and the widow he'd left holding a ledger full of shame. But cowardice is not the absence of the thing it fails to do. A man does not hide a will inside his life's masterpiece unless he wants it found. A man does not keep a stool for thirty years unless he expects someone to sit on it. A man does not say *remember that, when you have apprentices* unless the wound is still open in his hands.

"He never said it," Lior said. "Not in words, not to anyone, not that I ever found. But I'll tell you what I know the way a clockmaker knows things, which is not by listening but by looking at what a man builds. He kept your stool. He kept your letters — not the ones he answered; the ones you sent, all thirty years of them, in the bottom drawer of his desk, tied in ribbon, every one. He built this clock to testify, and the first thing it did when it finally spoke was give a forgotten child her name. That is a man trying, at the last possible moment, with the last strength in his failing hands, to undo exactly what he did to you. That is not love, Mr. Aldery. But it is the shape love leaves in a coward, and I have decided — after much anger, and much machining — that the shape counts for something. I think it may be all most of us ever manage."

For a long moment Aldery did not move at all.

Then he crossed the room — slowly, like an old man, though he was not old — and sat down on the stool. The stool his uncle had kept. It bore his weight without complaint, the way furniture bears the weight of grief, which is to say silently and without design.

"The letters," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he mastered it, barely. "They're in the desk. In his workshop. The workshop that —"

"That passes to the estate," Lior said. "Which is the girl's. And she is six years old, and generous, and I am her keeper, and I say the drawer goes to you. I'll bring them myself, when the judge is done with you. All thirty years. Every Christmas."

Aldery bowed his head over his hat, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and then were still, and he did not make a sound, and Lior looked away and gave him the privacy of the pendulum's ticking, which is the only privacy some men will ever accept.

When he rose, minutes later, his face was composed again, but composed differently — the way a repaired movement runs differently from a broken one, though a stranger couldn't say why.

"They'll still charge me," he said. "Friday. I won't fight it. Fighting it is the only skill I have left and I mean to retire from it." He put on his hat at the door, and paused there, hand on the iron latch, and looked back at the great case of Oscar gleaming in the lamplight. "Your master taught you well, Miss Winterbourne. Gently. A clock doesn't want force."

"It wants patience," Lior said.

"Yes." He opened the door onto the dark and the smell of the freed river. "It seems it got one."

He went down the stairs and across the wet grass and was gone, and Lior stood in the doorway a while, listening to his footsteps fade down the hill toward the town where in two days a judge would arrive to do to him what he had done to others, neatly.

Behind her, from the shadow of the clock housing, a small voice said, "He cried."

Lior turned. Elara stood in the doorway of the instrument room, blanket around her shoulders, eyes enormous.

"You were supposed to be asleep."

"I was listening. He cried like the ice sounds. Underneath." The girl considered this, gravely, the way she considered everything now, having learned at six what some people never learn — that the loudest things in a house are often silent. Then: "Lior. In the drawer with the letters. Grandfather kept one other thing. I saw it once, when I was very small, before Mama —"

She stopped. Her hand had gone to her throat, where a cord hung, where a stolen cog from the observatory still hung on its cord, pressed once against workhouse bars, a message in brass.

"What thing?" Lior asked gently. "Elara. What did he keep?"

The girl looked up at the clock — at Oscar, running sweetly, striking the quarter hour in the distance below them, one, two, three, four, patient as water.

"A key," she said. "He kept a key. And I think I know which lock it fits."
