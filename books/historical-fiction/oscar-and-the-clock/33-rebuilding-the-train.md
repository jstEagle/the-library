## 33. Rebuilding the Train

The flood had left a line on the observatory wall, a brown tidemark running level as a spirit gauge from the door of the instrument room to the foot of the stairs. Lior traced it with her thumb on the first morning after, when the light came through the east windows and found everything still damp and shining — the flagstones, the legs of the workbench, the great brass skirt of Oscar himself standing in his tower above it all like a man who had waded through a river without troubling to roll up his trousers.

The river had taken the ferry landing. It had taken Aldery's crates of documents, or drowned them where they lay; either way they were past use. It had taken three fences, a hay barn, and the Widow Kessel's goose run, which she would speak of for years as though she had lost a child. What it had not taken was anything that mattered, and Lior was learning to be grateful in a way that felt less like relief than like the slow settling of a house whose timbers have finally stopped shifting.

She set her tools out on the bench in their old order — files left to right by cut, burnishers nearest the hand, the oilstone worn into a valley by ten years of her master's elbow and then ten years of hers. The sabotaged escapement lay before her under a linen cloth, and she did not lift the cloth at once. She let it sit there the way you let a wound sit before you clean it, knowing what is underneath and needing one more moment of not looking.

Then she lifted it.

The broken pieces were the same ones she had found in March, arranged now in the order she would need them understood: the pallet arm snapped clean at the shoulder, the entry stone chipped, the exit stone gone entirely — she had never found it, and had come to believe it had been carried off deliberately, a trophy or a taunt. Beside them lay the replacement she had machined in her fourteen hours of fury, the one that had been smashed in the break-in. That too was broken, but differently: its fracture lines were ragged, angry, honest. Steel broken by a hammer tells you something steel broken by design does not.

"You're going to make it again," said a voice at her elbow.

The girl stood there with her sleeves pushed up past her thin wrists, hair tied back with the ribbon Lior had bought her at the market — blue, because the girl had pointed and said *that color is my mother's*, and no argument on earth could have moved the purchase elsewhere. She looked at the wreckage on the bench the way other children looked at sweets.

"I'm going to make it again," Lior agreed. "But properly this time."

"What does properly mean?"

"It means I don't make it out of anger." She picked up the snapped pallet arm and held it to the window light, turning it so the fracture caught the sun. "Anger cuts fast and leaves a rough edge. A rough edge in an escapement will chatter. It'll tick, but it won't keep time. And a clock that ticks but doesn't keep time is worse than a clock that's silent. At least silence doesn't lie."

The girl considered this with the gravity she gave to all matters of mechanism, which was more gravity than she gave to meals or bedtime. "Oscar didn't lie."

"No. He kept honest time for forty years until someone made him lie." She set the arm down. "So we're going to give him back his voice, and we're going to do it slowly, and you're going to hand me every tool I ask for, and if you drop one you'll tell me at once, because a dropped file with a hidden crack is worse than no file."

"And if I don't drop any?"

"Then by tonight you'll have earned the right to watch me fit the stones, which is the finest thing in clockmaking and the dullest, and most people can't bear both those facts at once."

The girl smiled — that quick, unguarded smile that had survived a workhouse and a false name and a night on cold steps, and which Lior privately regarded as the toughest piece of craftsmanship in Vellamarch.

* * *

They began with the stock, because everything begins with the stock. Lior chose a billet of gunmetal from the drawer her master had labeled in his small precise hand: *For trains worth trusting*. She had read that label a hundred times over ten years and only now, with the ledger's ugly columns behind her and the guild's papers folded in her coat, did she understand it might have been written as much for himself as for her.

The workshop's future sat on that uncertainty like frost on glass. The estate had passed to the child — to *Marguerite Elowen Vane*, though the register office had needed two attempts before the clerk's pen stopped shaking — and an estate belonging to a six-year-old was an estate belonging to lawyers, and lawyers belonged, in Vellamarch, to whoever paid them. The circuit judge would arrive on the first ferry whenever the river allowed a ferry, and he would untangle Aldery's sabotage, and Aldery's creditors' claims against her dead master's shop would rise again like weeds after rain. She might lose the bench. She might lose the lease. She might walk out of this spring owning nothing but her journeywoman's papers and a set of tools.

But the work in front of her was honest, and honesty, she was discovering, was a place to stand even when the floor around it was giving way.

"File," she said.

The girl placed the flat file in her palm, handle first, the way she'd been taught an hour ago. She had learned tool etiquette faster than she'd learned her letters, and took a craftsman's pride in it already.

Lior filed. The sound filled the instrument room — that long even *shhhk, shhhk* of steel surrendering its excess — and beneath it the girl hummed, tuneless, the way children hum when they are content and don't know it. Outside, the river ran loud and free where the ice had been, carrying the last pans of rotting ice down toward the sea, and the town beyond the windows was busy with hammers and saws, rebuilding what the water had taken.

"How long did it take you to learn?" the girl asked.

"To file flat? Two years. To know when it's flat without measuring? Five." She turned the work, sighted along it. "My master used to say the file teaches you the first hard lesson better than any book: you cannot hurry metal. You can only remove what isn't supposed to be there, and you must remove exactly that, no more."

"Like telling the truth?"

Lior's file paused for half a stroke. "Yes," she said. "Exactly like telling the truth. You can't add to it, you can't rush it, and you take away everything that isn't true, however long it takes." She resumed filing. "He also said most people would rather varnish a lie than sand a truth. He said it about clocks, mostly. I think he meant it about himself."

The girl absorbed this in her own fashion, which was to go quiet and hand over the next tool before it was asked for. She was doing that more often lately — anticipating. Watching Lior's hands the way Lior had once watched her master's, reading intention out of gesture. Somewhere in that small head a craft was assembling itself, joint by joint, and Lior felt the strange double vision of every teacher: grief and hope wearing each other's clothes.

By midmorning the blank had become a shape. She marked out the pallet arms with layout ink and her sharpest scriber, working from measurements she had taken twice and checked a third time against the original drawings she'd found pinned inside the case — her master's drawings, drawn in a hand steadier than his heart had apparently been. The drill bow sang. The piercing saw whispered through the metal along her lines, leaving the shape standing inside its web of waste like a bone inside a body.

"Can I try the saw?"

"When your hands grow another year. The frame would eat that blade alive and blame you for it." She blew the swarf from the cut. "Watch instead. Tell me if the blade wanders left."

"It wanders left on the curves."

"Good eye." She corrected her stance, letting the frame float rather than push. "That's half the trade, noticing. Anyone can move metal. Noticing is the rare part."

* * *

At noon they ate bread and cheese on the observatory steps, in the sun, among the debris the river had sorted and abandoned — silt fans, a drowned chair, reeds laid down in neat combed rows like the aftermath of some enormous gentle combing. The town bell rang the hour across the water, slightly early, as it always did, because the town clock had never been regulated to the observatory standard and no one had ever cared enough to fix it. Lior listened to it wrongness and made a note, not for the first time, that when Oscar ran again she would walk to the church herself with a pocket watch and settle the matter.

"After the hearing," the girl said, "when the judge comes. Will he take you away?"

Lior stopped chewing. "What makes you think he might?"

"The widow lady said things get taken away after judges come. Her goose. Our house." The girl tore her bread with great deliberation. "You said the workshop might not be ours anymore."

"Mine," Lior corrected gently. "It was never mine to begin with. It was your great-grandfather's, and then my master's, and the paper says—" She stopped, because explaining creditors to a six-year-old was explaining weather to a fish. "Here's what's true. The judge will decide about buildings and money. Those are heavy things, and heavy things get argued over. But what happened upstairs these last months — the clock, the papers, the hearing — that belongs to us whether or not anyone takes the building. Nobody can carry off the fact that we told the truth. It's not the kind of property that gets seized."

The girl thought about this while she chewed. Then: "Good. Because I want to learn the stones today."

Lior laughed — really laughed, from low down, the first time since before the summons had arrived in January — and the laugh went out over the muddy bright river and came back sounding like permission.

* * *

The fitting of the stones took the rest of the day, and it was, as promised, both the finest and the dullest work in the world.

The jewels — garnet, in the old style her master had favored over rubies for train work, cheaper and harder and a deep wine-red that caught fire in lamplight — came to the bench in a paper twist. Lior seated them in shellac warmed over the spirit lamp, one at a time, adjusting each by fractions of a hair's breadth, testing, scraping the shellac seat, warming again. The pivot entered the stone's hole or it did not, and there was no arguing with it, no almost, no *good enough*. An escapement is the place in a clock where time itself is handled — where the wheel train's fall is caught, checked, released, counted — and whatever character the clock has lives in that small red contact between steel and stone.

Too tight and the clock labors. Too loose and it stumbles. Correct, and it forgets itself entirely, and becomes merely truthful.

The girl watched from her stool with her chin on her fists, and did not fidget, and did not ask questions during the delicate operations, having learned — or perhaps simply knowing, in the way she knew things — that attention itself was a tool being handed over.

"There," Lior breathed at last, near dusk, releasing the pallets against the escape wheel and letting the train breathe. The click and fall of it came soft as a heartbeat heard through a wall. "Feel that." She guided the girl's fingertips to the case rail, not the mechanism — you never touched a running train — so she could feel the tremor of it, the faint live pulse of forty wheels deciding, together, to be patient.

"It feels like the ticking," the girl whispered. "From the first night. When I was on the steps."

Lior's hands went still. On the steps. Before she had ever opened the door. The clock had been dead for a week by then, sabotaged, silent — and yet the child, delivered in the dark by a woman in stolen livery, had pressed her ear to the wood and heard something.

"What did it sound like?" Lior asked quietly. "On the steps. If it wasn't ticking."

The girl frowned, reaching for it. "Like inside an egg," she said at last. "Like when you hold a hen's egg to your ear. Busy. But kind of... waiting." She shrugged, six years old again, mystery discharged. "I wasn't scared. Eggs always hatch."

Lior looked at her — at this child who had been posted to a doorstep like a letter addressed to whoever was brave enough to open her — and could not speak for a moment. Eggs always hatch. Well. Some do. Some are carried to warmth by strangers who have their own reasons and their own fears, and some wait ten years in the dark of a locked case, sealed by a dead man's careful hand, until someone honest enough comes along with a screwdriver and a conscience.

She lit the lamps. There was no reason to stop now except exhaustion, and exhaustion was a debt she had gotten good at ignoring.

"One more operation," she said. "Then we see if he runs."

* * *

They hung the new escapement in the last of the daylight and the first of the lamplight, both together, the observatory's old habit of doubling its sources. Lior assembled with the slowness of ceremony — cleaning each pivot, oiling each jewel with a single needle-tip drop, meshing the train wheel by wheel until the whole gear-train of the great clock lay connected from barrel to escapement like a sentence waiting only for its final word.

The girl handed her the last screw. Held it out flat on her palm, head toward Lior's fingers, exact.

"Thank you, apprentice," Lior said — and heard herself say it, and did not take it back.

The girl's whole face changed. She didn't grin; she went solemn, receiving the word like a diploma, and stood a little straighter on her stool, and watched with the fixed ferocity of someone witnessing her own future.

Lior set the screw home with two turns and a quarter. She drew up the driving weight — the great case was still weighted from the hearing, from the moment the chime had struck and the hidden compartment had opened and the founder's final will had lain in the light — and she put her finger against the pendulum and held it still.

"Now," she said. "When I let go, we don't cheer, we don't shout, we don't touch anything. We listen. Can you listen?"

"I'm the best listener in Vellamarch."

"Prove it."

She let go.

The pendulum swung wide, gathered itself, settled. The escape wheel stepped. Click. Stepped. Click. The tick went out into the tower room and came back off the stone walls doubled, and the seconds began to pile up the way seconds do when nothing interrupts them — one, and one, and one — and the sound was not dramatic at all. It was small. It was the smallest sound in the building. It was the sound of a promise keeping itself.

The girl's eyes were enormous. She did not cheer. She pressed both hands over her mouth, and above them her eyes shone, and she listened like the best listener in Vellamarch, and Lior stood beside her with her hands hanging empty at her sides, empty in the best possible way — the emptiness of finished work — and let ten years of noise drain out of her into the steady tick.

One minute. Five. Ten. The lamp flames leaned and straightened. Downriver the town lights came on one by one, and the church bell rang the hour, early as always, wrong as always, and Lior smiled at it without malice. Soon, friend. Soon we'll settle you.

"We did it," the girl whispered.

"*He* did it. We just removed what wasn't supposed to be there."

"Same thing."

And Lior laughed again, tired and clean, and reached for the logbook — her master's logbook, its earlier pages full of his small entries, its later pages holding the record of the hearing and the verdict in her own hand. She dipped the pen. The next chapter of the shop's life was uncertain; the judge was coming; the creditors would circle; the bench might belong to lawyers by summer.

But the entry in front of her was certain, and certain things deserved writing down.

She wrote: *Escapement rebuilt. New pallets, garnet stones fitted. Train in beat. Timekeeping to be observed.*

Below it, after a moment, in smaller letters, she added: *Apprentice present throughout. Steady hands. Notices wandering.*

She blotted the page, and was setting down the pen when the girl tugged her sleeve and pointed, wide-eyed, at the clock face high above them in the gloom — at the small subsidiary dial Lior had never once seen move in ten years of visits, a dial marked only with a single hand and no numerals at all.

Its hand was trembling. And beneath the floor, faint but unmistakable, something inside the great clock had begun — very softly — to count.
