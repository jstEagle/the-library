## 35. The Girl's Name

The register office smelled of ink and cold stone, and I liked it better than any place we had been since the ice broke. It was quiet the way the observatory was quiet at night, except instead of ticking there was scratching — pens going everywhere, little teeth of steel chewing paper. I counted them under my breath. Eleven. Lior had taught me to count when my chest got tight, because counting was a thing clocks did and clocks were never afraid.

"Stay close," Lior said. She said it quietly, but her hand found my shoulder anyway and stayed there, which meant she needed the staying-close more than I did.

The room had one long counter, worn pale in the middle where a hundred years of elbows had leaned, and behind it stood a man with spectacles pushed up into his hair like a second pair of eyes that had given up looking. He had a ledger open before him bigger than any book I had ever seen, even the ones in the observatory, even the one with Grandfather's visitors written down in it. This book was fatter than that one. This book held everyone.

"That's the town register," Lior told me, following my look. "Every person born here. Every name ever given."

"Even mine?"

"Not yet," the man said, without looking up. He had heard me, though his pen kept walking. "Not unless somebody puts you in."

I looked up at Lior. She was wearing her good coat, the grey one with the buttons she had sewn back on herself, and her chin was doing the thing it did when she was about to argue with someone important — set forward, jaw tight, like a door being latched against weather.

"Somebody is putting her in today," Lior said.

* * *

It had taken four days to get here. Four days since the guildmaster read out his verdict in the observatory with the river still roaring past the windows, so loud we all had to lean toward him to hear, as if the water were trying to shout us down and we would not let it. Four days since the magistrate sat on his bench with his face gone the color of porridge while men in guild colours took his papers away in a locked box. Four days since Aldery's counsel stopped talking mid-sentence, folded his notes, and left, and nobody stopped him.

I remembered most of it wrong, probably. That is what Lior says memory is — a clock that runs fast or slow depending on how frightened you are. When the verdict came I was sitting on the gallery steps with my feet not reaching the floor, holding the cog I had carried out of the workhouse in my fist for nine days until it had printed its shape into my palm. I still have that cog. It is on a ribbon around my neck now, and I will tell you why later, or maybe I won't, because some things are mine.

But the words themselves I remember exactly, because Lior made me say them back to her that night to be sure I hadn't dreamed them:

"The estate of Josiah Merrivale acknowledges as lawful heir—" and then the guildmaster had paused, and looked down at me, and waited. Everyone in the observatory had turned around to look at me. The magistrate. The clerks. The ferryman's boy, who winked. And I had stood up, because standing seemed correct, and said nothing, because I didn't know what I was supposed to be heir *of*. I knew I was somebody's granddaughter by then. Lior had shown me the register page weeks ago, the old visitor book with Mama's name written in it in Grandfather's hand — *Mirabel, with her daughter* — and the date, and I had touched the ink because touching it made it true. But a name on a page is not the same as a name in your mouth. A name on a page is a spring coiled inside a case. Someone has to let it go.

"You'll need to register her properly," the guildmaster had said afterward, folding his spectacles away. "Register office. Legal ink, legal hand. Until her name is in the town book, everything we ruled today is only talk."

So: four days, and then the cold stone room, and the man with the sleeping eyes, and the enormous book.

He finally looked up. He looked at me the way people had been looking at me all week, which was a way I was still getting used to — like I was something that had walked out of a story they half-remembered. His gaze went over my head to Lior.

"You're the clockmaker. Winterbourne."

"I am."

"And this is the foundling from the observatory steps." He said *foundling* the way you'd flick ash off a sleeve. "Except apparently she isn't one."

"No," Lior said. "She never was."

* * *

Here is what I remember about before. Not much, and none of it in order, and all of it the way dreams are — bright pieces with soft edges.

A carriage. Wheels on cobbles and then wheels on nothing, on frozen mud, and the sound changing under me like a song changing key. A woman singing — Mama, though I didn't have the word *Mama* attached to anything then; I just had the singing, low, in a minor key, a song about a river. I can still sing it. I sing it when I wind the case clock in the east corridor and no one is listening.

A man saying *not yet.* Not unkindly. The way you say *not yet* to bread in the oven. I used to think he meant not yet for something frightening. Now I think he meant not yet for me — not yet time to come out, not yet time to be known, because being known was dangerous. Being known got people hurt. That is the whole story, really. Everything else is gears.

And ticking. Always ticking. Under everything, the big slow heartbeat of a very large clock, which is why, the night Lior opened the observatory door and the sound rolled out over me on the cold steps, I stopped crying. She tells people I stopped crying because she gave me her shawl. It wasn't the shawl. It was that the house behind her was making the sound home makes.

Lior asked me my name that first night. I know she did, because she has told me, and because she would have — she asks questions the way other people breathe. But I couldn't answer. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. There was a word in me somewhere, a small warm thing, but every time I reached for it, it hid. So I said nothing, and after a while she said, "That's all right. We'll find it. Names keep."

*Names keep.* She says things like that. She says them the way she files gear teeth — precisely, and meaning more than the words weigh.

For a long time I was called nothing, and then I was called a false name in the workhouse — *Martha Cobb*, a name like a borrowed coat two sizes wrong — and then, after the guild dragged me out into the light, I was called *the child*, *the heir*, *the girl*, *Miss Somebody-or-other* by people who wanted something. None of those fit either. A coat, a coat, a coat.

Somewhere in me there was the word. I could feel it the way you feel a tooth coming loose — present, waiting, slightly frightening.

* * *

"So," said the man with the ledger. He dipped his pen. "Full name. In order: Christian name, then any middle names, then the family name. Who stands as witness to the parentage?"

"The register of the late Josiah Merrivale," Lior said, "which the guild holds certified copies of. Also the parish record of Mirabel Merrivale's death last autumn, and the testimony sworn at the assizes hearing, which you will find entered already in the circuit clerk's book. The child is the daughter of Mirabel Merrivale, deceased, and granddaughter of Josiah Merrivale, founder of this observatory, deceased. Both facts are on public record as of four days ago. What is *not* on public record—" and here her voice did the thing it does, the flat calm thing that frightens people more than shouting, "—is her Christian name. Because her mother gave it to no one. Because her mother was hiding her."

The pen-man looked at me again. "And does the child know her own name?"

Everyone always assumes the child knows nothing. They assume it kindly or unkindly, but they assume it. I was tired of it. I am seven years old and I have been in a workhouse and an assizes hearing and I have watched a grown man's lawyer fold up like a wet map. I know things.

"She knows," I said.

Both of them looked at me. My voice had come out smaller than I wanted. I tried again, louder, the way Lior had taught me to speak without hedging — *say the whole sentence, don't saw it in half and apologize for each piece.*

"She knows," I said. "She just hasn't said it out loud yet."

"And why is that?" the man asked. Not cruel. Curious. Pens ask curious questions; it is their nature.

I looked at Lior. She nodded once, small, and stepped back half a step, which was her telling me that this part was mine and she would not hold my shoulder through it, and somehow that made it possible.

"Because if I say it," I explained, "then it's real, and if it's real, then Mama is really dead, and Grandfather is really dead, and I'm really the only one left." I shrugged, because shrugging is what you do when the truth is too heavy to carry politely. "But it's been real for ages anyway. I've just been keeping it wound."

The man blinked. Lior made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed sideways.

"Well," the register man said. He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself — not the great ledger, a working draft, which I understood even then was wise; you do not put the first striking of a new mechanism straight into the finished case. "Whenever you're ready."

* * *

I had decided the order myself, days ago, lying in my bed in the observatory under the quilt that smelled faintly of machine oil no matter how many times it was washed. Three names. Three names, because a clock has three things it cannot do without: a motive force, a train, and an escapement. Something that drives you, something that carries you, something that keeps you honest.

Grandfather first. Josiah. Not because I remembered him — I don't, or I remember only hands, large ones, and the smell of brass polish, and possibly a beard, though the beard might be invented — but because his name was the weight that drove everything. Without his wanting a granddaughter, without his writing *with her daughter* in a visitor book, none of the rest of the train would turn. His name was the falling weight in the case. You don't see it working. You just run down on it.

Mama next. Mirabel. She was the train — the wheels, the singing, the carriage, the carrying of me through the frozen mud from wherever-we-were to the one place in Vellamarch with a heartbeat. She carried the drive along and handed it to me and then stopped, the way a wheel stops, and the stopping is not a failure, the stopping is just where the wheel ends. Lior says I think too much in mechanisms for a seven-year-old. Lior also thinks in mechanisms for a thirty-something-year-old, so I don't take the criticism seriously.

And then mine. My own. The escapement. The tick that is mine and no one else's, the small tooth that catches and releases, catches and releases, and turns all that falling weight into *time* — into a life that actually goes somewhere, second by second, instead of just dropping.

The problem was that I couldn't remember my own name. Truly couldn't. I had it — the shape of it, the warm hidden thing — but shapes are not words.

Then, two nights ago, Lior came to my room with the old visitor book and sat on the edge of my bed with the candle, and she said, "There's a line I've read a hundred times and I've only just understood it." She turned the pages to the autumn entry, Mama's entry, *Mirabel, with her daughter,* and beneath it, added later in smaller writing, squeezed into the margin the way you write something you're afraid to write but must:

*The little one is called* — and then the name.

Mama had come back to the observatory once, alone, before the end. She had come to the father who had hidden her and hidden her child, and she had trusted him with one thing only: the name. Written small, in a margin, where only someone reading slowly and lovingly would ever find it.

Lior read it aloud to me, once, and then closed the book and said, "That's yours. It was always yours. I only kept the shelf it sat on."

I didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, someday, by someone fair. I did not cry. I lay there feeling the name settle into me like a pallet of gears seating into plates — that small precise *clunk* when everything finds the hole it was cut for — and I slept better than I had slept since the carriage.

* * *

The pen hovered. The stone room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, workmen were calling to each other about the ferry landing, which was being rebuilt, which was its own long story involving a boy, a bribe, and a boat that had changed owners twice in one week.

I climbed onto the stool behind the counter — dignity is easier at height, another thing I learned early — and I said it.

"My name is Josephine."

The pen-man wrote *Josephine.*

"Mirabel," I said. "After Mama. She gets to be in the middle, where she can see everything."

He wrote *Mirabel.*

"And my family name is Merrivale." My voice cracked on it, just slightly, the way new ice cracks — a thin bright sound, nothing broken, everything opening. "Josiah Merrivale's granddaughter. Mirabel Merrivale's daughter. Josephine Mirabel Merrivale."

I said it all the way through, one motion, no hedges, and the strange thing — the thing I will remember when I am old — is that the room did not change. The pens kept scratching. The man kept writing. The stone stayed stone. The world did not stop and rearrange itself around my name, which is what I had half expected, having spent my whole life being told names were dangerous.

Instead the change was all inside me, and it was quiet, and it was enormous. It felt like winding. Like the moment when the last turn of the key goes in and the whole mechanism wakes and you feel the resistance come alive in your fingers and you know — you *know* — that the day ahead of you is going to happen, reliably, honestly, at its own speed, whether you watch it or not.

"There," the man said. He blotted the draft and slid it across for me to check. Reading upside down is easy; I'd been reading the register man's papers inverted for ten minutes out of boredom. I checked every letter. Lior had taught me that too — trust, but verify the train.

"Josephine Mirabel Merrivale," I read aloud, practicing. Then again, faster. Then again, just because I could.

"It suits you," Lior said.

"It fits," I corrected. "Suits is for coats. This isn't a coat. This is a *name.*"

She laughed — really laughed, the rare kind, the kind the widow Kessel says she didn't hear once in all the years of the silence, and which I intend to collect as many of as I can hold.

* * *

While the pen-man copied the fair entry into the great ledger — and oh, watching my name go into that book, watching the pen walk across the page and leave me there permanently, between a cooper's son born that morning and an old woman's death recorded the day before, me filed in among everybody, ordinary and official and *real* — Lior signed where witnesses sign. Her hand hesitated over the line marked *relationship to registrant.*

"What do I put?" she asked me. Not the clerk. Me. She has done that from the beginning, asked *me* instead of deciding over my head, and I think that is the single greatest thing anyone ever gave me, greater than the shawl, greater than the workshop, greater even than the truth.

I considered it seriously. Guardian was wrong. Ward was backwards. Apprentice was premature, though we both knew where that was heading, because I had been handing her tools for a month and she had stopped correcting my grip, which in Lior-language is a knighthood.

"Family," I said. "Put *family.* It doesn't say what kind. That's the best part."

Her throat moved. She wrote *family* in her small exact engineer's letters, and blotted it, and did not look at me while she did it, which is how I knew it mattered.

* * *

We came out of the register office into a morning that smelled of thaw and woodsmoke, the river running high and brown and free past the town, carrying the last of the ice out toward the sea in white leaping pieces. Spring had proper hold of Vellamarch now. Windows stood open. Someone was beating a rug on a balcony with great violence and satisfaction.

Lior bought hot chestnuts from the brazier by the bridge and we ate them on the rail, burning our fingers in the traditional manner, and neither of us said anything for a while. With Lior silence is not empty. Silence with Lior is a wound mainspring, full and coiled and ready.

"Josephine," she said at last, trying the name out loud, testing its weight like a balance staff. "Jo-se-phine."

"You don't have to shorten it," I said. "People will try. They'll call me Jo, or Josie, or worse. You're allowed to use the whole thing."

"I intend to." She passed me the last chestnut, which is how I knew she meant it. "Your mother chose well."

"She chose the name before she chose the hiding place," I said. "That's what the margin means, isn't it? She wrote it down when things were still good enough to plan ahead."

Lior looked at me for a long moment with an expression I had seen her wear at the bench, examining a movement she suspected of being finer than its maker's reputation suggested.

"Yes," she said finally. "That's exactly what it means."

We walked home along the river road, past the ferry landing with its new timbers, past the guildhall where the flag of the charter hung out, past the corner where the ferryman's boy waved with both arms as though hailing a ship. And all the way, I practiced. Under my breath, in rhythm with my boots on the cobbles, tick and tock and tick: *Josephine Mirabel Merrivale.* Catch and release. Catch and release. A name, I was discovering, is not a thing you are given. It is a thing you keep, the way you keep a clock — daily, attentively, with clean hands — and it keeps you back.

* * *

The observatory door was open when we reached it, which was unusual, and there was a figure in the entrance hall standing with his hat in his hands, and Lior's arm came across my chest before I'd even registered who it was — quick as an escapement, catching me on the fall.

Mr. Aldery.

He looked terrible, in the way of men who have been losing gracefully in public for a week. Thinner. Greyer. His fine coat sat on him like a borrowed story. But he did not look angry, and he did not look drunk, and when he saw us he did something I had never seen him do in all the hearings and dinners and corridors where I had watched him from under other people's conversations.

He bowed. To me. Low. To a seven-year-old in a patched dress with a cog on a ribbon around her neck and chestnut grease on her fingers.

"Miss Merrivale," he said, and my new name in his mouth sounded strange and formal and — I searched for it — respectful. Genuinely respectful. "Forgive the hour and the intrusion. I'm not here about the estate. That's settled, and settled fairly, and I've stopped arguing with fairness; it wins eventually regardless." His eyes went to Lior, then back to me. "I've come about something smaller. Something I'd rather ask in front of you than behind you, for once."

Lior's arm lowered from my chest, but slowly, the way you lower a tool you may need again.

"Ask, then," Lior said.

Mr. Aldery turned his hat over in his hands, once, twice, and I saw that his fingers were trembling, just slightly, the way a movement trembles when a pivot is worn — still turning, still true in its circle, but loose in its seat. And he began, in a voice quieter than any I had ever heard him use:

"They tell me you lived in his house. That you knew his habits. That you were with him, near the end, more than anyone." He swallowed. "Did my uncle ever speak of me? Not the law, not the money, not the will — I've had a week of the law. Did he ever—" the worn pivot caught, released, caught again— "*speak* of me?"

And I stood there in the doorway of my grandfather's observatory, with my brand-new name warming in my chest like a freshly wound spring, and watched Lior decide how much truth a man could bear, and how kindly it could be delivered, and whether the answer belonged to the living or the dead —

— and she opened her mouth to give it.
