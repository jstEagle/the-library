## 38. First Light in the Observatory

The clock had never been kept through a night before.

That was the thought that settled over Lior as the last of the daylight drained from the observatory's eastern windows and the great dial above her went pale as a moon. For ten years Oscar had run only by day, when someone could be spared to wind him, and had been left to die at dusk like a fire nobody tended. Even in her master's time, even at his best, the old man had gone home to Widow Kessel's supper and left the tower dark. The clock had known a hundred hands but no one who stayed.

Tonight there were two of them, and neither was going anywhere.

"Is he hungry?" The girl stood beside the winding crank, holding the iron key against her chest the way other children held dolls.

"Clocks don't get hungry."

"He does." She said it without doubt, without apology. "He was starved for ten years. I can hear it in him."

Lior considered arguing and found she didn't have the heart, or perhaps the honesty. Ten years of neglect did leave a sound in a mechanism — a thinness in the strike train, a hesitation where the lifting pins met the hammers, the whole movement running as though it had learned not to want too much. She had spent three weeks machining that hesitance out of him, and now that it was gone, she missed it the way one misses a scar that explained everything.

"He eats once a week," Lior said. "Sunday nights. That's his winding. But tonight we check him every hour anyway. First full night. We watch everything."

"What are we watching for?"

"Everything going wrong."

"And if nothing goes wrong?"

Lior wound the great weight up its shaft, hand over hand, feeling the drum take the strain through her shoulders and down her spine. "Then we write it down anyway. A clockkeeper's log is mostly a record of nothing. Nothing stretched, nothing slipped, nothing frozen. You fill pages with the absence of disaster and call it a good night."

The girl took this in seriously, the way she took everything in, filing it behind those gray eyes that missed less than any adult's Lior had ever known. "That sounds like being a mother," she said.

The word went through Lior like a mainspring snapping in a closed case — you heard the report but couldn't see the damage. She finished the winding. "It sounds like work," she said. "Sit down. Lesson one: how to listen."

* * *

She taught listening the way her master had taught it to her, at fourteen, on a bench half this size, with his breath smelling of tea gone cold and his voice pitched so low she'd had to lean into the machinery to hear him.

"You don't listen to a clock with your ears," she told the girl now, settling them both on stools before the open case, the movement laid out in its brass architecture, wheels within wheels, the escapement ticking at its patient two beats per second. "You listen with your hands first. Two fingers here, on the case wood, very light. Wood carries what air hides."

The girl pressed her fingers to the oak frame. Her face changed — Lior saw it happen, the small furrow smoothing out, the eyes losing their vigilance for the first time since Vellamarch had taken her in. Whatever she heard through the grain, it was better than what she heard with her ears.

"What is it?"

"The tick goes all the way down," the girl said. "Into the floor. Into my feet."

"That's right. Now close your eyes and count the beat. Don't count seconds — count him. One-two, one-two. Find where the two beats aren't the same."

They listened. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river moved under its rotting ice, groaning and shifting as it had for weeks, the sound of a giant turning in a sleep it would soon wake from. The girl counted under her breath, lips moving, fingers spread against the wood like a physician taking a pulse.

"There," she said at last. "The second tick is fatter."

"Fatter?"

"Wider. It takes up more room than the first one."

Lior felt something turn over in her chest that had nothing to do with gears. In nine years of apprenticeship she had never once described an uneven beat as fat. It was the kind of thing her master would have seized on — *say it your own way, girl; the trade needs new words or it dies of dignity* — and the kind of thing she had forgotten she believed until this child said it back to her across ten years and one ruined workshop.

"It is wider," Lior agreed. "The escape wheel tooth that releases second is riding a hair proud on its pivot. By morning it'll be worse. Tonight we just mark it. Tomorrow we burnish the pivot. Write it down."

The girl took the log book — the new one, the first page still smelling of sizing — and printed in careful capitals: *SECOND TICK FAT. PIVOT PROUD. BURNISH TOMORROW.* Then, after consideration: *HE IS NOT HUNGRY TONIGHT.*

Lior read it over her shoulder and did not correct it. Some entries belonged in the record whether the trade approved of them or not.

* * *

At eleven they made the first round.

This too was liturgy Lior had inherited and never expected to perform: the climb to the dial head, lantern held high, checking the hands' true position against the regulator's error chart; the descent to the pendulum, watching its arc swing true in the long stone throat of the tower; the pause at the strike train to confirm the locking plate had lifted clean. Her master had done it with a candle stub and a coat thrown over his nightshirt. She did it with a whale-oil lamp and a coat thrown over her dress, and behind her came six years of girl in bare feet, memorizing each station of the round as though it were a prayer route in a church whose god had recently returned.

On the dial level the cold was different — older, mineral, coming off the stones themselves. Through the slit windows the river showed as a paleness below the town, stitched with cracks that caught the starlight. It had begun talking to itself again after midnight, long reports traveling up the valley, the ice deciding in the dark where it would fail.

"The hearing man comes on the first ferry," the girl said. They had discussed it every day for a week; she repeated it now the way she checked a locked door, out of ritual rather than doubt.

"On the first ferry. And the guildmaster will bring the circuit judge's letter of standing, and the register, and the ledger stays in the guildhall safe where Aldery can't buy, borrow, or burn it. Yes. Again."

"I wasn't asking because I forgot."

"I know." Lior adjusted the lamp so its light wouldn't wash the dial face. "I was answering because I needed to hear it."

The girl looked at her — that frank, unchildlike look that undressed motives without malice — and then let it go, which was her own particular mercy. She had learned somewhere, in whatever year she'd spent being carried between houses and finally abandoned on steps, that some fears are better fed with repetition than reasoned with.

Below them the hour turned. Eleven strokes rolled up through the tower and filled the dial room, and Lior put her palm flat against the case and felt each one arrive in the wood like a heartbeat knocking on a door.

"He counts well tonight," the girl said.

"He counts beautifully tonight." And then, because the night was long and the truth was easier in the dark: "I used to hate him. Did I tell you that?"

"The clock?"

"This clock. When Master Ellery sent me up here the first time, alone, to oil the second-hand pivot — I was fourteen, I dropped the oil bottle inside the case, and I was so certain I'd ruined something enormous that I sat down behind the weights and cried. And while I cried I decided I hated him. All that brass and all that importance, keeping time for a town full of people who couldn't keep a promise. I hated that he never stopped. Never rested. Never forgave a lost second."

The girl considered this with the gravity she gave all testimony. "Do you still hate him?"

"No." Lior watched the minute hand crawl. "Now I think he was the only honest thing in Vellamarch for ten years. Everything else in this town was bought or silenced or both. Him and the river. He just kept striking the hours into a room where men were lying to each other, and none of them thought to make him stop, because stopping the clock would have meant admitting time was passing and debts were coming due." She smiled without much humor. "Aldery tried the quieter way. Sabotage. Let the clock fail and call it neglect. But clocks don't lie about why they stop, if anyone bothers to look. He should have known that. His uncle built this one."

"Your master helped build it."

"My master helped build it," Lior agreed. "And was paid for twenty years to help unbuild it. And hid the truth inside it anyway, in the end, hoping someone honest would take the case apart someday." She touched the brass housing, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "People are the only machines that can betray themselves and mean well. Remember that when you're keeping clocks. Clocks are easy. Clocks only ever do exactly what they're built to do, until something breaks them, and then they tell you plainly what broke them."

"Like the fat tick."

"Exactly like the fat tick."

* * *

At midnight they slept in shifts, or pretended to.

Lior had brought blankets and a straw pallet up to the keeper's room, a low stone cell beneath the movement floor where the founder's last keeper had lived out his final winters. There was a cot there, a stool, a shelf with a candle stub and a clay jug. Someone had swept it — the guildmaster's people, she supposed, or perhaps Aldery's men in their last days of watching the place, tidying after themselves like burglars with consciences. It smelled of stone and lamp oil and, faintly, of dust that had finally been allowed to settle after decades of disturbance.

She meant to take first sleep and give the girl the second watch, but the child would not lie down until Lior did, and so they compromised as families compromise: both of them on the narrow cot, backs to the wall, the girl's cold feet finding Lior's calves with the unerring instinct of children seeking warmth, Lior telling herself she would get up in an hour and not moving.

Through the floor, muffled to almost nothing, the clock ticked.

"Do you hear that?" the girl whispered.

"Yes."

"When I lived at the other places — before the steps — there was never a sound like that. Big houses are quiet. Quiet like holding your breath." Her voice had the flat reciting quality she used for memories she did not wish to be comforted about. "But when they carried me up the hill that night, and I heard the clock through the walls, ticking like this — I thought, that house is breathing. Houses that breathe won't smother you."

Lior lay in the dark and let the words find their depth. *Forget the face, finish the work,* the sealed note had said, and she had obeyed it for months, and disobeyed it forever on a morning she could name to the minute. But the girl had obeyed nothing. The girl had simply heard a heartbeat through stone and walked toward it.

"I'm glad the house was breathing," Lior said.

The child's hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, briefly, hard, the way she did when feeling was too large for words — and then withdrew, composed again, six years old and already fluent in the grammar of survival. Within minutes her breathing slowed and deepened. Children trust the way mechanisms run: entirely, and only while nothing jams the train.

Lior did not sleep. She lay listening to two rhythms — the girl's quick breath, the clock's slow one — and understood, with the calm of a machinist seeing a mechanism come into beat after years of rough running, that these were now the same problem. The same work. She had thought for months that she must choose between the ward and the craft, between the child and the workshop, and had paid the workshop gladly, and mourned it honestly. But lying there in the keeper's room with the founder's clock counting overhead, she saw the false division plain. There had never been two things to save. There had only been time — stolen from a child, hidden in a clock, hoarded by liars — and her whole life had been an apprenticeship in giving time back.

She got up softly, left the girl warm, and went to make the one o'clock round alone.

* * *

The tower at one in the morning belonged to the clock and the river.

Lior climbed with the lamp hooded, out of habit and superstition both — some part of her still believed the mechanism performed better unwitnessed, the way her master had believed you shouldn't count a fusee's turns aloud lest you tempt the wire to snap. At the movement floor she checked the maintaining power, confirmed the great barrel had fed out its line true, and stood a moment with her hand on the escapement bridge, feeling the pallets take and release, take and release, the newest metal in the building doing the oldest job in the world.

Her escapement. Machined in fourteen hours of fury, cut from steel she had chosen herself, finished to tolerances she had never before dared. The guild had certified it. The circuit judge's letter had praised it, in the dry language judges use for things they cannot fault. And tomorrow — today, now — the ferry would come if the river allowed, and the law would sit in the observatory's shadow and ratify what the ice and the guild and one stubborn apprentice had already decided.

But that was tomorrow's machinery. Tonight was the watch.

She made her rounds. Dial, true. Pendulum, swinging its full arc, neither short nor wandering. Strike train, locked and ready. On the chart she entered the regulator error — four seconds slow over the hour, correcting — and beneath it, in the column her master's charts had never possessed, the girl's entry from earlier: *PIVOT PROUD. BURNISH.*

At the window she stopped.

The river had opened its first black seam.

It ran along the far channel, a ribbon of open water fifty yards long, steaming faintly in the cold, edged with broken plates grinding against each other in the current with a sound like crockery in a giant's sink. The break was beginning — early, as everyone had whispered it might, the winter having been cruel enough to hurry itself. By dawn the seam would widen. By noon the ferry would either run or not run, and Aldery's bought papers and the circuit judge's honest ones would race each other up the valley on whichever conveyance the ice permitted.

A year ago — ten years ago, all her life — she would have stood at that window and felt fear gather in her stomach like water rising in a case. Now she watched the black water widen and felt only the attention of a clockmaker observing a train come into motion: everything engaged, nothing slipping, the moment approaching when the escapement lets go and the wheel turns.

"Let it break," she said to the river. "We're ready on this side of it."

From below, faint through stone, the clock struck one. She counted the strokes against her pulse and found them perfect.

* * *

She woke the girl at four for her first solo round, and followed her up the stairs without letting herself be seen.

The child performed it exactly as taught — lamp hooded, stations visited in order, the regulator error read off the dial against the chart, the strike train checked, the pendulum watched for a full sixty seconds with her lips moving through the count. At the movement floor she paused longer than the lesson required, and Lior, flattened in the stair shadow, saw why: the girl had pressed both palms to the case, eyes shut, listening downward through the wood the way she had taught Lior, in her fat-tick language, that a clock ought to be listened to.

Then she spoke — quietly, to the machine, in the tone of one reporting to a grandfather who was also a colleague.

"Second tick's still fat. We'll fix it tomorrow. Everyone's asleep except us. Keep counting."

And Oscar, being a clock, kept counting, and the girl wrote her entry in the log in her careful capitals —

*TWO O'CLOCK ERROR NIL. HE SLEPT WELL. SO DID I, MOSTLY.*

— and Lior withdrew down the stairs before she could be caught crying over a maintenance record, which would have set the trade back forty years.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of hammered pewter, and with it the sound.

Not the ferry horn — too early for that — but the river, giving way. The seam had become a channel; the channel became a lane; the lane became a mile of running black water carrying white plates downstream in stately, grinding procession, the whole winter's armor breaking up and moving at once. The town would be on the banks by midmorning, everyone from the mayor to the fishwives, watching the ice go as Vellamarch had watched it go for two hundred years, and this year the watching would have a different taste, because everyone knew what the break announced: the term, the ferry, the hearing, the end of the waiting.

Lior stood at the east window with the girl on a stool beside her so she could see, and together they watched the founder's river carry his enemies' delays away.

"They'll come today," the girl said. "The hearing people."

"Some of them. Whoever the ice lets through."

"And tonight we do the rounds again?"

"Every night. That's the commission now. Permanent. The guild papers said so — you signed as witness, remember? Your first signature in an official book."

"I remembered every letter." She had; Lior had checked.

Below them the observatory grounds were soft with thaw, the gravel paths surfacing from snowmelt, the first of the foundation beds showing green shoots that had waited, like everything else in Vellamarch, through a longer winter than most. Down the hill the town stirred — smoke from chimneys, a cart on the ferry road, the bell of Saint Aubin's ringing the hour a half-beat late, as it always did, and always would, because Saint Aubin's kept its own stubborn time and no guild would ever certify it.

Lior thought of the workshop on Cooper's Lane — sold now, passed with the estate to the child's inheritance, its benches empty, its sign coming down. She had fought ten months to save it and lost it in an afternoon, and discovered in the losing that it had never been the sanctuary she'd imagined. A workshop is a place where you hide from the world behind good work. An observatory, she was learning, is a place where you stand above the world and keep its time honestly, in the open, where everyone can see the dial.

The girl tugged her sleeve. "You're doing the thinking face."

"I'm allowed one thinking face per dawn. Guild rule."

"What are you thinking?"

Lior looked at her — at the sharp gray eyes and the stubborn chin and the small capable hands already reaching for the log book — and thought of the note folded in her breast pocket, the one that had started everything. *Forget the face, finish the work.* She had carried it through sabotage and hearings and hunger and grief, meaning to burn it when the matter was settled, and the matter was nearly settled now, and the paper had grown heavier rather than lighter, because burning it would mean the command had only been defeated instead of answered.

There was a better answer. She had been circling it for days. It would cost a little money and a visit to Herr Falk the limner, and it would put a child's face into the guild register in ink and wash, permanent as any will — proof on official record, for every clerk and magistrate and creditor yet unborn, that she had never been forgotten at all.

But not this morning. This morning there was a clock to keep.

"We're thinking about breakfast," Lior said. "And then the fat pivot. Come on — burnishing waits for daylight, and daylight's here."

They went down through the tower together, past the strike train and the great wheel and the pendulum swinging its unhurried arc, two pairs of footsteps in a building that had waited ten years for exactly this sound. At the keeper's room door the girl paused and looked back up the stairs, toward the ticking.

"Good morning, Oscar," she called formally. "You kept the night."

And from the movement floor, in the only language he had, the clock answered — one-two, one-two, steady as a promise — and began the town's first honest day.
