## 39. The Face Worth Remembering

The note had lived in her pocket for a year.

Lior had carried it through the hearing, through the flood, through the long weeks of rebuilding when the observatory smelled of river mud and scorched brass and the particular exhaustion of people who had won something and were not yet sure what it would cost them to keep it. She carried it through mending days and market mornings, through the night the child woke screaming about water and Lior sat with her until the candles guttered, through all of it — the small folded square of paper with its wax seal cracked and its single line of instruction pressed into it by a steady hand:

*Forget the face. Finish the work.*

She knew the hand now. She had spent a year learning hands — Aldery's, the magistrate's, the guildmaster's, her dead master's, all of them legible once you understood what they were trying not to say. This one was her master's too. She had compared it against his teaching notes a dozen times in the dark months, holding both papers to the window light like a jeweler checking for flaws, and the slant of the *f*, the way the ink pooled at the end of each stroke where he paused to think — there was no doubt. He had written it.

Which meant that somewhere between the founder's death and the child's delivery onto those frozen steps, her master — honest Master Winterbourne, who had taught her that a clock lied to no one or it was no clock at all — had become the kind of man who wrote instructions like this one.

Or the kind of man who wrote them hoping they would never be obeyed.

She had decided which, finally, in March, sitting on the cold floor of the gear room with the ledger open on her knees and the girl asleep against her shoulder. The coded apology between the lines of his teaching notes had settled it. A man who hid a will inside a clock, behind an escapement only an apprentice's patience could reach, and then sent the child to that clock's door with a note commanding forgetfulness — he had built the contradiction into the machine itself, the way you build a fuse into a movement so that if everything else fails, one spring still releases. He had trusted the contradiction. He had trusted *her*.

It was time to stop carrying the note like a relic and treat it like what it was: a thing written to be destroyed by whoever proved strong enough to destroy it.

* * *

She did not burn it in the workshop hearth. That felt too easy, too domestic, ash swallowed among the ordinary cinders of a working fire. She wanted witnesses of a kind. She wanted the burning to mean something she could point to later, on a day when memory got slippery.

So on a bright cold morning in late April, with the river running clean and high and the town's boats back on the water, she took the girl up to the observatory roof, where the founder used to make his observations, and where the wind came off the water carrying the smell of thawed earth and rope and fish. The town lay below them in its spring colors — slate roofs washed pale by rain, the market square striped with awnings, the ferry landing busy again since the circuit judge's arrival had loosened Aldery's grip on the crossings. Somewhere under all of it, the ice that had roared and broken in March was already a story people told their children.

Oscar stood in the cupola behind them, ticking. The girl had taken to calling him that within a week of the hearing — Oscar, after the founder's nickname, which the widow Kessel had supplied with wet eyes and a shaking voice — and now she spoke to him as to a grandfather. *Good morning, Oscar. We're going up on the roof, mind the house.* The clock ticked his answer. He always did.

Lior knelt at the low parapet and took out the note.

The girl watched her with those grave dark eyes, the eyes everyone in Vellamarch had learned to recognize by now, the eyes that were on the guild register and in the parish book and, as of last week, framed behind glass in the assizes clerk's office alongside the ruling the circuit judge had countersigned. Eyes that could not be forgotten anymore even by people who had tried very hard to forget them.

"Do you remember the night I found you?" Lior asked.

The girl shook her head. Then, being six going on seven and constitutionally incapable of leaving an answer incomplete: "I remember the bell. And your coat smelled like oil. And the steps were very cold." A pause. "I wasn't scared."

"You were shivering."

"That's different from scared."

"It is," Lior said. "Someone should have taught me that a long time ago."

She held the note out flat on her palm so the girl could read it. The child read slowly but she read — Lior had seen to that over the winter, letters traced in spilled clock oil on the bench top, syllables sounded out against the strike of the hours — and her brow folded as she made out the line.

"Forget the face," she read. "Whose face?"

"Yours."

"Why?"

"Because someone didn't want you counted. Because counting you would have cost them money and standing and the magistrate's ear. Because forgetting is cheaper than fighting." Lior turned her hand over and let the paper fall to the stones between them. "This is what my master was paid to write. And I think — I've thought about it every way a person can think about a thing — I believe he wrote it the way you build a weak link into a chain on purpose. So the chain breaks where *you* want it to break, and not where the weight falls."

The girl considered this with the seriousness she brought to all mechanisms, living or otherwise. "Did it break right?"

"It broke right." Lior struck a match. The wind fought it; she cupped the flame with both hands, an old apprentice habit, shielding fire the way she'd been taught to shield a solder seam. "But a broken chain still lies there looking like a chain. You have to pick up the pieces and melt them down or someone will try to use them again."

She touched the flame to the corner of the note.

It caught reluctantly, the way old paper does, curling brown at first and then black, the wax seal bubbling and releasing a smell like burnt honey. The words went last — ink holds on stubbornly past the paper around it — so that for a few seconds *forget the face* glowed whole and legible inside its ring of fire, a message burning outward-in, and then it didn't, and then it was only a black lace of ash lifting on the wind, turning over the parapet, scattering above the roofs of Vellamarch.

The girl reached out and caught one fleck of it. It dissolved against her finger before she could look at it properly.

"Now nobody can obey it," she said.

"Nobody can obey it," Lior agreed. Her throat was tight in a way that surprised her; she had expected relief, and relief was there, but underneath it ran something older and more tender, grief for her master doing its last slow work, the final payment of a debt she'd been settling all year. "Come downstairs. There's something else we're doing today, and I want you clean-handed for it. Bench rules."

"Bench rules," the girl repeated, solemn as scripture, and scrubbed her palms together though there was nothing on them.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of floor wax and lamp oil and the particular dust that accumulates around ledgers, which is a dust with authority in it, as if the paper itself exhaled importance. Lior had walked these halls perhaps four times in ten years — apprentices did not come here unless summoned, and she had never been summoned — and each of those visits had been during the bad season, when the guild debated her master's debts, so her memory of the place was all dark paneling and lowered voices. In April light, with the windows open to the street noise, it seemed almost friendly. Almost.

Guildmaster Herron met them in the register room.

He was an old rival of her master's and looked it in the best sense — the same broad hands, the same squint calibrated by decades of close work, but without the softness Winterbourne had worn like a disguise. Herron had voted against forgiving the workshop's debts. Herron had also filed habeas papers under charter law when every other door in Vellamarch was bought shut, and had stood in the flooded observatory while the river roared outside and ruled a six-year-old girl the lawful heir of the town's founding family. Lior had stopped trying to reconcile the two men months ago. Some people were simply built like escapements: rigid, exact, and on the side of whatever kept true time.

"Journeywoman Winterbourne." He said it without warmth and without irony, which from Herron was a kind of courtesy. His gaze moved to the girl, and softened by perhaps a degree, which from Herron was a parade. "And the heir. You've brought her to be enrolled."

"To be recorded." Lior set her satchel on the long table and began unpacking it with the deliberation she used for laying out tools. "There's a difference, and I want it understood before we start. Enrollment is administration. Anyone can be enrolled — names in a book, fees in a box, done by Friday. What I'm asking for is a record. A portrait, drawn by a proper hand, entered in the guild register beside her name and her line of descent, certified under seal, copies lodged with the circuit judge and the parish." She laid the items out as she spoke: the certified extract of the founder's will. The register page bearing her mother's name. The judge's countersigned ruling. Last, wrapped in flannel, the small brass cog the child had stolen from the workhouse and pressed through the grate — evidence, Herron had called it at the hearing, of identity asserted under confinement, and the girl still slept with it under her pillow some nights.

Herron studied the array. "The register records instruments and members. Portraits are not customary."

"No," Lior said. "Neither was filing habeas under charter law for a foundling, and the town managed to survive it." She heard herself and paused — not to retract, but to choose the next words carefully, because this mattered more than pride. "Guildmaster. Ten years ago someone wrote an instruction that a child should be forgotten, and the instruction worked. It worked because forgetting is cheap. It costs nothing to leave a name out of a book. Every harm that was done to her was done in paperwork — a false name in a workhouse roll, a bequest voided on a technicality, a magistrate's ruling drafted before any witness was heard. Paper did all of it." She touched the judge's ruling. "So paper has to undo it. Not just the rulings — the rulings are done, thank God and the river — but the *habit*. The habit of her being unrecorded. If she exists only in documents that can be contested, then she can be unmade by the next clever man with a pen. But a face, drawn, witnessed, sealed — that's harder to argue with. You can dispute a claim. It's much harder to dispute a person."

Herron was quiet long enough that the street sounds filled the room: a cart, gulls, the ferry bell marking the half hour. The girl stood perfectly still beside Lior, hands folded, watching the guildmaster the way she watched a movement she was trying to understand — not shy, not bold, simply attentive, cataloguing.

"The sketcher will need her to sit," Herron said at last. "Possibly more than once. Register portraits are done properly or not at all — if we're establishing precedent, it will be precedent worth citing." He opened a cabinet and drew out a slim volume bound in green leather, the current register, and turned to a fresh leaf with the unhurried precision of a man who had entered thousands of names and had never once smudged one. "Name."

The girl stepped forward before Lior could speak.

"Oscar Vellamarch," she said.

Lior had known this was coming. They had discussed the name for weeks — discussed whether to take the founder's surname whole, whether the girl might rather keep some trace of her mother's chosen alias, whether Vellamarch was a gift or a burden, a river town's whole history hung around one child's neck. The girl had listened to all of it with her chin on her fists and then announced her decision at breakfast with the finality of a ratchet clicking home. Her grandfather's name. Her mother's father. The man whose clock she tended every morning. *He waited for me,* she had said. *Even dead, he waited. That's what family is.*

Herron's pen hovered. "Oscar," he repeated. "The clock's name."

"The founder's name for the clock," Lior said quietly, "and the clock's name for him. She's keeping both alive under one word. I've given up pretending that isn't exactly the kind of thing this register exists for."

Something happened at the corner of the old man's mouth that in a less austere face would have been called a smile. He wrote. The nib scratched — *Oscar Vellamarch, lawful heir of Edvard Vellamarch, founder, by his daughter Miriam, deceased* — and beneath it, after a moment's consideration, he added a second line in smaller script, and Lior craned slightly to read it:

*Recorded under protection of the Guild Charter, per request of Journeywoman L. Winterbourne, who delivered her to this record.*

"Your name goes in too?" the girl asked.

"Someone has to vouch for you," Lior said. "That's what a record is. Not just that you exist — that somebody stood up and said so."

The girl absorbed this. Then, with terrible six-year-old logic: "Who vouched for you?"

Herron looked up sharply. Lior felt the question land somewhere beneath her ribs and stay there, warm and aching, because the answer was obvious and the girl had seen it instantly: *she had.* All year, in every entry, every certification, every countersignature — the child had been vouching for her with the simple fact of her own existence, the way a pendulum vouches for an escapement, each one releasing the other, neither able to keep time alone.

"You did," Lior said. "Every day since the steps."

The girl nodded, satisfied, as if this had merely been a test and Lior had passed it.

* * *

The sketcher came on Thursday and again on Saturday, because the girl would not hold still for longer than an hour and Herron refused a likeness done in haste. He was a lean, patient man named Corvel who did engraving plates for the guild's certificates, and he treated the sitting like horology, which won the girl over completely: angles measured, light adjusted in quarter-turns, long silences broken only by the scratch of silverpoint. She sat on a stool in the register room with the afternoon window-light falling across her face, wearing her good dress, the one with the collar the widow Kessel had embroidered with tiny wheel teeth because the widow Kessel could not do anything without putting gears in it.

Lior sat against the wall and watched.

She watched the sketcher's hand move and the face emerge — the grave eyes first, always the eyes, then the mouth with its stubborn set that Lior recognized with a start as her own stubbornness reflected back, acquired somehow in a single winter, inherited by exposure the way a clock acquires the rate of the room it lives in. She watched the girl hold still with heroic discipline, blinking only when permitted, and she thought about the night on the steps, the bundle in the blanket, the sealed note in her glove, the moment she had lifted the lantern and seen a small face looking up at her out of the cold — and how her whole body had said *no, walk away, you cannot afford this,* and how she had picked the child up anyway, because some decisions are not made by us but simply arrive, already made, like weather.

*Forget the face.*

She had forgotten nothing. That was the whole of it. Ten years of silence had taught her every technique of forgetting — the practiced blindness, the questions unasked, the ledger pages she'd never turned — and every one of those techniques had failed against a six-year-old on stone steps in the space of a single heartbeat. Some faces are cut too deep into you to wear smooth.

On Saturday evening Corvel turned the finished drawing toward them and the room went quiet.

It was very good. It was better than good; it was true. The girl looked out of it with her whole gravity intact — not sentimentalized, not prettied, just present, a person who had survived being erased and intended to remain legible. Herron examined it for a long moment, then blotted and dried the register page, tipped silver sand across the fresh ink, and pressed the guild seal into the red wax at the foot: the wheel-and-plumb emblem, bitten deep.

Three copies. One for the hall. One for the circuit judge, to travel with his own records. One for the girl herself, to keep.

She received hers with both hands, the way she received tools, and held it up to the failing light, and looked at her own face looking back at her.

"So now it's on record," she said. "Forever?"

"As forever as paper gets," Herron said. "Which, maintained properly, is quite a long while."

"Longer than clocks?"

"Longer than most clocks." He allowed himself the smile fully this time, brief as a striking train. "Though not," he added, gathering his pens, "longer than a well-kept one. Which is why we record things in registers *and* teach children to wind them. Redundancy, Miss Vellamarch. The first principle of honest engineering."

* * *

They walked home along the river in the blue end of the day. The water ran high and quick, carrying the last of the melt, and the ferry lamps were coming alight one by one down the bank like a mechanism finding its stroke. On the far shore, the observatory's dome caught the last sun and held it, a coal banking itself for the night.

The girl walked with the portrait flat against her chest, both arms wrapped around it, and hummed — the song from her fragments, the woman singing in the carriage, the melody she'd been humming more often lately, as though the record had given her permission to keep what was hers.

At the bend above the landing she stopped.

"Lior." She used the name plainly now; she'd earned it, or taken it. "The note you burned. Forget the face." She looked up, and the dusk made her eyes very dark and very steady. "Whose face did *he* mean? Yours or mine? When he wrote it."

Lior had turned this question over for months, and she gave the girl the answer she had arrived at, the one that had finally let her sleep.

"Both of ours," she said. "He meant mine because I was the one who'd have to carry the forgetting. He meant yours because you were the one who'd have to survive it. And I think — this is only a guess, but I've gotten good at reading him — I think he hoped we'd fail. Both commands. Forget nothing, finish everything. That's the repair order I've been working from ever since."

The girl considered this. Below them the ferry bell rang the hour, and behind them, faint and faithful, Oscar rang it too, two voices a river apart keeping the same time.

"Then we're doing it right," she said.

They walked on. And in Lior's pocket, where the note had lived for a year, there was nothing now but a scrap of flannel and a lingering smell of burnt honey, and the space felt not empty but cleared — a bench swept ready for the next day's work.

The next day's work was waiting. When they came up the observatory path, the widow Kessel was sitting on the steps with her hands folded and her good shawl pinned at the throat, and she rose when she saw them, and her face was the face of a woman who had rehearsed something difficult on the whole walk across town.

"Child, go on in and wind Oscar," she said gently. "Missus Winterbourne and I have a matter to settle — his bench. I've come about his bench."
