## 40. Oscar and the Clock

The clock has a name, though most people in Vellamarch have forgotten it ever mattered.

Oscar.

I found it the way I find most things — by listening when I was supposed to be sweeping. I was nine, maybe ten, and Lior had gone down to the Widow Kessel's with a basket of parts, and I was alone in the observatory with the great case looming over me like a wooden cathedral. I had my rag in one hand and my courage in the other, and I climbed the little folding ladder that Lior only used for winding, up past the weight channels, up to where the founder's initials were carved into an inner beam where no visitor would ever see them. And beneath the initials, small and half-worn, someone had cut three letters: *OSC*.

I asked Lior about it that evening while she was cleaning lacquer off her fingers with spirits. She went very still, the way she does — went still the way she did the night she opened the door to a shivering child with a sealed note pinned to her coat — and then she laughed. It was a real laugh, not her work laugh, not her careful-in-front-of-the-magistrate laugh. A laugh from low down.

"That's what he called it," she said. "The founder. My master told me once, late, after too much brandy, when his tongue forgot its training. *Oscar*, he said. The old man talked to it like a colleague. Complained to it. Confided in it." She looked up at the case, at the long pale column of it rising through the stairwell like something planted and grown rather than built. "A man who spent forty years alone in this room needed someone to talk to. So he named his clock."

"Did you talk to it?" I asked. "When you were fixing it. That winter."

Lior was quiet for a moment. Outside, the river was doing its spring talking, all gravel and groan, though that was years ago now and the ice is long gone and comes back gentler every year, as if even the water learned something.

"Yes," she said finally. "I told it things I couldn't tell anyone else. I suppose that's why it worked when I was done. Clocks keep secrets better than we do. They just turn them into time."

---

That was five years ago. I am sixteen now, and my hands are starting to look like hers.

Not identical — mine are still softer, the knuckles still smooth, and there's a scar on my left thumb I gave myself last autumn with a graver slip that Lior says every clockmaker earns eventually and that I should be proud of, which is the kind of thing she says that means *you were careless* and *you're one of us now* at the same time. But they are beginning to look like a clockmaker's hands: ink in the creases that never quite washes out, a permanent faint shine of brass polish on the fingertips, nails cut short as a man's because there is no use for long nails in a workshop and never has been.

I am apprenticed to Lior Winterbourne. It's on paper. The guild seal on the indenture papers is bigger than my hand, stamped in wax the color of dried blood, and the guildmaster himself signed it — the same guildmaster who once offered to take everything from Lior if she'd give up the bench forever, and who then, when she refused to take that bargain, sat through a whole night of argument with his own charter committee and came out the other side having decided that the guild's honor required giving her journeywoman's papers instead. People in Vellamarch still don't entirely agree about how that happened. The guildmaster says the charter spoke. Lior says people speak, and charters only repeat them. I think they're both right, which is a thing I've learned watching adults argue: usually both sides are holding different halves of the same true thing.

My indenture reads: *Apprenticed in perpetuity of craft to Journeywoman Lior Winterbourne, of the Observatory Workshop, Vellamarch, until such time as she shall judge the said apprentice fit.* There's a clause underneath in smaller script that the guild clerk added without being asked, because the clerk was there the night of the hearing, behind his desk, and heard everything:

*Said apprentice formerly registered under name unknown; lawful identity established by instrument of the Founder's will, certified under guild authority.*

Formerly registered under name unknown. That's what the ledger called me for six weeks of my life. Six weeks in the parish workhouse under a false name some Aldery functionary invented — *Marta Fenwick*, I think it was, though I've made myself forget it properly, the way you file away a tool you'll never need again. Six weeks of straw and gruel and the grate, and pressing a stolen cog against the bars so that Lior, coming down the corridor with her jaw set like a machined edge, would know: *I know who I am. Do you?*

She knew. She always knew. That was never the question. The question was whether anyone else in the world could be made to know it too, and whether knowing it would cost more than either of us had.

It cost the workshop. Everyone knows that story too, or thinks they do. Lior gave up her claim to Master Corvus's old shop on Tanner's Row — the shop she'd spent ten years protecting from the creditors, the whole reason she took the observatory commission in the first place — because the verdict put the workshop into the estate, into my inheritance, and my inheritance needed its value intact more than Lior needed her walls. She walked out of that building carrying two tool chests and a jar of turpentine, and she didn't look back, and she set up here at the observatory, where the commission became permanent and the rent was paid in the only currency the estate had, which was trust.

People say she sacrificed it for me. Lior hates it when people say that. She has a way of looking at you over her loupe when you say sentimental things near her benches, a look like a dial reading zero.

"The workshop was already gone," she told me once, when I was thirteen and full of guilt about it, because children absorb the debts of their guardians the way brass absorbs fingerprints. "The day your grandfather died and Aldery started buying paper against it, it was gone. All those years I kept the creditors circling, I wasn't saving anything. I was postponing arithmetic. What I actually saved—" and she stopped, and cleaned a pivot that didn't need cleaning, and finished the sentence in the direction of the clock instead of at me. "What I actually saved was standing in my doorway in the snow, eating my supper."

So. Here is what I know about how stories get told wrong: everyone in Vellamarch tells the story of the ice break and the hearing and the clock striking for the first time in ten years as if it were a story about justice falling out of the sky. And it was justice. But it was also fourteen hours of filing, and a ferryman's boy paid in coin and respect, and a widow who came expecting blame and found her husband's bench kept exactly as he'd left it, dust and all, and wept into her own two hands. Justice isn't weather. It's clockwork. Somebody builds it, somebody winds it, somebody stays up all night listening to make sure it doesn't stop.

Which is why I am writing this entry, and why it matters that I write it properly.

---

Today Lior handed me the new repair log.

It isn't much to look at — a plain quarto volume, marbled boards gone soft at the corners, bought secondhand from the stationer along with three others because Lior buys logs the way other people buy candles, against future need. But the first page is ruled and headed in her square, unhurried hand:

*Observatory Master Clock — Repair & Maintenance Register.*
*Instrument commissioned 1789. Rebuilt 1847. Named by its maker: OSCAR.*
*All entries to be dated, signed, and truthful. An untrue log is worse than no log; it is a lie with good handwriting.*

That last line is pure Lior. She has a hundred of them, aphorisms she dispenses while working, never stopping the work to deliver them, as if wisdom were a byproduct like filings. *Measure twice, apologize never.* *A mechanism forgives nothing but remembers everything.* *If you can't see what a part is for, you haven't understood the machine yet — including when the machine is a town.* I used to collect them secretly in a little notebook of my own. I stopped collecting them around the time I realized I'd started saying them myself, to apprentices' tasks, to the delivery boys, to the mirror. Inheritance works like that. Quietly, through the hands, before it ever reaches the head.

"You'll make the first entry," Lior said, and put the log in my hands, and stood there waiting, which is her way of teaching: she hands you the live thing and then refuses to rescue you from it.

"First entry of the register," I said. "Shouldn't it be yours? You rebuilt it. You found the will inside it. You—"

"I wound it," she said. "You'll maintain it. That's a different authorship. Besides—" and here she almost smiled, that dry flicker she gets, "—the founder wrote the first chapter of this machine's history, my master wrote the second whether he meant to or not, I wrote the third. Someone has to write the fourth, and I am not going to live forever, whatever the guild thinks. Start as you mean to continue. Dated. Signed. Truthful."

Then she went back to her bench and turned to the escapement she's been cutting for the tower clock at the church, and left me alone with the log and the great slow heartbeat of the observatory filling the whole stairwell, tick and tock and the deep pendulum breath beneath them, the sound I have slept inside since I was six years old and carried through a workhouse wall pressed against iron bars.

I sat down at the student desk by the north window — my desk, worn shiny where my elbows go — and I dipped the pen, and I thought about what *truthful* means in a repair log, which is not the same as what it means in testimony. In testimony, truth is what happened. In a log, truth is what you did, what you found, what you changed, and — the hard part, the part apprentices always fail — what you don't yet know. A log that hides uncertainty is lying by confidence. Lior's entries from the bad years prove it. I've read them all now, every surviving page: *Escapement replaced, cause of prior failure undetermined. Suspect interference. Cannot prove.* Even in the worst season of her life, with men watching the doors and her future burning down to a stub, she wrote *cannot prove* in her neat hand rather than inventing a tidy lie. That is the whole religion, right there, in one line of a maintenance record.

So here is my entry. I wrote it twice — the first attempt I tore out, because I had written it pretty instead of true, all flourishes and feeling, and Lior passed behind me without stopping and said, "Logs aren't love letters," and kept walking.

---

**Observatory Master Clock — Repair & Maintenance Register**

**Entry the First.**

*Date:* the twelfth of April, 1854. Fair, light southerly wind, river high with meltwater but behaving itself.

*Maintainer:* [my signature]. Apprentice. Third year of indenture.

*Work performed:* Quarterly service complete. Case dusted; movement dusted with bellows and soft brush, per standard. Escapement inspected — wear within tolerance, entry filed for attention next service. Pendulum rod checked for seasonal drift; rating nut advanced one-eighth turn; rate observed over four hours against the transit, error reduced to under two seconds daily, gaining. Weight lines inspected, no fray. Chime train oiled with the good oil, sparingly, as taught.

*Notes:* Struck eight correctly at noon. Chime hammers voiced well; third hammer slightly dull on the strike, adjusted, improved, not perfect — will listen again tomorrow and again after. Cause of slight gain undetermined. Suspect temperature. Will observe.

*Condition:* Keeps honest time.

---

I showed it to Lior without being asked, because the log belongs to the workshop and not to me, and rules are rules even — especially — when you're the one who benefits from them. She read it standing, holding it closer to the window than she needs to these days; her eyes are forty-one years old and have been looking at small brass things under magnification since she was seven, and she'll deny needing spectacles until the spectacles climb onto her face themselves. She read it twice. Then she read the notes section a third time, and tapped it once with her finger.

"*Undetermined. Suspect temperature. Will observe.*"

"Is that wrong?"

"It's the best part," she said. "Everything else is competent. That's honest." She closed the log and handed it back, and looked at me the way she looks at a movement she's just closed up and isn't quite ready to trust. "Two seconds gaining. When you can hold it under one for a month, you'll re-rate it yourself and sign that too. And Marta—"

My name. My real one. The founder's name, my mother's name, mine: three names braided into one, the way a rope is three threads pretending to be strong enough alone.

"—when you sign the next entry, sign it the same size. Not smaller than mine, not apologetic. Your name earned its space on that line. Spend it."

---

Tonight the observatory is quiet in the particular way it gets in spring, when the river has finished shouting and the swallows are arguing in the belfry louvers and the town below has banked its fires and gone to bed trusting us — actually trusting us, in the specific municipal way of a town whose church bells, whose harbor signals, whose assizes calendar, whose funerals and weddings and ferry timetables all quietly depend on one pendulum swinging above their roofs. They mostly forget we exist. That's fine. That's what a clock is *for*: to be reliable enough to be forgotten. Lior says fame is what happens to instruments that fail.

But I don't forget. Not tonight, not any night.

I sit sometimes — I'm sitting now, at my desk, with the lamp turned low and the pen drying — and I listen to Oscar talk, because that's what the founder did, apparently, for forty years: he talked to his clock, complained to it, confided in it, and somewhere in all that confiding he decided that if his family failed him — and his family did fail him, or he failed them, the hearing papers never fully settled which — then his machine would hold his last word safe until someone honest enough came to open the case. He hid a sealed will inside a chime mechanism. He built a dead man's testimony into a machine and set it ticking toward a decade of silence and a girl he would never meet.

He never met me. I try to imagine him sometimes, from the portraits in the guildhall, from the handwriting in the register — a stiff collar, a patient mouth, eyes that look like they got tired of being disappointed and decided to be exact instead. I have his chin. Lior says so, and the guildmaster confirmed it gravely at the hearing, comparing my face to the portrait with the air of a man verifying a hallmark. I have his patience, too, or I'm learning it; Lior taught me clockwork patience on long winter evenings when I was small, making me watch a gear train for a full hour before letting me touch it, until watching stopped being waiting and became understanding. That was the trade, really, those years: she taught me to wait, and I taught her to say things plainly. She still catches herself hedging — "possibly," "it may be," "one supposes" — and I give her the look now, the dial-at-zero look, her own look reflected back, and she stops, and says the plain thing. We fixed each other in ways neither of us planned. Most real repairs are like that. You go in for the escapement and discover the whole train needed rebuilding.

Somewhere across town, in the shop on Tanner's Row — my shop, technically, held in trust, rented out now to a cooper who keeps it clean and pays on time — my grandfather's daughter's daughter's future sits behind a counter selling barrel hoops. Someday I may take it back. Someday I may sell it and endow something. The estate papers sit in the guildhall strongroom beside the ledger that proved a good man was paid for decades to stay silent, and I have read that ledger, all of it, more than once, because Lior insists I understand that the people who save us are also the people who failed us, and that loving them anyway — precisely, with clear eyes — is the whole difficult art of being a person. She forgave her master without absolving him. She taught me the difference. Forgiveness says *I release you*. Absolution says *it didn't happen*. Only one of those is honest, and honesty is the entire trade.

Below me the town sleeps. Above me the weights descend, millimeter by millimeter, spending the gravity we wound into them yesterday, converting our attention into the town's tomorrow. On Thursday we wind again. On Sunday I check the chime hammers. Next quarter, my entry, signed the same size.

And in a year or three, when my hand is steady enough and my eye is trained enough, I will do the thing I have already asked Lior's leave to do, and received a nod so small you'd miss it if you didn't know her: I will cut a small plate of brass, engrave it myself in letters deep enough to outlast weather —

*OSCAR — named by his maker, 1789.
Repaired by an honest apprentice, 1847.
Kept by his family, ever since.*

— and fix it inside the case, on the inner beam, next to three half-worn letters and two sets of initials, where no visitor will ever see it, and where it will not matter in the slightest that no one sees it. Because the point was never the plaque. The point is what Lior said on my first night here, when I was six and freezing and she wrapped me in her own coat without hesitating, the first unguarded kindness anyone had shown me in a week: that a clock doesn't keep time for glory. It keeps time because keeping time is what it is. Everything else — the names, the registers, the hearings, the histories — is just the striking of the hour, marking what was already true between the ticks.

The lamp is low. The river is quiet. The pendulum swings the way it has swung through ten silent years and six loud ones, through ice and fire-sale and forgiveness, through a false name and a true one, through every silence that was safety and every silence that was complicity, arriving — tick, tock, tick, tock — at the only place a faithful mechanism ever arrives:

Now. And now. And now.

I dip the pen. Tomorrow's entry will begin the same way today's began, the way every entry in this book will begin until some other child, generations from now, climbs a ladder no one remembers the purpose of and finds our initials in the beam and asks the question that starts everything again:

*Dated. Signed. Truthful.*

*Keeps honest time.*
