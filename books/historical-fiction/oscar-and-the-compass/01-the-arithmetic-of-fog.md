## 1. The Arithmetic of Fog

The fog came in off the Atlantic the way debt comes to a careful household — slowly, then all at once, and never on a day you had budgeted for it.

Zephyrine Pemberton stood at the gallery rail of Gullwrack Point lighthouse and watched it swallow the horizon in long grey mouthfuls. By her arithmetic, she had perhaps forty minutes before the light became a matter of life rather than ritual. Forty minutes was enough. Forty minutes was always enough, if you spent them the way she spent everything: deliberately, in order, without waste.

She had learned to count her days in tasks. This was not a philosophy she had chosen so much as one that had chosen her, the way weather chooses a coastline. There were ninety-one steps from the cottage door to the lamp room, and she knew each one the way a mother knows the bones beneath a sleeping child's skin — which step complained in wet weather (the seventh, its iron lip worn concave by twenty years of her boots), which one iced first (the thirty-fourth, where the spray came round the tower's shoulder), which one she allowed herself to look up from (the fiftieth, because at that height the sea showed itself whole for a moment before the rail cut it into pieces again).

Ninety-one steps down. The wick trimmed by four o'clock, before the light failed. The lens wiped with chamois, clockwise, always clockwise, because counter-clockwise left streaks that caught the sun at dawn and made false flashes — and a false flash, on this coast, could bring a ship's master to trust the wrong thing at the wrong moment. She had a horror of teaching anyone to trust the wrong thing.

The oil reservoirs filled and gauged. The clockwork that turned the lens wound to its full fall — eleven hundred pounds of descending weight, patient as a confession. The log opened. And then the waiting, which was not empty but full, full as a hold with ballast, and she sat in the watch room with the log book squared before her and wrote the first entry of the storm season in her small, upright, unremarkable hand:

*October 3rd. Wind SW, freshening. Barometer 29.4 and falling. Fog bank sighted 14:20, moving east. All apparatus in good order.*

There. That was true, complete, and revealed nothing. She had been writing entries like that for twenty years, and if you read all six thousand of them back to back you would learn everything about the light and nothing whatsoever about the woman who kept it. She considered this a kind of masterpiece. Other women painted; other women embroidered; Zephyrine Pemberton had made of herself a document so clean that no historian could ever find a seam in it.

The fog reached the point itself at ten minutes to three. It did not roll in so much as arrive, the way bad news arrives, already inside the house before you hear the knock. One moment she could see the black teeth of the reef half a mile out; the next, the world ended at the end of the gallery rail, and beyond it there was only the colour of unwashed wool, beaded with droplets that gathered and ran like slow glass.

She went inside and lit the lamp.

* * *

There is an order to lighting a lighthouse, and Zephyrine loved the order the way some women loved hymns — not for what it said about God, but because it asked nothing of her except precision. Match to wick. Wick to flame. Flame given its two minutes to take hold while she stood with her hands clasped behind her back like a schoolgirl at assembly, watching the blue tongue of fire find its appetite. Then the mantle, then the pressure, then the great white bloom as the light stood up inside its own heat and began, degree by degree, to turn.

The lens turned above her. Below her, the fog lay against the rock like something listening.

"Evening," she said to it. She spoke aloud only here, in the lamp room, where there was nothing to hear her but machinery. "You're early."

The fog did not answer. Nothing answered. That was the arrangement, and she had signed it willingly enough once upon a time, though the signature — like so much else — belonged to a woman whose name she no longer used.

She used the name Pemberton now. She had used it for nineteen years, since the registrar in a rain-blurred town south of here had inked it onto a marriage certificate between her own cold hand and that of a decent, tired man named Edmund Pemberton, who had wanted a wife who would not ask him questions, and had found one. Edmund had been dead seven years come spring. He had died as he lived, quietly, in his sleep, having said almost nothing worth keeping and nothing at all worth regretting, and she had buried him beside the kirk wall with genuine gratitude, which she supposed was its own kind of love, or near enough to pass.

Before Pemberton there had been another name. She did not say it. She did not write it. She kept it the way one keeps a key to a house that has burned down: knowing exactly where it is, touching it never.

And before even that — before the name, before the marriage, before the silence — there had been Oscar.

Her brother. Who had marched away in 1914 with a compass in his pocket and a joke on his lips, who had written her letters full of terrible cheerfulness from Egypt and then Gallipoli, and whose death notice had arrived in the spring of 1915 in an envelope so thin it seemed the War Office had weighed the grief beforehand and decided how much of it she could carry. *Killed in action. The Lords Commissioners of the Admiralty express their sympathy.* Not even the Army — she remembered noticing that, some clerk's error, the Navy mourning a soldier. As if they couldn't keep their dead straight. As if there were too many of them to keep straight.

She had received the letter on a Tuesday. On the Wednesday she had climbed the ninety-one steps and lit the lamp and written *all apparatus in good order*, and that was how she had mourned him: in perfect entries, in a trimmed wick, in the clockwork turning above her head through the longest spring of her life.

She did not think about Oscar. This required discipline, and she had built the discipline herself, stone by stone, the way her father had built drystone walls on the farm of her childhood — course by course, choosing each stone for its fit, laying them so that wind could pass through the gaps but nothing larger. Twenty years of small silences were laid up in her like masonry. A marriage entered into for shelter. Letters written and never sent, burned one by one in the cottage stove until she stopped writing them at all. A name set down like a heavy load at the side of a road, walked away from, not looked back at. None of it had been dramatic. That was the trick of it. Ruin, when done properly, looks exactly like routine.

The barometer fell another tenth while she watched. She noted it.

* * *

At dusk the fog thickened from wool to water. It came into the lamp room itself, ghosting across the glazing, and the great beam swung round and round and round, cutting lanes in the whiteness that closed again behind it like water closing over a dropped coin. Somewhere out under the reef a gull cried once and was erased.

Zephyrine ate her supper in the watch room: bread, cheese, an apple going soft, tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in. She ate the way she did everything, in order, and afterwards she washed the plate and cup and dried them and set them in their places, and the doing of these things took forty minutes, and those forty minutes were not emptiness. They were the day's fourth quarter, accounted for.

People in the village — the few who thought about her at all — called her steady. Mrs. Calloway at the post office called her *that poor quiet soul*, in the tone reserved for widows and simpletons, and sent her parcels of shortbread she didn't want, addressed with a kindness Zephyrine accepted because refusing it would have required conversation. They thought her solitude was a misfortune that had happened to her. They did not know it was a structure she had raised, beam by beam, and that she inspected its joints nightly the way she inspected the lamp.

Loneliness, properly maintained, resembles peace the way a well-kept grave resembles a garden. From a distance, you cannot tell the difference. Up close, you can — but Zephyrine made sure no one came close.

Except once a month, when the stores boat came, and young Tam Bremner rowed out with flour and paraffin and the fortnight's post, and she stood on the shingle and exchanged with him perhaps eleven words, counted and rationed: *Thank you. The wind's rising. Mind the tide.* Tam, who was seventeen and thought himself a sailor, believed her taciturn. He told the others in the village that the lighthouse wife — keeper now, but old habits — was strange, touched by the sea, best left alone. This suited her perfectly. Reputation was a wall, and walls were load-bearing.

Tonight the stores boat would not come. Tonight the fog had the whole coast by the throat, and the steamers would be feeling their way blind past the headlands, and somewhere out in that grey blindness the masters of those ships would see her light sweep past every fifteen seconds — fifteen seconds of truth in a world of lies — and correct their course, and never know her name, and that was right. That was the whole architecture of her life. To be the fixed point. To be trusted absolutely and known by no one.

She checked the clockwork. Two hours' fall remaining. She rewound it, taking the weight of the crank against her hip, and the mechanism above her resumed its patient turning, and she felt the small satisfaction she felt every night of her adult life: the satisfaction of a system running true.

Then she went down to the cottage, took up her pen, and wrote the second entry of the season:

*October 3rd, continued. Fog dense. Visibility nil. Light revolving regular. Wind backing SSW. Barometer 29.1.*

She looked at the line for a long moment. Then, below it, in the same small upright hand, she wrote the sentence she wrote at the bottom of every page, the one no inspector had ever questioned because it looked like piety and was in fact a lock:

*I am alone, and all is well.*

It was true. That was the thing she clung to on nights like this, when the fog pressed at the windows and the sea made its low grinding argument against the rock below. It *was* true. She was alone, and all was well, and if there were hours — three o'clock hours, mostly, the hour when the body's fires burn lowest — when the words rang slightly hollow, like a bell cracked somewhere inside where no one could see it — well. A bell can be cracked and still tell the hour. She had made her peace with being a cracked bell. It was more than most people managed.

* * *

She slept in fragments, as she always did in storm season, waking at intervals to climb and check. At midnight the fog still held. At two the wind backed and freshened, hauling the fog seaward in rags, and by three there was a hard clear dark overhead with stars in it, and the sea had begun to make its intentions known.

By four the intentions were unmistakable. The swell came in long and deep, the kind of swell that is born a thousand miles away and travels all that distance to deliver a single message: *weather coming*. The barometer had fallen to 28.9 and was still dropping, the needle trembling like something frightened. Zephyrine stood in the watch room with her hands flat on the log book and watched the glass, and felt in her chest that particular sensation she had never found a better word for than *attention* — total, animal attention, the mind narrowing to a point.

A gale. A proper one, by the feel of it, the first real blow of the season, arriving ahead of its forecast like an uninvited relative.

She worked through the small hours with the economy of long practice. Lamp pressure checked twice. Spare mantles within reach. The shutters of the lantern tested. Log updated each hour in her upright hand: *Wind SSW, increasing. Sea very rough. Barometer falling steadily.* Each entry true. Each entry sealed. Each entry a brick in the wall.

Toward dawn the gale struck in earnest. It came off the sea in long flat hammerblows that leaned the tower and made the lamp flame gutter despite its glass, and the rain came sideways, and the reef disappeared entirely in the smoking white. Zephyrine stood at the gallery rail with the wind trying to peel her from the rock, hair whipping loose from its coil, coat snapping, and she looked out at the chaos and felt — as she always felt, and always refused to examine — the closest thing to happiness her life permitted. Because in a gale, there was no time for the cracked bell. In a gale there was only the work, and the work was honest, and the work asked nothing of her past.

The light turned. The ships, God help them, would be out there in it.

At first light, blown grey and ragged, the gale still blowing itself hoarse, she went down the ninety-one steps and along the shingle path to check the moorings of the stores boat — hers to look after in winter — and it was there, walking the tideline with the wind at her back pushing her forward like a hand between the shoulders, that she saw what the sea had brought up in the night.

At first she took it for driftwood. Then for a seal, dead in the surf line. Then the shape resolved, and she stopped walking, and the wind filled her ears, and for a long moment she simply stood and looked at the man lying face-down on the shingle among the wrack and bladder-weed, one arm flung forward, the other bent beneath him, clutching against his chest — even insensible, even drowned nearly to death, clutching — an oilskin packet tied with tarred cord.

The tide was coming in. She calculated it automatically, the way she calculated everything: forty minutes, perhaps less, before the water reached him.

Forty minutes was enough. Forty minutes was always enough, if you spent them the way she spent everything.

But standing there above the stranger, with the gale shouting and the packet dark against his ribs like something he'd stolen from a grave, Zephyrine Pemberton understood — in the wordless, certain way she understood falling barometers — that the arithmetic of her days had just changed forever.
