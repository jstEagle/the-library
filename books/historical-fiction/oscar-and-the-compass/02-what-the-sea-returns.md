## 2. What the Sea Returns

The gale began at dusk on the third of November and did not break its grip until nearly dawn.

Zephyrine Pemberton kept the watch as she always kept it: in small, exact motions that left no room for anything larger than the task at hand. She trimmed the wick of the great lamp to a hair's breadth, checked the mercury bath beneath the rotating carriage, wound the clockwork that turned the lens through its patient revolution — one flash every ten seconds, white over the shoals of Gullwrack, red where the Kestrel Rock lay waiting for careless hulls. The wind came off the Atlantic in long black fists and struck the tower so hard the glass hummed, but the lamp burned steady, because she had made it steady, because steadiness was the only prayer she had left.

She kept the log in a copperplate hand she had taught herself years ago, letter by letter, until her own handwriting had disappeared entirely into someone else's. *Wind SW by S, force 10, rising. Sea mountainous. Lamp satisfactory.* There was no line in any logbook for what she actually felt, so she did not look for one.

At two in the morning the light on the Kestrel buoy went dark. She watched the place where it should have been — a red eye swallowed by the chaos of spray — and calculated without being asked: if any vessel ran the channel tonight believing the rock marked, it would be arithmetic, not malice, that killed them. She logged the failure at 02:14. She could do nothing else. That was the whole of her authority in the world: to record things accurately while they destroyed themselves.

By five the wind had begun to spend itself, coming now in exhausted gusts rather than sustained fury, and the sky to the east showed the bruised grey that passes, on that coast, for mercy. Zephyrine climbed down from the lantern room with her joints complaining and put the kettle on the cottage stove, and it was while she stood waiting for water — hands wrapped around nothing, staring at the black window — that she heard the sea give something back.

It was not a sound, exactly. It was a change in the sound. The gale's retreat uncovered the shingle beach below the point, and the waves going out over the stones made a rattle and hiss like a great drawer closing slowly, and somewhere inside that noise was a smaller noise, a knock, irregular, wrong. Wood against stone. Or something like wood.

She told herself it was flotsam. A hatch cover, a spar, part of some poor decked boat whose crew were already past anyone's help. She told herself this while she pulled on her oilskins and took the storm lantern down from its hook, and she heard herself telling it, and knew it for the lie it was, because after four years alone on Gullwrack Point she knew every sound the beach made the way other women knew their children's voices, and this was not flotsam. Flotsam does not knock.

* * *

The shingle after a gale is a country under new management. Everything familiar has been rearranged; weed lies in ropes across the stones, timber stands on end where wood has no business standing, and the air smells of torn kelp and drowned things. Zephyrine walked it with the lantern held low, its light swallowed by the wet morning fog, following the knocking sound along the tideline.

She found him forty yards below the high-water mark, wedged in the lee of a fallen groyne like a thing the sea had tried twice to take and failed.

He was on his side, knees drawn up, one arm folded across his chest — and it was the arm that was making the knocking, or rather the bundle in the arm, an oilskin packet lashed to him with a length of codline knotted with the desperate competence of a man who had tied it in water. His lips were the colour of the fog. His breathing came in shallow, rattling pulls, and each pull moved the packet with it, up and down, up and down, so that he seemed less a man than a bellows devoted to a single purpose.

Zephyrine crouched beside him. She put two fingers to his throat and found a pulse — fast, thin, but there, stubborn as a wick in wind. He was perhaps thirty. Salt crusted his dark hair flat to his skull. His hands were dockworker's hands, rope-burned, split at the knuckles, and around his neck on a cord hung a small tin identity disc, which she did not read, because reading it would make him real, and real men required decisions.

The sensible thing — the thing the keeper of Gullwrack Point, twenty years practised in the discipline of not-being-involved, would have done — was to go back to the cottage and signal ashore. Hoist the distress flag. Let the lifeboat men from Porthaven earn their keep. A body on the beach was a matter for the authorities, and the authorities were precisely what she had arranged her entire existence to avoid.

But the tide was turning. She could see it in the weed-line already creeping back toward his boots, and the lifeboat would take two hours to launch in these seas even if the coastguard saw her flag, which they might not, in fog, from nine miles off.

"Damn you," she said — to the man, to the sea, to the morning generally, in the flat voice she used for the logbook — and got her arms under him.

He was lighter than he looked and heavier than she wanted. Getting him up the shingle took everything she had and cost her a wrenched shoulder and a boot full of freezing water, and twice she nearly lost them both to a retreating wave that gripped his ankles like intent. At the top, in the lee of the groyne, she stopped, gasping, and looked down at the packet still bound to his chest, the codline biting through the oilskin where he had clutched it through whatever voyage had broken him.

Whatever it was, he had chosen it over swimming freely. Men who drown clutching cargo are not carrying mail.

* * *

She got him to the cottage by increments, dragging him on an old tarpaulin across the rough ground, stopping when her breath gave out, starting again. In the kitchen she stripped his soaked clothes — clinically, without looking at more than she had to, the way she'd tended her husband's last illness — and put him in her own bed under every blanket she owned, having first dragged the bed close to the stove. He did not wake. His skin burned cold and then, by afternoon, burned hot instead, and she sat through the long hours doing the only nursing she knew: warmth, water pushed between cracked lips a teaspoon at a time, and watching.

Watching, because feverish men say things, and she had learned long ago that information is the one currency never devalued.

He said nothing useful. He said *hold the line*, twice, in a voice that belonged to some other place. He said *don't let it get wet* — which was interesting, said by a man the sea had done its worst to — and once, very clearly, near midnight, he said a name that stopped Zephyrine's spoon above the cup.

Not his own name. Not hers.

He said: "Oscar."

Just the one word, round and complete, the way one says a name one has been given instructions about. Then he turned his face to the wall and slept like the dead, which was a phrase Zephyrine had cause to hate.

She sat very still in the kitchen chair with the spoon dripping back into the cup, and the stove ticked, and the lamp overhead turned its ten-second circuit above them both, and she made herself breathe.

Four years. Four years since the telegram — *deeply regret to inform you* — since the printed form and the printed sympathy and the little printed medal that came later in a box lined with cotton wool, since she had buried Oscar Pemberton in the only grave that existed for him, which was a sentence in a War Office letter and a space in her heart shaped exactly like a boy who used to draw compass roses on everything, on letters, on scraps of paper, on the frosted window of their mother's parlour, north always slightly wrong because he insisted true north was a matter of opinion.

Dead at Gallipoli. Officially. With witnesses. With paperwork. The Empire was very good at paperwork.

Fever makes strangers talkative; it does not perform resurrections. Some other Oscar, then. It was a common enough name. The world was full of Oscars, and half of them were being called for by nurses in fever wards at this very moment. She poured the cold tea away and told herself this with the same fluency she had once told herself other necessary things — *the marriage will be safer for us both, the name doesn't matter, the letters are better unsent* — and found, as she always found, that the words worked perfectly and changed nothing.

In the morning she would deal with his effects properly, as procedure demanded. Inventory. Log. That much was due to the law, and law was a language she still spoke, however rustily.

She did not sleep. She sat by the stove and listened to a stranger breathe her brother's name.

* * *

The effects, laid out on the scrubbed kitchen table at dawn, were these:

One identity disc, tin, corroded, bearing a number and the initials T.W. — Thomas Wren, though she didn't know that yet, and set it aside face-down. One knife, one lucifer box gone soft with seawater, three coins, a photograph too damaged by salt to show anything but a blur that might have been a woman or a window. And the packet.

The oilskin was good quality, naval issue by the look of it, and the codline had been cut rather than untied — cut recently, by the man himself, probably on the beach, probably with numb fingers, which explained the ragged work. Inside the oilskin were three layers: a canvas wrap, waxed linen, and then, dry as the day they were sealed, papers.

Letters. A dozen or more, bundled and ordered, each folded square and addressed in ink on the outer fold — not to persons but to dates. *September 1915. October 1915.* Like chapters. Like entries.

And the handwriting —

Zephyrine had steeled herself for many things in twenty years of careful living. She had rehearsed fire drills and shipwreck protocols and the correct conduct upon finding bodies, and she had thought, somewhere below the level of rehearsal, that she had also rehearsed grief, spent it, closed that account with the same thoroughness she brought to everything.

She had not rehearsed this.

The hand on the topmost letter was her brother's. Not similar. Not reminiscent. It was his — the slight forward lean of the letters, the way the *r*s kicked out like small boots, the crossbar of the *t* extending leftward over the neighbouring letter because Oscar wrote fast and leaned into his own words. She had received perhaps two hundred letters in that hand before the war ate the sender, had read them by lamplight in another life, in another name, and she knew it the way she knew the sound of the fog signal: absolutely, involuntarily, in the bones.

Her legs went first. She caught the table's edge and lowered herself onto the bench without deciding to, the letter still in her fingers, and the kitchen went strange and bright-edged around her, the stove roaring softly, the fog pressing white against the window, the stranger's wet clothes steaming faintly on the rack by the fire as though the room itself were exhaling.

*Deeply regret to inform you,* said four years ago, in a printed hand, in a printed voice. And here was the unprinted answer, washed up on her own beach, tied to a drowning man with a knot Oscar himself might have taught the tying of — because he had been the one who showed her, when they were children on the quay at Cork, how to make a bowline that would never slip, saying *remember it, Zeph, a good knot holds when everything else lets go.*

She opened the letter. Her hands were steady — twenty years of trim and log and routine lived in those hands, and they did their work regardless of her — and the first lines swam and then resolved.

*If you are reading this, then the packet reached you, which means Wren got through, which means I owe a debt I cannot repay to a man I chose at random for his honesty and his desperation in roughly equal parts.*

Dated September 1915. Gallipoli. Written by a dead man, addressed to whoever held it, and beginning — she read it again, and a third time — with the calm of a man setting down testimony he expects to be killed for.

There was more. Underneath, the bundle went deep: accounts, dates, names of ships and officers, descriptions written in that leaning, urgent hand of things Oscar Pemberton had seen with his own eyes and recorded with a clerk's precision and a witness's fury — a village, she glimpsed, a village and guns firing on it and orders given, and she stopped reading because her vision had narrowed to a hot point and the kitchen was tilting.

She set the papers down. She pressed both palms flat on the scrubbed wood and breathed, the way she had breathed through the burial-that-wasn't, the way she had breathed through every silence of twenty years, and it did not work, none of it worked, the old discipline sliding off this like oilskin off a drowning man.

Because the letters were real. The handwriting was real. Which meant the telegram was a lie, or the letters were a forgery so perfect it constituted madness — and either way, the world she had built her twenty years of quiet upon had just taken a shell below the waterline and was filling, slowly, with cold dark sea.

On the bed, the stranger stirred and muttered, and the name he said was Oscar's again, softer this time, almost conversational, as though reporting in.

Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment. Fever-bright, hollow-cheeked, a hired hand — *a man I chose at random for his honesty and his desperation* — carrying a dead brother's testimony through a war's worth of ocean to a shore he had never aimed for.

She rose, took the kettle from the hob, and began, mechanically, to prepare the broth. It was what her hands knew. And while they worked she stared at the bundle on the table, at the topmost page where her brother's ink leaned forward off the line like a man walking into weather, and she understood, with the terrible clarity of the fully awake, that the sea had not returned flotsam to Gullwrack Point.

The sea had returned her brother — or the truth of him, which amounted to the same thing and was infinitely more dangerous — and it had delivered the two of them, the courier and the keeper, into the same small kitchen, together, alive, at exactly the moment in history when certain men in London were preparing to write the official version of everything.

The stranger's eyes opened.

They found her across the steam and the lamplight — grey eyes, fever-shot, sharp as gulls — and he looked at her the way a man looks when he wakes among strangers holding his life's one possession, and his hand went weakly to his chest, searching for the packet.

"It's here," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out level. Twenty years of practice, spent in six words. "Nothing's touched it."

He stared at her. And then, before the fever pulled him back under, he whispered — hoarse, salt-ruined, urgent as a man passing a message through a closing door:

"You've the accent." His fingers plucked at the blanket. "Cork. You're from Cork." A breath, a flicker of something that was not fever — recognition, or dread, or both. "He said... if the woman at the light..."
