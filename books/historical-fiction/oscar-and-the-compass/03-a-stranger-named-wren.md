## 3. A Stranger Named Wren

He dreamed of water that had opinions.

It wanted him, and it said so in the grammar of cold — *you are mine, you were always mine, come down where the ledgers are kept* — and he argued with it, because arguing was the one trade he'd ever mastered. He told the water he owed a debt. He told it there was a packet, an oilskin packet, tied with tarred twine, and that debts like that did not drown. The water laughed in the way water laughs, which is to say it didn't, and it pulled him by the heels toward a green darkness full of counting.

Then hands. Hands on his collar, hauling him up shingle that rattled like crockery, and a voice saying something short and practical — not a prayer, he noted even then, not *God help us*, but *hold still* — and he thought: good. Practical people saved your life. Praying people only attended the ceremony.

He woke in firelight.

The room swam. Stone walls gone gold at the edges, a peat-and-coal smell, a kettle muttering on a stove like an old man agreeing with himself. He was stripped to the skin under rough blankets, and every joint in his body had been replaced with someone else's worse joints. His throat felt scoured. When he tried to move, the room moved first, tilting like a deck, and he lay back and breathed through his mouth and took inventory instead, because inventory was safe, inventory didn't sway.

*One.* He was alive, which was inconvenient and also, he supposed, preferable. *Two.* He was undressed by a stranger, which meant the stranger had gone through his clothes, which meant —

His hand shot out before his mind approved it, patting the blanket, the floor beside him, finding nothing but flagstone cold as a dead man's cheek. The packet was gone.

Panic arrived quietly, which was how the worst things always arrived. Not a flood but a single degree of heat, a small bright coal dropped into the middle of him. He'd carried that packet from a cellar in Boulogne through three checkpoints and a hold full of fish, and he had promised a dying man — no, not dying, *determined*, which was more frightening — that it would reach its address whole. He didn't know the address. That had been part of the arrangement: carry, don't read, don't ask, deliver. A courier who doesn't know anything is worth more alive than a courier who knows everything is worth dead. He had done the arithmetic himself, once, in another life, on the other side of that particular ledger, and he knew both columns by heart.

The door opened and the woman came in carrying a bucket, and he played dead so well he nearly convinced himself.

She crossed to the stove without looking at him — or looking at him without seeming to, which was worse, because he knew that trick, he'd invented half of it — and set the bucket down and fed the fire two lumps of coal, each placed rather than thrown. She moved like a person who had practiced being unremarkable until she'd achieved it. He catalogued her the way he catalogued everything now: mid-forties, maybe older, sea-kept hands, a face built for weather and arranged against being read. Lighthouse keeper's widow or lighthouse keeper, hard to say, and on this coast in this year, hard to say meant *careful*.

"You're awake," she said, to the fire.

"Am I," he managed. It came out as gravel poured through a sieve.

"You were for ten minutes last night. You asked if the boat had made Cork." She turned. Her eyes were gray and level and gave away precisely nothing, and he understood, with the sinking certainty of a man recognizing a colleague across enemy lines, that she was taking his inventory too. "I told you the boat was at the bottom of the sea and you wept about a parcel."

"I don't weep," he said.

"No," she agreed. "You argue with blankets."

He looked down. He had, apparently, been fighting the blanket in his sleep; it lay twisted off him like a surrender flag run up the wrong pole. Fever sweat had soaked through his shirt — her shirt, he realized, some man's shirt too big for her, smelling of camphor and salt — and he became suddenly, acutely aware of his own nakedness under the wool, of how completely a drowned man is at the mercy of whoever fishes him out.

"Where are my things?"

"Drying." A nod toward a chair by the window, where his jacket hung open like a gutted fish, pockets pulled out, seams sagging. "Your boots are full of the sea and will be for some time. Your papers are ruined except the ones I couldn't ruin, and those are in the tin box on the mantel, and I haven't read them."

"Why not?" The question left him before wisdom could catch it.

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of one professional acknowledging another's poor form.

"Because they aren't mine," she said. "And because a person who reads what isn't theirs starts believing they have a right to it. I've seen where that road goes." She picked up the bucket. "Fever's still in you. Sleep, or don't. There's broth when you want it. Don't get up to fetch it yourself; the floor has opinions and yours are weaker than its."

She left. The door closed with the soft click of a latch designed to be quiet, and he lay listening to her footsteps cross stone — measured, unhurried, the walk of someone who had decided years ago exactly how fast to move and had never once deviated — and he thought: *she went through my pockets and put everything back and lied about none of it, and either she is honest, which would be new, or she is very much better at this than I am.*

Both possibilities frightened him equally.

* * *

The fever broke on the second night, or perhaps the third — time had gone soft at the edges, days folding into one another like wet paper — and when it broke it left him wrung out and strangely clear, lying in the dark listening to the great slow heartbeat of the lamp above them turning.

He'd heard the light all through the illness. Every fourteen seconds or so a pale wedge swept the ceiling, slid across the beams, and vanished, and he had learned to count it the way a prisoner counts bars. In the worst hours the wedge had become a searchlight and he had become a man flat in a beet field outside Lens with his breath held and his heart going like a sewing machine, waiting for the beam to pass, *pass, please pass* — and it passed, every time, and he came back to the cottage ceiling and the peat smoke and the sound of the woman moving below, and he understood that he had been given a strange gift: four years of war and its aftermath, and here at last a place where the light that swept over him was only trying to save ships.

On the clear night he got up. His legs held, mostly. He dressed in his ruined clothes because a man feels less naked in ruined clothes than in borrowed ones, and went down the stairs on the balls of his feet out of pure habit, though the fourth step announced him anyway with a groan like a gull.

She was at the table with the logbook open and a pen in her hand, and she didn't startle. She simply finished the line she was writing — he watched her complete the figure, set the pen parallel to the page's edge, square the book — and then looked up.

"You should be horizontal."

"I should be in Cork," he said. "What day is it?"

"The ninth."

"Of—"

"November."

He sat down across from her without asking, and she let him, and neither of them pretended this wasn't a negotiation. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside the wind worried at the corner of the tower like a dog at a bone.

"Three days," he said. "I've cost you three days."

"You've cost yourself considerably more. You came ashore with lungs full of the Irish Sea and a fever of a hundred and four, and you spent the first night telling me about a man named Aubrey who owed you eleven francs."

"He does owe me eleven francs."

"I'm sure he does." She rose, ladled broth from a pot into a bowl, and set it in front of him with the economy of someone who resented nothing and wasted nothing, including gestures. "Eat. Then tell me your name, since you wouldn't give it to the fever and I doubt you'll give it to me."

He ate. It was fish broth with barley, salted properly, hot enough to hurt going down, and it was the best thing he had tasted since a café in Le Havre in the spring of '17, and he hated how much he meant that.

"Wren," he said.

"That's a bird."

"It's also a name. It was my mother's idea. She said wrens build anywhere and sing regardless, and that a boy with a name like that would never starve for lack of nerve." He shrugged. "She was wrong about the singing. Right about the building."

"And before the bird?"

He looked at her. Steam rose between them. Outside, the light swept its wedge across the black window, once, twice, patient as a metronome.

"Before the bird is a long story," he said, "with an unhappy ending."

"Most of them are." She folded her hands. "I'm not asking for the story. I'm asking whether the name before the bird is written anywhere I should worry about."

And there it was — not curiosity, not kindness dressed up as curiosity, but the flat practical question of a person assessing risk, the same question he would ask, the same question anyone in the trade would ask, and he felt something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was tight. Because it meant she knew there was a trade. It meant the last seventy-two hours of silence and broth and correctly-read fevers had been conducted between two people who both understood that names were ammunition and that you did not hand ammunition to strangers, even strangers whose broth could raise the dead.

"No," he said. "Nothing on me carries it. I made sure of that in Boulogne. What's written on me—" he nodded toward the tin box on the mantel "—isn't mine either."

"No. It's Oscar's."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

He kept his face still — he was good at that, had been paid to be good at that — but inside, everything tilted. Because the man in the cellar in Boulogne had said *take it to the coast, take it to Gullwrack Point, there's a keeper there who will know what to do*, and had refused to say more, and had pressed the oilskin into his hands with fingers that smelled of carbolic and iodine, and Wren had assumed the keeper was a man, had assumed a great many convenient things, and here was a widow alone on a rock with a dead brother's name coming out of her mouth like it was a coin she'd been keeping under her tongue for years.

"You know him," Wren said.

"I knew him."

"Dead?"

"Four years." She said it the way you'd report a tide. "Gallipoli. We had the notification. We had—" her voice did something very small, a hairline crack in poured concrete, gone before it finished happening "—a letter. Before."

Wren looked at the tin box. Tarred twine, he remembered. He'd retied it twice on the crossing, checking the knots the way other men checked their watches. Inside it: forty-some pages in a careful clerk's hand, pages he had sworn not to read, wrapped around a bundle of documents whose corners he could feel through the oilskin whenever he'd shifted it in the fish hold — the crisp edges of service papers, the particular weight of carbon copies, the flat metallic rattle of something small and metal he had decided not to think about.

A dead man's handwriting. He'd seen the envelope in Boulogne before it was sealed — addressed in a hand so steady it looked printed — and the courier who hired him, a sharp-faced little man with a cough, had said *the writer is dead, the writing isn't, that's the whole value of it.*

"Mrs.—" he began.

"Pemberton," she said, and gave him nothing else.

"Mrs. Pemberton. The man who engaged me — the one with the cough — he said the packet goes to a journalist in Cork. By the end of the month. He didn't say why, and I didn't ask, because asking is how couriers stop being couriers." He turned the bowl a quarter-turn on the table, a fidget he despised in others. "But I'll tell you what I can work out, because you've earned it. Three nights of broth buys a man the truth as far as I've got it."

She waited. She was very good at waiting. He suspected she'd had decades of practice.

"Whoever wrote those letters is dead on paper and alive in ink. Somebody went to considerable trouble to make sure of the first part, and somebody else went to considerable trouble to make sure of the second. The papers underneath the letters are official — I've handled enough government stationery to know the watermark when I feel it. And the man with the cough was frightened in a way I've only seen twice before, and both times it involved soldiers and both times it ended badly for the soldiers." He looked up. "Whatever your brother wrote down, someone with rank doesn't want it read. And the month ends in twenty-two days, and I am currently a hundred and eighty miles of bad coast away from the only address I have, with no boots, no money, and a face that half the fishing fleet between here and Cork has now seen."

Silence. The lamp swept its wedge. Nine, ten, eleven — he counted without meaning to.

"And you brought it here anyway," she said at last.

"I was told to bring it here. To a keeper at Gullwrack Point." He spread his hands. "I assumed a man. I assumed it would be simple. I have since revised both assumptions."

For the first time, something moved fully across her face — not warmth, nothing so spendable as warmth, but a kind of grim recognition, the look of a woman finding herself dealt into a game she'd walked twenty years into the wilderness to avoid.

"My brother was dead," she said slowly, "and now he may not be, and the proof of whatever he did is sitting in a biscuit tin on my mantel, and two governments are going to want it before the month is out." She stood, took the tin down, and set it on the table between them with a click that sounded final as a bolt. "I buried him once, Mr. Wren. I find I am not prepared to do it twice. So before I decide whether you're a blessing or a catastrophe — and you may well be both — you're going to tell me everything the man with the cough told you. Starting with why a dead man needed a living stranger."

Outside, the light turned above them, patient and blind, sweeping its fourteen-second mercy over a sea that had already tried once to keep them both.
