## 5. The Dead Man's Handwriting

The fever broke on the third morning, and Wren knew it by the smell of the room.

For two days the cottage had come to him in fragments — a ceiling of whitewashed beams crossed by a dark water stain shaped like a running dog; the tick and groan of a stove; a woman's footsteps crossing floorboards with a regularity that was almost military, except that soldiers never moved that way, soldiers moved like men who wanted to be somewhere else. Now the fever's wool was gone from his ears, and he could smell bread baking, salt, lamp oil, and beneath it all the cold mineral breath of the sea pressing against every seam of the house.

He lay still and took inventory, because that was what you did when you woke in an unfamiliar place: inventory first, movement second, speech last. Two hands — his own, chapped, the nails torn where the rocks had done their work. A body bruised along the left side but obedient. Boots, dried stiff as slate, set by the stove. And on the chair beside the bed —

His stomach dropped through the floorboards.

The oilskin packet sat on the chair, squared neatly, tied with its original cord. Beside it, folded once, lay a single letter, opened, its paper gone soft at the creases as though someone had held it a long time.

*She read one,* he thought. *Only one.*

He should have felt relief. He told himself he did. But there was another thing underneath it, a colder current, and he lay staring at the running-dog stain while he tried to name it. It was this: she had read one letter and then put the packet back and said nothing. Four days — no, three; he'd counted the meals — she had fed him broth and changed the bandage on his scalp and never asked him a single question about what he carried. Not *who are you*, not *where were you bound*, not *why does a dead man write to no one*. She simply went on trimming wicks and keeping her log, and the silence around the packet was so complete it had weight, like a stone on a table everyone walks around.

People who didn't ask questions were people who already knew the answers. Or people who were very good at not knowing them.

Wren had been both, at different times in his life, and he recognized the posture the way you recognize your own handwriting.

He got up slowly, dressed, and stood a moment with his hand on the door before opening it, listening. The sea said what the sea always says. Underneath it, from the tower, came the small mechanical heartbeat of the clockwork turning the lens, and beneath that — nothing. No voices. No second set of boots. Whatever this place was, it was nearly empty of everything except her.

* * *

He found her at the kitchen table, writing in the logbook, and he understood immediately that the logbook was not a record. It was a wall. She wrote with the flat, unhurried diligence of a woman building something brick by brick between herself and the world, and when she looked up at him her face did exactly nothing — no alarm, no welcome, just the brief acknowledgment of a lighthouse keeper noting weather.

"You shouldn't be up."

"I've been up for years," Wren said. "This is only the most recent occasion." His voice came out rusted. He sat down across from her without being invited, which was a test, and she let it pass, which told him more than any answer would have. "You read one of the letters."

Her pen stopped. It stopped for perhaps a second and a half, and then continued, finishing the line before she spoke. "I put it back in order."

"I know. I'm asking why only one."

"Because one was enough to tell me whose hand it was."

"And whose hand is it?"

Now she did look at him, fully, and he saw what the fever hadn't let him see before: eyes the grey of the winter channel, set in a face built for being forgotten — ordinary, lined, deliberately unremarkable, the kind of face you'd struggle to describe to a magistrate an hour after leaving the room. Except for the eyes. Those you'd remember.

"That," she said, "is the wrong question coming from you. You carried that packet across half of Ireland in a war zone. You were paid. I imagine you were told nothing, because men who know things can't be trusted to know nothing under pressure — and yet here you sit, four days later, asking me who wrote it. Which means either you're a fool, or you never looked."

"I looked at the outside. I didn't open them."

"Why not?"

"Because the man who hired me told me that opening one would be the last mistake I ever made, and he had the look of a man who keeps his promises." Wren turned the oilskin packet over in his hands, feeling its weight change nothing and everything. "But I'm not being paid anymore. The sea saw to my contract. And I find I want to know what nearly drowned me."

She watched him for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"There's bread on the sill," she said finally. "If you're going to read dead men's letters, do it fed. It's bad luck otherwise."

* * *

He ate standing at the window first, because his legs weren't sure of themselves yet, and then he carried the packet to the table and untied the cord with fingers that insisted on shaking, and he hated them for it.

There were eleven letters. Eleven envelopes, each addressed in the same hand — a strong, slanted, rather beautiful hand, the kind of hand that had been taught properly and then improved by use — each addressed to no one, or rather to a name that meant nothing: *Mr. Ambrose, care of the Harbour Office, Cork.* Each sealed with plain wax. Each dated, in the corner, in that same confident script, with dates running back through 1915 and into 1916.

Wren laid them out on the table in a row, oldest first, the way you'd lay out instruments before surgery, and the keeper watched him do it without expression, her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she showed no sign of drinking.

"The dates," she said quietly. "Look at the dates."

He looked. The earliest was April 1915. The latest was August of that year. He frowned. "That's —"

"Gallipoli," she said. "That's Gallipoli. Every date falls inside the campaign." Her voice was level, but he heard the effort holding it level, the way you hear a rope taking strain. "The man who wrote these has been listed among the dead for four years. Officially. There's a memorial stone with his name on it in a churchyard I could walk you to."

Wren picked up the earliest letter and broke the seal.

*Mr. Ambrose —*

*I am told by the man who arranges our post that you are the only address in Ireland worth trusting, and that you will know what to do with these even if I cannot say what that is. Forgive the cipher of my situation: I am writing to you from a place where I am supposed to have died, and everything I send you must therefore be true twice over — true in itself, and true to the fact of my survival, which must remain hidden until you decide otherwise.*

*I will be plain, because plainness is the only luxury left to me.*

*On the night of the ninth of May, orders came down for the shelling of Karayel village. I want you to hold that word — Karayel — because you will search every dispatch, every communiqué, every official history that is ever written of this campaign, and you will not find it. It will not appear. That is the purpose of what I do now.*

*Karayel was a fishing village on the coast north of our position. Twelve houses, a mosque, maybe sixty souls, none of them soldiers. Our guns had ranged on it twice that week for practice, and the village had done nothing whatsoever to deserve the honour, which is precisely why it was chosen — because a battery commander wished to demonstrate the effect of delayed-action fuses on masonry construction, and required a target that would produce results worth photographing. The photographs, Mr. Ambrose. Remember that there are photographs.*

*The order was countersigned by Major Hallowes and approved verbally by Colonel Vane-Smythe, who visited the battery that afternoon and remarked — I heard him, I was carrying his map case — that it was a pity the Turks had no villages worth sparing. The bombardment began at dusk and continued for forty minutes. I will not describe what the guns did to twelve houses. I will describe instead what I did afterward, which was to walk through the village with the burial detail and copy down, in my notebook, the number of the dead: fifty-one. Forty-one of them women, children, and old men. I copied also the names of every officer present, the battery positions, the fuse settings, and the time of the order, and I have enclosed a copy of my notebook page in this envelope, because notebooks can be confiscated, and mine has been threatened twice already.*

*I am officially dead as of the landing, you understand. My death is a convenience to certain gentlemen, arranged through channels I will describe in a future letter, and it buys me a freedom no living soldier possesses: I can write what I have seen without anyone thinking to stop me, because no one reads the correspondence of the dead. The dead are the perfect correspondents, Mr. Ambrose. We require no censorship.*

*What I ask of you is nothing yet. Only keep these. Keep them safe, keep them secret, and keep them in order, because they are chapters of a single argument, and an argument delivered out of order is merely a pile of grievances. When the war ends — truly ends, not pauses — the historians will come with their pens and their official access, and they will write the story of this campaign as the gentlemen who ran it wish it remembered. Against that day, I am building a different record. One written by a dead man, which no committee can suppress, because you cannot court-martial a ghost and you cannot promote him either.*

*More follows. Burn nothing.*

*— O.P.*

Wren set the letter down. His hands were steady now — steadier than they'd been all week — and that frightened him, because he understood that the steadiness was shock, the body's way of going quiet before it decided what to feel.

"Fifty-one," he said. The word came out strange, foreign, like a coin from another country.

The keeper said nothing. She was looking at the letters on the table the way a woman looks at a grave she dug herself.

* * *

He read the second letter that afternoon, and the third, and by evening the fourth, and he made himself stop there, because the fourth contained the photographs.

They were small, printed on thin stock, six of them, and Oscar Pemberton — for the hand was signed O.P., and the keeper had confirmed it with a nod so slight it might have been a muscle spasm, and the surname Pemberton now hung in the cottage like smoke that wouldn't clear — Oscar Pemberton had risked his neck developing them somewhere, God knew how, in some dugout darkroom or bribed field lab. They showed the village before and the village after, and the after photographs were the kind that Wren knew would hang behind the eyes of any decent person until the day they died. One of them showed a mosque with its minaret snapped off clean, like a broken finger. Another showed a row of bodies laid out on the ground with numbered markers, the burial detail's grim bookkeeping, and the numbers went up to fifty-one.

Wren had spent the war carrying things. That was his profession, his talent, his whole biography reduced to a single verb: he carried. Messages, parcels, sums of money, other men's secrets — he had moved them through Dublin's dockside alleys and across county lines in the dark, and he had made himself a rule early on, the rule that had kept him alive and kept him fed and kept him, if he was honest, small: *carry, don't open. Deliver, don't know.* Ignorance was armour. An ignorant man under interrogation had nothing to give up, and a man with nothing to give up could survive anything except his own conscience.

He had thought himself ignorant. He had carried this packet from a farmhouse outside Tralee to a boat at Fenit, and from Fenit through three days of storm to wherever the sea had decided to spit him out, and all that time he had believed he was carrying paper. Paper was nothing. Paper was weight and bulk, a job, a wage, seven pounds and passage money promised on delivery.

Sitting at the keeper's table with the lamp turned low, the photographs spread out in front of him like a hand of terrible cards, Wren understood that what he had carried was not paper.

It was evidence. Court-martial evidence. The kind of evidence that ended careers and started inquiries, the kind that got couriers questioned in cellars and couriers' bodies found in canals, and he had slept with it under his jacket, warm against his ribs, like a loaf of bread.

"They photographed it," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "They stood there with a camera and they *photographed* it. Like a harvest."

"Hallowes wanted proof of the fuses' performance," the keeper said. Her voice was flat, controlled, but Wren had spent enough days in this cottage now to hear the seams in it, the places where enormous force was being held back by discipline alone. "Oscar writes that in the fifth letter. The photographs were taken for a report. A technical report. Delayed-action fuses against civilian masonry." She paused. "They measured it, Mr. Wren. They measured how well the shells worked on houses with families in them."

"Wren." He looked up. "You've never once asked my name, and now you say it like you've known it for days."

"I read the manifest sewn into your coat collar. It's a habit. I apologize."

"No," Wren said slowly, "don't. It saves time." He gathered the photographs together, aligned their edges, squared the stack — small compulsions, the hands doing something orderly while the mind refused order entirely. "Who is Colonel Vane-Smythe? Do you know?"

"I know the name." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick and deep, a fish under ice. "Every officer in these letters is named. Dates, battery positions, fuse calibrations. He documented everything, because documentation was the only weapon a dead man could carry." She rose abruptly and went to the window, and stood there with her back to him, looking out at the darkening water. "Four years. Four years I lit that lamp every night and mourned my brother, and he was alive the whole time, writing letters. Writing letters about *that*." Her hand came up and pressed briefly against her mouth, and when it came away her voice was level again, and the levelness was worse than weeping. "Do you understand what he's done, Mr. Wren? He didn't just survive. He made his survival *useful*. He turned being dead into a post."

"To whom does he intend these to go? This Mr. Ambrose in Cork —"

"A journalist, I think. Eventually. When the war ends and the sealing begins." She turned around. In the lamplight her face had gone very still, the stillness of deep water. "And that's the question, isn't it. These were meant to reach Cork weeks ago. You were meant to deliver them weeks ago. Instead the sea took you and the packet sits on my table and somewhere in London there are men whose entire profession is making sure that documents like these never see print — men whose war isn't fought with guns but with filing cabinets." She came back to the table and stood over the eleven envelopes, and Wren watched her hand hover above them, not quite touching, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping child or a loaded gun. "You carried these without knowing. That's over now. The moment you opened the first seal, you stopped being a courier and became a witness. Do you understand the difference?"

"I'm beginning to."

"A courier can be replaced. A courier can be paid off, frightened off, buried in an unmarked grave, and the cargo simply finds another pair of legs. But a witness—" she tapped the table once, softly, beside the letters, "—a witness knows things. And knowledge doesn't drown. Knowledge washes ashore and gets fed bread and asks questions, and the men from the Ministry cannot allow witnesses to keep breathing once they start remembering." She looked at him straight on. "You've been in this cottage four days. Sooner or later, someone will come looking for you — the man who hired you, or the men he answers to, or worse, the polite kind, the ones with credentials and questions. When they come, they will find two people in this house who know about Karayel. Fifty-one dead, Mr. Wren. Photographed, measured, countersigned. Everything Oscar needed to make the argument, sitting in an oilskin packet in a kitchen on the edge of nowhere."

Wren looked down at the letters, at the strong, slanted, unmistakable hand of a man four years dead who was neither dead nor finished, and he felt the full weight of what he had carried settle onto him at last — not the physical weight, which was nothing, less than a loaf of bread, but the other weight, the terrible specific gravity of fifty-one names and six photographs and a word he would now carry in his skull until the day he died: *Karayel, Karayel, Karayel.*

He had informed on a man once. He had never told anyone that, had buried it so deep that even he rarely visited the grave, and he had told himself for eleven years that a hungry man's sins weighed less than other men's sins, that the arithmetic of feeding a family excused what the family never had to know. Sitting here now, in the lamplight, with the dead man's handwriting spread out before him, Wren understood suddenly and completely why Oscar Pemberton had chosen to become a ghost: because some truths are so heavy that no living man can carry them openly, and so necessary that no honest man can leave them buried.

He had carried the packet for a stranger's pay. Now he would carry it for nothing, or for penance, and he suspected the two amounts came to the same figure.

"When they come," Wren said quietly, "what do we tell them?"

The keeper — Pemberton, Zephyrine Pemberton, sister to a ghost, keeper of a light and now of something far more dangerous than a light — looked at the window, where the dark had come down over the water like a lid closing.

"We?" she said. "We tell them nothing, Mr. Wren. Because they haven't come yet. And when they do—" she blew out the lamp, and in the sudden darkness her voice was calm as tide-tables, "—we'll be ready. Now help me trim the wicks. If we're going to be hanged for what's on this table, we may as well keep the light burning while we wait."

Outside, far down the coast road, a motor engine droned — too steady, too deliberate, moving toward the point through the night fog.

Neither of them moved.
