## 6. Visitors from the Ministry

The fog came in on the morning tide the way it always did at Gullwrack — not arriving so much as assembling itself, piece by piece, until the world beyond her windowsill was a rumor. Zephyrine had learned to love fog for exactly that reason: it made the coast a place where nothing could be confirmed. A boat could pass within fifty yards and leave no evidence it had ever existed. She had sometimes wished she could live the way fog lived — present everywhere, provable nowhere.

She was trimming the wicks when she saw them.

Two figures on the cliff path from the village side, walking with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once had to guess where they were going. That was the first wrongness. Fishermen hurried between weather and shelter; farmers walked like their boots were too heavy; even Mrs. Calloway, when she came up with eggs and gossip, moved with the rolling gait of a woman who had spent forty years outpacing the post. These two walked as though the ground had been arranged for them in advance.

Zephyrine set down the wick scissors and stood very still, watching through the salt-hazed glass of the lantern room while the great lens turned above her head, sweeping its beam through the murk in long, patient strokes.

They were both in dark coats. The taller one carried himself like a man accustomed to being obeyed by furniture. The shorter one had a leather satchel and the soft, apologetic posture of someone whose job was to be underestimated. Neither wore uniform, which told her everything. Men in uniform wanted to be seen. Men without it wanted you to forget them until it was too late.

*Wren*, she thought, and the name arrived in her mind with a cold shock of possession, as if she'd been saying it for years instead of nine days. He was downstairs in the cottage, still convalescing, his ribs strapped, his fever finally broken three nights ago. She had left him sleeping — or pretending to sleep; he was a man who pretended to sleep the way other men breathed — with instructions to stay abed and away from the windows.

Nine days since the sea had given him back to her along with an oilskin packet of letters in her dead brother's hand. Nine days since the arithmetic of her careful life had stopped balancing.

She descended the spiral stair with the ease of twenty years' practice, each step counted without her noticing, and came into the cottage kitchen where Wren lay propped on blankets before the stove. His eyes were open. Of course they were open. He had heard them coming before she had — she could see it in the way his whole body had arranged itself into stillness, the stillness of a man calculating distances to doors.

"Two men," she said quietly. "From the path. Not fishermen."

He was already trying to sit up. She pressed him back with one hand flat against his chest, feeling the heat of him through the blanket, the bird-light fragility of a body that had nearly been spent by the sea.

"Where?" he whispered.

"The oilskin is where I put it. You're where you are. If anyone asks, I keep this light alone and have done for twenty years." She took down the kettle and filled it, because there was nothing more disarming than a woman making tea. "Can you be quiet?"

"I can be furniture," he said.

"Then be it."

A knock at the door. Three raps, evenly spaced — the knock of a man who knocked the same way on every door in England and expected the same result.

* * *

She opened the door to fog and two smiles.

"G'morning to you." The tall one removed his hat, revealing hair cropped so short it was almost a confession. "Forgive the intrusion, ma'am. Foul morning for it." He produced a card from inside his coat with the smooth economy of a conjurer. "Mr. Aldous Vane. This is my colleague, Mr. Pettifer. We're from the Ministry of Shipping and Transport — records division, if you please."

Zephyrine read the card without touching it. It named nothing useful. Ministry cards never did; they were designed to end conversations, not begin them.

"Pemberton," she said. "Mrs. Pemberton. Widow." Let them have all of it at once, the whole dreary inventory. Widows were invisible twice over.

"Mrs. Pemberton. Thank you for your time — I know how precious it must be, keeping a light alone." Vane's voice was warm, modulated, pitched at exactly the frequency that made a person feel listened to. She recognized the technique because she had spent twenty years practicing its opposite. "We're looking for a missing man. A courier — civilian contractor, carrying dispatches down the coast from the north. He was due in Cork by month's end and appears to have gone astray somewhere along this stretch. You'd have seen nothing, I expect, but we're obliged to ask at every dwelling."

"Astray," Zephyrine repeated. She let the word sit there, dull as a stone. "In this weather?"

"The storm last week was severe," Pettifer said. He had a small notebook out now, though he hadn't written anything in it yet. The notebook was a prop. Everything about these men was a prop except their eyes. "The sea gives things back, they say."

"Sometimes," Zephyrine agreed. She stepped back and opened the door wider, because refusing entry would be a confession and welcoming it would be boredom, and boredom was the armor she'd worn longest. "You'll take tea. There's nothing else on this rock worth offering."

"That's very kind," Vane said, and they came in.

* * *

The cottage was small and she had arranged it well over nine sleepless nights. Wren lay behind the blanket hung across the alcove off the kitchen — the bed-curtain every coastal cottage had against drafts — and if a visitor glanced toward it they would see only bedding, a washstand, the shadow of a convalescent woman's clutter. She had scattered her own things there: her spare shawl over a chair, a woman's boot visible past the curtain's hem. Small fictions, laid out like place settings.

Vane sat where she indicated and looked at exactly what she allowed him to look at. Pettifer stood near the window with his notebook and watched her hands.

"You've kept this light alone, Mrs. Pemberton? Since your husband?"

"Since 1904." True, and therefore the best kind of lie to build on. "He drowned in the spring of '04. The Board was good enough to let me stay on. A widow keeper is cheap, Mr. Vane, and Gullwrack is far enough from anywhere that nobody better wants it."

"And no family?" Pettifer asked. Mildly. Almost kindly.

The question landed precisely where it was aimed, and she felt it strike bone — Oscar, four years dead, four years *officially* dead — and she felt, underneath the pain, something older waking up and stretching. Something that remembered how to do this.

"My people are gone," she said. "I had a brother. The war took him. Gallipoli, 1915." She said the name of the place without flinching, because flinching would have been information, and gave them instead the flat grief of a woman who had long ago folded that loss away with the winter clothes. "There's no one else."

"I'm sorry for it," Vane said, and meant perhaps a tenth of what he said, and delivered even that tenth beautifully. "These are hard years for everyone's ledgers."

She poured tea. Her hands did not shake. She noted that they did not shake, and noted that she was noting it, and understood that some machine in her was running diagnostics the way the lamp mechanism ran — automatically, ceaselessly, checking itself against failure.

"Your missing courier," she said. "Describe him."

Pettifer's pencil actually touched paper at that. "Mid-thirties. Dark hair. Thin — half-starved, really, the way they come out of those contracting offices. He'd have been carrying a leather case and wearing—" Pettifer paused, consulting memory rather than notes, "—a fisherman's oilskin, borrowed, we think, from a boat he jumped at Ardfallen."

"And why does the Ministry of Shipping chase one lost clerk?"

"Dispatches concerning shipping losses," Vane said smoothly. "Tonnage figures. The Admiralty likes its numbers kept tidy, and the peace negotiations have everyone's ledgers open just now. Nothing that would interest a lighthouse keeper, I assure you."

Everything about the sentence was designed to close a door. So she opened it instead.

"Tonnage," she said, and yawned behind her hand — an insolence so domestic it couldn't be punished. "You've come eighty miles through fog for tonnage figures. Must be some figure."

"It's a matter of thoroughness, ma'am." Vane smiled. "The war taught us all thoroughness. Now — the night of the storm. The eleventh. You'd recall it."

"Everyone on this coast recalls it."

"What do you recall?"

And here it was, the real examination, dressed as conversation. She gave them the truth first, generously, because truth was the best mortar for lies: the wind backing northwest after midnight, the sea coming over the low wall, the lamp burning steady while everything else on God's earth screamed. She described the sound of the gale the way only a keeper could describe it — the different voices of wind through the gallery rail versus the ventilators, the note the tower itself sang at force ten. Vane listened like a man buying a horse, and she knew he wasn't hearing weather at all. He was hearing whether she was the sort of woman whose account could be trusted, and every accurate detail was a brick in the wall she was building between him and the truth.

"Toward dawn," Vane said, when she finished, "did you see anything on the water? Or the shore?"

"Fog," Zephyrine said. "After dawn the fog came in and the world ended. That's the whole of my report, Mr. Vane. Fog."

"No lights? No boat? No — wreckage washed up?"

"Wreckage always washes up. Spars, weed, a ship's biscuit tin once, year before the war. I burn the salvage that burns and bury what doesn't. If your courier drowned, he's under the kelp with everyone else the sea keeps." She refilled their cups without asking. "If he didn't drown, then he walked inland, and that's beyond my light's jurisdiction entirely."

Pettifer wrote something. Vane turned his cup in its saucer, a full rotation, considering.

"May we see the station, Mrs. Pemberton? The log, especially. The Board likes its records cross-checked — a keeper's log is legal evidence of weather, you know. If your log shows clear conditions on the twelfth, it helps us fix the timeline of when the courier might have come ashore."

There it was. The log. Her log — the one true continuous document of her twenty years, the thing she had written faithfully every single day, four hours on watch and four hours off, through mourning and madness and the slow erosion of her own name. And now they wanted to read it, because a log that recorded *fog* on the twelfth was a log that recorded nothing — and a log that recorded nothing proved nothing happened, and a man could drown in a blank page as easily as in the sea.

"Certainly," she said.

* * *

She fetched the current volume from its shelf and also, unasked, three completed volumes going back seven years, and dealt them onto the table like a winning hand. "Read whatever serves you. My handwriting's poor and my ink worse, but the entries are honest. Weather doesn't care who it inconveniences, and neither do I."

It was audacity dressed as cooperation, and she saw it land — saw Vane recalibrate, the faintest flicker behind the warmth, the reassessment of the widow from obstacle to instrument. Useful people were left alive. That was the arithmetic she was betting on.

While they bent over the pages she stood at the stove with her back half-turned, feeding peat to the fire, and let her mind move fast and cold beneath her still face.

They knew a courier was missing. They knew the route. They knew the storm date. They had come to a lighthouse at the exact midpoint of the coastline — not searching, *arriving*. Which meant either extraordinary diligence or prior knowledge, and men like Vane did not walk cliffs in fog out of diligence. Someone had talked. A boatman at Ardfallen, a quartermaster, a name extracted from a ledger of contracted couriers. The net was not cast wide; it was cast *here*, and the question was how much they already held.

Wren coughed.

One cough, muffled by the blanket and the curtain — the kind of cough any sick woman might make. But she heard Vane's attention turn toward the alcove the way a compass needle turns, quick and total, and she moved without deciding to move: crossed to the alcove, drew the curtain back a hand's width, and said, in the voice of long-suffering routine, "Hush, mother. The gentlemen are reading."

Behind the blanket, in the dim, she saw Wren's eyes — wide, understanding instantly, playing his part by becoming absolutely nothing. She let the curtain fall.

"Your mother lives with you?" Vane asked. Pleasantly. Too pleasantly.

"Since the autumn. Her mind goes in and out, poor thing. She was a keeper's wife forty years; storms excite her." Zephyrine returned to the kettle and lifted it onto the plate. "You'll forgive her noise."

"We're sorry to have disturbed her," Pettifer said, and for the first time his mildness sounded genuine, and she hated how much easier that made lying to him.

"She'll sleep till noon now. She always does after a fuss." She poured fresh tea. "More?"

* * *

They stayed forty minutes. She timed it against the lamp mechanism's rotation without meaning to — thirty-one rotations, roughly — and in that time they asked her, in one guise or another, eleven questions, and she answered nineteen, burying each interrogation under a surplus of mundane detail: the cost of paraffin since the war began, the state of the stairs, the difficulty of getting flour brought out by boat, the death of her husband, the death of her brother, the death, apparently, of every man she had ever known, offered up freely and mournfully until the room grew so heavy with her widowed, orphaned, childless solitude that even Vane seemed to want to escape it.

That was the trick she hadn't known she remembered: give them grief until grief becomes a fog of its own. Nobody probes deeply into a woman's sorrow; they flee it.

At the door, Vane settled his hat and paused.

"One last thing, Mrs. Pemberton. If a stranger were to come ashore here — injured, say, or hiding — you'd report it to the constable in Kilmore?"

"Instantly," Zephyrine said. "This light is Crown property, Mr. Vane. I am a servant of the Crown, such as the Crown remembers me." She smiled — a small, tired, truthful-seeming smile. "Whatever the sea brings me, the sea brings me for the Crown's benefit."

Something in Vane's face relaxed by a degree, and she understood she had found the master key: he had needed her to be small and loyal, and she had shown him a small loyal woman, and the door of him had swung open onto an empty room.

"Good day to you, ma'am. Thank you for the tea." He hesitated on the threshold, the fog standing behind him like a second visitor. "Oh — and if you should happen across anything washed up. Papers, particularly. Sealed packets, documents, correspondence." His eyes rested on hers with perfect courtesy. "Burn it, don't read it. Some papers are dangerous just to have opened."

"How dramatic," Zephyrine said mildly. "I'll be sure to burn anything the sea sends, unread. Good day, gentlemen."

She watched them go down the cliff path until the fog took them — first their outlines, then their suggestion, then nothing at all — and she stood in the doorway a long moment afterward, listening to the gulls arguing over something on the shingle below.

Then she shut the door, put her back against it, and discovered that her heart was going like a hammer and her hands, faithful hands, steady all through, were shaking now that it was over.

"Burn it, don't read it," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and heard in her own voice something she hadn't heard in twenty years.

Not fear. Interest.

She crossed to the alcove and pulled the curtain aside. Wren was sitting up, gray-faced, sweat standing on his brow from the effort of stillness, and his eyes went over her face as though reading a chart.

"They'll come back," he said hoarsely. "Men like that always come back."

"Then we have time between visits, and not much of it." She straightened, and the shaking in her hands stopped, deliberately, the way she would trim a guttering wick. "You'll tell me everything, Mr. Whoever-You-Are. Starting with who those letters belong to — and why a Ministry man's voice changed when he said the word *Cork*."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like a listener.

"He's your brother," Wren said. "The man who wrote those letters. Isn't he."

It was not a question, and she did not answer it, and in not answering she answered everything.
