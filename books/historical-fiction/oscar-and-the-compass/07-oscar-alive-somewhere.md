## 7. Oscar, Alive, Somewhere

The fever broke on the third night, and Wren woke to the smell of paraffin and bread.

He lay still in the narrow bed beneath a quilt that smelled of lavender and lamp oil, listening to the house around him the way he'd once listened to dockyards — mapping its sounds, counting its exits. Wind against glass. The sea, farther off, working at the rocks with patient hands. Below him, floorboards creaked under a woman's weight: steady, unhurried, going about some business she did not intend to explain to him.

Not dead, then. Not in custody either, or he'd have woken to different smells entirely — carbolic, wet wool, the particular staleness of rooms where men asked questions. He'd been in enough of those to know them by nose.

He catalogued himself like cargo. Ribs intact, though bruised where the sea had handled him. Left ankle swollen but bearing weight when he tested it against the bedframe's iron rail. Hands — his hands were his living, and they were whole, cracked from salt but whole. And the packet?

His heart went cold before he finished the thought. He sat up too fast and the room tilted, grey light through salt-hazed windows swinging like a lantern on a hook.

The coat was gone from the chair where he half-remembered dropping it. His boots were gone. He was wearing a nightshirt that smelled of someone else, someone older, a man's shirt kept long past its usefulness — and every instinct he owned stood up and screamed.

*They've taken it. She's taken it. You've carried it four hundred miles and lost it in the last hundred yards.*

He swung his legs down. The floor bit him with cold. He made it two steps toward the door before it opened.

She stood in the frame with a tray — bread, tea, a dish of dripping — and looked at him standing there swaying in her dead husband's nightshirt, and something in her face told him she had already measured this exact moment. Already decided what to do with it.

"Sit down before you fall down," Zephyrine Pemberton said. "You'll want your strength for lying to me, and I'll want mine for watching you do it."

---

He ate because refusing food was a luxury his body could no longer afford, and she watched him eat without touching anything herself. She had set the tray across her own knees out of some old habit of economy, he thought, not hospitality — as if feeding him were simply one more task in the day's arithmetic and she would waste nothing, neither bread nor attention.

"You were carrying letters," she said.

Wren kept chewing. It bought him seconds.

"In an oilskin packet," she continued, "tied with waxed cord, sealed twice. You held onto it while you were drowning, which means whoever gave it to you convinced you it mattered more than breathing." Her eyes were the colour of the sea before weather — not blue, not green, just depth. "I want to know who. And I want to know why."

"And if I don't tell you?"

"Then I'll put you back on the shingle," she said, without heat, without malice, the way one might say *then it will rain*, "and let the sea finish its work."

He believed her. That was the remarkable thing. Most threats came dressed in anger; hers wore apron-grey calm, and it was far more convincing. Wren set down the crust.

"My name's Thomas Wren."

"I know. Your jacket has a laundry tag."

Of course it did. He almost smiled. "Thomas Wren. Late of Liverpool, late of a dozen other places. I'm a courier. Not government," he added quickly. "Freelance. There are men who need things moved and men who move them, and I'm the second kind. I don't read what I carry. That's the trade. You don't read, you don't know, and what you don't know can't be tortured out of you."

"But you read these."

The tea trembled slightly in his cup. He set it down. "No."

"No?"

"I didn't have to open them." He looked at his hands. "The handwriting showed through the paper. Whoever wrote them pressed hard. I could see enough of the words to wish I hadn't seen any." He paused. "There were dates. Places. Names of officers. Descriptions of—" His throat closed. He had rehearsed this confession on the boat, somewhere between the Irish coast and God knew what, telling an imaginary listener what he carried, and now the real listener sat across from him and the words came out anyway, dragged by their own weight. "Descriptions of a village being shelled. By our side. With the guns walked across it slow, street by street, so nothing would be missed. And then accounts written after — witnesses, counts, a chaplain's letter. Written by a man whose death notice I saw pinned up in Holyhead. Killed at Gallipoli, 1915. Four years dead. Except he wrote those pages in 1917."

Silence. The fire ticked. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

Zephyrine Pemberton did not move. But Wren had spent years learning to read stillness — dockers deciding whether to break a picket line, wives reading telegrams, officers deciding whom to believe. Stillness was never empty. Hers was full to the brim, and trembling.

"The name," she said. Very quietly. "On the death notice."

"Pemberton," Wren said. "Oscar Pemberton. Second Lieutenant, Seventh—"

"Stop."

She said it the way you'd say *stop* to a clock. As if time itself had been running and she could order it halted. And for three or four heartbeats it seemed to obey her; even the wind seemed to hold its breath against the window.

When she spoke again, her voice had changed registers — lower, stripped of its keeper's flatness, and underneath it Wren heard something young. Something that had been buried alive for years under the woman sitting here.

"What else do you remember about him," she said, "from the notice. Exactly."

"It said *beloved son*. There was a verse. *Greater love hath no man.*" Wren frowned, dredging. "And there was a line at the end, in italics. A request. It said the family wished no flowers, no visitors, no correspondence — that all enquiries should go to a solicitor in London. I remember thinking it was odd. Cold. Like a door closing rather than a grave opening."

Her hand had risen, without her seeming to know it, to the collar of her dress, to a thin chain there, and her fingers closed around something beneath the fabric.

"That wasn't grief," she said. "That was him. That was Oscar writing his own death notice and paying a solicitor to make sure nobody came looking." She laughed — a single syllable, dry as a struck match. "No flowers. No visitors. No correspondence. He always did hate fuss."

"You know him."

"He's my brother."

---

After that the afternoon rearranged itself. She brought more tea. She lit the lamp early, though the dark was hours off, and sat where the light caught her face, and for the first time since he'd washed up on her rocks she talked like a person and not like a lighthouse — fixed, rotating, giving everyone the same beam.

Not everything. He understood that. She gave him the shape of things and withheld the interior, the way he himself parcelled out truth to interrogators: enough to be credible, never enough to be dangerous.

Oscar Pemberton, younger by six years, the brilliant one, the one who'd gone into the army because the war was supposed to be short and honourable and their father had wanted one son in uniform the way other fathers wanted one son in the church. Zephyrine had married at nineteen to get out of a house that smelled of disappointment; her husband had been a keeper here, a quiet man who asked nothing and offered less, and when he died she had simply stayed, and the staying had become twenty years. She had written to Oscar through '15 and into '16. Then the letters stopped coming back, and then came the printed card, and then nothing — and she had built a life on top of the nothing the way you build a road over marsh, layering silence on silence until it held your weight.

"And now you're telling me," she said, "that the nothing was furniture. Arranged. He stood behind a curtain marked *dead* for three years and watched us mourn him."

"Maybe he had to."

"Don't defend him to me, Mr. Wren. Defend him to yourself first, and tell me if it works." But there was no venom in it. There was something worse: hope, fighting discipline for control of her face, and losing ground inch by inch. "Alive. You're saying he's alive."

"I'm saying a dead man's handwriting is dated 1917. Draw your own conclusions."

"Where is he? Do you know where he is?"

"No." And that much was true, and he watched her take the blow and absorb it, and hated the honesty that required it. "I was hired in Dublin. A man called Fennerty — a publican with a bad leg and good connections. He gave me the packet, forty pounds, half up front, and instructions: south along the coast to Cork, ask at a certain address for a journalist named Halloran, deliver before the end of November. I wasn't told what was in it. I wasn't told who wrote it. Fennerty said the sender was 'a friend of Ireland' and left it at that, and I was fool enough to think that was the whole of it."

"When?" she said. "Month's end — how far off is that?"

Wren calculated. Days lost to fever blurred, but the storm had come on the twentieth or near it; he'd lain here three nights, maybe four. "It's the twenty-fourth today. Perhaps the twenty-fifth. Month's end is five days away. Six."

"Cork is two hundred miles."

"Three hundred, the way I'd travel. Down the coast, across water, avoiding every port with a customs officer in it." He shook his head. "It can't be done. Not by me, not now, not with my ankle and not with half the coastline crawling with men asking questions about a missing courier. I'm sorry. I know what that sounds like — I failed. Whatever he needed delivered by December, it doesn't get delivered."

"Why the deadline?" she demanded. "Why does it matter when it arrives? Letters keep. Truth keeps."

"No it doesn't." He heard his own voice go flat and certain, the certainty of a man who had watched how power worked from the bottom rung. "Truth is perishable, ma'am. It's got a season like fish. Right now the war's ending and everyone knows it — armistice talk in every bar from Belfast to Cork. And when it ends, the historians arrive. Ours and theirs. They'll write the official record, and the official record gets sealed — thirty years, fifty years, whatever the ministries decide — and by the time anyone can legally look inside, everyone who remembers will be old or dead or persuaded otherwise. If Halloran prints this before the sealing, it's news, it's scandal, it's on the record while the record is still soft. After the sealing, it's a rumour in an archive. Dust with a date on it."

Zephyrine had gone very pale. Not with fear — he'd learn later to distinguish her fears, each with its own weather — but with the particular pallor of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want done.

"So the deadline isn't a formality."

"It's the whole point. Fennerty wasn't hiring a courier. He was racing a clock."

---

The lamp above them turned. Through the ceiling he could hear it faintly, the great lens walking its circle, and he found the sound steadying in a way he couldn't have explained — a machine keeping faith with the dark, hour upon hour, asking nothing.

She rose and went to the dresser and stood with her back to him for a long moment. When she turned, she was holding the oilskin packet. It was dry now, re-tied, the seals broken cleanly along one edge and pressed closed again — she had opened it, then. Of course she had opened it. He'd have thought less of her if she hadn't.

"How much," she said, "did they pay you?"

"Forty pounds. Twenty delivered."

"And the other twenty?"

"Payable in Cork. Which I'll never see." He shrugged. "I've been paid worse for dirtier work."

"Would you take payment from me instead?"

He looked up sharply. "What kind?"

"The same kind. Money, passage, a place to sleep until your ankle holds you. In exchange for the job you were already doing — only the destination's changed." She laid the packet on the table between them, her palm flat on top of it, and Wren saw that her hand was not quite steady and that she was spending real effort to make it look as though it were. "Halloran can't be reached by month's end. Fine. Then the letters don't go to Cork. They go where they can do the most good before the record seals — and I am the only person on this coast who can read the cipher my brother hides inside his tide tables, and the only person alive with reason to trust what's written in his hand. So the courier becomes unnecessary, and the sister becomes necessary, and you, Mr. Wren, become the man who carries for me."

"Carries you where?"

"To wherever the record is being written. Before it dries." Her jaw set. "There will be inquiries. There are always inquiries when wars end — commissions, committees, gentlemen in good coats collecting testimony to arrange into history. If we can't bring the truth to print, we bring it to the one room in this country where a civilian woman can stand up and say *this happened, and here is the proof, and put it in your minutes.*"

"They'll bury you with it. Women don't testify. Keepers especially don't testify. And you—" he hesitated, then committed, because the alliance he could feel forming deserved at least the courtesy of honesty, "you've spent twenty years making sure no one knows your name. This plan asks you to hand it over."

"Yes," she said simply.

"And you'd do it? For a brother who let you grieve him for four years?"

For a moment he thought she would strike him, and he found he wouldn't have blamed her. But she only looked at the packet under her hand, and her voice, when it came, was quieter than the lamp turning above them.

"I burned one of his letters yesterday," she said. "Did you know that? One page of his hand, in the stove, unread, because I was afraid of what it would say." She lifted her chin. "I won't burn the rest. I've done my arithmetic, Mr. Wren. Twenty years of silence against one true thing said out loud. The silence loses."

Wren sat with that. Outside, the light was failing; the window had gone from grey to pewter, and the first star stood over Gullwrack Point like a pin in a chart. Everything sensible in him — every instinct honed in warehouses and courtrooms and the backs of police vans — told him to refuse. To take his swollen ankle and his forty pounds minus twenty and disappear into the next county. Sensible men survived. He was, by his own accounting, a sensible man.

But he had also informed on a docker once, for money, to feed children who weren't even his, and he had carried that ledger ever since, and the packet had been meant to balance it, and the packet was sitting on this table under this woman's palm, and the sea had carried it four hundred miles to exactly the wrong address — or the right one, if you believed the sea kept books too.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "I need to know one thing. Not where he is — I know you don't know. Whether he's alive at all. Because if I carry this for a ghost, I want to know I'm carrying for a ghost. If he's alive—" His voice caught on something he hadn't planned. "If he's alive, then somewhere tonight a man who's been dead four years is waiting to find out whether his truth made it ashore. And I'd like to be the reason it did."

Zephyrine Pemberton studied him across the lamplight for a long moment — longer than the question warranted, long enough that he understood she was weighing not his answer but his worth, the way she weighed oil and wicks and bread, wasting nothing.

"He's alive," she said. "Somewhere. A man who writes tide tables in cipher and pays solicitors to close doors behind him is a man who plans to come back through one." She pushed the packet across the table until it touched his knuckles. "We leave when your ankle lets us. And Mr. Wren — the next lie you tell me will be your last on this rock. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Wren said.

It was only later, lying awake under the dead husband's quilt with the lamp turning overhead, that he remembered what Fennerty had said when he handed over the packet, the last instruction, the one he'd filed away as superstition:

*"If it goes wrong — if you can't make Cork — find the lighthouse at Gullwrack Point. Give it to the keeper. The sender said the keeper would know what to do."*

The sender had known. Oscar Pemberton, four years dead, had aimed his brother-in-all-but-name at his sister's door from the far side of the sea — and Wren, half-drowned, had landed precisely where a dead man's compass pointed.

Which meant someone, somewhere, was still steering.

And which meant — Wren realized, staring into the dark, cold settling along his spine like ballast — that whoever had hired Fennerty might not be finished checking on the delivery.
