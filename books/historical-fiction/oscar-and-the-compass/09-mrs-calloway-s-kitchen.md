## 9. Mrs. Calloway's Kitchen

Wren learned the village the way a man learns a foreign shore: by what it refused to show him.

From the cottage window at Gullwrack Point he could see the huddle of Gullwrack Lower below the bluff — eleven roofs and a church spire, smoke standing straight up out of chimneys in the windless grey of late afternoon. A place that looked like nothing. He had carried enough secrets in his life to know that places that looked like nothing were usually where everything was kept.

His ribs had mended enough that Zephyrine no longer flinched when she saw him lift the coal scuttle. The fever was a week gone. His hands still trembled in the mornings, but he had begun to distrust whether that was illness or something older.

"You'll walk down today," Zephyrine said. It was not quite an invitation. She stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the village as if it might watch her back. "Mrs. Calloway will want to look at you. She looks at everyone who comes through this door."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's an inventory." She turned. In the flat November light her face gave away very little; he had learned to read her anyway, in the way of men who have spent years reading foremen and policemen and landladies — by the set of the mouth, the pause before a word. "She keeps the post office. She also keeps every letter anyone has written since 1897, in her head. She knows my husband died before I came here. She does not know much else, and she has never asked, which is its own kind of knowledge. You will not lie to her. She can smell it, like fish gone off."

"And if she asks me things?"

Zephyrine considered him for a long moment. He felt the weight of the decision in her silence — he understood now that everything with her went through some interior arithmetic, tallies made and struck out, accounts kept in a ledger no one else could see.

"She won't ask," she said finally. "That's why we're going."

* * *

The post office was the front room of a stone house on the one street that bent like a question mark through the village. Its window held stamps and string and a jar of barley sugar gone cloudy with age, and behind the counter sat a woman built like a mooring post — broad, salt-stained, immovable — with spectacles pushed up into grey hair and a pen tucked behind one ear like a carpenter's pencil.

"Wren," said Mrs. Calloway, before he had spoken. "That's the name she's using for you. It'll do. Come past the counter, boy, you're dripping on my floor."

He wasn't dripping. But he came past the counter, because there was no force on earth, he suspected, that would stop Mrs. Calloway from having her way about a floor.

The kitchen behind the shop was warm and low-ceilinged and smelled of bread and paraffin and, underneath it, iodine. Two people already sat at the table. One was a man of perhaps forty with a fisherman's rolled shoulders and a face that kept trying to be forgettable and failing at it — too watchful, eyes that moved to the window whenever boots sounded on the lane outside. The other was a woman in a nurse's grey dress washed thin as tissue, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms mapped with small white scars he recognized without wanting to: shrapnel nicks, or worse, the marks of a field hospital's daily accidents.

"Edith Vane," the nurse said, extending a hand with a grip that meant business. "Sister, once. Queen Alexandra's. Four years of France, then they sent me home to wait for a husband who'd stopped writing, and then they stopped sending letters at all." She did not make it tragic; she made it a fact, laid on the table like a scalpel. "And that's Tam Breen, who is not here."

Tam Breen raised his teacup half an inch. "Not here," he confirmed. "Haven't been here since March. There's no Tam Breen registered anywhere in this county, and if a patrol boat comes round the point asking after deserters, everyone in this room will say they've never seen such a man."

"You deserted?" Wren asked, before he could stop himself.

"Fishing boat, Kingstown station," Breen said. "March 1916. We were told to put to sea in weather that would drown the devil himself, because the Admiralty wanted the fleet's fishing screen maintained and didn't care what the sea thought of it. My brother went out instead of me. The *Providence* came back with four of nine hands." He drank his tea. "I've been not-here ever since."

The plainness of it undid Wren more than any confession would have. This man had done the thing Wren had never dared — had said *no*, out loud, with his body — and paid for it with a life spent indoors, listening for boots.

"I informed once," Wren heard himself say. The words came out of him like water finding a crack. "A dockworker, Cork. Strike committee. I told his foreman where he'd be on Thursday nights. They broke his hand so he'd never hold a hook again. I did it for two pounds a month, because I had a mother and a sister and rent." He stared into his tea. "I'm not telling you so you'll forgive me. I'm telling you so you know what's sitting at your table."

The kitchen went quiet. Outside, a gull argued with the wind about something eternal.

"Well," said Mrs. Calloway, setting down a plate of soda bread with the finality of a judge's gavel. "Now that we're all criminals, pass the butter."

* * *

It happened over the bread, the way important things happen in kitchens — sideways, between mouthfuls, while someone's back is turned to the range.

Zephyrine had come down with them and sat near the fire, saying almost nothing, which Wren had gathered was her way of saying everything. It was Mrs. Calloway who took up the thread.

"Now. Let's talk plainly, because the kettle's on and plainness goes well with strong tea." She untied her apron and hung it on a hook, a gesture that somehow changed her from postmistress to general. "Zeph — may I call you Zeph? No? Zephyrine, then. Zephyrine has a packet. I don't know what's in the packet and I haven't asked, because asking is how people hang. But I know this: two gentlemen came through my post office three days ago asking after couriers, showing a photograph of a man drowned off the rocks, and their questions were wrong."

"Wrong how?" Edith Vane asked.

"Too neat." Mrs. Calloway counted on thick fingers. "They knew the courier's route. They knew the date. They knew the tide. What they didn't know was the name of the inn at Dunmore Head where the coach stops — and any man genuinely hunting a lost courier along this coast would have slept in that inn, because it's the only roof within nine miles. They'd memorized a file. They hadn't walked the ground." She let that sit. "Also their boots were London boots. Shined. On a coastal road in November. Either fools or officers, and officers are rarely fools twice."

"They came to the light," Zephyrine said quietly. It was the most words she'd strung together all afternoon.

"Ay, and you lied to them, by your face." Mrs. Calloway smiled without warmth. "Good girl. Now hear the rest. Edith sees the doctor's patients from Kilmara to the point — she hears who's sick and who's lying about being sick. Tam fishes at night, which is illegal and therefore useful; nothing moves on this water without him seeing it. And I open, read when needed, reroute, and delay every piece of paper in this parish." She tapped the table. "You have a packet that men in London boots want badly. So the question isn't whether you're in danger. The question is what this table can do about it."

Wren looked around the kitchen. The nurse with her scarred forearms. The deserter who did not exist. The postmistress with a nation's gossip filed under headings only she could see. The keeper who was not really a keeper, sitting with her hands folded like a woman at her own trial.

And himself — a courier who'd been hired blind, fished out of the sea, and was beginning to understand that he had never delivered anything so heavy in his life.

"Why?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "None of you needed this. You had quiet lives. Small lives. Safe ones." He thought of his own kitchen in Cork, of his sister's laugh, of two pounds a month, of how cheaply safety had always bought him. "Why stand up now?"

Tam Breen answered first, turning his cup slowly on the table. "Because I stood down once, and my brother didn't come home. That sums it."

"Because I counted the dead for four years," Edith said, "and the counting was never allowed to become a number anyone official would write down. Numbers get buried. I'd rather not bury this one too."

Mrs. Calloway simply looked at Zephyrine, and something passed between the two women that Wren could not follow — some old agreement, or the making of a new one.

"Because," Mrs. Calloway said, "I have spent thirty years carrying other people's words, and I have always believed that someday a word would come through my window worth the carrying. I think it's arrived. Slightly damp and sitting in my kitchen eating my bread, but arrived."

Wren found, to his considerable embarrassment, that his throat had closed. He busied himself with the tea.

* * *

They worked until the lamplight and the firelight traded places.

It was Edith who organized them — the nurse's instinct, triage and roster. "The packet stays at the light, but not in the cottage. Somewhere a search party won't think of inside ten minutes." She looked at Tam. "Your bothy above the strand. The loose stone under the peat bin."

"The stone's for my own papers."

"Then your own papers can share with history."

Breen grumbled, but Wren watched him write down nothing, memorize everything, and nod once — a man accepting an order he'd have refused from anyone wearing a uniform.

Mrs. Calloway laid out the paper routes like a field marshal: which roads were watched, which constable took bribes and which took notes, how many days' warning they'd have if London boots appeared again on the coach. "Anything you need moved, it moves inside bread deliveries. Nobody searches a bread delivery. It's beneath everyone's dignity."

"And me?" Wren asked.

"You," said Edith, looking at him with clinical frankness, "are still grey around the gills, and you'll do nobody good dead. You'll stay at the light, you'll learn the coast from Zephyrine — tides, paths, the old smugglers' cut above the shingle — because if this goes badly, someone in this room will need to run, and running blind gets people caught." She paused. "And you'll tell us everything you remember about the man who hired you. Every word. Faces keep files too."

So he told them. The public house in Cork, the fog of pipe smoke, the soft-spoken man who had called himself Mr. Ashe and paid half in advance and said only: *a packet to a journalist in Cork by month's end, and if anything happens to me, take it to the light at Gullwrack Point.* He had thought, at the time, that the instruction was strange — why a lighthouse? — and had been too hungry and too well-paid to wonder hard.

Now, in the warmth of Mrs. Calloway's kitchen, with the packet itself locked in a box across the room and five people arranging themselves around it like stones around a hearth, he wondered at last, and aloud:

"He knew. Whoever hired me — he knew the packet would end up here. With her." He looked at Zephyrine. "This was never a delivery that failed. It was a delivery that landed exactly where it was aimed."

The fire ticked. Zephyrine's face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing water, and was still again.

"Then he knew me," she said, "before I knew myself. Again." Her voice was dry, but Wren had learned to hear what dried-out things cost. "Very well. Then we'd best be worth aiming at."

* * *

They walked back up the cliff path in the last light, Zephyrine ahead of him with the parcel of bread and the lamp unlit — she saved the wick, she said, even now, even for a ten-minute walk; economy was a religion with her, though Wren was starting to suspect it was camouflage for something else entirely.

At the bend where the path turned toward the tower she stopped. Below, the village showed its early lamps, and among them the post office window, yellow and steady.

"You understand what we did tonight," she said. She didn't turn around. "Every person at that table is now findable. Because of a packet you carried and a brother I mourned. If this ends badly, it ends badly for all of us, and it will be my choice that brought them in."

"That's not how it felt from where I sat."

"How did it feel?"

Wren thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to her had begun to seem not just dangerous but wasteful.

"It felt like the first time since the war started that I've been warm on purpose," he said.

She was quiet for a moment. The sea worked at the rocks below, patient as a clerk.

"Yes," she said at last. "That's exactly what frightens me."

She walked on. But at the door of the cottage, setting down the bread, she paused and said, almost too low for him to catch it: "Mrs. Calloway mentioned a gentleman. An historian. Going keeper to keeper along the coast, collecting accounts for the peace delegation's record. Polite fellow, she says. Staying at the Dunmore Head inn." She looked at Wren, and her eyes in the doorway's shadow were the grey of the winter sea. "He arrives tomorrow morning. He's asked to see the Gullwrack Point log."
