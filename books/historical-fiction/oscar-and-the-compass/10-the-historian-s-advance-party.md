## 10. The Historian's Advance Party

He came on the fourth day after the fog lifted, walking up from the village with a leather satchel and an umbrella he did not need, because the sky had spent its violence and was now merely grey, like a dog that had barked itself hoarse. Zephyrine saw him first from the gallery — a small figure in a good coat picking his way along the cliff path, pausing twice to look out at the water as though the view were a thing to be collected.

She went down and put the kettle on before he knocked. It was what a keeper did for visitors; it was also what a liar did for visitors, because a woman who offered tea had nothing to hide, and nothing to hide was the costume Zephyrine Pemberton wore best.

The knock came at half past ten exactly. Not early, not late. Punctual men frightened her more than rough ones ever had.

"Mrs. Pemberton?" He removed his hat on the step, revealing hair the colour of wet sand going grey at the temples, and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever been required to be anything but pleasant. "My name is Halloran. Edmund Halloran. I wonder if I might impose on you for a short while."

"Visitors are rare," she said, which was true, and stood aside.

* * *

He took his tea the way he did everything else — correctly, without fuss, two sugars stirred four times, the spoon laid parallel to the saucer's rim when finished. He complimented the cottage. He admired the brass clock. He remarked that the light at Gullwrack must be one of the lonelier postings on this coast, and asked whether she found the solitude agreeable.

"I'm used to it," she said.

"Yes," he said, and smiled, and wrote something down.

Zephyrine watched the pencil move across his little notebook and understood, with the cold clarity of a lamp lens focusing, that everything she said would go into that book, and everything she did not say would be inferred from it. She had faced men with warrants before — not these particular men, not yet, but their kind, in another life, under another name — and men with warrants were simple creatures; they wanted a specific thing and you gave it to them or you didn't. But this man wanted a story. He wanted the whole coast to tell him one story, the same story, in the same tidy hand, and he would assemble it page by page until the war made sense, or rather until it made a shape that could be signed.

"And what is it you're writing, Mr. Halloran?"

"The official narrative." He said it without embarrassment, which somehow made it worse. "For the peace delegation. When the armistice comes — and it will come soon now, everyone believes so — there will be histories written. Histories commissioned properly, with access to the records." He turned his hands palm-up, a gesture of modest apology. "Someone must decide which facts belong in them. The Ministry has engaged a small team. I have the western coast. Lighthouses, signal stations, coastguard posts. The eyes of the shoreline, as it were."

"The eyes of the shoreline," she repeated.

"It's a phrase of theirs," he admitted. "I confess I find it useful. You keepers see a great deal. Ships in distress, lights where lights shouldn't be, boats moving at night that have no business moving." He smiled again, gently, as though offering her a gift. "You see, Mrs. Pemberton, history is not made by generals alone. It is made by witnesses. And witnesses must be gathered while they can still be gathered — while memories are fresh and records unsealed. After the armistice, so much will be closed away for decades. Classification, you understand. It's all quite necessary. Wars leave wounds, and wounds shouldn't be picked at by every amateur with a pen."

"And so you pick at them instead."

His smile widened a fraction. He liked her, she could see that. He decided quickly to like people; it was part of his method. A man who disliked his subjects would get defiance, and defiance made bad testimony.

"I gather," he said. There was a world of difference between the two words and they both knew it. "May I ask you some questions? Nothing difficult. Dates, weather, sightings. The sort of thing you'd have in your logbook in any case."

"My logbook is official property. It goes to the Commissioners."

"Of course. I wouldn't dream of asking to see it. Your memory will serve better — logs record weather, but memory records truth." He opened the notebook to a fresh page. "Now. This autumn. The gales have been exceptional. Tell me about the night of the ninth of November."

The date landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Zephyrine kept her hands still around her cup. The ninth of November. The gale that had brought Wren ashore on the shingle, half-drowned, clutching an oilskin packet full of her dead brother's living handwriting. She had logged that storm herself, in her own careful hand, wind southwest force nine shifting west-northwest, sea over the wall, lamp trimmed twice against salt spray. The log would show the storm. The log would show nothing else.

"A bad night," she said. "One of the worst this season. I kept the light. In the morning there was wreckage on the beach — spars, a ship's boat stove in, some crates. That's all ordinary enough after a blow like that."

"Wreckage," Halloran said. "And no bodies?"

"One man. Alive. A sailor off some coaster, I judged. Half drowned. I patched him up and sent him on his way when he was able." The lie came smoothly, and she noted its smoothness the way one notes a blade's edge — with respect, and a certain alarm. Three years of silence and the skill came back like a language learned in childhood. *I patched him up and sent him on his way.* Wren was at this moment asleep upstairs, feverish still, his ribs strapped, eating her bread and calling her ma'am in a voice that cracked.

"Sailors are lucky people," Halloran said mildly. "Or unlucky. Depending on how you count." He wrote something. "This survivor — did he give a name?"

"He was in no condition to talk much. He left before I thought to ask."

"Left. Walking?"

"There's a coast road."

"Mm." Halloran turned the page. "You'll forgive my thoroughness. A vessel did go down along this stretch around that date — a small freighter, the *Kestrel*, out of Milford. Her papers were irregular, to put it kindly. Running cargo that wasn't cargo, if you follow me. There are those in the Ministry very interested in what she carried and who she met." He looked up, and his eyes were pale blue and entirely without malice. "You didn't happen to see her?"

"I saw a storm," Zephyrine said. "Storms don't show colours."

"No," he agreed. "They don't. But they do show lights." He let that sit for a moment, then closed the notebook halfway, as if to signal the interview's formal end, and took a slow look around the kitchen — at the dresser, the range, the row of preserves on the high shelf, the door to the stairs. His gaze touched each object the way a customs officer touches luggage. Not searching. Cataloguing.

"You live here alone," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Since my husband died."

"Ah. My condolences. How long?"

"Six years."

"And before your husband — you came from...?"

"England. A long time ago. There's nothing there now."

"Everyone says that," Halloran said softly. "About the places they've left. And it's nearly always true, and nearly always a lie." He rose, replaced his hat, thanked her for the tea with genuine warmth, and paused at the door. "Two things, Mrs. Pemberton, and then I'll trouble you no further. First: my colleagues from the Ministry may call. Two gentlemen. They're looking for a missing courier — a man carrying documents, apparently of no great importance, but procedures being procedures." He pronounced *procedures* the way other men pronounced a curse. "If such a person passed through, washed ashore, turned up at your door — you'd send word to the station at Carrickmore, wouldn't you?"

"If someone needed sending word about," Zephyrine said, "I'd send it."

"Second." He settled his hat. "Your brother."

The word *brother* went through her like a fish-gaff through a hake's jaw. She did not move. Twenty years of practice held her face in place, and she was grateful to every one of those years in that instant.

"What brother?"

"Oscar Pemberton. Killed at Gallipoli, 1915 — the lists were published, it would have been in the papers here. I only mention it because—" Halloran consulted the notebook, though Zephyrine was quite sure he did not need to. "—because I interviewed the postmistress at Carrickmore yesterday. Mrs. Calloway. Thorough woman. She mentioned that the keeper at Gullwrack had lost a brother at the Dardanelles, and that the keeper never once spoke of him. Never spoke of him, Mrs. Calloway said. Four years, and not a word." He smiled his decent, terrible smile. "Grief takes people differently. Some shout it from rooftops. Others lock it in a room and post the key back to God. But a historian notices silences. Silences are where the interesting things are kept."

"Is there a question in there, Mr. Halloran?"

"No question at all. Only an observation, freely given, as yours were freely given to me." He stepped out onto the path and turned back once. "When the histories are written, they'll say what we let them say. Every one of us holds a piece of the pen. Think about which piece is yours."

Then he was walking away down the cliff path, umbrella furled, satchel swinging, pausing twice more to admire the view.

* * *

She did not go up to Wren immediately. She washed the cups. She banked the fire. She wound the clock that did not need winding. These were the motions her body performed while her mind ran ahead of her, the way a horse runs ahead of a cart it no longer trusts.

*Calloway told him.* Or Calloway mentioned it carelessly — but Calloway was never careless; Calloway weighed every syllable she released into the world like a moneylender weighing coin. Which meant either the old woman had been frightened, or the old woman had been clever. Zephyrinne could not yet tell which possibility was worse. If frightened, then Halloran's questions had already gone deeper than courtesy. If clever — if Calloway had fed him the brother deliberately, a morsel to satisfy an appetite she hoped to turn away from larger meat — then the network around her stove was already making decisions on her behalf, and Zephyrine had not been consulted.

She climbed the narrow stair.

Wren was awake, propped against the headboard, grey-faced but lucid, watching the window where the gulls wheeled. He read her expression before she said anything — she saw him do it, saw the courier's reflex wake behind his eyes.

"Someone came."

"A man named Halloran. He writes the official history of this coast for the peace delegation."

"History." Wren almost laughed; it cost him a wince. "They've started writing it before it's finished ending. That's quick work, even for them."

"He knows about Oscar."

That got him upright. "Knows what, exactly?"

"That I had a brother. Dead at Gallipoli. He doesn't know the rest — I don't think — but he knows the shape of the hole, and he told me to my face that silences are where the interesting things are kept." She sat on the edge of the bed, and heard her own voice come out low and even, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "He also said two men from the Ministry are coming. Looking for a missing courier. They may bring a warrant."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "How long do we have?"

"Days. Perhaps less. He'll write up his notes tonight. Whatever he puts in that book about me will travel faster than any launch on this water." She looked toward the window, toward the tower standing patient against the grey. "I've spent twenty years building a life no one looks at, Wren. Today a man with a notebook looked straight at it and called it interesting."

"Then we move the packet tonight," Wren said. "Before they come with paper and authority."

"Where?"

"Calloway moved it once already. She'll have a place in mind. But there's something else." He pulled himself straighter, and the fever-brightness in his eyes had hardened into something steadier. "Halloran's right about one thing, whether he means to be or not. Once the armistice is signed, the records seal. Everything. For decades. If Oscar's testimony isn't on official paper before that happens, it dies in the dark with everything else." He met her gaze. "It has to be entered somewhere real, Mrs. Pemberton. Somewhere they can't burn. An inquiry. A commission. Anything with a seal and a clerk."

"And to enter it," Zephyrine said slowly, feeling the words arrive in her mouth like stones carried up from deep water, "someone will have to stand up and say whose brother wrote them. Say it aloud. Under her own name."

The lamp turned above them, steady, indifferent, sweeping its white fan across the water and the dark and the water again.

"Say it under mine," Zephyrine said.
