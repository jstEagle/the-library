## 12. Intercepted

The warrant came on the third morning after the fog lifted, and it arrived the way bad weather does at Gullwrack Point — first as a wrongness in the light, then as a sound too small to name, and only last as the thing itself.

Zephyrine was trimming the lamp wicks when she saw the boat. Not the grey navy launch she had learned to dread but a hired skiff, white-hulled, civilian-painted, the kind of boat that wanted very much not to be noticed and therefore could not be anything but. It hugged the coastline with the caution of a man walking past a house where he owed money. Two figures sat in it, and even at that distance she knew their silhouettes: the tall one with his cap set square, the shorter one who moved his hands when he talked.

Hobbes. And the one whose name she had never been given, only shown on a card held at a distance like a communion wafer.

She set down the trimming scissors. Her hands were steady. That was the strange mercy of her life — that in the moments when steadiness mattered most, her body simply provided it, the way a lighthouse provides light without being asked whether it wishes to shine.

*They've come back because they know something,* she told herself. *Or because they suspect something. Or because men like this never stop coming back once they've started.*

Three days ago they had stood in her kitchen with their rehearsed courtesy and their questions sharpened to needles, asking after a missing courier, a man named Wren, an oilskin packet. She had lied to them — fluently, cleanly, for the first time in years feeling the old machinery of concealment turn over inside her like an engine long kept oiled against just such a morning. She had told them Wren was a shipwrecked sailor, half-mad from exposure, gone along the coast road toward Cork. She had watched them write it down and had thought: *that's done, then.*

It was not done. Nothing was ever done. That was the lesson of twenty years she kept forgetting precisely because remembering it hurt so much.

She went down the spiral stair two steps at a time, out into the cold bright air, across the shingle path to the cottage where smoke rose thin and ordinary from the chimney. Inside, Wren stood at the window with his coat already on.

He had heard them too. Of course he had. The man had spent the war being hunted by his own side; he read approaching footsteps the way she read cloud banks.

"They're early," he said. His voice was level but his eyes were not. "The warrant. I told you they'd have one by now. I said three days."

"You said four."

"I said three." He turned from the window. He looked better than he had — color back in his face, the fever long broken, the cut above his eye healed to a pale seam — but he still moved like a man whose body remembered things his mind refused to. He crossed to the hearth and crouched, checking behind the loose stone where they had hidden nothing for three days, because Mrs. Calloway had taken the oilskin packet away in her market basket the morning after the Ministry men's first visit, sewn into the lining of a flour sack, carried through the village under the eyes of everyone and no one.

"Is it safe?" Zephyrine asked. Not *where is it* — never that, not anymore. They had agreed: neither would know where the letters slept. If one of them broke, the other could honestly say they did not know. It was Mrs. Calloway's rule, delivered over tea with the mild authority of a woman who had buried a husband and raised six children and run the post office through two wars' worth of other people's secrets. *"Neither of you knows where it is. That way your faces stay honest even if your tongues fail you."*

"Nothing's safe," Wren said. "There's only safer, and there's only longer." He straightened. "Listen to me. They'll search the cottage. They'll find nothing here — good. But they'll want me. A courier goes missing, and a courier turns up living at a lighthouse keeper's cottage? I'm the loose thread. They don't need the packet to hang me. They need me talking."

"Then don't talk."

He smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on a living face. "Everyone says that until the second day. You can beat the first day on pride. The second day takes technique." He flexed his fingers, looking at them as though they belonged to someone else. "I have some technique. Not enough. But some."

Outside, gravel crunched. Boots on the path. A knock — polite, measured, three raps exactly alike.

Wren looked at her, and in that look passed everything that needed passing between them: the plan they had made and the plan they now needed instead, the fear they were both carrying and pretending not to carry, and beneath all of it something that had no tactical value whatsoever and that both of them were careful never to name.

"Back door," he murmured. "The rocks below the point. Halloran's boat could—"

"No."

"Zephyrine—"

"No." She said it quietly, and it landed harder than shouting. "If you run, they take me apart instead. And if they take me apart, they find what I know, which is more than what you know. Sit down. Have tea. Be a shipwrecked sailor with a head injury who remembers nothing." She was moving toward the door as she spoke, arranging her face into its keeper's expression — flat, weathered, faintly bored. "Let me answer the knock. It's my door."

"It's my grave they're digging."

"Then we'll discuss the depth of it afterward."

She opened the door.

Hobbes stood on her step with his cap square and his smile square and a folded paper in his gloved hand, and behind him the smaller man — Voss, she'd finally caught the name on the second visit, Voss of the Ministry's Records and Requisitions section, whatever that meant — was already looking past her shoulder into the cottage with the frank appetite of a man reading another man's mail.

"Mrs. Sandoval." Hobbes offered the paper. "I'm afraid we return with less pleasant business. A warrant authorizing search of these premises and the detention for questioning of one Thomas Wren, believed to be sheltering here under false pretenses. I trust you'll forgive the formality."

*Under false pretenses.* She took the paper and read it slowly, letting her finger trace the lines, letting them believe she needed the time to decipher official language. In truth she was buying seconds for her heart to settle and for Wren, behind her, to arrange himself — she heard the scrape of a chair, the clink of the teapot lid, the performance of an invalid's afternoon beginning.

"This says the Crown," she said. "This says His Majesty's Government has jurisdiction over my home?"

"The war grants unusual jurisdictions, ma'am. We hope briefly."

"The war ended eleven days ago."

"The paperwork, Mrs. Sandoval, is still catching up." Hobbes's smile didn't move. It was a painted smile, nailed to the face, and behind it his eyes did all the actual work. "May we come in?"

She stepped aside.

They came in the way water comes into a holed hull — politely at first, then everywhere. Voss went straight to the shelves, tilting books, running fingers along spines. Hobbes took the main room with methodical courtesy, lifting cushions, opening the sea chest, apologizing under his breath for each intrusion as though apology were a solvent that dissolved trespass. Two soldiers waited outside on the path, young, bored, holding rifles with the casual cruelty of men who had not yet decided what the rifles were for.

Zephyrine poured tea. Four cups. It cost her something to pour the fourth and hand it to Voss, who accepted it without thanks, and it cost her more to watch Wren receive his own cup with the blank gratitude of a simple sailor. He played the part well. Too well, perhaps — his hands trembled slightly as he took it, and she couldn't tell how much was performance and how much was the honest shake of a man watching his future arrive in a warrant.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find. Mrs. Calloway had seen to that with a flour sack and forty years of practice at moving things that mustn't be seen moving. Zephyrine watched Hobbes close the empty sea chest and felt something loosen in her chest — and then immediately tighten again, because emptiness was not safety. Emptiness was only the pause before the question.

And here it came.

"Well." Hobbes dusted his palms together as though the cottage itself had dirtied them. "The premises appear to be in order. Which leaves us the matter of Mr. Wren." He turned to the chair by the fire. "Thomas Wren. Courier. Employed through intermediaries to transport documents from France to Ireland. Last seen boarding a coastal packet at Dunmore East. And found — how shall we put it — convalescing at Gullwrack Point."

"Shipwrecked," Zephyrine said. "As I told you before."

"Yes. As you told us before." Hobbes sipped his tea. "Only here is our difficulty, ma'am. There was no wreck reported on this stretch of coast in the relevant week. No wreckage washed up. No distress signals logged. The sea, in short, tells no story about this man — which means someone else must be telling it for him." He set down the cup. "You see our problem."

"The sea doesn't report to the Ministry?" Zephyrine said. "Then the Ministry should spend less time reading warrants and more time reading tides."

Something flickered across Voss's face — the first genuine expression she'd seen on him, and it looked almost like admiration, which frightened her more than hostility would have.

Hobbes laughed, a dry single note. "You're a remarkable woman, Mrs. Sandoval. Truly. I mean that." He turned to Wren. "Mr. Wren. You'll come with us. Routine inquiries. A few days at most — a comfortable room, decent food, questions asked politely and answered the same way." He paused. "Or not politely. That part depends entirely on the answers."

"And if he refuses?" Zephyrine asked.

"He signed papers when he took the work, ma'am. Papers with clauses in them he likely didn't read. Men rarely do." Hobbes stood. "The clauses are quite thorough."

Wren set down his cup. The sound of porcelain on saucer was very small and very loud. He rose, and for a moment he looked at Zephyrine, and the whole cottage seemed to contract around that look.

"Don't follow," he said lightly. Loudly enough for the room. Quietly enough for her. "You don't know me. Shipwrecked sailor, head injury, remembers nothing. That's the story. Keep telling it."

"I don't know what he means," Zephyrine said to Hobbes. "He talks strangely since the water. Doctors say drowning does that to some men."

"Doctors say many things." Hobbes gestured, and the two soldiers came in from the path, and the room filled with the smell of wet wool and gun oil and government.

They walked Wren out between them. At the door he stopped, turned half around, and looked back — not at her face but at the room, at the lamp oil on the shelf, at the tide tables stacked by the window, at the whole small fortress of her silence — and then at her, and he touched two fingers to his brow in a mockery of a salute that was not a mockery at all.

"Mind the light keeps turning," he said.

"It always turns," Zephyrine said.

And then he was gone, down the path between the soldiers, out to the white-hulled skiff, and the water took him the way the water takes everything at Gullwrack Point — without ceremony, without argument, leaving only a wake that smoothed itself away as though he had never been.

* * *

She stood at the window until the skiff vanished south around the headland. Then she stood at the window a while longer, because standing was easier than thinking and thinking was easier than feeling, and she intended to do all three in strict order or none at all.

*I promised to keep him safe.* The words arrived without permission and sat down in her chest like an uninvited guest. Three days ago, in Mrs. Calloway's kitchen, over bread and strong tea, she had said it aloud: *he carried Oscar's letters through water and gunfire to get them here; I will not let them take him.* And Mrs. Calloway had nodded, and Halloran the deserter had grunted, and Nurse Pike — quiet Nurse Pike, who had seen enough dying to recognize it in advance — had said only, *"Then we'd best be cleverer than they are."*

They had been clever. The oilskin was gone, sewn into flour, sleeping in God-knew-what cupboard of the postmistress's back room. The letters were safe.

Wren was not.

That was the arithmetic of it, and she hated arithmetic, she had always hated it, ever since the war had taught her that numbers were how governments counted the dead without ever having to imagine them. The letters were safe; the man was taken. Protection, she thought, is not a wall you build. It's a roof with holes in it, and the rain falls through onto whoever stands nearest, and today the rain had chosen Wren.

She made herself move. Grief in motion was manageable; grief at rest bred. She banked the fire, checked the lamp mechanism though it needed no checking, wound the clock though it was true to the minute. Then she took down her coat and her boots and walked the cliff path north to the village, because there was one thing the Ministry men had not searched, one thing their warrant had no line-item for, and that was the interior of Edith Calloway's kitchen.

The postmistress opened her door before Zephyrine knocked. She had seen the skiff pass the harbor mouth — of course she had — and she had already put the kettle on.

"He's taken, then," Mrs. Calloway said. It was not a question. Nothing in her kitchen ever was.

"Taken for questioning. Hobbes called it routine."

"Hobbes would call a firing squad routine." Mrs. Calloway poured tea without asking, pushed the cup across the scrubbed table, and sat, folding her hands. Behind her, on the dresser, sat a plain flour sack among other flour sacks, indistinguishable, patient. "Now. Before you say anything, girl, understand: I've been doing sums since dawn. Here's mine. They took Wren because they think he knows where the packet is. He doesn't. You don't. I do. Which means every hour they sit on him, they learn nothing, and every hour he sits in their cell, he suffers for knowing nothing — and a man suffering for nothing has only so much nothing left in him before he starts inventing somethings. Do you follow?"

"He won't talk. He said he has technique."

"Technique." Mrs. Calloway sniffed. "My Tom had technique too, God rest him. Held out three days in a German camp before they broke him. Everyone breaks. The trick isn't preventing it. The trick is making sure that when a man breaks, what spills out is worth nothing." She leaned forward. "So. How long do we have before he invents a hiding place? A day? Two? And when he does, they'll tear this village apart, and they'll start with the postmistress, because couriers always end at the post."

Zephyrine stared into her tea. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the flour sack needs to move again, tonight, somewhere they'll never think to look — and I'm saying we have perhaps thirty-six hours to decide whether we're hiding evidence or delivering it." Mrs. Calloway's eyes, mild as milk, fixed on her. "Because there's a journalist in Cork waiting on those letters, and there's a deadline attached, and there's a man in a cell who took your brother's truth through the surf on his back. Hiding it forever protects no one. Delivering it might save him. Might. If we're fast."

Through the kitchen window, far off, the lighthouse turned above the point, steady as a pulse.

"Fast," Zephyrine repeated. The word felt strange in her mouth, like a key to a door she'd kept locked for twenty years. "Edith. What exactly are you proposing?"

Mrs. Calloway reached for the flour sack.
