## 13. The Long Dark Watch

The storm had spent itself by midnight, and what remained was the long quiet after — the kind of quiet that pressed against the windows like a hand. Zephyrine climbed the tower stairs with the decoded papers sewn into a canvas satchel she carried against her hip, the way other women carried infants. She had not meant to bring them up here. Up here was work: the wick, the clockwork, the mercury bath that let the great lens float and turn. But her hands had made the decision before her mind consented, and now she stood in the lantern room with Oscar's cipher spread across the chart table like a wound opened for cleaning.

The lamp turned above her. Four seconds bright, four seconds dark, the beam sweeping out over black water and returning again, faithful as breathing. She had lived beneath that rhythm for six years at Gullwrack, and longer than that if you counted the lights at Skerries and Cape Clear, before the marriage, before the name. Twenty years of keeping things lit so that other people would not break against rocks they could not see.

She wondered what it said about her that she had never once thought of herself as one of those ships.

* * *

She worked first, because working was the only prayer she trusted. The tide tables appended to each letter were genuine — Oscar had always been meticulous, even as a boy labeling his tin soldiers by regiment — but the marginal figures were his own arithmetic, the cipher Mrs. Calloway's sharp eyes had first suspected and Zephyrine's long nights had finally broken. A substitution built on the old signal-book code their father had taught them both on the estuary, when they were children playing at war without knowing what war cost.

The pages told her things she had already half-guessed and some things she had not. The village was called Ardmore Beg — no, the letters spelled it differently, Ard Mór Beag, transliterated badly by an English clerk's hand — shelled for three hours on the fourth of June, 1915, by naval guns whose ranging officers had walked its streets the week before posing as cartographers. Forty-one dead. The number was written twice, once in Oscar's careful figures and once underlined, as though he feared even his own future self might flinch from it.

And beneath that, names. Captain Ellery Voss. Major Havelock Staines. A lieutenant called Prydderch who had refused the order and been listed among the enemy dead. And at the bottom of the third page, in ink darker than the rest, as if written later and pressed hard: *They know I have this. That is why I am dead already. If you are reading this, I am sorry, and I am alive, and one of those things is more important than the other.*

Zephyrine read that line eleven times. On the twelfth she stopped counting, because counting was how she held herself together and she wanted, just then, to come apart a little — privately, in the rotating light, where no one living could see.

* * *

At two o'clock she went down for tea she did not drink and came back up with two sheets of paper and the good pen, the one with the nib Oscar had given her for her seventeenth birthday, before everything. She set the evidence aside. Evidence was for inquiries, ministries, historians with decent faces. What she held now was smaller and harder: a decision, and she had learned that decisions were best made the way lamps were trimmed — deliberately, in the dark, before the weather came.

She wrote at the top of the first sheet: *If I say nothing.*

Her pen moved steadily. That was the strange thing; it did not hesitate. Perhaps because this version had been drafted for years, revised nightly in the ledger of her silence, and tonight she was only copying out the fair copy.

*If I say nothing:* Wren goes free within the week. The Ministry men are thorough but they are not patient, and a courier who can prove he never knew the contents of his packet is worth more to them walking than buried. He would take the coast road south and vanish into the enormous postwar anonymity of Europe, and he would carry his guilt like a stone in his coat, and he would survive it, because men like Wren always survive. She would go back to trimming the wick. The log would record storms, shipping, routine maintenance. In ten years the armistice archives would seal themselves for a generation, and whatever clerk inherited Oscar's file would stamp it CLOSED and shelve it in a room she would never see, next to forty-one other files that ended in numbers instead of names.

And Oscar — Oscar would wait in the Quaker house outside Dublin with his ruined lungs and his unrepentant heart, waiting for a delivery that never came, until either the safe house was found or his body simply concluded what the War Office had begun. He would die officially dead, which meant he would die unmourned by anyone but her, and she would learn of it the way she learned of Gallipoli: a printed line, a form, someone else's tidy hand.

*If I say nothing,* she wrote at the bottom, *I keep my life.*

Then, because honesty was the one discipline she had never abandoned: *I keep what is left of my life. Which is the light, and the silence, and the shape of a woman where a person used to be.*

The lamp swept round. Bright, dark. Bright, dark. It caught the page and released it, caught and released, like something deciding.

* * *

The second sheet took longer.

She sat with the pen uncapped for a long while, watching the beam travel the water, and understood that the difficulty was not fear. Fear she knew intimately; fear was an old lodger who paid no rent and asked no questions. The difficulty was that every word she wrote on this second page would be a word taken out of the first, and she had built twenty years on the architecture of subtraction.

*If I speak.* She began.

*If I speak:* the inquiry convenes — it was already convening, the historian's advance party had seen to that, polite letters and politer questions compiling the official narrative before the ink on the armistice was dry — and she walks into some requisitioned customs house in Cork or Queenstown and says her name. Not Pemberton. Not the name she traded away in a church vestry in 1898 to a kind, dull man who wanted a keeper's widow for a wife and got a keeper instead. Her name. The one on the parish register in Wexford, the one that connected her to a boy who labeled his tin soldiers and grew into a man who counted corpses because no one else would.

Saying it aloud would end everything she had constructed. The Ministry men would not need warrants after that; they would need only patience. They would find the marriage that dissolved, the letters never sent, the twenty years of small silences, and they would assemble her into something suspicious — a woman who hid, therefore a woman with something to hide. The lighthouse posting would be reviewed. Her character would be reviewed. She had watched enough committees work to know that a woman alone was always guilty of the crime of being alone.

And Wren — Wren was in a barracks cell tonight because of her promise to protect him. If she spoke and the machinery turned on her, it would turn on him first, because he was easier to break. She had promised. She had looked a man in the eye across her own kitchen table, a man who informed on a dockworker to feed his children and had spent years trying to pay the debt, and she had said *you will be safe here*, and promises were the only currency she had never debased.

But.

But the second page had its own column of costs, and she forced herself to write them too, because a ledger with only losses on one side was not a ledger; it was a wish.

*If I speak:* Oscar's testimony enters the record. Not sealed — entered. Copied. Three copies, three hands, scattered beyond any single ministry's reach. Forty-one people who died as a rounding error in a field report would die instead as forty-one names, and Lieutenant Prydderch, who refused, would be a man who refused rather than a discrepancy. Voss and Staines would wake tomorrow knowing that history had teeth. Perhaps nothing would happen to them — the war forgave its officers with astonishing appetite — but something would happen to the record, and records were the only immortality ordinary people got.

And Oscar would not wait alone. Whatever came of the inquiry — verdict, no verdict, whitewash, ruin — he would know that the packet arrived. That the last thing he built in this world reached the hands it was built for. She thought of the line written in darker ink. *I am sorry, and I am alive.* He had apologized for surviving. She intended to make him take it back.

*If I speak,* she wrote, *I lose my life and get back a person I have not met in twenty years.*

She looked at the sentence a long time. Then she added, in smaller letters, the way one adds a figure one has checked three times: *It may be worth meeting her.*

* * *

Toward four o'clock the wind shifted north and brought the cold with it, and she heard the sea change its voice below the point — a deeper note, the sound of water finding new rock. She rose stiffly to check the clockwork, oil the escapement, perform the small liturgies that kept the light honest. This was the hour when loneliness used to come for her, in the old years, before she had learned to call the loneliness peace. Tonight it did not come. Tonight there was too much company in her head: a brother writing in dark ink, a courier sleeping off sedatives in the cottage below, a postmistress who moved contraband oilskin packets the way other women moved flowerpots, a nurse, a deserter, a historian with a decent face, two Ministry men rehearsing courtesy. Twenty years of solitude, undone in nine days by one drowned-looking man on the shingle.

She almost laughed. Almost.

The truth she circled, unable to land on, was this: both futures were survivable. That was the obscenity of it. She could stay silent and live, diminished but intact, and the sea would keep giving back its fog and fish and occasional corpses, and she would grow old the way lighthouses grow old — useful, unmanned, enduring. Or she could speak and live, hunted but present, and discover whether the woman under the silence was anyone at all. There was no version in which the choice killed her outright. The cost was subtler than death. The cost was paid out slowly, in the currency of selves, and the exchange rate differed, and she was the only auditor who would ever review the books.

Wren had told her once, feverish, in the second week: *I didn't choose to inform on him. I chose between two bad things and pretended one of them was neutral.* She had pitied him then. Standing at the chart table with two pages drying in the lamplight, she recognized the maneuver as her own native tongue.

* * *

Dawn did not so much arrive as seep, gray filling the east like watercolor into wet paper. Below her the cottage chimney put up its smoke — Wren was awake, then, and had managed the stove, and had probably burned the porridge, and the small domesticity of it made her throat tighten in a way she refused to examine.

She gathered the two pages. She did not burn either one; that surprised her. Instead she folded them together, the silent life and the speaking life, and slid them into the back of the official logbook, behind the year's records, where no searcher looking for seditious papers would think to look among tide heights and lamp consumption. Let them wait there together, those two women she might become.

On her way down the stair she paused at the window where the lens swept past, and for a moment she saw her reflection riding the glass, doubled by the mirror panels behind it — one Zephyrine facing the sea, one facing the land, and the light passing through both of them every four seconds as if it could not tell the difference.

The lamp did not decide anything. She had kept enough lights to know that. A lamp only showed you the rocks; steering was the helmsman's sin or salvation, always.

She came down into the kitchen smelling of scorched oats, and Wren stood at the stove looking sheepish and bruised and alive, and Mrs. Calloway's knock came at the door earlier than any postmistress had business knocking — and the look on her face, when Zephyrine opened it, was the look of a woman carrying news she had rehearsed not to drop.

"They've moved him," she said, before the door was fully open. "The fisherman's boy saw the van leave the barracks at five this morning. Southbound. They're taking your Mr. Wren to Cork."
