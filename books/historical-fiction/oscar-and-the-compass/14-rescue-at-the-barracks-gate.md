## 14. Rescue at the Barracks Gate

They took his boots.

That was the detail Wren fixed on, in the first hour, because it was the only detail he could do anything about. They had taken his boots at the gate of the barracks at Dunmore Head — a corrugated iron compound that smelled of wet wool and carbolic and the ghost of horses — and a corporal with a chilblained face had said it was standard procedure, nothing personal, and Wren had stood in his stockinged feet on the cold flagstones while a man with a ledger wrote down *T. Wren, no trade given, no address given* as though he were cargo to be inventoried rather than a man who could be made to disappear.

He had been made to disappear before. He knew the machinery of it. He had fed men into it himself, once upon a time, when he was nineteen and hungry and told himself that a dockworker who organized strike committees was fair game because he had no family, no one to miss him, no one whose life would gutter out when he did. Wren had signed a statement then, too. He remembered how easy the pen moved. That was the thing nobody warned you about — not the cruelty of the machine but its tidiness, its ledgers, its standard procedures, its chilblained corporals who meant you no personal harm while they processed you into nothing.

So he gave them what they wanted from him, which was nothing, and he did it by giving them everything else.

"Routine inquiries," the captain had said. Captain Aldous Vane — Wren had read the name off the desk plate in the interrogation room, a small brass rectangle screwed slightly crooked, and had thought: *a man who screws down a nameplate crooked is a man who does not expect visitors who matter.* The room itself was a converted tack room, still smelling faintly of liniment, a table bolted to the floor, two chairs, a high window with frosted glass through which the afternoon came down like milk.

"Routine inquiries," Vane repeated now, folding his hands. He was maybe forty, soft at the middle, with the kind of face designed for parish committee photographs. "You were found on the coast road near Gullwrack Point, half-dead, carrying nothing. Is that right?"

"That's right."

"No papers?"

"Washed ashore without them." This was true, or true enough. The oilskin packet was gone — sewn into Zephyrine's coat lining by now, if Mrs. Calloway's plan had held — and the letters' cipher hid inside tide tables that would look like nothing more than arithmetic to any man who opened them. He had memorized what mattered anyway, in the fever nights at the cottage: dates, ship names, the village called Kilmorra that appeared on no casualty list. He carried it all behind his eyes where no ledger could reach.

Vane studied him. "You understand we're at some pains to account for every stranger moving along this coast. There's an armistice coming, they say. Negotiations. And when peace comes, there will be men writing histories, and historians require tidy records. Every courier accounted for. Every packet logged." He smiled. "You'd be amazed what turns up in the files afterward. Things a man thought were buried."

"I wouldn't be amazed," Wren said. "I worked the docks at Cork for six years. I know exactly what turns up in files."

Something flickered across Vane's face — recognition, perhaps, or the memory of a file with Wren's own signature in it. Wren watched the flicker and understood that Vane knew him, or knew of him, and that this was either very good or very bad.

"You were an informant," Vane said mildly.

"I was hungry."

"And now?"

"Now I deliver parcels."

"For whom?"

"For whoever pays the freight."

The captain leaned back. Through the frosted glass, somewhere beyond, a bugle sounded the change of watch, thin and mournful. Wren counted the notes automatically — he couldn't help it; four years of coastal living had made clocks of his ears — and used the count to slow his breathing. Panic was a tide. You didn't beat it. You waited for it to turn.

Here was what he knew about tides, and here was the thought he kept returning to as the hours went on: the barracks sat at the head of Dunmore harbor, and Dunmore harbor was a drying harbor. At low water the channel emptied to a ribbon of mud and shingle wide enough to walk across, barely ankle-deep at the deepest channel mark, for nearly an hour either side of slack. He had run fish out of this coast long enough to know it the way you know your own pulse. The patrol launch that everyone feared drew six feet even light. It could not come within three hundred yards of the gate at low water. Whatever anyone intended tonight, they would have to do it on foot, or wait for the flood.

He filed this away the way he had once filed away the shift patterns of picket captains, and hated himself a little for how naturally the old skill came back.

* * *

The interrogations came in shifts, which told him they expected a long stay, which told him someone above Vane wanted him broken slowly rather than quickly, which told him they believed he knew something worth breaking for.

The first evening it was a sergeant named Doyle who smelled of pipe smoke and asked friendly questions about fishing grounds. The second morning it was Vane again, patient as erosion. The third day a new man, younger, with ink-stained fingers — a clerk, a historian's advance man, one of the tidy ones — who spread photographs on the table and asked him to identify faces. One of the photographs was of a woman standing outside a whitewashed cottage with a lighthouse beyond, her face turned half away.

"Do you know her?" the clerk asked.

"She looks like every keeper's wife on this coast," Wren said. "They all dress the same. It's the salt air."

"The keeper at Gullwrack Point is a widow. Pemberton. Quiet woman. Keeps to herself."

"Most widows do."

The clerk gathered up the photographs with fingers that never hurried. "Mr. Wren. A word of professional advice. In a few weeks — perhaps days — the war ends, and everything that happened in it gets sealed. Sealed records are a kind of mercy, if you think about it. What's written down stays written; what isn't written down stops existing. Some men spend their whole lives trying to become unwritten." He tucked the folder under his arm. "We can arrange that for you. Or we can write you down thoroughly. It depends entirely on how cooperative the next twenty-four hours are."

After he left, Wren sat with the tide tables running in his head. Low water tomorrow: 4:40 in the morning. The window would open around three and close near five and a half. If anyone was coming, it would be in that black cold hour, and it would be clumsy, and it might get them all killed.

He lay on the plank bed and did the only useful thing left to him, which was to be uncooperative in ways that looked like stupidity. When Doyle asked about shipping lanes, Wren gave him a lecture so dense with sandbars and spring-tide corrections that the sergeant's eyes glazed. When Vane pressed on the packet, Wren confessed to smuggling tobacco, detailed and remorseful, and produced a confession of such tedious specificity — brands, weights, contact points, prices — that it consumed two full interviews and led nowhere except toward a fine he could never pay. He watched Vane realize, around the fourth hour of the third day, that his prisoner was either a fool or a fortress, and that there was no way to tell which without spending time they did not have.

Time. That was the other clock ticking. Armistice rumors ran through the barracks like fire through stubble. Men spoke of signing dates in whispers and got shouted at for jinxing it. Wren heard a lance-corporal say *Thursday, they're saying Thursday* and felt the floor tilt under him. If the record sealed on Thursday, then every hour Vane spent extracting nothing from Thomas Wren was an hour the Ministry could have spent finding the letters.

Keep me boring, he told himself. Be the most boring man in Ireland. Be tobacco. Be sandbars.

On the fourth night, as the flood tide began to whisper against the harbor wall, they took his statement again — the same questions, the same order, a ritual now — and Wren answered in the same words, and noticed that Vane had stopped taking notes. Noting nothing. That was bad. A man noting nothing has decided what you are.

"You'll be transferred," Vane said, capping his pen. "Cork, probably. There are better facilities for thorough work."

"When?"

"Soon."

* * *

They came at twenty past three in the morning, and it was a catastrophe from the first moment, and it saved his life.

Wren woke not to sound but to silence — the sentry's boots on the gravel path outside had stopped — and then the silence filled with small wrong noises: a clink, a muffled curse, the wet slap of canvas. He was off the plank bed and standing barefoot on the flags before his mind caught up with his body.

The window of the cell — barred, but the bars set in rotten mortar, mortar gone soft with a century of sea damp — flexed when he pushed it. He hadn't tried before. Why would he? Hope was a luxury that got informants killed. But the bars moved like bad teeth, and he worked the middle one back and forth, back and forth, flaking rust and crumbling lime, listening to the small noises outside resolve into voices.

One voice he knew. Colm Halloran — the deserter fisherman from Mrs. Calloway's kitchen, the big quiet man who mended nets and never talked about the uniform buried under his floorboards. His voice came low and urgent through the dark: *"The channel's dry till five, we walked the whole way over the mudflat, don't ask me why I agreed to this—"*

Another voice answered, female, fierce, whispered: *"Because I asked you to, you great coward, now hold the blanket up."*

Nora Calloway. The postmistress. Sixty-one years old and holding a horse blanket up against the frost-glazed window of the barracks latrine block like a screen in a photographer's studio.

It was, objectively, the worst rescue plan ever conceived by human beings. Later Wren would learn its full architecture: that Halloran had spent two evenings drinking with the sentries and learned the night watch changed at three and the relief man always stopped at the cookhouse for tea; that Mrs. Calloway had established, through three weeks of telegrams she had personally routed, that the duty officer's log showed Wren scheduled for transfer on the morning tide; that the whole scheme rested on a blanket, a bar of soap rubbed into the latch mechanism, a borrowed bicycle with a wicker basket, and the mudflat. No guns. Guns would make it a crime; without guns it was merely unlawful, and Mrs. Calloway had drawn this distinction with the gravity of a bishop. *"We are not criminals," she had said. "We are irregular postal workers."*

The middle bar came free with a crack that sounded, to Wren, like a cathedral bell.

Everything after that was noise and terror. He squeezed through — shoulders screaming, the broken bar tearing his shirt and the skin beneath — dropped onto frozen mud, and found himself face to face with Halloran, who thrust a pair of boots at him.

"Yours," Halloran breathed. "Stole 'em back off the stores shelf. Put them on walking, not standing, we've got a mile of mudflat and the tide doesn't care about your feelings—"

They went over the wall by way of a coal heap and a stack of lobster pots that collapsed under Mrs. Calloway, who swore with astonishing fluency and got up without stopping. Then the mudflat, the drying harbor, the world reduced to gray ooze and starlight and the sucking grab of the ground under every step. Wren ran badly and fell twice. Behind them, alarm bells began — late, confused, because a sentry reporting a missing prisoner also had to report a missing bar, a missing blanket, and a postmistress's bicycle abandoned artistically against the guardroom door as though she'd simply called on official business and stepped inside.

"They'll bring the launch," Wren gasped.

"Launch can't float on a dry harbor," Halloran panted. "Six-foot draft. They'll have to wait for water, and by water we're—" he stumbled, swore, hauled himself upright, "—we're over the ridge and into Calloway's cousin's cart, and after that God and the fog take over."

They crested the shingle ridge as the first search lanterns swept the empty mud below, beams swinging over miles of glistening flat where no footprints lasted longer than the tide's memory. Somewhere a whistle blew. Somewhere Captain Aldous Vane was waking to a report that would read like nonsense.

At the cart, Mrs. Calloway handed Wren a flask of tea and a telegram, both steaming in the cold.

"What's this?"

"Read it in the cart. It came for you care of my office, marked urgent." Her face, in the lantern light, was grim as slate. "And Mr. Wren — there's a second sheet. About the lighthouse. You should sit down for the second sheet."

The cart lurched forward into the fog. Wren unfolded the telegram with numb fingers, and the first lines swam, and steadied:

*OSCAR PEMBERTON ALIVE STOP QUAKER HOUSE BALLYBRACK OUTSIDE DUBLIN STOP TOO ILL TO TRAVEL STOP ASKS FOR SISTER BY HER TRUE NAME.*

And beneath it, in Mrs. Calloway's hand, added in pencil because the wire clerk refused to transmit it:

*THE MINISTRY MEN LEFT FOR GULLWRACK POINT AT MIDNIGHT WITH A WARRANT.*
