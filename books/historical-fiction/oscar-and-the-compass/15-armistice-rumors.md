## 15. Armistice Rumors

The news came to Gullwrack the way all news came — by boat, by rumor, and finally by wire, each version less reliable than the last.

Mrs. Calloway heard it first, as she heard everything first. A trawler out of Kinsale had put in at the village quay on Tuesday with its nets fouled and its crew wild-eyed, and before the fish were even ashore the youngest of them was shouting that the Americans had a telegram, that the Germans had sent envoys in a submarine, that the war would be over inside the week. He said it twice to be sure, once in English and once in the raw Gaelic of excitement, and by nightfall the village had made of it six different stories, none of which matched.

By Thursday it was in the *Cork Examiner*, which made it nearly true: armistice negotiations underway, terms under discussion, an announcement expected within days. The paper used the word *imminent*. Mrs. Calloway read it standing behind her counter, her spectacles low on her nose, and felt something cold walk up her spine that had nothing to do with the draft under the door.

She had lived through enough endings to know what they cost. Wars did not simply stop; they were closed up like ledgers, and whoever held the pen wrote the totals. She thought of the oilskin packet sleeping in the flour bin beneath her counter, wrapped now in waxed cloth against damp, and she thought of the word *imminent* sitting there in the newspaper like a fuse already lit.

That evening she put on her good shawl and walked the cliff path to the lighthouse in weather no sensible woman would have chosen, because some messages were too dangerous for the post even when you were the postmistress yourself.

* * *

Zephyrine saw her coming from the gallery and had the kettle on before the knock came. That was the arithmetic of their friendship now: one woman who noticed everything, one woman who noticed her noticing, and between them a stove that had become the warmest and most dangerous place on the coast.

"They're saying days," Mrs. Calloway said, without preamble, shrugging off the wet shawl. "Signing within the week, maybe sooner. And after—" She accepted the tea but didn't drink it. "After, they seal the records. All of them. Forty years, fifty, no one knows. Everything goes into boxes with wax seals and stays there until everyone who could contradict the boxes is dead."

Zephyrine stood at the window with her back to the room. Below her, the sea worked at the shingle with its patient, indifferent hands.

"I've known that was coming," she said quietly. "Since the day I understood what Oscar carried. The war ends, and the war's papers go into the dark, and whatever the victors want the war to have been — that's what it will have been. Officially. Forever."

"Then we're out of time."

"We were always out of time. We just didn't feel it." Zephyrine turned. In the lamplight her face was the face Mrs. Calloway had learned to read the way sailors read water — stillness on the surface, currents underneath running hard. "How much do they know? The Ministry men?"

"That the courier exists. That he went missing somewhere north of the point. They don't know about the letters' contents — if they did, they wouldn't be asking questions, they'd be doing worse than taking Wren." She set down the untouched tea. "They took him Tuesday. He's been at the barracks three days."

"I know." Zephyrine's voice did not change, but something behind it did. "Tam told me. Every day he rows out past the point and watches the gate, and every day he comes back and tells me nothing has happened, and every day I pretend that's comfort instead of torment."

"And what are we going to do about it?"

The question hung in the small warm room. Outside, the lamp turned above them, sweeping its long white arm across the water and gathering it back again, sweep and gather, sweep and gather, like a heart deciding.

"What we planned," Zephyrine said at last. "But faster. If the signing comes, we don't just lose our deadline — we lose the inquiry itself. The commission sits only while the war is technically alive. Once peace is signed, there'll be no civilian body convened, no record entered, no witnesses heard. Just sealed boxes." She looked at Mrs. Calloway steadily. "So we get it on the record first. Whatever it costs."

Mrs. Calloway nodded slowly, and then, being Mrs. Calloway, reached into her basket and produced the thing she had actually come about: a folded copy of the shipping notices, marked in her own pencil. "Then you'll want to know the patrol launch out of the naval station has been reassigned to escort duty for the armistice delegations. It leaves Friday. The water between here and Cork will be the emptiest it's been in four years."

For the first time that evening, Zephyrine smiled. It was not a comfortable smile. It was the smile of a woman looking at a door she had spent twenty years pretending wasn't there.

"Friday," she repeated.

* * *

Wren counted his days in interrogations now, the way Zephyrine counted hers in tasks.

There had been five. Each followed the same shape: the same two officers, the same tidy room with its map of the coast, the same courteous questions arranged in slightly different orders, as though truth were a lock that yielded to permutation. *Where did you take the packet. From whom. For whom. What did the man who hired you look like. Describe his hands.* They never raised their voices. That was the worst of it. Anger would have been information; courtesy was only a wall.

On the fifth day, waiting in the narrow cell with its blanket that smelled of other men's fear, Wren heard the guards talking through the door, and the talk was not about him at all.

"—signing any day now, my sister says the church bells are being looked at, oiled and tested—"

"—they'll want the band up from Cork for the celebrations—"

Armistice. Days.

He sat down slowly on the bunk and pressed his palms against his eyes and let the fact settle over him like cold water. He had known, abstractly, that the war was dying; everyone had known since September. But knowing it and feeling the floor tilt beneath the knowledge were different things. Because when the war died, the inquiry died with it. The commission convening in Cork — hastily, grudgingly, over military objections — existed only in the gap between ceasefire and signature. After the signatures, the records went into the vaults. The village. The shells falling on houses full of people who had done nothing. The officers who had ordered it and the staff who had written it up as *legitimate targets*. All of it boxed, sealed, forgotten on schedule — unless someone put it into a living record first.

Unless someone spoke while speaking was still possible.

And the someone who could speak — the someone whose handwriting proved a dead man lived, whose testimony anchored every page of that oilskin packet — was four hundred miles away in a Quaker safe house outside Dublin, too ill to travel, trusting his evidence to a failed informer locked in a barracks cell.

Wren laughed aloud, alone in the dark, a short ugly sound. Then he got up and began, methodically, to do what he had been doing since childhood in the dockside alleys of Liverpool: to study the walls, the guard rotations, the habits of the men who held him. Not escape. Not yet. But preparation. Because he had a growing suspicion — fed by Tam Beal's face appearing at odd hours beyond the perimeter fence, wearing expressions of catastrophic intent — that ordinary people were about to attempt something extraordinary and unlawful on his behalf, and the least he could do was know where the loose boards were.

* * *

Sister Agnes Deverell had delivered babies through bombardments and held dying men's hands through gas, and she had thought herself past surprise. Then she sat in Mrs. Calloway's kitchen on Thursday night and listened to a plan take shape around her widowed friend's stove, and discovered she was wrong.

They were all there. Mrs. Calloway presiding from her chair by the oven. Tam Beal perched on the woodbox turning his cap in his hands, a man assembled from guilt and courage in roughly equal parts. Young Nellie Quin from the bakery, who had appointed herself to everything since the day she'd helped move the packet and considered it the great adventure of her life. And Zephyrine, who had come down from the point in daylight for the first time in living memory, her hood back, her face bare, looking — Agnes thought — like a woman stepping out of a photograph of herself.

"The commission sits in Cork," Zephyrine was saying. "It was convened to hear claims ahead of the settlement — reparations, atrocities, anything that might affect the terms. Its authority dies at the signing. So we bring the testimony to it before the signing. Not the whole packet — they'd confiscate it and it would vanish into exactly the sealing we're trying to defeat. We enter the testimony. Oscar's account, read into the record, witnessed, copied. Three copies, three hands. Once it's in the record, it can't be quietly buried; burying it becomes a decision someone has to make in the open."

"And you'd give your name," Agnes said. It was not a question. Everyone at that stove knew what the name cost; they had all, in their separate ways, spent weeks learning the shape of Zephyrine Pemberton's silence.

"I'd give my name." Zephyrine's hands were steady around her cup. "Twenty years ago I traded it away to disappear. It turns out disappearing was only ever borrowed time. The war is ending either way. The only question is whether it ends with the truth alive or entombed."

Silence around the stove. Then Tam Beal cleared his throat, and his voice came out rougher than he intended.

"There's the matter of getting to Cork. And the matter of them." He meant the Ministry men; everyone always meant the Ministry men now. "And the matter of Wren, who's sat in that barracks going on a week for a crime that's mostly being inconvenient."

"One thing at a time," Mrs. Calloway said, in the voice she used to quiet queues and riots alike. "The launch is gone Friday. That gives us a window on the water. As for Wren—" she exchanged a glance with Tam that contained an entire conspiratorial conversation — "we're working on Wren."

"You're working on Wren?" Agnes said sharply. "Molly, he's a courier with a guilty conscience, not a cargo. What exactly—"

"He's a witness too," Nellie Quin said unexpectedly. Everyone turned. The girl flushed but held on. "Isn't he? He carried it. He can testify to the chain of custody, to where it came from, to the man who hired him. If the packet's evidence, then he's part of the evidence." She lifted her chin. "You don't leave part of the evidence in a barracks."

Agnes looked at the girl, and then at the faces around the stove — the postmistress, the deserter, the keeper who had been a ghost for twenty years and was choosing, visibly, agonizingly, to stop — and felt the strange heat rise in her chest that she had last felt in a field hospital in Flanders, that mixture of terror and purpose that she had privately named *the beginning of the thing worth doing.*

"Well then," she said. "If we're doing this, we do it properly. I'll write to the commission secretary tonight — I nursed his mother through her last winter, and he owes me a debt he's been avoiding. A formal request to hear testimony, dated and documented, before the signing. Paper first, boats second. If we arrive unannounced they can turn us away; if we arrive summoned, turning us away becomes their scandal."

"Paper first, boats second," Mrs. Calloway repeated approvingly, and took out her ledger to begin assigning tasks as though organizing a parish fete, which somehow made it all more real and more terrifying at once.

* * *

Late, when the others had gone and the stove had burned down to its red heart, Zephyrine stood in Mrs. Calloway's doorway looking east toward the point, where her lamp swept and gathered above the dark water.

"It'll work or it won't," Mrs. Calloway said behind her. "But you should know — I've watched you since you came to this coast, and I've never once seen you afraid for yourself. Only for the secret. Tonight you looked afraid for yourself, and it suited you far better."

Zephyrine huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Twenty years of practice at not existing, Molly. It turns out existence takes nerve."

"Everything worth doing does." Mrs. Calloway pressed a parcel into her hands — bread, and beneath the bread, wrapped in waxed cloth, the oilskin packet itself. "Keep it close tonight. Tomorrow Nellie sews it into the lining of your coat, and after that it doesn't leave your body until it's spoken aloud in front of God and the commission and whichever Ministry gentleman gets there first."

Zephyrine held the packet a moment, feeling through the cloth the faint ridges of the tide-table pages, the cipher she had cracked by lamplight, her brother's careful hand marching through it toward her across four years and a faked grave.

"Days," she said softly. "They say the signing is days away."

"Then we'd best not be slow." Mrs. Calloway glanced down the dark lane toward the harbor, where Tam Beal's boat knocked gently at its mooring, loaded — though he had told no one yet — with tarps, spare oars, and every scrap of courage he owned. "And Zeph. When you get to Cork. When you stand up in that room and say your name out loud for the first time in twenty years."

"Yes?"

"Say it loudly. Some of us have been waiting a long time to hear it."

Zephyrine walked home along the cliff path under a sky rinsed clean by wind, the packet against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and above her the lamp turned, and turned, and did not waver.
