## 16. The Compass Pointing Home

The tide tables had been sitting in the bread tin behind the flour bin for eleven days before I understood them.

Not the letters — those I had long since committed to memory, every damning line of them, the village called Belan-something that no longer appeared on maps because there was nothing left of it to map, the officers' names written in a hand so steady it frightened me. Steady hands had always frightened me since Gallipoli. Steady meant a man who had decided what he could live with.

But the tide tables themselves — four sheets of them, folded into the oilskin packet beneath the letters like packing straw, like an afterthought — those I had dismissed. Wren had carried them without knowing why. Mrs. Calloway had moved them twice without looking at them. And I had stacked them under the salt crock and thought: *Oscar was always tidy. He would pad his parcel properly.*

It took the storm on the eleventh night to open my eyes.

* * *

There is a particular quality to a February gale at Gullwrack Point — a sound like the whole Atlantic drawing breath to say one word and then deciding against it. I had trimmed the lamp, wound the clockwork, logged the wind at force ten with the barometer falling in a way that made my teeth ache. Then, with nothing left to do but wait for dawn, I did what I had done every sleepless night since the packet arrived: I sat at the kitchen table with the tide tables spread out before me and let my eyes move over figures I already knew by heart.

Tides are a keeper's alphabet. I had read them nightly for twenty years — heights, times, the moon's slow arithmetic. I knew what a normal table for this coast looked like the way I knew my own hands.

These were wrong.

Not dramatically. A novice would have seen nothing amiss. But the soundings column — depths in fathoms, taken at stated points — ran two digits high in certain rows. Two digits high, consistently, and only in rows whose dates fell within the span of the shelling Oscar described. Rows outside that span ran true. I checked against the Admiralty tables in the lamp room, the ones issued to all stations, and found the discrepancies repeated like a signature.

A code. Hidden in the most boring document a soldier could lay hands on, invisible to any censor, any thief, any Ministry man who flipped through the packet looking for incriminating paper. The letters were the confession; the tide tables were the *proof* — names, dates, grid references, unit designations, all of it dressed up as hydrography.

I sat very still while the gale leaned against the cottage and the lamp turned its patient circles overhead, and I understood that my brother had been playing this game for four years. Writing to a dead woman. Encoding his evidence in numbers no one reads. Trusting the sea to deliver him.

Then I saw the last page, and stopped breathing.

* * *

The final sheet was different. The soundings ran true throughout — genuine figures, or close enough — but the times of high water were impossible. Tides do not come at twenty past three and again at forty minutes past nine on the same day, not on this coast, not on any coast. Times, then. Not depths. The times spelled something if you knew how to listen.

I grew up with Oscar inventing ciphers the way other boys invented excuses. He once encoded a birthday greeting in the margins of a hymnal using the positions of flyspecks. He believed, with a child's ferocious faith, that anything worth saying was worth hiding, and anything hidden well enough became a gift — a secret kept warm for the one person clever enough to unwrap it.

The times gave numbers. Numbers past twelve wrapped around. Letters followed: 1=A, 2=B, and so on through the alphabet, then starting again with a shift — he'd always loved a shift, our Oscar, a second lock inside the first.

By the time the wind dropped toward dawn I had it, and the words stood up off the page in my own handwriting where I'd copied them out, shaking:

*ZEPHYRINE. IF THIS REACHES YOU THEN WREN DID NOT FAIL AND NEITHER DID I. THE FARM IS QUAKER, THE FAMILY CALLED WALSH, THREE MILES SOUTH OF RATHFARNHAM OUTSIDE DUBLIN. ASK FOR ELIAS. I CANNOT TRAVEL. THE DOCTORS USE WORDS LIKE SHADOW AND WAIT. MY LEGS HAVE FORGOTTEN THEIR BUSINESS BUT MY HANDS STILL WORK AS YOU SEE. I AM SENDING EVERYTHING BECAUSE I MAY NOT GET ANOTHER CHANCE AND BECAUSE THERE IS NO ONE ELSE. YOU ARE THE COMPASS, ZEZZIE. ALL MY POINTINGS LEAD TO YOU. FORGIVE THE LIE THAT KEPT ME DEAD. IT KEPT OTHERS ALIVE TOO. COME IF YOU CAN. IF YOU CANNOT, BURN THIS AND LIVE.*

I read it six times. On the sixth reading my vision blurred and I discovered I had been crying without noticing, the way rain arrives at sea — no drama, just wetness where dryness used to be.

*Come if you cannot burn this and live.* As if those were the only two doors. As if my brother, who had spent four years being dead, had any right to hand me a choice between burning and crossing a war-torn sea to Dublin while Ministry men circled Gullwrack like gulls over a gutted fish.

He had every right. He was the only person alive with the standing to ask it.

And underneath the fury — underneath the four years of grief I had buried with such discipline, such tidiness, such *arithmetic* — something else was rising, warm and terrible and unfamiliar. It took me a moment to name it.

Purpose. The word fit like a key.

* * *

Wren found me at the table when he came down at noon, hollow-eyed from his own broken night. He had taken to sleeping in the boat loft since the barracks, since the men in grey coats took him and the fisherman Hollis brought him back bruised and half-frozen and swearing he'd told them nothing they didn't already know. He moved like a man relearning trust in floors.

"You've been at it all night," he said. Not a question. He nodded at the papers, at the candle stubs, at the tide tables weighted flat under the salt crock. "You've cracked it."

"The last sheet. The rest I'd done days ago — locations, dates, the officers." I slid the copied cipher text across the wood to him. "This came after."

Wren read. I watched his face do what faces do when the ground shifts: first blankness, then the flicker of comprehension, then something older and more private. His thumb pressed hard against the edge of the table until the knuckle went white.

"He's alive," Wren said. "Your brother. He's actually—"

"In a Quaker house outside Dublin. Ill. Too ill to travel." I heard my own voice and marveled at it — level, factual, a keeper logging conditions. Inside, everything was weather. "He calls himself dead because death protected him. If he'd come home a witness instead of a casualty, he'd have been silenced quietly and the village would have stayed a rumor."

"And now?"

"Now the armistice is coming, and when it comes, the records seal. We have days. Perhaps less." I gathered the sheets, squared their edges, and felt the strange calm of a decision already made announcing itself to the part of me still catching up. "The letters were bound for a journalist in Cork. That deadline died with the storm. But testimony entered before the sealing — testimony with a living witness behind it — that can't be unwritten."

Wren set the paper down carefully, as if it might startle. "You're thinking of the inquiry. The civilian commission. They convened one in Cork — I heard the guards talking, before they released me. Sitting through the end of the month, taking depositions ahead of the peace signing."

"I'm thinking of many things," I said. "Mostly I'm thinking that my brother asked me to come, and for the first time in twenty years someone has asked me for something I want to give."

* * *

That afternoon I walked the point.

It was a habit I had never examined closely, because examining it might have revealed what it was: a ritual of self-erasure. Twenty years of walking the same quarter mile of shingle and turf, learning it so completely that I disappeared into it. The gulls knew my stride and parted around me without interest. The sea made its arguments and I declined them all. This was my country: ninety acres of indifference, and I its perfectly adapted creature.

Today the walk was different. Today I walked it as a woman measuring a distance she intended to leave.

Gullwrack from the seaward side was a fist of black rock with the tower rising from it like a raised finger. From the landward side — the side facing the village, the mainland, the world — it was almost gentle, a green slope stitched with thrift and sheep-cropped grass, descending to the shingle where Wren had washed up four months ago like a message in a bottle. I stood on the shingle and looked south along the coast, the way the fishing boats went when they ran for Cork, and I tried to feel fear.

I found some. I want to be honest about that. Fear was as native to me as the thrift on the cliff tops. But it had changed shape. For twenty years fear had been a wall I maintained — course upon course of small silences, mortared tight. Now it was more like weather. Present, moving, survivable. You did not refuse to sail because of weather. You waited for your window, and you went.

Because here was the thing the cipher had shown me, the thing I kept turning over like a stone with gold underneath: Oscar's compass had pointed at me. Four years in the dark, half a continent away, betrayed by his own army, watching his legs fail him — and when he assembled the sum of what he knew and asked himself where truth could be safely kept, the needle swung home to a lighthouse on a rock off the Irish coast, to a sister who had buried her own name so thoroughly that even her husband had known only half of it. *You are the compass, Zezzie. All my pointings lead to you.*

I had spent two decades believing I had no direction. It turned out I had been the direction all along — the fixed point everyone else navigated by. The keeper keeps the light not for herself but for whoever is out on the water. That was the whole of the trade. I had simply never imagined the water would bring me my brother's soul in an oilskin packet and ask me to carry it back.

* * *

Mrs. Calloway arrived at dusk with a basket she pretended contained eggs and which actually contained three eggs, a heel of ham, a jar of goose grease, and every piece of coastal intelligence she had gathered in a week of listening behind her post-office wicket.

"They're steaming out of Queenstown twice daily now," she said, laying it out on my table like a general deploying cavalry. "Patrol launches, I mean. The Navy's twitchy — they think the U-boats will make a last spiteful run before the guns stop. Which means every hull south of Kinsale gets hailed and boarded." She looked at me over her spectacles with the frank appraisal of a woman who had run illegal letters through three regimes and considered the current one merely the latest weather. "You'll not make Cork on the mail boat. Your face is on a list somewhere, girl, whatever name you travel under. So is his." A nod at Wren, who was mending a net with Hollis's borrowed needles and pretending not to listen, badly.

"So we don't take the mail boat," Hollis said from the doorway. The deserter had a voice like gravel poured slowly, and a face that suggested the gravel had been an improvement. "My cousin's boat, the *Maggie B*. She's fast, she's registered for herring, and herring boats get waved through more often than not. Weather window Thursday. After that the front comes back and stays."

"Thursday," I said.

"Thursday," said Hollis. "Corkwater by Friday night if the patrol launch is blind, Saturday if she's curious, and the bottom of the sea if she's thorough." He shrugged, a man offering three equally reasonable options. "I've sailed worse odds for worse cargo."

Mrs. Calloway began repacking her basket with the briskness of a woman who has heard enough sentiment for one day. "The evidence goes sewn into a coat, not carried loose. I'll do the stitching myself — I sewed dispatches through the Rising, don't look at me like that, I was younger and angrier. And the copies—" She paused, and her voice gentled by a degree, which from Mrs. Calloway was practically a benediction. "The copies stay here. With me, and with Nurse Ahearn, and one in the church chest under the vestments. Three hands, three places. Whatever happens on the water, the record survives."

I looked around my kitchen — at the postmistress folding her basket, the deserter rolling a cigarette he was too polite to light indoors, Wren threading his needle with a seaman's economy — and felt the full weight and warmth of what had grown around my stove these past weeks without my quite permitting it. Found things. Found people. A network held together not by oath or payment but by the simple, reckless decision to be useful to one another.

Twenty years ago I had traded every connection for safety, and safety had proven to be a room with no windows. These people were building me a door.

"Thank you," I said. It sounded insufficient. It was insufficient. Mrs. Calloway snorted, which I understood to be acceptance.

* * *

That night I wrote two letters.

The first was to Elias Walsh, care of the farm south of Rathfarnham, in the plainest language I owned: *Your guest's sister is coming. Tell him the compass has turned. Tell him to hold on.* I sealed it and gave it to Mrs. Calloway for the morning coach, trusting the Quaker mails the way Oscar evidently trusted them — as the one channel even armies leave alone.

The second was harder. It was to the inquiry commission itself, requesting a deposition slot, signed — I sat with the pen hovering over the line for a long time, the lamp turning above me, the winter sea making its old arguments below — signed *Zephyrine Pemberton*, the name I had worn like a coat for twenty years, because the commission needed a name it could find records for, and my real name, the name I had buried with such ceremony in another life, would follow in the room itself, spoken aloud, where it belonged.

Two names. One for the road and one for the record. I found I could carry both, now, the way a keeper carries both the lamp and the dark.

Before I slept I took the cipher sheet out one last time and read the last lines again. *Forgive the lie that kept me dead. It kept others alive too. Come if you can.*

"I'm coming, Oscar," I said to the empty kitchen, to the turning lamp, to the brother four years dead and three miles south of Rathfarnham with failing legs and working hands. "And I'm bringing everything."

Outside, the barometer was already lifting. Two days to the weather window. Two days to Cork, to the inquiry room, to whatever waited on the far side of the water — patrols, historians, the whole machinery of the official record grinding toward its seal.

Let it grind. I had a heading now.
