## 17. Crossing to Cork

**Zephyrine**

The needle went through wool like a lie through a polite conversation — smooth, invisible, deniable. She had learned to sew in a house where every hem might be searched, and her hands remembered what her mind had spent twenty years pretending to forget.

She worked by lamplight at the kitchen table, the coat spread before her like something dissected. Into the lining, between canvas and cloth, she laid the letters one at a time: Oscar's hand marching across the pages, dates and villages and names of officers, the whole terrible ledger sewn flat against her ribs. The tide-table cipher pages she folded into eighths and set over her heart. When she finished, the coat hung on its hook looking exactly as it had before — a widow's coat, salt-stiffened, unremarkable. She put it on and buttoned it and felt nothing move against her body at all.

"You'll want to sit still if they board us," Wren said from the doorway. He had his cap in his hands, turning it the way he turned everything when he was nervous — slowly, methodically, as though the object might yield an answer under sufficient pressure.

"If they board us," Zephyrine said, "I am taking my brother's coat to Cork for alteration, because he has grown thin in the camps and I have not seen him in four years, because he is dead, madam, and dead men do not grow anything." She looked up. "Say it with me."

"Because he is dead," Wren repeated. "Madam."

"And you?"

"I'm a deckhand. Calloway's sister's boy, come down from Kinsale for the herring. I've papers."

"Papers Calloway wrote on his own table with a borrowed stamp."

"Papers are paper," Wren said. "It's the face that has to hold."

She studied him. Four days since the barracks gate, since the fisherman Harkness had walked him out between two barrels of sprats while the sentry argued about a chit, and Wren still flinched at engine noise. But there was something new in him too — a steadiness, or the skeleton of one. A man who had been taken once and come back was either broken or finished being afraid of the right things.

"They'll be watching the water," he said. "They let me go too easily. Harkness knows it. He says a patrol launch has been making circuits off the point twice a day since Tuesday."

"Twice a day is a schedule," Zephyrine said. "Schedules can be counted." She took down the logbook — her logbook, twenty years of weather in her small square hand — and opened it to the page where she had been keeping her own arithmetic. "Tuesday, the launch passed northbound at half past nine, southbound at noon. Wednesday, ten o'clock and half past twelve. Today, quarter past ten and one. It keeps station toward the headland in between." She tapped the column. "Which gives us a window after the second pass of roughly ninety minutes, if the fog holds, and Calloway says it will until the tide turns."

Wren stared at the page. "You've been logging them."

"I log everything. It's the only prayer I know." She closed the book. "Terror shared is terror halved — that's what our mother used to say when the storms came and Oscar cried and I didn't. She was wrong about most things, but perhaps not that." She stood, and the coat settled around her, and Oscar rode against her ribs without weight. "We go tonight. Get your bag."

* * *

**Wren**

The boat was called *Molly Breen*, which Harkness said was his late wife's name and bad luck besides, and she smelled of fish, tar, and thirty years of a man's stubbornness. She was forty feet if she was an inch, patched at every seam, with an engine that coughed twice before catching and then settled into a rhythm like a tired man climbing stairs.

Wren crouched in the stern beside Zephyrine and tried to make himself small. He had never been good at being small. That had been his whole trouble in life — too large a presence in rooms where smallness was survival. Informing on Danny Ferrell had been, in the end, a failure of smallness: he'd wanted to be seen, to matter, to be the man who knew things. And now here he was, the man who carried things, crouching in fish-smell with the most dangerous cargo on the Irish coast sewn into a widow's coat.

Gullwrack Point fell away behind them, the lighthouse sweeping its slow white arm across the water — once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. He had lived five weeks inside that rhythm. It occurred to him, with a strange lurch, that he did not want to leave it.

"Keep low when we clear the headland," Harkness called forward. He steered standing, one hand on the wheel, a shapeless hat jammed down to his eyebrows. "Fog's thick past the shoals but it thins toward the channel. If we're lucky we're through before it lifts. If we're not—" He spat over the side. "Well. Then we're fishing. Everyone's fishing. You're both my crew and you've been crew for years, so look bored."

"Bored," Wren said.

"It's harder than it sounds. Most men can act brave. Almost none can act bored."

The fog came down around them like wet wool, and the world contracted to the compass light, the engine's stuttering pulse, and the dark shapes of Zephyrine and Harkness. Wren found he could hear everything — the sea slapping the hull, the creak of the wheel rope, Zephyrine breathing. Steady breaths, counted, he'd have bet money they were counted. She did that when she was afraid; he'd watched it happen all week. In through the nose, out through the mouth, eight apiece, like a woman walking herself along the edge of a roof.

He started counting with her.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The fog lifted exactly when the fog decided to lift, which was to say: too soon.

One moment the world was wool and engine-noise; the next, grey daylight spilled across the water and the coastline stood up out of the sea to starboard — cliffs the color of old bone, gulls hanging in the air like scraps of paper — and Zephyrine felt the change in her chest before her eyes confirmed it. Exposure. Distance. Sightlines.

Harkness saw it too. He eased back on the throttle without being told, and the *Molly Breen* slowed to a fisherman's dawdle, lines trailing astern that no one had put out.

"There." Wren's voice came very quietly. He was looking south.

The patrol launch sat on the water two miles off, grey hull, thin smoke, moving — and then not moving, and then moving again, angling.

"It's seen us or it hasn't," Harkness said. "If it hasn't, we keep fishing. If it has—" He squinted. "It's coming round. It has."

Zephyrine's hands went still in her lap. She noticed this the way she noticed everything now, in two layers: the woman afraid, and the keeper observing the woman afraid, taking notes. *Hands still. Heart fast. Breath shallow.* The observer noted that the fear was enormous and entirely specific — not of death, but of the coat. Of Oscar's words going to the bottom of the Celtic Sea in the belly of a burned boat, or worse, into a Ministry strongroom in a labeled packet. Twenty years of silence, and now silence would be permanent, enforced, official. The victors' historians would write their clean paragraphs and Oscar would stay dead forever, properly dead this time, filed and sealed.

"No," she said aloud. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"Ma'am?" Wren turned.

"Not to the bottom. Not into their hands." She was already moving, pulling the tarpaulin back from the fish boxes, dragging one across the deck. "Harkness. How much oil do you carry? Lamp oil, engine oil — anything."

"Two drums aft, and the paraffin for the stove—"

"Pour it. All of it. Over the stern, in our wake, wide as you can."

Wren stared at her. Then understanding crossed his face like weather. "Smoke. You want us to lay smoke."

"The wind's off the land and falling calm. Oil on water burns long and lies low." She heard her own voice come out level and cold and recognized, distantly, her father in it — the man who had run munitions ledgers through three governments and taught his children that smoke was just truth wearing a disguise. "And while it burns, we turn north into the fog bank off the shoals. They'll expect a runner to run south for Cork. Nobody runs north away from their destination."

"Nobody with sense," Harkness muttered, and was already unscrewing the drum cap.

* * *

**Wren**

The sea caught fire behind them.

Not dramatically — not at first. Harkness trailed the oil in a broad lazy arc as they ran north, and then Zephyrine, of all people, took the flare pistol from the emergency locker, checked it with a housekeeper's brisk competence, and fired two shells low across their own wake. There was a soft whump, a spreading bloom of yellow-orange, and then the surface of the sea was burning, a long low ribbon of flame lying on the swells and pouring up smoke, black and greasy and endless, feeding on itself.

Wren watched it grow in the mirror of the water and felt something crack open in his chest that might have been laughter. "You've done this before."

"I've kept a lamp burning for twenty years," Zephyrine said. "Fire obeys rules. Men pretend to."

They ran north into the fog bank over the shoals, engine laboring, the *Molly Breen* slamming and shuddering through a short ugly chop, and behind them the smoke climbed into the sky like a second coastline. Through the thinning haze Wren saw the patrol launch stop. Saw it circle the fire. Saw its silhouette hang uncertain at the edge of the smoke, probing, and then — slowly, grudgingly, the way bureaucracy does everything — begin to work the edge of the burn rather than cross it.

"They think we're afire," Harkness said. "Or they think it's a decoy, but they daren't ignore it, because what if it isn't?" His grin was mostly teeth. "That's the beauty of it, missus. A lie costs them nothing to disbelieve. A fire costs them everything to ignore."

They came about in the fog, quiet now, engine throttled to a mutter, and ran south along the deep-water channel with the burning sea at their backs and the coast a grey rumor to port. Wren sat close beside Zephyrine — closer than propriety allowed, neither of them caring — and found that his hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last ten minutes and he couldn't say precisely when.

"Terror shared," he said.

"Halved," she finished. Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Mother would be insufferably pleased with herself."

The fog closed over them like a door.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

Cork harbor opened before them in the late afternoon like a held breath finally let out — masts and funnels and moored hulls, colliers and trawlers and one hospital ship painted dazzle-grey, and everywhere the noise of a port living under armistice rumors: bells, whistles, newsboys already shouting editions that changed by the hour.

Harkness brought the *Molly Breen* alongside at the fish quay with a bump and a curse and a line thrown to a boy who caught it out of pure professional reflex. On the quay, ordinary life proceeded: crates, carts, women in shawls, soldiers loitering by the customs shed with the particular boredom of men who had been told to watch everything and understood nothing.

Zephyrine stepped down onto Irish soil with Oscar's testimony sewn against her heart and her face arranged into its oldest expression — the mild, forgettable face of a widow come about a coat.

"Customs first," Wren murmured. "Then the newspaper office is four streets up, Harcourt's Buildings. The journalist's name is Deasy. We're expected by month's end, and today is—"

"Today is the day after month's end," Zephyrine said. "I can count."

They joined the queue at the customs shed. The officer there was young, freckled, exhausted, waving people through with the energy of a candle guttering out. Two ahead of them, then one.

Then a hand closed on Wren's shoulder.

Zephyrine turned and found herself looking at a tall man in a civilian raincoat that fit like a uniform, with the neat, unhurried face of the historian from the coast road — Mr. Ambrose Vane, who interviewed lighthouse keepers politely and wrote everything down. Except he was not supposed to be in Cork. He was supposed to be a hundred miles north, compiling the official narrative.

"Mrs. Sandoval," Vane said pleasantly. "What a coincidence. Though of course, that isn't your name, is it? It took some finding, I confess. Pemberton was better buried than you thought." His gaze moved, gently, deliberately, to her coat. "You'll want to come with me. Both of you. There's been a development — and I'm afraid your brother's timing has become a great deal more urgent than anyone planned."
