## 18. The Inquiry Room

The courthouse in Cork had been a grain exchange before the war, and it still smelled of it — dust and sweetness and the ghost of sacks stacked to the rafters. Now the trading floor held folding chairs, a long table draped in green baize, three commissioners seated behind it with the wary expressions of men asked to perform surgery with kitchen knives. A stenographer sat at a smaller table with a typewriter that had already eaten her patience; Zephyrine could tell by the way she struck each key like an insult.

Zephyrine stood at the back with Wren and watched the room fill.

Mrs. Calloway had come up from Gullwrack on the morning mail boat wearing her second-best hat and the expression of a woman attending a funeral she intended to speak at. Tully the fisherman had refused to come inside — "too many roofs between me and the water" — but he waited on the quay with his engine warm, and he'd shaken Zephyrine's hand at the gangway with a grip that said everything his mouth wouldn't. Nurse Brigid O'Halloran sat two rows forward, hands folded, spine straight as a mast, in the gray uniform of a woman who had seen four years of what the commissioners were only now beginning to imagine.

"You don't have to do this," Wren murmured beside her. He still moved stiffly; the barracks had left marks on him that didn't show. "There are other ways. The journalist in Cork—"

"Is dead," Zephyrine said quietly. "Or gone. Or bought. The letters told us which risks were worth taking, Oscar did. He trusted paper more than people, and look where paper got him." She smoothed the front of her coat, where the evidence lay sewn flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "No. It goes into the record or it goes nowhere. And I am done choosing nowhere."

A clerk called the session to order with a bell meant for closing markets. The middle commissioner — a barrister named Feeney, gray-faced and precise, with the air of a man who had been given three days and no budget — read out the commission's purpose: to receive, before the cessation of hostilities rendered all wartime documentation subject to sealed classification, sworn testimony concerning the conduct of His Majesty's forces in specific incidents along the western approaches, 1915 through 1918.

"Specific incidents," repeated the commissioner to Feeney's right, a younger man with a soldier's posture and a civilian's discomfort. "We should be clear for the record what jurisdiction—"

"We are clear, Colonel Ashe." Feeney's voice was dry as old rope. "Clearer than some would prefer. Next witness."

They had assembled this commission in eleven days. Eleven days, Mrs. Calloway had reported, of telegrams flying between Dublin and London like sparks off a grindstone, because someone in the Ministry had realized that if the armistice came — and everyone now whispered *when*, within the week, perhaps within days — every file in every archive would be closed like a hand shutting over a coin. The victors' historians were already sharpening their pens. This room was the last open window, and both sides knew it.

"Witnesses will state their names for the record."

*Their names.*

Zephyrine had rehearsed this on the boat crossing, in the dark hold among the herring barrels, saying it silently to herself until the syllables lost their edges: *My name is.* Two words and then twenty years falling away like a coat dropped on a floor. She had practiced until it felt almost easy.

It was not almost easy.

The first witnesses went before her — a coastguard man who testified to signal irregularities in June of 1917, a customs officer whose evidence amounted to dates and tonnages, useful and bloodless. Then Brigid O'Halloran took the chair, and the temperature of the room changed. She described the field hospital at a place whose name made one of the commissioners look down at his papers. She described wounded men brought in after a shelling that had no military justification, officers present who gave no orders to stop, a colonel whose name she spelled twice for the stenographer. Her voice never rose. That was the terrible thing about it — the flatness, the precision of a woman who had learned that volume was a luxury the dying could not afford.

When she stepped down, her eyes found Zephyrine's across the room, and she nodded once, small, like a runner passing a baton.

"The commission will hear Mrs. —" Feeney consulted his list, and frowned slightly, as though the ink had smudged. "The witness from Gullwrack Point."

Zephyrine walked to the chair. It seemed very far, those thirty feet, farther than any stretch of water she had ever crossed. She heard her own footsteps on the boards, heard Wren shift behind her, heard the typewriter keys go silent as the stenographer looked up.

She sat. The chair was hard. Someone had placed a glass of water at her elbow and she did not touch it, because her hands wanted something to do and shaking a glass on a saucer would tell the room everything before she said a word.

"State your name for the record," Feeney said.

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a tram bell rang, ordinary as bread.

And here was the thing she had not anticipated: it was not fear she felt. It was grief, and under the grief, an enormous, rising relief — the feeling of a splint coming off a limb that has healed crooked, pain and freedom in the same motion. For twenty years she had been Mrs. Pemberton, keeper's wife, then keeper's widow, a woman defined by another man's name twice over. Before that she had been a girl who wrote letters she never sent, who signed them with a name she had buried so deep she sometimes woke unsure it had ever been hers.

Oscar had carried that name into Gallipoli and out again, into the dark, into the sea, into an oilskin packet that had washed ashore at her feet. He had kept it alive for four years among the dead. What right did she have to be a coward about it?

"My name," Zephyrine said, and her voice came out steady, to her own surprise, steady as a lamp in weather, "is Zephyrine Marchmain."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the stenographer's keys hesitate, resume. She saw Feeney glance at his list again, and this time not at a smudge.

"I am the sister of Lieutenant Oscar Marchmain, Fourth Battalion, Royal Munster Fusiliers, listed killed in action at Gallipoli, August 1915. I am also — since the twelfth of October last — the person in lawful possession of documents written in his hand, dated after his death, which I am prepared to enter into evidence under oath." She drew a breath. "I was married in 1899 to Thomas Pemberton, keeper of the Gullwrack Point light. He died in the spring. My marriage was —" and here she paused, and chose honesty the way you choose a blade — "my marriage was a transaction, arranged so that a woman named Marchmain could stop existing. It succeeded for nineteen years. I intend to describe why, and what I have, and whom I believe my brother's documents accuse. I ask the commission's patience, because I intend to waste none of its time."

Feeney leaned back. "You understand, madam, that testimony given here may expose you to — scrutiny. Legal, official, otherwise."

"I lived twenty years without a name, Commissioner. Scrutiny is the cheaper loss."

Something moved through the room then — not quite laughter, but the shape of it held respectfully in check. In the third row, Mrs. Calloway's chin lifted like a figurehead.

The oath was administered. Zephyrine stood, hand on the book, and spoke the words, and when she sat again she found that her hands had stopped trembling altogether. There was a strange arithmetic to courage, she thought. It subtracted nothing. It only revealed what had always been there, the way a lamp reveals a coastline that existed all along in the dark.

Then she began.

She began where Oscar had begun, in his own words, reading aloud from the first letter while the clerk passed copies — Wren had copied them fair through three nights at Mrs. Calloway's kitchen table, his informant's neat hand pressed into service one last time — and she watched the commissioners' faces as the sentences went in like splinters. The village. The date, which she gave twice. The order given by Major Harrow-Vane to fire on the harbor cottages after the garrison had surrendered, to make an example, in the major's phrase, *of the water*. The second officer who objected and was reassigned within the week. The third who signed the report afterward and was promoted.

She read Oscar's account of how he had come to write it all down: not heroism, he wrote, but bookkeeping. *Someone must keep the ledger honest. If I die, let the ledger survive me.*

"He did die," Zephyrine said, setting down the page. "Officially. He let himself be listed among the fallen because he understood that a living witness can be silenced, but a dead man's testimony must be explained away — and explanations leave traces. He spent four years assembling this record from inside the machine that wants it destroyed. He is too ill to travel. He sent it to me because —" her voice caught, just once, and she let it, and the room waited — "because his compass pointed home. To me. To Gullwrack Point. He marked the tide tables in cipher, and I decoded them, and every location, every date, every name in these pages checks against shipping records, hospital ledgers, and the testimony you heard this morning from Nurse O'Halloran, whose dates match his to the day."

"Commissioner Feeney." The young colonel, Ashe, spoke for the first time, carefully. "These are grave allegations against serving officers. Allegations advanced by a document we cannot verify was written by the man claimed as its author, who cannot appear, and introduced by a witness who has — by her own account — lived under a false identity for two decades."

"Yes," Zephyrine said.

Ashe blinked. Whatever objection he had been constructing had assumed she would deny it.

"I lied to two Ministry men in my kitchen in November, and I would lie to them again tomorrow, because they came to bury this record, not to read it. But I have not lied to you. Ask me anything. Check everything. I am asking you to. A record that survives only because nobody checked is worth exactly nothing — my brother taught me that too. Verify the cipher against the tide tables for the quarter days of 1916. Verify Nurse O'Halloran's casualty figures against the Cork hospital register. Verify that Major Harrow-Vane's regimental diary has pages missing for precisely the fortnight in question — my brother noted the gap in his own hand, four years before anyone thought to look." She folded her hands. "The truth keeps books better than the Ministry does. It has had more practice surviving."

For a moment the only sound was the tram bell outside, receding.

Feeney cleared his throat. "The commission accepts the documents into evidence. Marked Exhibit Fourteen." The clerk carried them forward, and Zephyrine watched the oilskin-wrapped packet — she had insisted it keep its wrapping; let them see what salt water could not destroy — pass out of her hands for the first time since the shingle. It felt less like surrender than like launching a boat. You did not keep a seaworthy thing ashore. You gave it to the water and prayed the water was honest.

"One further matter for the record," Feeney said, shuffling his pages. "The witness's statement references a courier who conveyed the documents from — the relevant party — to Gullwrack Point. Is that courier present?"

Beside her, Wren stood up.

Zephyrine had not told him he might be called. She had not dared. She watched him walk to the chair with the careful gait of a man whose ribs remembered the barracks, watched him take the oath, watched him give his name — *Wren, Elias Wren, no other name offered and none required, sir* — and begin, in a low, unhurried voice, to describe the delivery: the man who hired him in a Liverpool public house, the payment, the instructions, the ambush at the harbor steps that put him into the sea with the packet strapped to his chest. He did not embellish. He did not apologize. When Feeney asked whether he had known what he carried, Wren said, "No, sir. Which is why I'm the best witness you've got. Everything I say, I learned from the documents themselves, same as you're learning it now."

And then the doors at the back of the hall opened.

Zephyrine felt it before she turned — the particular change in a room's weather when someone enters who believes the room belongs to him. She knew the tread. She had heard it cross her kitchen floor in November, polite as frost.

Mr. Aldous Crane, of the Ministry, came down the aisle between the benches with a document case under his arm and an expression of courteous regret worn like a good overcoat. Behind him came a second man she did not know — younger, softer, holding a leather folio — and behind *him*, unhurried, a tall figure in civilian clothes with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's eyes, who surveyed the commission table, the stenographer, the exhibits clerk, and finally Zephyrine herself, with the mild professional interest of a man appraising a manuscript he intends to revise.

"That will be all for today," Feeney was saying, gathering his papers, "the commission resumes tomorrow at ten, at which time —"

"Begging the commission's pardon." Crane's voice filled the hall without effort, trained for it. "Materials relevant to the matter under inquiry have this morning been placed before the War Office. In the interest of accuracy —" the word landed gently, deliberately, like a coin on a counter — "the Office has engaged Professor Ambrose Kettering, the distinguished military historian, to assist the commission in verifying documentary claims. Professor Kettering will wish to examine Exhibit Fourteen at his earliest convenience."

The tall man smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the worst thing about it.

Zephyrine sat very still, her hands flat on her knees, and felt the room tilt around a new axis. Tomorrow, then. The professor's pen against Oscar's ledger, the victors' version against the drowned man's truth, and somewhere across the water in a Quaker safe house outside Dublin, a dying man waiting to learn whether his compass had pointed true.

Professor Kettering's gaze rested on her one moment longer — cataloguing her, filing her — before he inclined his head, almost courteously, and took his seat at the back of the room.

Tomorrow at ten. She would be ready. She had spent twenty years learning how to disappear; it had taken her brother's ghost to teach her that disappearing was only half the craft.

The other half was being seen.
