## 20. Signature and Seal

The eleventh hour of the eleventh day arrived in Cork not with bells but with a silence so total it seemed the city had been struck dumb.

Zephyrine felt it before she understood it. She was standing at the window of the inquiry room — a borrowed chamber in the courthouse, all tall windows and institutional green paint, the panes rattling faintly in a wind that had followed them down the coast from Gullwrack — when the noise of the street outside simply stopped. The carts, the shouts of costermongers, the hammering from the shipyard: all of it ceased at once, as though someone had laid a hand over the mouth of the world.

She knew what it was. Everyone in the room knew. But no one moved to confirm it. The commissioners sat behind their long table with their papers squared and their pens capped, as if they too were waiting for permission to believe.

Then the bells began. St. Anne's first, and Shandon's great carillon after it, and then every church in the city throwing its voice into the grey November air until the sound was less like ringing than like weather. A cheer went up somewhere near the river, ragged and enormous. Someone on the quays was blowing a ship's whistle in long, unbroken blasts, and beneath it all came a sound Zephyrine had not heard since childhood: church bells rung *in celebration*, not in mourning.

In the corner of the room, Wren stood up so abruptly he knocked his chair over. He didn't right it. He pressed both hands flat against the wall and hung his head against them, his shoulders shaking — laughing or weeping or some animal third thing, she couldn't tell and didn't try.

Mrs. Calloway took off her spectacles, polished them on her apron — she had worn her good apron to the courthouse, over her best dress, because she said an apron was armor for women who worked — and put them back on. "Well," she said, in the tone she used when a parcel arrived damaged but the contents were intact. "Well."

The nurse, Brigid Halloran, said nothing at all. She had gone very still in her seat by the window, her hands folded, her face turned toward the bells, and tears ran down it freely without her appearing to notice them. Four years of lists of the dead, Zephyrine thought. Four years of writing names in ledgers while the war made more names faster than any pen could follow. And now this.

Zephyrine herself felt nothing she could name. Not joy. Something older and stranger — the sensation of a tide turning, felt in the body before the eye can find any evidence of it. The war was ending. The record was not yet sealed. And she was standing in a room full of strangers who had become, without her consent or quite without her notice, the people she would trust.

*Three copies,* she reminded herself. *Three hands. Nothing is finished until nothing can be undone.*

---

The commissioners reconvened at two o'clock, and the session resumed with the strange formality of men performing procedure inside an earthquake. Outside, Cork was celebrating; fireworks — hoarded, illicit, glorious — cracked and bloomed over the river. Inside, Commissioner Devereux called the room to order and asked that the testimony of Mrs. Zephyrine Sandoval be read back into the record for verification.

Her name. Her real name, spoken aloud by a man with an official voice, entered into an official ledger. She had given it yesterday, and still the hearing of it did something to her spine — straightened it, or broke something in it; she couldn't decide which.

The reading-back took the better part of an hour. The clerk's voice was dry and unhurried, and hearing Oscar's account recited in that bureaucratic monotone gave it a terrible new texture. *At approximately 0400 hours on the night of 14–15 October, 1915, artillery fire was directed upon the village of Kavala Tepe by batteries of the — regiment, under orders signed by —* The dashes where names should have been, redacted even here, even now. But the dates were intact. The grid references were intact. The number of civilian dead, counted by a soldier who had officially died at Gallipoli four years before he ever picked up his pen, was intact.

"Mrs. Sandoval." Devereux removed his glasses. He was the eldest of the three commissioners, a barrister with a face like a folded map, creased along lines of long habit. "You understand that this commission was convened under extraordinary circumstances, with a mandate that expires — formally expires — at the sealing of armistice records. We cannot promise you justice. I want that said plainly, in front of witnesses."

"I don't require justice," Zephyrine said. She heard her own voice come out level, lighthouse-keeper's voice, pitched to carry over weather. "I require that it be written down. Justice can find the page later, if it goes looking. But if there is no page—"

"—there is nothing to find," Devereux finished. He almost smiled. "Yes. You have made that argument persuasively these three days. More persuasively, I confess, than I expected to be persuaded by anything this week."

The second commissioner, a younger man named Quill whose sympathies had been visibly at war with his instructions since the first morning, cleared his throat. "There remains the matter of authentication. The handwriting analysis concluded yesterday — the samples provided by the War Office match the letters' hand beyond reasonable doubt. The testimony is genuine. What we lack—" and here his voice tightened, "—is the testimony of the author himself. Major Aldous Vane has been summoned. The summons went out two days ago."

"And Major Vane," said the third commissioner, a retired naval officer named Struan who had said perhaps forty words in three days, all of them load-bearing, "has acknowledged receipt and requested a deferral on grounds of ill health."

"He'll come," Wren said quietly, from the wall.

Every head turned. He had recovered his chair and sat in it now leaning forward, elbows on knees, looking older than his years in the way men did when they'd spent four years learning how governments lied. "He'll come," Wren repeated. "Because he knows that if he doesn't, the absence becomes its own entry in your record. He can't risk that. Men like Vane survive on paperwork. They die of it too."

Struan made a note. It was the longest sentence anyone had heard him inspire.

Devereux rapped the table. "We will adjourn for the day. Tomorrow — Armistice Day proper being today, tomorrow then being the twelfth — we convene at nine o'clock to consider testimony from the witnesses named by Mrs. Sandoval. If Major Vane appears, he appears. If he does not—" the folded-map face hardened, "—his non-appearance appears instead. This commission adjourns."

---

They came out of the courthouse into a city gone mad with relief.

It was disorienting, stepping through those heavy doors into the middle of a country's exhalation. Confetti — actual paper, cut by hand, people had emptied their drawers into the streets — drifted through the air like dirty snow. Soldiers who should have been mustering for demobilization danced with shopgirls on Patrick Street. An old man stood on a barrel playing a fiddle badly and nobody minded. Children ran everywhere carrying flags of uncertain provenance, some Union Jacks, some tricolors, and no one, remarkably, was fighting about it. For one afternoon the whole question of what Ireland was to be had been set down beside the larger fact that the killing had stopped, and everyone was drunk on the setting-down.

Zephyrine walked through it like a woman wading upstream. Twenty years of practice had taught her to move through crowds without being part of them, and the skill held even now — except that Wren kept pace at her shoulder, and Mrs. Calloway gripped her elbow, and Brigid Halloran walked close enough that their coats brushed. She was not invisible. She was escorted. The difference felt like a coat sewn around her shoulders in the dark of a boat.

A woman pressed flowers on them — paper flowers, someone's winter craft — and kissed Mrs. Calloway's cheek, and Mrs. Calloway, who had ruled Gullwrack village post office with the mild tyranny of an absolute monarch, burst into tears on the spot and pretended it was the confetti in her eyes.

"There's a house," Brigid said over the din, "on Summer Lane. My sister keeps it. There's room, and a stove, and she won't ask questions because she owes me more questions than anyone alive." She paused, and her chin lifted with the particular stubbornness Zephyrine had watched her deploy on commissioners. "And there's a table big enough for five."

"The Ministry men," Wren murmured, low, as they turned off the main street. "They've withdrawn. I watched them go. Collier and the thin one — Marsh — they packed their files before noon. Before the bells, even. They knew."

"They knew the signing was coming," Zephyrine said.

"They knew something worse." Wren glanced back at the courthouse, its green dome rising over the rooftops. "They knew the commission wasn't going to fold. Three days of testimony, corroborated, cross-referenced, authenticated by their own War Office analysts. You can't unring that. So they've gone back to London to argue about us in rooms we'll never see. Which means—"

"—which means the fight moves," Zephyrine finished. "But it moves onto paper. And paper is patient."

Wren looked at her sideways. "You know, when I washed up on your beach, I thought you were the most frightened person I'd ever seen."

"I was."

"You hide it better now."

"I'm not hiding it," Zephyrine said. "I'm using it. There's a difference, and I learned it only last week."

---

Summer Lane was narrow and crooked and smelled of coal smoke and boiling cabbage, and Brigid's sister Nóra Halloran turned out to be a small, quick woman with her late husband's watch chain looped across her waistcoat and the manner of someone who had buried grief and decided, on reflection, to be furious instead of sad. She fed them colcannon and fried herrings and strong tea, and she asked exactly one question, which was whether they'd eaten at the courthouse, and when told the courthouse food was a disgrace she nodded as though a great injustice had finally been named.

Afterward they gathered at her table — the big table, scrubbed pale, scarred by decades of knives and homework — and Nóra brought out paper.

It had been Wren's idea, in the corridor after the reading-back, delivered in his careful courier's cadence: *the record exists in one place, in their building, in their keeping, and everything in their keeping can be lost.* Devereux had already agreed in principle — a commission that might be dissolved by telegram needed its work distributed — but agreements in principle died easily. What survived was ink and hands.

So now Mrs. Calloway wrote the first copy, in the beautiful copperplate of thirty years of postal service, copying from the clerk's fair transcript while Zephyrine read aloud in a steady voice. Every date. Every grid reference. Every name Oscar had dared to set down — the officers, the orders, the countersignatures, the village, the count of the dead. When her voice caught on a number — ninety-one, ninety-one civilians, of whom twenty-three were children — she stopped, breathed once, the way she breathed between the strikes of the fog bell, and went on. Nobody hurried her. Nobody looked away. That was the thing she would remember afterward, she thought: not the words but the not-looking-away.

Brigid copied the second, her nurse's hand smaller and swifter. Wren copied the third, and his writing was the cramped utilitarian script of a man who had spent years making lists for other people's use, and halfway through the second page his pen slowed and Zephyrine saw him press hard enough to nearly tear the paper, and she reached across and covered his hand with hers until he loosened it, and he finished.

Three copies. Three different hands. No single fire, no single theft, no single order from any ministry could now erase what a soldier who was officially dead had witnessed on a hillside in October 1915.

When they finished, near midnight, Mrs. Calloway dried the ink with blotting paper and said, "Now the seals."

"I have wax," said Nóra, producing sticks of sealing wax from a drawer that seemed to contain everything a household might need across several generations. "Red. It was Thomas's. He used it for his union correspondence." Her mouth went thin and satisfied. "I think he'd approve of the occasion."

They melted the wax over the lamp flame and dripped it, and here the ceremony found its shape, unplanned. Each copy was sealed by a different hand: Mrs. Calloway pressed her thumbprint into the first, Brigid hers into the second, and for the third, Wren held the stick toward Zephyrine.

"You carried it ashore," he said. "You should close it."

She pressed her thumb into the soft red wax and felt the warmth of it through her skin, and thought: *this is what a seal is. Not a lock. A promise.*

The copies were wrapped — one in oilcloth, the ghost of the original packet, which made Wren laugh shortly and strangely — and distributed. One would remain with Devereux, lodged formally with the commission's papers. One would go to Brigid's contact at the Cork Examiner, who had promised, in writing, to hold it unopened unless and until the commission's findings were suppressed. One — the third, Zephyrine's own thumbprint — would travel north with Mrs. Calloway, to be deposited in the strongroom of the Bank of Ireland under a name Mrs. Calloway had invented on the spot, *Miss Ada Trevanion, spinster, of no fixed address*, which caused her such delight she had to sit down.

"Unsealable," Mrs. Calloway said, patting the parcels. "That's the word you used, Mrs. Sandoval, and I intend to hold you to it."

"It's not a word lawyers use."

"No," agreed Mrs. Calloway. "It's better."

---

Later, past one in the morning, the house finally quieted. Nóra had gone up; Brigid slept in a chair with a shawl over her feet; Wren lay on the settle by the dead fire, asleep instantly and completely, the sleep of a man who had been running for months and had at last been given leave to stop.

Zephyrine stood at the window of the little back bedroom, watching Cork celebrate itself below — the fires burning low now, the singing thinner, a few stubborn revelers still in the lanes — and let herself take inventory, the way she took inventory of the lamp stores.

The victory, examined honestly, was small. No verdict. No arrest. Major Vane summoned but not yet appeared; the Ministry men withdrawn but not defeated; the war over but the peace already being drafted by the same pens that had drafted the war. Somewhere in London, historians were even now deciding what the last four years would be permitted to mean. Against all that, she had: three copies of a dead man's testimony, four allies, and her own name, spoken aloud, entered in a ledger, irretrievable.

It was not much. It was also, she understood suddenly, standing there with the cold coming through the glass, everything. Twenty years she had believed safety was subtraction — remove yourself, remove your name, remove your wants, until nothing remained that could be taken. And here was the discovery at the end of it: that what could not be taken was not the residue of subtraction at all. It was what you built with other people. Testimony. Copies. Thumbprints in red wax. A table big enough for five.

The imperfect victory. Honestly won. She turned the phrase over and found it fit her hand the way the lamp key fit her palm.

Down in the street, a child was singing — tunelessly, wonderfully, some half-remembered anthem — and Zephyrine realized she was smiling, and let herself.

Behind her, on the settle, Wren stirred. "You're awake," he mumbled.

"Yes."

"There's a rider just come up the lane. Fast. From the direction of the barracks." He was sitting up now, fully awake, and the old wary courier had returned to his face like a tide filling a channel. "And Mrs. Sandoval — there's a light in the front room that wasn't lit when I fell asleep. Somebody's lit the lamp in the parlor."

Zephyrine turned. Down through the floorboards, faint and yellow, a bar of lamplight showed beneath the door where no lamp should be burning at two in the morning in a sleeping house.

On the parlor table, when they descended together, Wren's hand finding hers in the dark stairwell, lay a single envelope. No stamp. No postmark. Hand-delivered.

The handwriting on it was unmistakable — the same hand that had filled four years of letters, the same hand she had burned one page of and deciphered a hundred more.

It was addressed simply: *To my sister.*
