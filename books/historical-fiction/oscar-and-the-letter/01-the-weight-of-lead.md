## 1. The Weight of Lead

My father liked to say that lead remembers everything. He said it the way other men said grace — not because it was true, exactly, but because saying it made the work feel like a kind of prayer. I was eleven when he first put a graver in my hand and told me that every letter we cut would outlive everyone who ever used it, including us, including him, including whatever king or colonel happened to be inconveniencing the town that year. I believed him then. I believe it still, though these days the belief sits in me differently, the way a stone sits in a boot: familiar, and heavy, and no longer comfortable.

The morning the ledger business began — though of course I didn't know it was the beginning of anything; it felt like a Tuesday, and Tuesdays in Aldenmoor are famously indistinguishable from one another — I was cutting matrices for a commission from the parish church. The reverend wanted a new title page for his register of baptisms, something dignified, and had specified, with the fussy precision of a man who has never once set type himself, letters "of appropriate gravity." Gravity. As if letters could be weighed. Though my father was right, in his way. They can.

I had been at the cutting bench since before dawn, which was usual. The foundry is quietest then, before Oscar comes clattering down the stairs with his hair doing whatever it does, before the furnace gets up its appetite, before the day arrives to make demands. There is an hour — sometimes two if the light is gray and slow — when it is only me and the steel and the small bright argument between them. A punch is cut by hand, stroke by stroke, and each stroke must be made as if it were the last one you will ever be permitted. You cannot take back steel. You can only add to what remains, and hope your additions are wise. My father taught me that too, though he never said it outright. He just let me watch him ruin three punches learning it himself, back before I was born, and kept the ruined ones on the shelf above the bench where he could see them. *Humility,* he called them. *In metal.*

The church letter was coming along well. An E with a serif like a small firm handshake. I was just beginning the R, which is always a negotiation — that leg wants to kick out, wants attention — when the bell over the shop door rang and Frau Ostergaard came in with her annual grievance.

"You've spelled my name wrong," she announced, before the door had even shut behind her.

"Good morning, Frau Ostergaard."

"It is not a good morning. It is a morning on which I have discovered that the most literate family in Aldenmoor cannot spell Ostergaard."

"We haven't misspelled anything," I said, because this was also annual. "Your husband's name has an extra *r* in our books because your husband insisted on the extra *r* in 1839, and I have the correspondence to prove it."

"He was wrong."

"He was very clear about being right. Those are different things, but they look identical in lead."

She sniffed, which meant she had conceded, which meant she would return next year. She bought a pound of printer's ink she did not need — she bought it every year; I suspect she simply enjoyed the transaction — and left. And I went back to my R, and the morning resumed its shape, and nothing about it suggested that within the month I would be deciding, alone at a bench much like this one, whose names lived and whose did not.

That is the thing about beginnings. They never introduce themselves.

* * *

Oscar came down at half seven, smelling of sleep and the river, and stood behind me reading over my shoulder as he always did, close enough that his breath moved the hair at my neck.

"That R's leg is crooked," he said.

"The R's leg is confident."

"The R's leg is drunk."

"The R," I said, "has been drinking with your hair, apparently, and they've agreed on a style."

He laughed, and stole a piece of bread from the tin on the shelf, and settled into his corner of the shop to sort cases for the day's jobs. This was our rhythm, worn smooth as an old matrix: I cut, he composed, Father supervised from his chair by the window where the cough had lately installed him like a tenant who paid no rent and intended to stay. The Voss Type Foundry had stood on Tanner's Row for sixty years. Grandfather had cast type for three newspapers that no longer existed, a Bible that did, and a wedding invitation so ornate that the bride's mother reportedly fainted at the bill. We were not rich. But we were necessary, and necessity, in a river town of nine hundred souls, is a kind of wealth.

By mid-morning I turned to the ledger itself, because Tuesday was cataloguing day. You should understand what the ledger was, though in October of 1848 nobody yet understood it fully — least of all me, who kept it. It looked unremarkable: a great folio bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked in the way of things that have been opened ten thousand times. Inside were names. Every family in Aldenmoor that had ever done business with the foundry had an entry — accounts, debts, commissions — but layered beneath the commerce, in my father's smaller hand and then mine, was the other record. Births confirmed by baptismal registers we'd printed. Land deeds copied at a family's request when the originals were lost to fire or flood or a landlord's convenient amnesia. Passage rights, inheritance claims, dowry agreements. Sixty years of proof, in short. Proof of who owned what, who was owed what, who had a right to stand where and go where and claim what. My father had begun keeping it during the boundary disputes of '22, when half the farms along the bend discovered their titles had evaporated, and he had never stopped. *Paper burns,* he said. *So we keep the memory twice.* Once in the ledger. Once in lead, in the standing forms locked in the chases along the back wall — the whole town's legal history, set and ready to print, because paper burns but you cannot burn a stereotype without knowing it exists.

I worked down the page in my neat hand: the Brenner family, their mill claim renewed and witnessed. The widow Harkness, her late husband's berth-right on the packet boats, held in trust for her son. The Ilves brothers, splitting their father's tannery, God help the fence between them. Name after name after name. Nine hundred people, give or take, reduced to ink entries in my writing, which my father said was the finest hand in the valley and which I privately thought merely adequate.

It was comforting work. That is what I want to record honestly, before everything else happened: that it was comforting. There is a deep peace in order, in a page that balances, in a world — even a false one, even a temporary one — where every name has its line and every line has its place. I have since learned what such pages are worth and what they cost. But that morning, the peace was real. I catalogued the town while rain came down the river valley in long gray curtains, and the furnace ticked, and my brother sorted type two rooms away, and my father dozed in his chair with the coughing held temporarily at bay, and I believed, with the whole of my foolish heart, that this was what safety looked like.

* * *

I noticed the first wrong thing at dusk, and it was such a small wrong thing that I nearly filed it under nothing and moved on. Nearly.

Oscar had gone out after supper — again, which was itself becoming a pattern I hadn't yet admitted was a pattern — saying he was walking the levee to clear his head. He'd been taking these walks for perhaps six weeks. He came back after dark, cheeks red with cold, cheerful in a way that sat slightly wrong on him, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man. And he went straight past the wash basin to the type room, which he never did. Oscar was many things, some of them exasperating, but he was fastidious about lead. You washed before you touched the cases. Everyone knew that. Lead dust finds its way into blood, and blood is not where you want it.

But that night he went straight to the cases, and when I came in behind him with the lamp, he had his sleeves pushed up and was sorting pica with a concentration that was almost theatrical, and there — on the white of his inner cuff, where the lamplight caught it — was a smudge of dark dust.

I am not proud of what I did next. I am not ashamed either. I lit no dramatic confrontation. I simply waited until he went up to bed, and then I took his shirt from the mending basket, held the cuff to the lamp, and studied it the way I would study any material brought into my shop. Because that is what I am, underneath everything else. I am a person who identifies materials.

And the dust on Oscar's cuff matched no alloy we used.

Our type metal is lead, tin, and antimony, in proportions my grandfather set and my father saw no reason to change. I knew its color the way I know my own hands. This smudge was darker, softer-looking, with a faint oily sheen that ours never has — more lead in the mix than any sane founder would use, metal that would cast soft and wear fast and ruin quickly, cheap metal, improvised metal. Someone was casting type — or plates, or something — with inferior alloy, somewhere outside my shop, and my brother's cuffs were telling me about it.

I stood in the empty type room holding his shirt and felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way it does on the packet boats when the current shifts beneath you and the boat hasn't moved at all.

Oscar had been strange since summer. Not dramatically — Oscar does nothing dramatically except laugh and argue — but there had been absences with thin explanations, evenings when his eyes went distant mid-conversation as if listening to something across the river, a new careful quality to him when talk turned to the troubles in the capital. The papers we printed for the Aldenmoor Courier carried the government's version of events: unrest among students, regrettable disorder, firm measures restoring calm. Oscar read those columns now with his jaw set, and once — I remembered this clearly as I stood there — once he had read a paragraph aloud about printers arrested in the capital, and his voice had caught on the word *presses*, caught and broken, and he'd covered it with a cough and left the room.

I folded his shirt, put it back in the basket, and told myself a series of reasonable things. That boys his age took up causes the way they took up fevers. That he might have been at the ferry landing where strangers passed through carrying all sorts of dust from all sorts of towns. That whatever it was, it would keep until morning, and morning would make it smaller, the way mornings do.

None of it was true. I knew it wasn't true while I was thinking it. But there is a discipline in the foundry that becomes a discipline everywhere, if you let it: you do not act on a form until it is locked. You gather your sorts, you check them twice, you wait for the whole line to stand before you commit it to pressure. I gathered my sorts that night and said nothing, and lay awake listening to the rain, and through the wall I heard Oscar turn over, and turn again, the restless turning of a man rehearsing something.

* * *

Father woke coughing near midnight, and I went down with the honey-and-water he pretended to hate. He sat propped in his chair by the dead fire, gray-faced, seventy years old suddenly instead of fifty-eight, and waved off my fussing with the hand that had cut ten thousand letters and now shook unless he pressed it flat against his knee.

"You're still up," he said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Mm." He looked at me for a long moment. My father's eyes were pale river-gray and missed nothing; I come by my habit of noticing honestly. "Something's troubling you."

"Frau Ostergaard's spelling," I said.

"She's been dead wrong about that *r* for nine years and you both enjoy it. Try again."

I almost told him. I want to be honest about that too — I stood there with the words actually forming, *Oscar's cuffs, the alloy's wrong, he's hiding something* — and then I looked at his chest, at the effort each breath was costing him, at the way he gripped the chair arm through the coughing fit that came, and I swallowed the words like a bad proof. Whatever Oscar was carrying, it could sit locked in its chase one more day. My father had enough weight in his chest already.

"Nothing," I said. "A long catalogue today. Names make me melancholy."

"Names shouldn't." He took the cup, and his hand steadied around it, the old craft overriding the failing flesh for a moment. "Names are the most durable thing we make. Kings fall, Isandro. Empires fall. Rivers change course and take the towns with them. But you set a man's name in lead properly, and a hundred years from now someone will hold it in their hand and he will exist again, entire, just that long. We're not in the metal trade, girl. We're in the resurrection business."

"You're sentimental when you're ill."

"I'm always sentimental. I'm simply better hidden when I'm well." He drank, and looked toward the stairs, toward where Oscar slept, and something crossed his face — a flicker, a shadow, there and gone, that I chose then not to read and have spent every day since trying to un-choose. Did he know? Had he already seen what I'd seen, smelled the wrong alloy on his son's hands weeks before I did? I have never asked. I never will be able to ask. "Go to bed," he said. "Lock the shop."

I banked the furnace. I checked the chases along the back wall — the standing forms, the town's whole testimony in locked lead, sleeping in the dark — and I locked the door, and climbed the stairs past Oscar's room, where the candle was finally out.

Below the window, the river ran black and patient toward the capital, carrying the rain away, carrying everything away eventually, and I fell asleep believing — this is the last time I will say it plainly, so mark it — believing that whatever came down that river would come slowly enough for us to see it, and that my family would meet it together, and that the ledger on its shelf was safe because it was true, and that truth was a kind of shelter.

Two days later, the first refugees came through on the north road, driving carts heaped with bedding and children, and they brought the news with them: the capital had risen, the printing offices were burning, and the army was coming south.

And that same evening, Oscar burned a letter at the kitchen stove — I watched him feed it to the flames piece by piece, watching the door behind him the whole while — and when he turned and found me looking at him, he smiled, and it was the worst smile I had ever seen on his face, because it was the smile of a boy asking permission for something he had already done.
