## 2. Smoke from the Capital

The trouble with running a foundry is that you learn to smell metal before you see it. Lead has a scent — sweetish, faintly rotten, like a coin held too long in the mouth. By the third week of March, Aldenmoor smelled of something else entirely: woodsmoke that wasn't ours, and under it, riding the river wind from the south, a charred-paper tang I couldn't place until I could, and wished I hadn't.

Papa noticed it first. He was sorting galleys at the long bench, his cough rattling around the shop like loose type in a shaken case, when he stopped and stood very still.

"Isandro," he said. "Do you smell burning?"

"Kilns," I said. "The brickworks."

"It isn't kilns." He set down his galley with the care of a man handling a sleeping child. "Kilns don't burn paper."

I wanted to tell him he was being dramatic. Papa had a talent for drama that he reserved for moments of genuine catastrophe, which is either wisdom or a curse, depending on how often the catastrophes come. I went back to my matrices instead, because that is what I do when the world tilts: I cut lead. There is no problem so large that it cannot be made smaller by reducing it to a point twenty-six points high.

Oscar came down the stairs at half past seven, dressed for the counting house but with his collar crooked, and asked what smelled like a library fire. Nobody laughed. That told him everything, and I watched him decide, visibly, to know nothing about it.

* * *

The news proper arrived on Tuesday, in the person of Herr Falk, who runs the ferry landing and therefore hears everything before the newspapers do, on the principle that water carries gossip faster than ink.

"Capital's gone over," he announced from the doorway, hat in his hands, which was how we knew it was serious; Falk wore his hat indoors the way other men wore their opinions. "Students first, then the printers, then half the garrison threw down their arms and joined them. They've got the arsenal. They're reading proclamations off the palace steps."

"Reading them off *what*?" I said, because my mind goes to strange places under stress.

"The steps, girl. Out loud. To crowds." He said this the way another man might say the dead had been seen walking. "There's a press at the center of it. Some printer fellow turned the whole city into an audience."

Papa sat down slowly on his stool. "A printer started it?"

"A printer's words started it. Whether a printer can stop it is another matter." Falk put his hat back on, which meant the audience was over. "They say the government's fled to Kesselberg. They say there'll be reprisals. They say—" he paused, choosing, "—they say anyone caught with seditious type will hang from his own gallows frame."

He left. The three of us stood in the shop among our forty cases of type, every one of them technically seditious, since letters are only innocent until someone sets them.

"Well," said Oscar lightly, too lightly, the way a man walks across ice he has privately tested. "At least business should pick up."

Papa looked at him for a long moment. I looked at him longer. Oscar has never once in his life been able to meet my eyes while lying, which makes him a terrible liar and a decent brother, since he always tells me the truth eventually, just not the truth I'm asking about.

* * *

The refugees began Thursday.

I want to be precise about this, because later — much later, after everything — people would ask me what those days were like, as if history were weather one could describe. So: they came up the river road in family groups, mostly, carts drawn thin as the horses pulling them, and they came through town without stopping, which was somehow worse than if they'd stopped. Aldenmoor was a way-station between the burning south and the indifferent north, and we watched several hundred futures pass through us like water through a sluice.

Some few did stop. A woman named Marta Brenner came into the shop with two children and asked if we did printing work, because her husband had been a compositor in the capital and she needed to sell his tools. She unwrapped them on my bench: a composing stick worn bright at the thumb-groove, a bodkin, a brass rule gone dark with handling. Tools that had fed a family until they couldn't.

"He printed handbills," she said. "Nothing bad. Wedding announcements, funeral notices, prices of grain. Then in February the new censor came through and read one of his handbills and decided the price of grain was inflammatory." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was still trying to be polite. "So now they're yours, if you want them. For whatever you can give."

I gave her too much. Papa would have said that's bad bookkeeping. Papa also coughed blood into his handkerchief twice that morning, so I've stopped taking his advice on economy.

While I wrapped the tools, the older boy — maybe twelve, all elbows and watchfulness — studied our shop with an expression I recognized. It was the look of someone cataloguing exits. I'd worn it myself during the tax assessor's visits, back when Papa's politics were merely expensive rather than dangerous.

"You print things here?" the boy asked me.

"We cast type," I said. "Letters. For presses all along the river."

"And sometimes you print things yourselves?"

"Sometimes."

He nodded slowly, filing this away wherever boys file things that will matter later. His mother pulled him out by the sleeve. At the door she turned back.

"Burn your waste paper," she said. "Every night. Whatever you don't want found. Burn it or sink it. Paper remembers."

She left with her children and her husband's ghost-tools, and I stood holding a composing stick that had outlived its owner's livelihood, feeling the first cold drop of something I refused to name.

That night, I burned our waste paper. All of it, including three weeks of ordinary invoices that had nothing whatsoever to hide. It felt absurd. It felt necessary. Both feelings can occupy the same person at once; I've since learned they usually do.

* * *

Oscar started disappearing on Sunday nights.

Not dramatically. Oscar does nothing dramatically; his rebellion, whatever shape it took, would arrive wearing an apron and carrying an excuse. He would announce after supper that he had an errand — a delivery to the Weiss farm, an order to collect, a bill to chase — and Papa would nod, and I would watch him go with the specific unease of a sister who knows her brother's errands the way she knows her own hands.

Because here is the thing about growing up inside a business with your sibling: you become an auditor without meaning to. I knew Oscar's customers the way Papa knew his punches. I knew the Weiss farm ordered type twice a year, in spring and autumn, and it was neither spring work nor autumn. I knew the mill accounts were settled monthly and it was the wrong week. I knew every legitimate reason my brother might walk four miles upriver after dark, and none of them fit.

I said nothing for two weeks. This requires explanation, and the explanation is this: I trusted him. Trust, in a family, functions like the register of a well-kept case — you assume each letter sits in its proper compartment until the day you reach for one and find the compartment empty. You don't check daily. Checking daily would mean you'd already lost faith in the whole system.

On the third Sunday, I checked.

* * *

It was the type dust that undid him. Type dust, of all things — the fine grey powder of lead and antimony that accumulates under a caster, that gets into cuffs and creases and the seams of men's souls. Every trade has its signature residue. Bakers wear flour. Masons wear lime. My family wears lead, and I can read it like handwriting.

Oscar came home near midnight, quiet as a debt collector, and hung his coat on the peg by the stove as he always did. And I, who had risen to bank the fire, took that coat down afterward to brush it — an act of sisterly habit, nothing more, I told myself, and believed it for almost four full seconds.

The cuffs were grey.

Not shop-grey. Shop-grey has a particular character: mixed with lampblack from the casting room, flecked with the coppery dust from the matrix cabinet, smelling faintly of our particular flux. This was different. Finer. Drier. With a grain to it that spoke of hand-casting, small batches, a charcoal-fired pot rather than our gas ring. Someone had been setting type by hand somewhere that wasn't ours, casting it somewhere that wasn't ours, cleaning it somewhere that wasn't ours, and then coming home to my brother's coat.

I stood in the dark kitchen holding the coat like evidence, which is exactly what it was, and felt the floor tilt in that particular way floors tilt when a load-bearing assumption gives way.

I did not sleep. I inventoried instead, because inventory is my form of prayer. Our job book, the great ledger of commissions going back to Grandfather Voss, records every pound of type alloy issued, every job cast, every client billed. Monday morning, while Oscar slept in, I went through it page by page, cross-referencing against the stock drawers, weighing the lead pigs against their recorded weights.

Three pigs light. Not missing — light, by a few pounds each, the sort of discrepancy a careless man explains and a careful man investigates. Cast-off ends, someone might say. Natural wastage.

Natural wastage does not leave fine dry grey dust on my brother's cuffs, dust that matches no job in the book, dust that smells of a charcoal fire and someone else's shop.

* * *

I confronted no one. Let the record show that Isandro Voss, who confronts difficult arithmetic daily and difficult colonels later, confronted nothing. I carried the knowledge around for six days like a splinter working toward the bone, and I performed my duties, and I watched my brother.

Watching Oscar was instructive. He had developed habits. He whistled while he worked — he never whistled — old marching songs, of all things, cheerful and revolutionary and completely unlike him. He read the newspaper twice through, front to back, including the shipping notices. He asked Papa casual questions about the old censorship laws, phrased with the delicacy of a man defusing something. And on Wednesday, when a government courier clattered through the square posting a proclamation — martial law extended to the river districts, possession of unauthorized printed matter punishable by imprisonment — Oscar read it three times and then went back to the caster and worked with a concentration I had only ever seen in Papa.

My brother was afraid. That was clear enough. But he was afraid the way a man is afraid on a road he has chosen, not one he's fallen onto, and there is all the difference in the world between those fears, and I hated that I could tell them apart.

Friday evening Papa called us both to the bench. He had a proof sheet in his hands — the quarterly accounts — and he laid it flat and smoothed it, though it didn't need smoothing, the way men handle paper when their hands need occupation.

"The capital has fallen," he said. "You've heard. What you haven't heard is that Colonel Brandt's regiment is moving up the valley. Requisitioning. First the granaries, then the forges, then — if the reports hold true — the print shops." He looked at us both over the proof sheet. "An army that burns pamphlets learns to burn shops. I've seen it before, in 'thirty-one. They start with the papers and end with the presses, because a press is just papers that haven't happened yet."

"Papa," Oscar said, "that's speculation—"

"It's memory, which is worse, because it's already been true once." Papa folded the sheet. "Whatever comes through that door, this shop keeps its books honest. Every entry, every name, every figure. Do you understand me? A foundry that falsifies its record deserves to lose it. The ledger is the only thing we own that tells the truth about us."

He said it plainly, tiredly, a dying man arranging his affairs, and I agreed because agreement cost nothing. Oscar agreed too, quickly, and looked at the floor, and I thought: *there*. There it is again, the flinch, the downward glance, the boy who cannot lie to my face.

After Papa went up to bed — the stairs taking him a long time now, each step a negotiation — Oscar lingered by the stove. The fire had gone low. His face was half shadow.

"Isandro." He said my name carefully, testing its weight. "If I ever did something you didn't understand. Something that looked like betrayal of the shop, or of Papa. Would you—" He stopped. Started again. "Would you want to know? Or would you want to be able to say you didn't?"

I looked at my brother, whom I had dressed in wool coats against winters before I could write, whose hands I had pulled from a type-case lid when he was five, whose first word had been *more*, pointing at the alphabet wall Papa made us. I looked at the grey ghost of dust I knew was on his cuffs even though he'd changed the coat.

"I would want to know," I said. "I always want to know. That's the whole trouble with me."

Something moved behind his face — relief and regret fighting for the same ground, like two compositors reaching for the same letter. He nodded once, and said good night, and went upstairs, and left me standing in the dying firelight with a question I hadn't answered honestly after all.

Because the truth was worse than either option he'd offered. The truth was that I already suspected, and had suspected for a week, and had done nothing, because some part of me understood — with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own proof sheets — that once I named what Oscar was doing, I would have to choose between him and the shop. Between my brother and the ledger. Between the boy who said *more* and the books that must stay honest.

And I wasn't ready. God forgive the cowardice, I wasn't ready.

* * *

Saturday, the smoke from the capital was visible from the church hill — a smudge on the southern horizon like a thumbprint on clean glass. Half the town walked up to look at it, quietly, families together, nobody speaking above a murmur. We stood in the graveyard among our own dead and watched someone else's future burn.

Papa didn't come. He sent us instead — Oscar and me, side by side, our breath steaming — and I understood the errand for what it was. He wanted us to see it together. He wanted us standing side by side when the world arrived at our door, because he knew, the way dying men know things, that soon enough we would be standing on opposite sides of it.

Oscar's shoulder touched mine. Neither of us moved away.

"They'll come here next," he said. Not a question. Not quite a fear, either. There was something underneath his voice I couldn't name yet — something almost like readiness.

"Yes."

"And when they ask for the shop's records—"

"They won't ask," I said. "Armies requisition. They take."

He was quiet a moment. Down in the valley, the river ran brown with spring melt, carrying ice and whatever else southward, away from us. Somewhere downriver was the capital, and the smoke, and a printer whose words had started all of this, though I didn't know his name yet. I wouldn't learn it for weeks. I would learn it in the worst possible way.

"Isandro." Oscar was looking at the horizon, not at me. "What would you do — if keeping everyone safe and telling the truth couldn't both be done? If you had to pick?"

The question sat between us like hot metal.

"That's not a real question," I said finally. "That's a question for people who've already decided something."

"Maybe." He smiled, small and crooked, my brother's smile, the one that used to get us extra bread from Frau Hessler. "But answer it anyway. For me. Someday. When it's real."

We walked down the hill together, and behind us the smoke kept rising, patient as type waiting in a case for the hand that will lift it and make it speak.

That night I checked the lead pigs again. Four light now.

And pinned inside Oscar's coat, where a careless brusher would never look but a careful sister had finally thought to — folded small, in handwriting I didn't recognize, in ink the color of dried blood — was a note:

*"One more edition. Then I'm finished, I swear it. — A.R."*

I stood in the dark shop holding my brother's secret in both hands, and downstairs in the cellar, beneath forty years of honest weight, something waited to be found.
