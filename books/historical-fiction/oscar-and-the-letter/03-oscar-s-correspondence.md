## 3. Oscar's Correspondence

My sister believes I cannot keep a secret because I once told her where Father hid the brandy when I was nine years old. She has held this against me for a decade, and she is wrong in a way that would astonish her: I have been keeping a secret for eight months now, and it has grown so large inside me that I have begun to think of it as a lodger — one who pays no rent, eats all my sleep, and occasionally hums revolutionary songs while I'm trying to set type.

His name is Anselm Riehl.

I have never spoken it aloud. Not once. I say it only in my head, and even there I say it quietly, the way you'd speak in a sickroom.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things do, with an errand that seemed harmless.

Last spring a man came into the foundry wanting two hundred calling cards for a cousin's wedding in Vellmark. He was ordinary in every respect except his hands, which were inked to the second knuckle in a way no wedding guest's ever are. Printer's hands. I know them the way Isandro knows the weight of a matrix by touch — instantly, helplessly, with a kind of love.

He ordered his cards, paid too much, tipped too generously, and left. But at the door he paused and said, "Your father taught in Kesselstadt, didn't he? Before the move here?"

"He did," I said.

"Then tell him Anselm remembers the man who gave him his first case of type." And he was gone before my heart finished its first irregular beat.

Anselm Riehl. The name had been in every paper south of the river for two years. The printer of Kesselstadt who published what the government called *incendiary filth* and everyone else called, in lowered voices, *the truth*. He'd fled the capital when they raided his shop, and the official story was that he'd crossed the mountains and was living fat under another name somewhere far from trouble.

Instead he was standing in my father's foundry ordering wedding cards for a fictional cousin, looking at me with eyes that said: *I know exactly whose son you are.*

* * *

You should understand something about me before we go further, something Isandro has never understood and never will, though God knows she's tried to beat it into me with logic the way she beats dross off lead.

I am not brave.

Isandro is brave. She cuts metal for a living; her courage is dense and cold and holds its edge. Mine is different. Mine is the courage of a man reading by candlelight past the hour he should be asleep — the coward's courage, the courage that says *just one more page*. That is what printing has always been to me. Father taught us both the craft, but Isandro took the craft and I took the reading. She loves type the way a mason loves stone. I love it the way a sinner loves scripture.

So when Riehl came back the following week — alone this time, after closing, asking not for cards but whether "the young man with careful hands" might do some private work — I said yes before he'd finished the sentence. I want to be honest about that. There was no long struggle of conscience. My conscience arrived late, as it always does, knocking apologetically at a door already open.

The work was pamphlets. Two pages, octavo, three hundred copies. His prose, my composition. He wrote about the requisition laws — how the Crown had been seizing farmland along the river valleys for a decade and calling it *administrative reallocation*, how the families displaced were given paper claims worth less than the paper they were printed on. He wrote it plainly, without thunder, which somehow made it worse. Thunder you can dismiss. Plainness gets inside you.

We printed at night. That was the arrangement: I worked the hand press in the old finishing room after Isandro and Father went home, and I returned everything to order before dawn. The plates — Riehl engraved them himself on pewter, crude but serviceable — I hid where no officer searching our shop would ever think to look.

Inside the lead pigs.

Every foundry keeps pigs of raw type-metal stacked like firewood, and ours are cast in molds Father bought thirty years ago, uniform as loaves. No one counts them. No one weighs them. They sit in their rack by the melting furnace looking exactly as stupid and inert as lead ought to look. Riehl showed me how to cast certain pigs hollow, with a cavity down the center sealed by a plug of the same alloy, invisible unless you knew which pigs — and you knew by a file mark on the base so small you'd take it for a casting flaw. Inside each hollow pig, wrapped in oilcloth, a plate. We had eleven plates hidden among four hundred pigs, and I will tell you that walking past that rack every morning, watching Isandro sort type six feet away, was either the finest discipline of my life or proof that I have no imagination whatsoever.

Probably both.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about treason: it is mostly tedium.

I had imagined — when I let myself imagine at all — that sedition would feel like the pamphlets read. Fire, urgency, history pressing at your back. In truth it felt like locking the door, trimming wicks, inking a press by feel in a room dark enough that I worked half from memory. It felt like blisters. It felt like lying awake doing arithmetic: *three hundred copies, each passed hand to hand, perhaps five readers per copy, fifteen hundred people, minus the ones who burn theirs out of fear, plus the ones who copy passages into letters—*

That arithmetic was my opium. Every night I ran the numbers and every night they came out the same way: *words travel faster than soldiers.* A column of infantry moves twenty miles a day and must be fed. A paragraph moves at the speed of gossip and feeds itself. The army can occupy a town; it cannot occupy a sentence. You can bayonet a man, but his account of the bayoneting is already in three other mouths.

I believed this. I believe it still, most days. Riehl said something once that I have carried like a coin in my pocket: "They burned the printer in Kesselstadt's square two hundred years ago. The book they burned him for is in the university library now, catalogued and safe. Tell me who won."

But belief is cheap at midnight and expensive at dawn, and dawn kept bringing me back to the same ledger of debts I couldn't pay.

Because here is the other thing about treason, the thing that actually hurts: it is made of lies told to people who love you.

* * *

Isandro noticed, of course. She notices everything; it's her curse and her genius and the reason our books balance to the quarter-penny. She noticed that the tin of lamp oil emptied faster than the jobs justified. She noticed that the finishing room smelled faintly of solvents I had no honest use for. Last month she stood across the sorting table from me, holding up my cuff, and said, "Type dust. Six-point, nonpareil mix. We haven't run anything smaller than fourteen-point since the hymnals in March. Where were you setting six-point, Oscar?"

"A private commission," I said. "A gentleman who wants discretion."

"What gentleman needs six-point?"

"A modest gentleman."

She looked at me for a long moment — she has Father's gray eyes, the color of uninked type — and then put my cuff down and went back to work, and I understood that she had decided not to believe me, and that her deciding not to believe me was worse than any accusation. She was giving me room to confess. She was leaving the door open the way she used to leave the shop door unlocked for me when I stayed late as a boy.

And every night I walked past that open door carrying a hollow pig under my coat.

Guilt, I have learned, is not a knife. A knife you can pull out. Guilt is more like type metal itself — heavy, dull, and melted down and recast daily into new shapes, each one a little cruder than the last. Some nights I told myself I was protecting her: *if she doesn't know, she can't be charged.* Other nights I admitted the truth, which is that I was protecting myself — from her disappointment, from the particular way she goes quiet when someone has failed her, a quiet worse than shouting. And on the very worst nights I told myself she wouldn't understand anyway, that Isandro with her ledgers and her exact weights had never once in her life done anything that wasn't correct, and that what Riehl and I were doing was *beyond* correctness, above it —

— which is the lie traitors tell themselves, and I knew it was a lie even as I told it, because Isandro is the most moral person I know precisely because she is exact. She thinks honesty is a material, like lead. Something you don't adulterate. And I was adulterating it nightly, and telling myself the alloy would hold.

* * *

Three nights ago Riehl came to the finishing room with news, and the news changed the temperature of everything.

"They've reached Marrenford," he said. Marrenford being two days' ride downstream. "The capital fell Tuesday. The King's taken ship. And Brandt is moving north along the valley with the Second Regiment — requisitioning everything. Grain, boats, presses." He looked around our little room, at Father's press, at the racks, and I watched him do the arithmetic I hadn't yet dared to do myself. "They'll come here within the fortnight. Aldenmoor is the last working foundry on the river."

"Then we stop," I said. "We melt the plates. We stop."

"No," he said, and sat down heavily on Father's stool, which struck me as an intrusion I couldn't explain even to myself. "No, Oscar. This is the moment everything we've printed has been rehearsal for. When they requisition this valley — and they will — they'll need lists. Manifests. Ledgers. Your father's trade is going to become the machinery of the occupation, and the question is whether it also becomes its record." He leaned forward. "I'm asking for one more pamphlet. The last one. About Brandt specifically. What he did in Marrenford last autumn — I have witnesses' accounts, names, dates. If it's in print before his regiment arrives, before he can seal the roads—"

"Riehl." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, Father's voice, and it frightened me a little. "If Brandt finds a pamphlet naming him, he won't search the town. He'll burn it. He'll start with us."

"Yes," Riehl said simply. "That's the risk. I've never lied to you about it."

"And Isandro? And Father? Do they get a vote?"

Something moved behind his eyes then — regret, or recognition, or just tiredness wearing regret's face. "Your sister," he said carefully, "is not part of this."

"No," I agreed. "She isn't. Because I've been very careful. And because I'm a liar. And those are the same fact, Riehl, however you dress them."

He didn't argue. He gathered his papers, and at the door he stopped, the way he'd stopped that first day over the wedding cards, and said: "Eight months ago you asked me why words matter. I told you they outrun armies. Let me add the rest of it, since you're angry enough to hear it. Words also outlive us. Everything else — the regiment, the colonel, the requisitions, you and me — all of it goes into the ground. The printed page is the only thing that stays above it. Print the truth while you can still hold the type, boy. After that, other people decide what your silence meant."

Then he was gone, and I stood in the dark smelling ink and lamp oil and my own fear, and I thought about Isandro asleep upstairs, her hands surely stained even in sleep, her whole faith in the honesty of names and numbers and metal — and I could not make the two halves of my life fit together anymore. I had spent eight months believing the press could outrun the army. I had never once asked what happens to the people standing next to the press when the army arrives.

* * *

Tonight I went down early and pulled the rack of lead pigs into the light of the furnace, and I found the marked ones, all eleven, and I held the newest plate in both hands — Riehl's last pamphlet, already delivered, already waiting — and I stood there for a long time doing the only arithmetic left to me.

Print it, and maybe the town wakes before Brandt seals the roads. Or maybe Brandt burns the foundry with my father in it.

Melt it, and we're safe. Or safe the way a man is safe who watched a crime through a window and said nothing, and lives afterward in a town full of people who will someday learn he saw.

I slid the plate back into its pig and pressed the plug home with my thumb, and I was still standing there, hands black to the wrist, when I heard the street door of the shop open above me, and my sister's voice call down into the dark:

"Oscar? There's smoke on the road from the south. A lot of it. And Father says — Oscar, Father says there are soldiers with it."

I put the pig back in the rack. I wiped my hands on my apron, badly, the way a guilty man does, and I climbed the stairs toward her voice, carrying everything I hadn't told her up into the light.
