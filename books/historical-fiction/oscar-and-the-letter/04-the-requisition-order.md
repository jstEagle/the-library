## 4. The Requisition Order

I had always imagined that if history ever came to Aldenmoor, it would come politely — by post, perhaps, or secondhand through a peddler with news from the capital. I imagined it the way one imagines a flood in another county: regrettable, distant, unlikely to reach the cellar steps.

It came instead on a Tuesday morning, at half past eight, on the sound of hooves.

* * *

I was at the casting bench when the first drum sounded up the river road, that flat military rattle that makes even birds reconsider their opinions. Oscar was out — he had been out more mornings than not lately, returning smelling of river fog and something I couldn't name — and Father was upstairs in the room we had stopped calling his sickroom because he threatened to cast anyone who used the word. So it was I alone who stood in the doorway of Voss & Son Type Foundry, wiping lead dust on my apron, and watched four hundred soldiers walk into my town like they owned the cobblestones.

In fairness, by noon they did.

They came down from the north road in columns, grey coats gone greenish with river damp, bayonets catching the early light so that the whole street seemed briefly to be made of knives. The townspeople did what townspeople do, which is to say they gathered on their doorsteps with the expressions of people watching a house fire that is not yet theirs. Frau Albrecht clutched her washing basket like a shield. Old Pietr stood in front of the chandlery with his arms crossed, as though his disapproval alone might turn an infantry regiment. It did not.

At their head rode a man on a black horse too fine for him, the way a title is sometimes too fine for the man wearing it. He sat very straight. He had the kind of face that has been told since childhood that it is handsome and has never once doubted the report. His uniform was immaculate in a way that struck me, even then, as obscene — every button bright, every crease sharp, while behind him marched boys whose boots were held together with string.

He drew rein in front of the market cross, unrolled a paper with the ceremony of a priest elevating a relic, and read.

I will not reproduce it all. Official language is designed to be forgotten; that is its principal virtue and its entire purpose. But I remember the bones of it, because those bones went into me and stayed:

That by order of the Provisional Authority, the town of Aldenmoor was placed under military administration. That civilian movement beyond the town limits was prohibited. That in consequence of the strategic situation — a phrase doing heroic work, there, carrying more than it could bear — the population would be evacuated by river aboard the steam barge *Kronprinz* and such lighters as could be attached to her. That berths would be assigned according to a schedule of lawful claims, to be verified against existing records. That all records bearing upon property, residency, debt, and entitlement within the district were thereby requisitioned for inspection.

Requisitioned. As though the ledger were a horse, or a sack of grain. As though forty years of names could be loaded onto a cart.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it is a bad one, the colonel rolled up his paper and looked directly at me. At our shop. At the sign above the door — VOSS & SON — which my father had painted himself when I was nine and which had been inaccurate in both directions ever after, since neither of us was a son in the way the sign meant.

"You keep records here," he said. Not a question. A soldier's version of a question, which is a sentence with a period where the uncertainty should be.

"We cut type," I said. "We keep accounts. Ours."

"Accounts of commissions. Ledgers of work done for the town." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that gets reproduced on monuments. "The registry office burned last month, I'm told. Deliberately, some say. Your ledgers may be the only complete record of who lives in this district, and on what claim."

Behind him, the column kept marching. Boots on stone, thousands of them, a sound like the town's own heartbeat being replaced by someone else's.

"Then you'll want the registry clerk," I said, "not a type foundry."

"The registry clerk," he said pleasantly, "is in the capital. Or dead. The paperwork is unclear." He turned his horse a half-circle, surveying our windows, our shelves visible through the glass, the great press that dominated the back room like a sleeping animal. "You'll receive written notice regarding the surrender of documents. I'd have it ready."

He did not wait for my answer, which was fortunate, because my answer would have been unwise. He put his heels to the horse and moved off down the street, reading another paper over the heads of another crowd, and I stood in my own doorway feeling the ground tilt beneath me in a way it had no geological right to do.

* * *

Father heard the drums before I reached the stairs. Of course he did. The man's hearing had begun its long departure two years ago, but it made exceptions for anything that might threaten his family or his type, and it made them with perfect reliability.

"They're here, then," he said. He was sitting up in bed with a proof sheet on his knees — he still pulled proofs daily, checking the state of his own decline against earlier impressions the way other men check the weather. His hands, which had once been the finest punchcutter's hands between the mountains and the sea, lay quiet on the paper now, spotted dark at the nails.

"Four hundred infantry and a colonel with a horse worth more than our roof."

"And what did the colonel want?"

"The ledger." I said it plainly. There was no point dressing it. "All of them. Every record in town. They're evacuating us by barge, Father, and the berths go by 'lawful claim,' and apparently we're the only lawful record left standing."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the drums had stopped and the shouting had started — the particular organized shouting of soldiers establishing themselves, billets being assigned, doors being knocked upon. Our street had become a garrison in the time it took me to climb two flights of stairs.

"The ledger," my father repeated, and then he laughed — a short, terrible cough of a laugh that ended in something wetter than laughter. "Forty years I've kept that book honest. Forty years. Do you know how many men offered me money to lose a page? How many offered money to add one?"

"I know," I said. "I've heard the stories."

"Stories." He shook his head. "They're not stories, Isandro. They're Tuesday afternoons. Herr Falkmann offering three months' wages to move his brother-in-law ahead of the millers' petition. The Kessler widow offering something else entirely, God forgive her, when her husband's debt entry wouldn't die. And every time, the same answer." He looked at his hands. "The book says what happened. That's all a book can do. That's everything a book can do."

I sat on the edge of his bed. Downstairs, someone hammered on our shutters — not our door, our shutters, as though the distinction mattered to men with rifles.

"When Brandt's notice comes," Father said, "you will give them nothing without my mark on the surrender. Nothing. Promise me."

"Father—"

"Promise me. If they take it, they take it whole and witnessed, with a receipt signed by a name I can curse in the next world. No pages 'borrowed.' No entries 'corrected for review.' You understand me? A book is only true until one hand touches it with intent. After that—" he coughed, and pressed the proof sheet to his mouth, and when it came away there was rust on it, "—after that it's just paper with ambitions."

I promised. I want that recorded, since this account will likely be my only monument: I promised, and I meant it, and I believed I would keep it. The fact that I did not is not a contradiction I intend to resolve here. Perhaps nowhere. Some contradictions are load-bearing.

* * *

The written notice arrived before noon, delivered by a corporal with a face like a closed ledger himself — nothing given away, nothing asked twice. By then the town had transformed around us. Soldiers at the bridge. Soldiers at the mill. A field kitchen steaming in the market square, filling the streets with a smell of boiled meat that made the whole town hungrier by association. The *Kronprinz* lay at the coal dock where she had been tied since spring, and I saw — everyone saw — that she was not large enough. That was the arithmetic nobody said aloud and everybody did instantly: however many names the ledger held, the barge held fewer.

The corporal read the notice to me in the shop, though I could read it perfectly well myself, and I suspected the reading aloud was itself part of the procedure — a small theater of authority, performed for the benefit of any neighbors peering through the glass. Which they were. Half of Aldenmoor seemed to be peering through our glass, and why not. If the ledger lived in our back room, then our back room had just become the most important room in the valley.

"...all books, registers, rolls, and schedules pertaining to persons resident within the district shall be surrendered to the Office of the Military Administrator within seventy-two hours of this notice..." He looked up. "Where is the ledger in question?"

"In question," I repeated. "There are several."

"Then all of them."

"Several thousand pounds of account books going back to 1809. You'll need a cart."

"I'll need a cart," he agreed, without irony, writing it down. The army, I was learning, could absorb almost any absurdity provided it arrived in writing.

When he had gone, I stood among my cases and my moulds and felt the shop differently than I had that morning. For eleven years this room had been a place where letters were made — where a thought got weighed in metal and became permanent, became repeatable, became something that could outlive the mouth that first spoke it. Now the same room was a vault. Every name in town was sitting on my shelves in the form of accounts receivable and delivery dates, and outside the window stood four hundred men whose colonel wanted those names, and whose government needed fewer berths than there were families to fill them.

I did the arithmetic again, because I couldn't help it. Two hundred and six names on the current roll. The *Kronprinz*, by Father's old knowledge of river tonnage, perhaps one hundred and sixty souls if the lighters didn't come through.

Forty-six families. Give or take.

Give or take was about to become the most violent phrase in the German language.

* * *

Oscar came home at dusk, slipping in through the yard gate with the furtive economy that had become his signature these past weeks. He took in the scene in one turn of his head — the notice pinned to the door frame, the soldiers' shadows moving past the windows, my face, which I am told does not conceal well.

"So it's real," he said.

"It's real."

"The ledger. They'll take the ledger."

"In three days, with a cart and a receipt."

He set down the satchel he carried everywhere now, the one I had stopped asking about. For a moment he looked younger than his twenty years, and older than mine, both at once, which I have since learned is the standard condition of people to whom history is happening.

"Isandro." He lowered his voice, though the soldiers outside couldn't possibly hear us over their own boots. "What's in that ledger — the claims, the passages, who holds title to what — do you understand what it means to them? To *him*?" He meant Brandt; everyone already meant Brandt. "A berth is a life. Whoever holds the book holds the town's throat."

"I had noticed," I said. My wryness was failing me, which frightened me more than the soldiers did. I have always been able to joke my way across a burning floor. That evening the jokes wouldn't come, and I understood that something had changed in me permanently, the way metal changes once it has been poured and cooled.

From upstairs came Father's cough, long and wet and rhythmic as a press running. Oscar and I looked at each other, and in that look we conducted the entire negotiation we would spend the rest of this story conducting: *what do we protect, and what do we risk, and who are we when the two refuse to fit in the same case?*

Neither of us won the argument. Neither of us knew yet that we were having it.

Above the mantel hangs the first thing Father ever cast — a single line of type, framed under glass, the motto of the shop and, unknowingly, of everything that followed:

*WHAT IS SET IN LEAD STAYS SET.*

He meant it as a boast about durability. Standing there in the lamplight, with a requisition notice flapping at my back and forty-six families' worth of arithmetic pressing on my chest, I began to suspect it was also a warning.

Three days. Seventy-two hours. Somewhere in this building sat the truest object in Aldenmoor, and somewhere in this building — though I did not yet know where, though I would not know for days — sat the thing that would make it necessary to lie.
