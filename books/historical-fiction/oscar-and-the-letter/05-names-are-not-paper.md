## 5. Names Are Not Paper

By the third day of the occupation I had learned the soldiers' schedule the way one learns a difficult font — by repetition, by failure, by the small humiliations of getting it wrong twice before getting it right. They changed the guard at six and at two. The sergeant with the walrus mustache came for his ration of ink at nine, ostensibly to requisition it for official correspondence, though from the smudges on his cuffs he was mostly writing letters to a woman in the capital who was either his wife or not. The officers dined at seven and were loud by eight.

And at all hours, it seemed, the townsfolk of Aldenmoor came to my door.

They began politely. Frau Kessler arrived on the first morning after the requisition order, dressed in her church clothes as if the shop were a pew and God might be taking attendance, and asked — delicately, the way you ask about someone's health when you suspect the answer — whether her family's entry in the ledger was *quite* legible. Whether the ink had held these forty years. Whether perhaps a careful eye should check it.

"It's legible," I said. "Your name is there. It has been there since your husband signed it."

"Yes," she said, and did not leave. She looked at the ledger through the glass of its case the way people look at relics. "Yes, well. Ink fades."

"Ink doesn't fade in a closed case, Frau Kessler."

"Everything fades," she said, with the certainty of a woman who had buried two children and a husband, and left.

By noon there were four of them waiting outside. By evening, twelve. Old Brenner the cooper, whose hands were too ruined for barrels anymore but who still stood like a man expecting wood to obey him. The Halvard brothers, who hated each other in the ordinary way of brothers and stood on opposite sides of my doorway in pointed silence. The young widow Marta Ilse, whom I did not yet know by any other name than that, holding a sleeping child against her shoulder as though the child were a document she feared would be confiscated.

Word had gone around Aldenmoor with the speed that word travels in river towns — which is to say faster than the river, faster than sense — that the ledger in the Voss foundry was now the only proof in the world of who owned what: who held a berth on the evacuation barge, who had a legal claim to passage, who was somebody and who, in the army's arithmetic, was nobody. Colonel Brandt's men had posted the proclamation on the church door in type so crude it could only have been set by a soldier, every letter standing at attention and meaning nothing. *Claims shall be verified against the registry of record. Claims not verified shall be forfeit.*

*Registry of record.* As if our ledger were some government thing, born in an office, instead of a book my grandfather had started with a goose quill and a bad temper.

"They'll come for it tomorrow," Brenner said, once they'd crowded into the type room, filling it past capacity the way a form is filled past the edge of its matrix. "The soldiers. Tomorrow or the day after."

"The colonel said within the week," I said.

"The colonel says many things." He spat, thought better of it, given my floor, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Voss girl. You cut letters. Can't you cut us a little mercy?"

There it was. Two days into the occupation, and already the question had arrived at my doorstep wearing its work clothes, ready to move in.

"I don't cut mercy," I said. "I cut letters."

"Same thing, maybe," said one of the Halvards, the younger one, Henrik, who fancied himself clever. "A name is just letters, isn't it? Add a letter here, move a line there. Who'd know?"

"Everyone," I said, "eventually. That's rather the trouble with everyone."

Marta Ilse shifted the child to her other shoulder. She hadn't spoken yet, and when she did, her voice was quiet enough that the room had to lean in to hear it, which is a power most loud men never learn.

"My husband's name is in your book," she said. "He died in March. If his claim isn't honored, my son and I have nowhere the barge is going. We have nowhere at all. I'm not asking you to lie, Miss Voss. I'm asking you to tell me the truth will hold."

And there it was again, the weight — except this time it wasn't the abstract weight I'd carried all my life, the weight of being the competent daughter in a house of lead and ink, the one who checked the proofs because Father's eyes tired. This was a different alloy entirely. Trust is heavier than lead. Lead at least has the decency to stay where you put it.

* * *

Father heard them from upstairs.

He'd been hearing everything for three days, I think, though he pretended otherwise, conserving his breath the way a miser counts coins. The cough had worsened since the soldiers came — fear does that to a sick man, loosens whatever the illness has nailed down — and that morning I had seen red on his handkerchief, more than a fleck this time, enough that we both pretended I hadn't seen it, which is its own kind of lying, and I was beginning to lose count of how many kinds there were.

He came down the stairs one step at a time, gripping the rail, wearing his good coat over his nightshirt because dignity was the last tool in his kit and he intended to use it. The crowd hushed. Half of Aldenmoor had learned their letters at his benches; the other half had learned them at his father's. When Elias Voss walked into a room, the alphabet itself seemed to stand up straighter.

"You want me to protect your names," he said. No preamble. His voice had gone thin as foil but it still carried, the way a good punch carries through soft metal.

"Yes," said Brenner, speaking for all of them, the way coopers do.

"Then hear what protection costs." He lowered himself into the chair behind the counting desk, and I moved to help him and was waved off, and I let myself be waved off because some men need to arrive under their own power. "This book has one virtue. Not beauty — you've seen my father's handwriting, it's a crime against geese. Not completeness — we've lost years to fire and flood and my own carelessness. One virtue only: it is true. Every name in it went in honest, witnessed, signed. That is why Brandt wants it. Not because it's paper. Because it's *true*, and truth in Aldenmoor has gotten rare enough to be worth requisitioning."

"But sir," Henrik Halvard began, "if a few names were—"

"If a few names were adjusted," Father said, "then the book becomes a weapon pointed at whoever the adjuster dislikes. Today you'd point it at Brandt. Fine. Good aim. And next year? Next year some man with a grudge and access to my bench adjusts it the other way, and your grandson learns that the family name was sold for a favor in '48 and no one remembers by whom." He coughed then, briefly, horribly, into the handkerchief, and folded it away before anyone could see what I had already seen. "Names are not paper. Paper you can reprint. A name is what your neighbors call you when you're dead. I will not teach this town that its names can be bought and moved about like furniture."

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

It was Marta Ilse who broke it, because it is always the quiet ones. "Then we're asking you to be honest in a dishonest time, Mr. Voss. And honesty gets people hung."

"Honesty got my grandfather run out of two cities," Father said, "and it built this shop, and it put every name in that book, and it is the only inheritance I have to leave my children that won't rust." He looked at me then — just a glance, half a second, but fathers of dying men spend glances carefully and this one was spent on purpose. "Isandro keeps the books now. Her hand is steadier than mine. But hear me: she'll keep them honest if I have to come back and haunt the composing stick."

A few of them laughed. It was that laughter that undid me a little — nervous, grateful laughter, the laughter of people handing a stranger their most valuable possession and hoping the stranger turns out to be honest. Twelve faces, and behind each face a household, and behind each household a string of names going back further than the ledger did. They weren't trusting me because I was trustworthy. They were trusting me because I was *there*, and the alternative was a colonel with beautiful manners and a price list.

They filed out as the light went. Marta Ilse was last. At the door she turned.

"He'd have liked you," she said. "My husband. He used to say a town is just a lot of names agreeing to be responsible for each other." Then she was gone, down the dark street, and I was alone with a ledger full of agreements.

* * *

Oscar came home late, smelling of cold air and something else, something acrid that I couldn't place and didn't yet have a word for. Smoke, I would learn later. Lampblack and haste. But that night I only knew he smelled of secrets, and that his cuffs — again, *again* — carried traces of type dust that matched no job in our books.

"You heard them," I said. He'd stopped in the doorway of the kitchen, coat still on.

"Half the town was in the shop. Hard to miss."

"What do you think we ought to do?"

He took off his coat slowly, buying time the way he always did, folding it with excessive care. My brother, who could set a page of body text faster than anyone on the river, could not fold a coat at a normal speed when he was deciding whether to lie to me.

"I think," he said, "that Father is right. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"There are worse things than a lie, Isandro. Sometimes the truth is just what powerful men use to sort the drowning from the swimming." He said it lightly, tossing it off, but his eyes didn't match his voice, and that mismatch had been growing between us all autumn like a crack in a forme that shows only when the press comes down.

"Whose truth are you protecting, Oscar?" I asked. "The town's? Or whoever's been keeping you out past midnight with printer's dirt on your sleeves?"

I watched him decide. I actually watched it happen — the small muscular event behind his eyes, the weighing of me, of what I might do, of what I might forgive. And I watched him choose the lie.

"Nobody," he said. "You're tired. We're all tired."

He kissed the top of my head, the way he had since we were children, and went up to bed, and I sat a long while in the kitchen with the banked stove ticking and the ledger case visible through the doorway, holding its forty years of honest names in the dark.

Somewhere across town a soldier laughed at nothing. Somewhere a widow was putting her son to bed in a house that existed, tonight, only because a line of ink in a closed case said it did. And somewhere out in that same night, I understood without knowing any details yet — the way you know weather coming in your knuckles — that my brother was carrying something that would eventually land on this kitchen table, and that when it did, Father's one-virtue book and Oscar's midnight errands would be pulling on the same page from opposite directions.

I banked the stove. I locked the case. I wrote nothing down, which for a keeper of records is its own kind of entry.

The knock came at dawn — not the polite knock of Frau Kessler and her fading-ink anxieties, but the flat double rap of a glove on a door, and through the frosted glass, the silhouette of a peaked cap, patient as punctuation.

Colonel Brandt had decided the week was over early.
