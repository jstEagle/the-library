## 6. What Anselm Left

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, exactly like diligence. I had been practicing it for three weeks — sweeping the shop, oiling the press that didn't need oiling, sorting sorts into cases that were already sorted — all so that when my sister finally cornered me, I could point at something useful and say, *you see? Busy hands. Nothing to hide here.*

Isandro did not corner me so much as simply exist in my path, which was worse. She had a way of standing in a doorway the way our father's oldest galley stood on the stone bench: immovable, load-bearing, and quietly judging everything set upon it.

"Oscar," she said.

"Isandro," I returned, with the breezy confidence of a man whose cuffs had been scrubbed twice and still smelled faintly of lampblack.

"You've been printing."

"I've been *breathing*. It's a thing people do near presses. The smell gets everywhere."

"The smell," she said, "gets everywhere. But you have type dust under your nails from a size we don't stock, on a paper stock we don't buy, for a job that isn't in the ledger." She crossed her arms. She did this often enough that I sometimes thought of her arms as a second set of quoins, locking everything down tight. "And last Tuesday you came home at two in the morning smelling of the river and someone else's tobacco. You don't smoke. You think it's a vulgar habit. You told Herr Baumann's son it would rot his lungs *and* his prose."

"That was an unfair characterization of my views. I said it rotted his lungs and his *margins*."

"Don't." Her voice dropped, and there it was — not anger, not yet. Fear wearing anger's coat. "Don't make jokes at me, Oscar. Not now. Not with soldiers in the square and Father coughing blood into his handkerchief like he's paying a tithe to Death himself. Whatever you're doing — whoever you're doing it *for* — I need to know. Because if they find out before I do, then I'm a liar too, by proximity, and proximity has never once saved anyone in this town."

I want to say I stood firm. I want to tell you I looked my sister in the eye and held my silence like a man holds a door against wind. What actually happened is that my knees sat down on a crate of twelve-point Baskerville without asking my permission first.

"It's Anselm Riehl," I said.

The name went through the room like cold water through a cracked casting. Isandro's face did several things very quickly and settled on none of them.

"Riehl is dead," she said.

"Riehl is *reported* dead. There's a difference, and the difference is currently living in our cellar."

She looked toward the cellar door as though it might confirm or deny. "Our cellar. The one full of lead pigs."

"The one full of lead pigs," I agreed. "Which are, some of them, not entirely pigs."

* * *

Here is how a man becomes a traitor without ever deciding to be one: gradually, and then in a single evening, and then forever.

It began in September, when Riehl's pamphlets started appearing in Aldenmoor — those cheap, furious little sheets, *The Common Hand*, printed on paper so thin you could read three lies through it at once. Everyone read them. Everyone burned them immediately after, in the way of people who enjoy fire and fear witnesses. The army called him a seditionist. The town called him a printer. My father called him *that fool with the opinions*, which in our house was nearly a canonization.

I met him by accident, which is how most important meetings go. I'd taken a delivery of antimony upriver and found a man with a burned left hand and a scholar's stoop waiting at the dock, watching our crates with the expression of a starving man watching a bakery window.

"You're Voss," he said. "The foundry."

"We're a foundry," I said carefully. "There are several Vosses. Some of us are dull."

"And some of you aren't." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had already decided something about you and was being polite about the interval. "I need someone who can cast type quickly and ask nothing."

"I ask everything. It's a family failing."

"So do I." And then he told me what he needed, and I told him no, and I went home, and I lay awake until four in the morning composing arguments against myself, and every argument lost.

That's the confession I've never made cleanly, even to Isandro, especially to Isandro: I wasn't recruited. I *volunteered*. I walked back down to the dock two days later like a dog that had remembered where dinner comes from, and I said, "What do you need first?"

What he needed first was type. Then plates. Then a press run in a barn outside town while I kept watch and sweated through my shirt in October. Then more type. He paid in coin that smelled of other provinces, and I hid the money in the flour bin because even traitors are practical about storage.

By November I was casting his headlines between my father's commercial orders — *THE ARMY FEEDS ITSELF AND LETS THE TOWN STARVE*, *THEY SELL YOUR ROADS TO MEN WHO NEVER WALKED THEM* — and each time I locked the chase and pulled the lever, I felt what I can only describe as the opposite of guilt. Guilt is heavy. This was lightness. Terrible, weightless lightness, like the moment a barge lets go of its mooring lines.

Words outrun soldiers. That was the whole of my philosophy, and I believed it with the pure, untroubled faith of a twenty-three-year-old man who had never been shot at. Words travel. Words multiply. You can burn a pamphlet but you cannot burn an idea, etcetera, etcetera. I believed it so thoroughly that I never once asked the question Isandro asked within thirty seconds of hearing everything:

"And what happens to the people holding the words when the soldiers come?"

* * *

So there we were, brother and sister, standing over a false wall behind the pig racks, and I was explaining to her — with the eloquence of a man digging his own grave in real time — how I'd built it. A loose brick, a shelf on leather hinges, and inside, forty pounds of cast type wrapped in oilcloth, three engraved plates, a ream of thin paper, and one wounded fugitive's entire remaining arsenal.

"He's here," Isandro said. "In our cellar. Under Father's shop. Under *Father*."

"He arrived three nights ago. He was shot getting out of Marrenholt. Through the shoulder. It's bad, but it missed anything that matters."

"Missed anything that matters," she repeated, in the tone she used when a customer insisted a misprint was intentional. "Oscar. There are soldiers billeted in the granary. There is a colonel requisitioning half the street. Our father is dying upstairs, and you have been keeping a hunted man beneath his floorboards like — like a cache of smuggled brandy."

"I was going to tell you."

"When?"

"...After."

"After what?"

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was *after it stopped being dangerous*, and we both knew that day was not on any calendar either of us owned.

She turned away from me and pressed both hands flat on the pig rack, and I watched her shoulders do the arithmetic. Isandro calculates the way other people breathe; I've seen her price a job, cut the matrices, and grieve a loss all in the same hour. Now I could practically hear the abacus clicking: fugitive in cellar, plus army in town, plus dying father, plus ledger, equals—

"The ledger," she said suddenly, turning. "You knew. About the requisition. About what the ledger means now — berths, claims, the whole evacuation. You knew when you let me catalog names into it last week like a good daughter doing her duty."

"I knew the army wanted records. I didn't know about the berths."

"But Riehl knew. Didn't he. He always knows." She laughed, one short ugly note. "He knows everything except how to keep himself unshot."

"He wants to see you," I said. "Both of us. He has a proposal."

"A proposal. From a bleeding seditionist in our coal cellar. Wonderful. Fetch me my dancing slippers."

But she followed me down the stairs, because she is our father's daughter, and in our family curiosity beats caution the way a mallet beats a matrix — regularly, and with intent.

* * *

Anselm Riehl, propped against grain sacks on a pallet I'd made of sacking and guilt, looked less like a revolutionary than like a schoolmaster who had lost a fight with his own curriculum. He was gray around the mouth. His bandaged shoulder smelled of the poultice I'd bought off Frau Kessler, who asked no questions because she charged triple for silence and considered it a growth industry.

"Fräulein Voss," he said. His voice was roughened, but the mind behind it was working fine; you could see it moving behind his eyes like type in a composing stick. "Your brother speaks well of you."

"My brother speaks well of everyone he's deceiving. It's how he manages."

"Isandro—"

"No. Let him talk," she said, pulling a crate over and sitting on it with her back straight, arms folded, the human quoin. "Then let him listen while I say what happens next."

Riehl inclined his head, gracious as a duelist. "I'll be brief. Your brother has been my hands in this town since September, and I trust him with my life, which is fortunate, because I am presently spending it. I have one job left in me, and it requires your foundry."

"The army took your press in Marrenholt," I supplied.

"The army took *two* presses. They found neither of mine." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "A press is only wood and iron, Fräulein. The press is the man who sets it. Which brings me to the matter at hand." He shifted, winced, continued. "Colonel Brandt is selling berths on the evacuation barge."

Isandro went very still. Even the lamplight seemed to lean in.

"Not rationed by claim, as the proclamation says. Not by the ledger's order. Sold. To loyalists, to profiteers, to anyone with gold and no conscience — and there is no shortage of either. I have the evidence." He patted a packet of papers beside him, oilcloth-wrapped, soft-cornered with handling. "Manifests. Receipts. Two letters in Brandt's own hand arranging payment through a merchant in Grelstadt. Names, sums, dates. Enough to hang him legally if the law still existed, and to ruin him publicly if it doesn't."

"And you want it printed," Isandro said flatly.

"I want it *published*. There's a distinction a printer's daughter will appreciate. A pamphlet nailed to doors does one thing. An edition — hundreds of copies, passed hand to hand, copied, carried downriver, reaching consuls and newspapers beyond his reach — does another. The first is graffiti. The second is testimony. Brandt can arrest graffiti. He cannot arrest a fact that has already left the province in fifty pockets."

"And when he traces it back?" Isandro's voice stayed level, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "When his officers find the type dust, the paper stock, the press that ran at night in a requisitioned foundry? Who does he arrest, Herr Riehl? You? You're already a corpse on paper. He'll arrest the printers. He'll arrest the *family*. He'll arrest my father, who cannot walk to the privy without resting twice, and put him in front of a wall because his children printed the truth."

"That is a risk," Riehl said quietly.

"It's a certainty wearing a risk's clothing!"

"And the alternative?" For the first time his voice sharpened, and I saw the pamphleteer underneath the invalid, the man who had talked entire print shops into ruin for the pleasure of a headline. "The alternative is that Brandt sells every berth on that barge, the families with legal claim rot on the dock, and when the ledger is finally demanded, it shows exactly the order he wants it to show, because no one alive can prove otherwise. Your father spent his life making sure names were true, Fräulein. I am offering the only chance to make sure they stay true *out loud*, where a lie can't sit on top of them."

Silence. The coal cellar held its breath. Somewhere above us, floorboards creaked — Father, walking his small painful circuit, or perhaps just the house settling, houses settle when families crack; I'm convinced of it.

Isandro stood. She looked at Riehl, then at me, and I braced for the verdict.

"Oscar," she said, "show me the plates."

I blinked. "You're not saying no?"

"I'm saying I haven't decided. Show me the plates."

I lifted the oilcloth and unwrapped the engravings — Riehl's own cutting, done in hiding, the letters sharp despite his ruined hand. Isandro took them the way she takes everything: carefully, competently, turning them to the lamp, reading the receipts upside down and right side up, lips moving slightly over the sums.

"These amounts," she murmured. "This is — Oscar, this is a year of the foundry's clear profit per *berth*. Times forty." She looked up. "Do you understand what this means? Half the town thinks the barge list is their salvation. It's merchandise."

"Now you understand why I couldn't stay quiet," Riehl said softly.

"I understand why you couldn't stay quiet." She set the plates down with a click that sounded like a sort dropping home. "I don't yet understand why *we* should pay your bill. Because make no mistake, gentlemen — if we print this, and it fails, it will not be your name on the arrest warrant. It will be *Voss*. Both of them. And a father who won't live to see spring regardless."

"Then don't do it for me," Riehl said. "Do it for the names."

"The names," Isandro repeated, and something crossed her face — I saw it and filed it away without understanding it, the way you file a misprint you'll puzzle out later at two in the morning. "Everyone keeps handing me the names lately. As if they were mine to carry."

"They are yours to carry," Riehl said simply. "You're the one with the foundry."

She looked at me then, long and level, and whatever she was deciding, I understood that I had already lost part of her — that the brother she'd swept the shop beside all these years was now, in some ledger of her keeping, entered under a different heading. *Assets previously undisclosed.* I hated it. I would do it all again.

"One condition," she said at last.

"Name it," said Riehl.

"If we print — and I haven't said we print — the edition runs *here*, on our schedule, after Father sleeps, and Oscar tells me everything from now on. Everything. No more river-smelling secrets, no more flour-bin money. If you lie to me again, Oscar Voss, I will melt your type down myself and cast it into a paperweight, and I will engrave *LIAR* on it in fourteen point."

"That seems proportionate," I said.

"And Herr Riehl." She crouched to his level, and for a moment I saw our father in her posture — the same terrible gentleness. "When your shoulder heals enough to travel, you leave. This cellar is a larder, not a sanctuary. I won't bury my brother for your cause, however just. Do we understand each other?"

"Perfectly," Riehl said, and smiled, and I noticed — and I'm certain she noticed — that he did not agree to anything. Revolutionaries rarely do. They simply survive until the next necessary crime.

We climbed back up into the shop, and Isandro paused on the stairs, one hand on the rail, not looking at me.

"How long," she said, "have you known about the berth sales?"

"Since Tuesday."

"Tuesday." She nodded slowly. "Three days. While I inked Father's proofs and smiled at customers and catalogued names into that ledger like they meant something. Three days you watched me do it, knowing the list was already bought."

"Isandro—"

"Goodnight, Oscar."

She went up. I stood in the dark of the shop among the cases and the galleys, in the smell of lead and oil that has been the whole architecture of my life, and I understood that the fracture Riehl's arrival had opened wasn't between us and the army at all. It ran through the middle of the workbench, through the middle of the family, straight down the center of me.

Above my head, through the floorboards, I heard the front door open — early visitors, before eight, when the shop wasn't yet lit. Boots. Several pairs. The particular unhurried confidence of boots that expect doors to open for them.

"Voss establishment?" said a voice, smooth as fresh-cast type. "How fortuitous. I was in the neighborhood, inspecting requisitions. Colonel Brandt. Do fetch your father — and whoever else keeps the books."

I blew out the lamp.
