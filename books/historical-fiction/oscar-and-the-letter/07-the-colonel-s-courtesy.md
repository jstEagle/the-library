## 7. The Colonel's Courtesy

Colonel Brandt came to the foundry on a Thursday, which I remember because Thursdays were the days we cast the small sorts — the commas, the periods, the hyphens — the punctuation of the world, my father called them, the little marks that tell a sentence when to breathe. He arrived at ten in the morning with two soldiers and no visible weapon, which was itself a kind of weapon, the way an empty holster is louder than a full one.

I knew he was coming before the knock. The whole street had gone quiet in that particular way towns go quiet when authority walks through them — not silent, exactly, but performing silence, every hammer and kettle suddenly discovering urgent business elsewhere. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"Fraulein Voss." He removed his hat in the doorway, and I will grant him this: he had excellent manners, the sort you can't buy but only inherit, along with the land and the titles and the conviction that both are owed to you. "I hope the hour is convenient."

"It isn't," I said, because lying to him about small things seemed like good practice for the large ones.

"Then I shall make it so, as best I can." He smiled at his own joke, waited for me to smile too, and when I didn't, smiled harder, as though politeness were a pump handle and persistence would eventually draw water.

My father received him in the front room, the one with the sample books and the framed broadsides we'd printed for the town over forty years — wedding announcements, funeral notices, one memorable proclamation about a runaway pig that had become local legend. Father sat in his chair like a king holding court from a sickbed, gray-faced, upright by sheer spite, his hands folded over the blanket as if they were resting between tasks.

"Colonel." My father's voice was gravel and iron filings. "You've come about the ledger."

"I've come about many things," Brandt said pleasantly, settling into the chair opposite without being asked. His soldiers remained at the door, studying our walls as if typography might be contraband. "But yes. The ledger foremost."

"Then let us speak of it plainly. I am an old man and my hours are rationed."

"Plainly, then." The colonel crossed his legs, arranged his coat, performed comfort. "The barge *Kestrel* departs in eleven days. She has berths for three hundred souls. Your ledger establishes legal claim — priority of registration, family standing, property held against passage rights. It is, I'm told, the only document of its kind in Aldenmoor."

"It is."

"And it is accurate?"

"It is true," my father said, and I heard the distinction he was drawing, the way a compositor hears a kerned pair sitting wrong even before he sees it.

"Truth." Brandt tasted the word like a wine he suspected of being watered. "Herr Voss, may I be frank? Wars make truth expensive. The capital has fallen. The provinces burn. This town has been requisitioned, as you know, for the movement of troops and matériel, and the civilian evacuation must proceed under military administration. Which means the berths on the *Kestrel* fall under my authority." He paused, letting that settle like dust after casting. "Yours is a family of printers. You understand documents. A document is not merely a record of what happened. It is an instrument of what happens next."

In the type room behind us, I could hear Oscar stop working. Not quietly. The sudden absence of sound, the way a man holds his breath.

"You are proposing," my father said slowly, "that we alter the ledger."

"I am proposing," said Brandt, "that we *correct* it. There are families in this town whose claims are... complicated. Whose loyalties were ambiguous during the troubles. And there are others — loyal families, productive families — whose registration came late through no fault of their own. An error of sequence. A clerical misfortune." He spread his hands. "The ledger stands between these families and justice. All I ask is that it lean."

"Toward whom?"

"Toward order." He said it so reasonably that for half a second I almost admired it. It takes real craft to make theft sound like bookkeeping.

My father coughed then — the deep, wet cough that had been emptying him all autumn — and covered his mouth with a handkerchief that came away spotted, and folded it away again with the practiced discretion of a man who had been hiding his dying for months. But Brandt saw. Of course he saw. Men like Brandt see everything; it is how they decide what to take.

"You are ill," the colonel said, gently now, mercy putting on its coat.

"I am old. The distinction matters less every day."

"The voyage is hard. Cold river, crowded decks, disease in the ports." He shook his head with genuine-seeming sorrow. "A man in your condition — forgive me — the crossing would be cruel to you. And your children would spend their strength hauling you instead of building their lives." He leaned forward. "Herr Voss. Three berths. Guaranteed. Priority boarding, cabin space, provisions. For you, for your son, for your daughter. In exchange for a corrected edition of the ledger. Nothing more. No one need ever know the hand that moved the names."

* * *

I want to tell you that I felt nothing. That temptation came to me wearing horns and I knew it on sight. But temptation doesn't wear horns. It wears a uniform and removes its hat in the doorway. It offers you your father's life in exchange for other people's names, and it calls the trade *mercy*, and for one long, filthy moment, standing in my father's front room beneath forty years of wedding announcements, I wanted it.

Three berths. My father, who coughed blood into linen and pretended otherwise. Oscar, who was — though I didn't yet know how much — already tangled in something that would need a fast boat. Myself. Safe. Warm. Alive.

And all it would cost was the widow Halm's place on the list. The Brenner boys and their mother. Old Toma with his bad lungs and his granddaughter. Names I had set into that ledger myself, line by line, in my own careful hand, back when setting names meant honoring them.

"May we have until evening?" I said.

Brandt turned to me as if noticing me for the first time, which was itself a performance — he had been watching me since the door; I'd felt it like a drafts under the sill. "Of course. Grief requires deliberation, and this is a kind of grief." He rose, replaced his hat. At the door he paused. "One thing more. A formality. We have reports of seditious printing in the district. Pamphlets. Filth, really — the kind of words that get honest people hanged alongside guilty ones." He smiled at my father. "Your establishment, naturally, is beyond suspicion. But if such material should pass through your hands — if it should arrive, unbidden, unwanted — you will bring it to me. As a courtesy between colleagues of the press."

"We are not colleagues," my father said.

"No," Brandt agreed, at the threshold, not turning around. "But we are both men who understand what ink can do. Good day."

* * *

The door closed. Neither soldier looked at us as they left, which frightened me more than staring would have. Staring means suspicion. Not-staring means they already know whatever they came to know.

For a long time nobody spoke. The kettle in the back room ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, the first bold sound the town had made in an hour.

"Well," my father said at last. "He offers a fair price for stolen goods. That is more honesty than most thieves manage."

"Father—"

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Don't defend it and don't condemn it. Not yet. Sit down, both of you." And we sat, Oscar and I, on either side of him like flanking columns of text, and he looked at us for a while with eyes that had gone yellow at the edges.

"When your mother died," he said, "the priest told me grief was a debt we pay for love. I thought it pretty nonsense. But I have learned since that most transactions are dressed as gifts, and most gifts as transactions, and the only protection is to know which side of the counter you're standing on." He tapped the arm of his chair. "That man offered me three lives for three hundred names. He thinks that because I am dying, I will find the arithmetic kind."

"Would it be so terrible?" The words were out of me before I could weigh them, and once out they kept talking, the way lead keeps pouring. "Father — three berths. *Guaranteed.* You've seen the lists of what's happening in the occupied towns. If we refuse and he simply takes the ledger anyway—"

"He cannot take it. He needs it credible. A seized ledger is a confiscated document, subject to challenge in every port and chancery downriver. An *volunteered* correction—" my father's mouth twisted, "—that is gold to him. He came courting because he must. That is the one weapon we hold, and you'd hand it to him for a warm berth and a clear conscience."

"A clear conscience bought with a widow's drowning," Oscar said quietly.

We both looked at him. He hadn't spoken since the colonel left, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — guilt, certainly, but guilt braided with something hotter, something almost like relief, as if the colonel's visit had settled some private argument.

"The widow Halm registered her claim fourteen years ago," Oscar went on. "Her son is nineteen. If her name moves down the list, he goes into the conscription levies instead of onto the barge, and the levies are going to the eastern works, where men don't come home. That isn't arithmetic, Isandro. That's just killing with better handwriting."

"And what would you do?" I asked. "Burn the ledger? Let Brandt seize it and forge whatever he likes with no one left who knows the true entries?" I heard my voice climbing and hated it. "There are three hundred berths and nine hundred souls in this town, Oscar. Every arrangement is a betrayal of someone. The only question is whether we choose the betrayal or it chooses us."

"That's what he wants you to believe. That there's no clean hand to play, so any hand is clean enough."

"Enough." My father's voice cracked across the room like a quoit dropped on stone. We shut up. He breathed for a while — I watched him do it, deliberately, each breath a small act of construction — and then he said, "Isandro. Bring me the ledger."

"Father—"

"*Bring me the ledger.*"

I brought it. The great book with its brass corners and its forty years, heavy as sin, heavier. I laid it on his knees and he opened it to the current page and put his palm flat on the column of names, the way you'd lay a hand on a child's head.

"Every name here is a promise this shop made," he said. "Not the army's promise. Not the government's. Ours. When the Brenners registered their claim, they trusted *us* to keep it — not the law, not the colonel, us, because paper is only as honest as the hands that set it." He looked up at me, and his eyes were clear in a way that frightened me worse than the coughing did. Clear the way water is clear right before it freezes. "I will die soon. Probably before the barge sails. And when I do, this shop, this ledger, this trust passes to you two. So I will not decide now. But hear me: whatever you choose, choose knowing the price is not paid in berths. The price is paid in what this town believes about itself afterward. Names are not paper, Isandro. They are the only immortality poor people get."

He closed the book. The sound of it was very small and very final, like a period set at the end of a long sentence.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, and near midnight I went down to the type room to be among the cases, which is where I go to pray, if praying is sitting alone with ten thousand small metal voices and asking them to mean something.

Oscar was there.

He stood at the far composing stone in the dark, not lighting the lamp, and he started violently when I spoke — actually jumped, a man caught, though he covered it fast, saying something about not sleeping either. But I had seen where his hands were. They'd been resting on the shelf where we keep the lead pigs, the raw ingots, the dullest stock in the shop.

Nobody caresses lead pigs at midnight.

"Oscar," I said. "What is in the pigs?"

And in the dark, my brother — my gentle, maddening, idealistic brother, who still cried at funeral notices he himself had set — went very still, and said:

"If I tell you, Isandro, you'll be in it. Whatever happens after, you'll be inside it with me. So ask me again tomorrow, if you want to. Ask me once you're sure."

I stood there with the cold of the floor coming up through my shoes and the taste of the colonel's offer still in my mouth, sweetish, like medicine, and I understood that the day's arithmetic had only just begun. Three berths on one side of the scale. On the other — God knew what, hidden in ingots of lead, breathing in my brother's silence.

I should have gone upstairs. Any sensible woman would have gone upstairs, locked the door, and sold her conscience to the nearest uniform by breakfast.

I struck a match instead.
