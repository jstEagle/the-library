## 8. Father's Hands

My father woke before the soldiers did. He always had — a habit formed in forty years at the foundry, where dawn is not a poetic suggestion but a deadline, since lead poured cold is lead poured wrong — and now that his lungs were failing him he treated waking as one more job to be done properly, on schedule, regardless of how much it cost him.

I found him in the workshop at half past five, sitting at the old bench with his coat already on, as if he'd dressed for company instead of for coughing. The lamps were lit. Two punches lay on the felt: an *h* and an *a*, blank steel still, waiting to be told what they were.

"You're supposed to be resting," I said.

"I am resting." He gestured at the stool beside him. "This is what rest looks like when you've done it right your whole life. Sit down, Isandro."

I sat. Outside, Aldenmoor was doing its best impression of a town not occupied by a colonel's regiment — shutters opening, bread baking, someone's dog conducting its usual inquest into the garbage cart. It almost worked. Almost.

"Your brother is asleep?" my father asked.

"Oscar sleeps like a man with nothing on his conscience. Which tells you either everything or nothing about Oscar."

My father smiled at that, a small private smile, the kind he kept in the same drawer as his finest gravers — brought out rarely and handled carefully. "Today," he said, "we cut letters."

"Papa. We have orders to finish. Brandt's clerk wants—"

"Brandt's clerk wants a great many things, and none of them are letters." He picked up the first punch and held it out to me, handle first, the way you hand someone a knife you trust them with. "The army can requisition the press. They can requisition the ledger, God help us. They cannot requisition what's in your hands unless you let them take your hands. So today we put something in them worth keeping."

I want to be honest, since honesty turned out to matter so much later: part of me wanted to refuse. Part of me wanted to say there was no time, that the world outside the shop had become a ledger itself — names entered, names struck through, names sold — and that cutting letters for love of it was a luxury for towns that hadn't been invaded yet. But my father looked at me across that bench with eyes that had gone from iron-grey to smoke-grey over the winter, and I understood that this wasn't a lesson. It was a will being read aloud, in the only language he trusted not to lie.

* * *

Punchcutting, for those fortunate enough never to have needed to know, is the art of carving the negative of a letter into the end of a steel bar — backwards, raised, exact — so that striking it into copper leaves the matrix from which type is cast. Everything you have ever read began with somebody hunched over such a bar, filing away everything that was not the letter.

"It is subtraction," my father liked to say. "You do not make the letter. You remove everything else."

He had learned it from his father, who had learned it from a master in the old country whose name our family pronounced incorrectly on purpose, out of some long-dead grievance. Three generations of Voss hands had worn the same groove into the same bench. My fingers fit it without looking. That should have made me feel rooted. That morning it mostly made me feel like furniture.

"Now," he said. "Show me your counterpunch stroke."

I took up the graver. The *h* waited on the felt, its crossbar roughed in from yesterday's session. I set the tool against the steel where the counter — the enclosed white space, the letter's breath — would go, and I pushed.

"Stop." His hand closed over mine, light as paper. He was lighter than paper now; I could feel every bone. "You're pushing."

"I'm cutting."

"You're pushing. Pushing is fear wearing an apron. You're afraid the steel will win, so you lean on it. Steel doesn't win, Isandro. Steel waits. You guide it or you fight it, and if you fight it, it takes years off your life and puts a burr on your letter." He repositioned my wrist by a degree so small I barely felt it, and suddenly the graver moved like it belonged to me instead of to my stubbornness. "There. Feel that? That's not force. That's agreement."

"That's a strange word for it."

"It's the correct word. Every good cut is an agreement between the metal and the hand. Every bad one is a negotiation conducted by shouting." He sat back, breathing carefully through whatever was happening in his chest, refusing to let it interrupt him. "Where did you learn to push?"

"From watching you."

That earned me another drawer-smile. "From watching me hurry. There's a difference, and I've spent thirty years hoping you'd never learn it."

We worked. The lamp burned. Somewhere below us the forge ticked as it cooled, and above us the floorboards announced the shop's small population of mice going about their own requisitions. I cut the counter of the *h*, and it came out clean, and my father examined it under the loupe for a long time without saying anything, which from him was applause.

"Again," he said. "Slower."

* * *

By mid-morning we had finished the *h* and begun the *a*, which is a harder letter than people think. Everyone believes the *o* is difficult because it's round. The *o* is honest — it's round all the way around and has nothing to hide. The *a* has a bowl and a stem and a joint where they meet, and the joint is where a cutter's character shows. A timid *a* has a weak joint. A proud one has a fat one.

"The joint," my father said, "is where two decisions meet. You must cut both strokes as though you don't know the other exists, and then they must meet perfectly anyway."

"How is that possible?"

"It isn't. That's why it takes a lifetime." He turned the punch toward the window light. "Your grandfather used to say a letter is a promise made twice — once to the eye and once to the hand — and the joint is where you find out whether the maker kept both."

I set down the graver and flexed my fingers. There was steel dust in the creases of them, grey as ash, and I thought — not for the first time that season — of the other dust I'd been finding lately, in odd corners of the shop, on cuffs, on ledgers' edges. Type dust that matched no job in our books. I did not say anything about it. I am telling you now what I could not tell anyone then: that I knew, in the way sisters know, that my brother was carrying something heavy through our house at night, and that I chose the *a*'s joint over the question because the question would have broken the morning.

Perhaps that was cowardice. Perhaps it was the same lesson my father was teaching — that some things you cut slowly, when the steel is ready.

"Papa," I said instead, "why today?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. He was past pretending; illness had burned the varnish off him and left the grain showing. "Because tomorrow the colonel may come again, or the day after, and after that there will be no quiet mornings left in this shop for a while. And because—" He stopped. He picked up the *h*, turned it over in his fingers like a coin he was deciding whether to spend.

"Because when I was young," he went on, "my father taught me on a morning exactly like this one, and I resented it. I wanted to be casting, selling, doing. Letters seemed slow to me then. Now I understand he wasn't teaching me letters at all. He was teaching me what to hold onto. Because everything else—" the gesture he made took in the shop, the river, the soldiers drilling on the green with their pretty terrible precision, the whole wobbling year of 1848 — "everything else gets taken, Isandro. Towns. Names. Fathers. What they cannot take is the thing your hands know without you. So. Today we cut letters."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we'll see what the world demands." He said it lightly, but his hand went briefly to his chest, and I saw him fold the pain away the way he folded proofs flat — quickly, neatly, to be dealt with later, or not at all.

* * *

At noon I made tea and bread and he ate half of each, which was a triumph we did not celebrate because celebrating it would have acknowledged what it was a triumph over. Oscar came down at some point, hair uncombed, wearing the expression of a young man rehearsing innocence in a mirror, and asked what we were doing.

"Learning," I said.

"Teaching," my father corrected, and looked at Oscar a moment too long. Whatever passed between them, Oscar absorbed it and went up to the casting room, and the floorboards told me he spent the next hour moving things around in there very quietly, which was itself suspicious, since Oscar had never once moved anything quietly in his life.

Afterward my father called me back to the bench.

"One more thing," he said. "The hardest stroke. Not the hardest technically — the hardest morally."

"There's a morally hard stroke?"

"Every trade has one. In ours it's this." He set a fresh punch before me, unmarked steel. "Cutting a letter you don't believe in. Cutting it beautifully. Anyone can carve badly on purpose — that's sabotage, that's easy, that's even noble sometimes. But to give a lie perfect proportions, a true counter, a strong joint — to make falsehood so well-made that the world reads it gladly — that is the stroke that ruins men. Do you understand?"

I thought of the ledger upstairs. Of course I thought of the ledger upstairs; I thought of it the way you think of a stone in your boot. Names entered in my father's hand, true as scripture, and an army upstairs from hell wanting them corrected. I thought of the widow Marta Kellner and her entry near the bottom of the list, and her son, and the way she had gripped my wrist in the doorway three days ago hard enough to leave marks, saying nothing, just *please*, in the grammar of the desperate.

"I think I'm beginning to," I said.

"No, you're not. You're beginning to think you're beginning to. That's different, and it's dangerous." He laid his hand flat over the blank punch, covering it, as though it might answer questions on its own. "When the moment comes — and it will come, child, it's already walking up the road — remember this bench. Remember that every mark you make is an agreement. Ask yourself, before you cut anything ever again: is this an agreement with the truth, or am I just negotiating loudly with a lie?"

"And if the lie saves lives?"

He was quiet for a while. From the green outside came the sound of boots, hundreds of them, in step, the new music of our town.

"Then you'll have to live with the most beautiful forgery in the world," he said at last, "and hope God grades on the joint." He closed my fingers around the graver. "But decide it here first, at the bench, with steady hands. Never decide it in front of them. Men who decide in front of soldiers decide with their fear, and fear has terrible handwriting."

* * *

We worked until the light failed. He showed me the finishing strokes, the way you draw the burr off with a file held at the angle of forgiveness; he showed me how to test a punch against smoked glass so the letter appears right-reading, whole, itself. By dusk I had an *h* and an *a* that were, if I say so myself, worthy of the name Voss beneath them — and I had something else, too, though I couldn't have named it yet. Call it a spine. Call it a template. Call it the knowledge of what my hands were for.

He was tired past hiding by then. I helped him up the stairs, feeling the architecture of him through my arm — the way a house feels when you lean on its doorframe and realize the frame has gone soft. At his door he stopped.

"Isandro. One more thing, and then I'll sleep and stop being a fountain of wisdom, I promise."

"Papa."

"Whatever you choose — the ledger, the boy, any of it — choose it as a printer. Not as a daughter trying to save everyone, not as a sister, not as a frightened woman in an occupied town. As a printer. Because a printer knows that ink dries, that paper burns, but that a well-cut letter survives everything, and that the truth set properly—" he paused, and the sentence cost him breath he visibly could not spare, and he paid it anyway, "—the truth set properly is the only inheritance that can't be requisitioned."

Then he went in, and shut the door, and I stood in the hallway listening to him cough, each spasm a little proof pulled off the press of him, and I went downstairs and washed the steel dust from my hands and could not get it all out from under the nails.

It was full dark when I heard it: hoofbeats on the cobbles, unhurried, deliberate, the cadence of a man who owns the street he rides down. I blew out the workshop lamp and moved to the window, and below, dismounting in front of the foundry with two officers behind him and the courtesy already arranged on his face like type in a stick, sat Colonel Brandt.

In his gloved hand he carried a document. In his eyes, when he glanced up at our darkened windows, he carried something else entirely.

He knocked on our door the way a man knocks when he already knows it will be opened.
