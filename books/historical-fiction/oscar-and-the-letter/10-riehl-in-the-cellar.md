## 10. Riehl in the Cellar

There is a particular sound a man makes when he has been shot and is trying very hard not to be shot *again* — or rather, not to be found. It is not a groan. Groaning is for men who have given themselves permission to be wounded. The sound Anselm Riehl made in my ear that night was closer to the noise a press makes when something small and wrong has gotten into the gears: a dry, patient clicking, as if to say, *I will jam eventually, but not on your schedule.*

"Almost there," I told him.

I had been telling him "almost there" for forty minutes, which by any honest accounting was a lie, and lying, I have found, comes easier to me than it ought to for a man who prints truth for a living. We were behind the tannery, then along the millrace, then in the reeds where the river goes shallow and mean, and every step of it Riehl leaned on my shoulder with his whole weight and apologized for it between his teeth. He apologized the way other men swore. It was practically liturgy.

"The cellar door sticks," I said. "Lift, don't pull."

"Lift," he repeated, as though committing it to memory for a report he would file later. That was Riehl all over. Even bleeding into my coat he was composing. I had met him two years ago through a chain of people whose names I still don't know, a man with ink under his nails and a price on his head and a voice like a warm stove, and he had said, *You have a press, boy. Do you know what that makes you?* And I had said no, and he had smiled, and I had spent two years finding out.

The cellar door stuck. He lifted. We went down into the dark of my family's foundations, among the casks and the old paper stock and the smell of damp stone, and I got him onto the pallet I'd hidden there three days ago with a foresight I can only describe as guilty.

Then I lit the lamp, and saw him properly for the first time since the bridge.

* * *

He had been shot at the shoulder, high on the left side, and the ball had gone through rather than stayed — which the surgeon's almanac in my head said was luck, though looking at him now, gray as wet ash, luck seemed an optimistic word for it. His shirt was stiff with blood gone black. Someone had bound it already, badly, with a strip of what looked like a banner. A tricolor ribbon, if I'm honest, torn into bandage length. There was a joke in there somewhere about the regime dressing its own wounds, but neither of us was in a state to find it.

"You look terrible," I said, because sympathy has never once helped a wound, and criticism at least keeps a man arguing, and arguing keeps a man awake, and awake was the entire strategy.

"I've been *described*," Riehl said, "as many things, but never well." He tried to sit up, failed with dignity, and settled for propping himself against a cask of size paste. "Oscar. Listen to me."

"I'm listening."

"No. You're *bracing*. There's a difference." He closed his eyes a moment. When he opened them they were clear and terrible, the way they got when he talked about the pamphlets, about the words outrunning the soldiers. "They took the bridge crossing at Kessel. They're going house to house in the capital districts. Every printer I know is dead, fled, or writing denunciations to save their necks. Everything we built — everything we *wrote* — it exists now in exactly one place."

"Your head," I said.

"My head is attached to a body currently leaking," he said. "No. It exists in your shop. In lead. In the plates inside your pigs." He meant the hiding place I'd shown no one — the hollowed ingots of type metal stacked in the far corner of the store room, each one concealing a stereotype plate, each plate carrying columns of the true ledger's cousin: the lists, the names, the accounts of what Brandt's requisition had taken from whom. Not the ledger itself. But its shadow. Its argument.

"And you want them out," I said.

"I want them *used*."

I should tell you here that I had imagined this conversation before. Of course I had. Lying awake above my sister's room, listening to Father cough through the wall, I had rehearsed arguments on both sides like a lawyer who suspects he'll lose either way. What I had not rehearsed was doing it while the man making the request left a slow dark stain spreading across my best pallet.

"Anselm," I said, "you haven't seen the ledger."

"I don't need to see it."

"You do. That's the whole point. It isn't a pamphlet. It isn't four pages of righteous fire you can slip into a baker's basket. It's a book. It's *the* book. Every legal claim in this town to passage on that barge lives in it, and half the town believes that if it burns, they burn with it. Isandro catalogs those names the way monks used to copy scripture. You're asking her to hand over scripture."

"I'm asking her to make a copy."

"A copy that leaves this town is a document in enemy hands. If Brandt intercepts it — and he will, because the roads are nothing but checkpoints now — he doesn't learn anything he doesn't already know. He learns that *we* copied it. He learns the Voss foundry is seditious. And then he doesn't just take berths away from people. He takes them away from people *and hangs the reason on us*."

Riehl watched me work myself up with the expression of a man watching weather he had predicted days ago. "Finished?"

"No. Yes. No."

"Sit down, Oscar."

I sat down on a cask opposite him, which put our eyes level, which I suspect was the point.

* * *

Here is what I could never explain to Isandro, though God knows I tried, mostly by not trying: what it does to a man to set words in type and watch them leave the shop and *not come back*, and to hear, weeks later, that they arrived. That a woman in Harrow's Ford read the third pamphlet aloud in her market square until the constable came, and that when he came she simply started again louder. Words are supposed to be light. Everyone says so. Ink on paper, a breath carries it away. But once you've cast them in lead, you learn the opposite: words are the heaviest thing there is. Heavier than the man who wrote them. Heavy enough that they keep moving after the writer stops.

Riehl had taught me that. Riehl had also taught me, without ever saying it outright, that heaviness falls on someone.

"You think I don't understand cost," he said quietly. "I buried a compositor in March. Marek. Nineteen years old. He set my last edition with his hands shaking and I let him, because the edition mattered more than his hands. He was arrested on the road north. I've written his name into four documents since, and every time, it costs me the same as the first." He shifted, winced, kept going out of pure professional habit. "So when I ask for the ledger, understand I am not asking lightly. I am asking because the alternative is that it burns with the town, and the only proof that these people existed — that they held claims, that they were *robbed* — dies with it. Brandt wins twice, Oscar. Once when he sells the berths, and once when no record survives to say they were sold."

"He won't burn it," I said. "It's too useful. It's the instrument of the whole theft."

"It is useful right up until the moment it becomes evidence. Then it's kindling." He looked at his own shoulder, at the ruined ribbon. "I have watched useful books become kindling in four towns."

The lamp guttered. Down here the river was audible through the stones, a low continuous mutter, like the town talking in its sleep.

"And Isandro?" I said. "Did you factor her in? Because she isn't an abstraction. She isn't Marek. She's the person who has kept this shop alive through three bad years while I was off playing underground publisher, and if this goes wrong —"

"If this goes wrong, it goes wrong regardless. That's what I need you to hear. The army is in Aldenmoor. The audit has begun, whatever polite name Brandt gives it. Your sister's forgery — yes, I know about the widow; you have the face of a man who knows things badly — is already a crack in the ice. The question is not whether the ledger becomes dangerous. It became dangerous the day Brandt demanded it. The question is whether it becomes dangerous *for them* or *for him*."

That landed somewhere below my ribs and sat there.

Because that was the argument I couldn't beat, and I hated him a little for having it ready, polished, waiting — the way he had everything ready. A fugitive learns to pack light and argue heavy.

"There's another way," I said, and heard my own voice do something hopeful and stupid.

"Tell me."

"We print it. Here. The whole ledger, reset, run off on our own press — hundreds of copies, scattered through the town before anyone can stop it. Then it's not a document being smuggled toward a border. It's everywhere. You can't confiscate everywhere."

Riehl was quiet for so long I thought the blood loss had finally caught up with him. Then he laughed — a real laugh, cracked at the edges, immediately punished by his shoulder — and said, "Do you know how long I've waited for one of my people to out-argue me? Two years, Oscar. And it takes a typesetter's son with flour on his collar."

"I don't have flour on my collar."

"You do. It's charming. Now listen." He leaned forward despite the shoulder, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for the last paragraph of every pamphlet, the one designed to be remembered on the walk home. "Printing it here means your sister sets every name with her own hands. It means your press becomes the crime scene. It means when Brandt finds out — and he will find out — there is no distance between the Voss family and the evidence. Smuggling the ledger out gives you a chance, thin as it is, that the town survives and only you are lost. Printing it here means the town is *in it*, all of them, complicit in ink. Which is also, I will grant, the only way a town has ever actually been free."

"So you want me to choose."

"I want you to stop pretending you aren't choosing. You chose the day you hid my plates. Your sister chose the day she moved a widow up a list. The choice was always happening, boy. All that's left is to admit it and pick the version of ourselves we can live inside."

* * *

I went up the stairs slowly, because going up fast would have meant arriving, and I did not yet know what I'd say when I arrived.

The shop at midnight is a different country. The cases stand in their rows like a congregation. The press waits with its long arm folded, patient as a gallows or a pulpit, depending. Isandro's lamp was burning in the type room — it always burned late now, since the requisition, since the widow, since the crack in the ice — and I stood in the doorway watching her check matrices by candlelight, her lips moving faintly around the names as she verified them, and I understood with sudden awful clarity that I was looking at the most dangerous person in Aldenmoor.

She looked up. "You've been downstairs," she said. Not asking. My sister reads a cellar the way I read a galley — instantly, and without mercy. "And you weren't alone."

"How —"

"You tracked river mud past the second stair and there's lamp oil missing from the good can, and you've been walking around all evening like a man rehearsing a speech." She set down her matrix. "Whoever it is, Oscar — whoever you've been hiding down there with my father's roof over him — he'd better come up and say what he wants, because I already know it's about the ledger, and I already know I'm going to hate it."

From the bottom of the stairs, faint but steady, came the warm-stove voice: "Madam Voss. Forgive the mud. I bring an argument you're going to hate even more than your brother does."

Isandro closed her eyes. When she opened them, she picked up the lamp and turned toward the cellar stairs, and I followed her down into the dark knowing that whatever we decided tonight, the town would wake up different tomorrow — and that the deciding had, in truth, ended the moment a bleeding man's boots touched our stairs.

The only thing left to choose was which of us paid for it.
