## 12. Berth for a Price

The colonel sent for me on a Thursday, which I remember because Thursdays were the days Father had always let us knock off early to drink cheap beer at the Anchor and complain about customers. It seemed a strange day for a man to decide my future. Then again, Colonel Brandt struck me as a man who did his best work on other people's holidays.

The summons came not as a summons — nothing so crude — but as an invitation. A corporal appeared at the foundry door with a folded card, cream-colored, with actual engraved borders, the kind of thing you'd send to announce a christening. *Colonel E. Brandt requests the pleasure of Mr. Oscar Voss's company at his quarters, four o'clock.*

*Requests the pleasure.* The army had been in Aldenmoor six weeks and had already learned to dress its appetite in lace.

"He wants to talk about the ledger," Isandro said. She didn't look up from the case she was setting. Her hands kept moving, but her voice had gone flat in that particular way of hers, the way it went when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "He'll want something. Nobody sends engraved cards to ask after the weather."

"Maybe he's lonely."

"Oscar."

"I know." I pulled on my good coat, the one without lead dust in the seams, and discovered there was no such coat anymore. Every garment I owned had quietly become a work garment. My whole life had turned into a work garment. "I know what he wants. I've known since he started being polite to us. Politeness is just violence that's still deciding where to bite."

"That's very poetic," she said. "Try not to be poetic at him. Poetic men sign things they shouldn't."

I would have liked to tell her she was wrong. I would have liked to say something brave and brotherly and true. What I said was, "If I'm not back by six, feed the dog."

"We don't have a dog."

"Then start one. It'll give you something to do besides worry."

She almost smiled. Almost. In our family, almost-smiling was the highest form of farewell.

* * *

Brandt's quarters were the Harrow house, which had belonged to the Harrows for ninety years until the Harrows were given two hours and a cart. The army hadn't even moved the furniture. I recognized the chairs, the ugly blue wallpaper Mrs. Harrow had defended against three generations of interior decorators. Now a rifle rack stood where her embroidery frame had been, and the colonel's maps were pinned straight through the wallpaper with no more ceremony than you'd pin a moth.

He received me alone. No officers, no guards in the room, though I had no doubt whatsoever about what stood in the hallway. He poured wine himself — two glasses, unhurried, the gesture of a host rather than an interrogator — and handed me one before I'd decided whether to take it.

"Voss," he said, tasting the name. "Your father built a fine business."

"My father built a fine family," I said. "The business came along with it."

Something flickered across his face. Amusement, possibly. Or the expression of a man filing a fact away in a drawer he'd open later. "Sit, please."

I sat. The chair was Mrs. Harrow's reading chair, and sitting in it felt like trespassing in someone else's memory. Brandt settled across from me, crossed one ankle over his knee, and regarded me with the frank, appraising patience of a man buying a horse.

"You're the younger one," he said. "The sister runs the shop. Interesting arrangement. Unusual."

"She's better at it than anyone else alive. That's the only unusual thing about it."

"Mm." He sipped his wine. Outside the window, a squad was drilling in the square, boots striking cobbles in a rhythm designed, I suspected, to remind everyone within earshot that rhythm could be a weapon. "Let me tell you what I know, Voss, so we can skip past the part of this conversation where we insult each other's intelligence. I know your foundry has produced pamphlets. I know they were printed on your type, on your press, with ink bought from your own supplier in Grelling. I know the hand that set them was practiced but young. And I know that Anselm Riehl has been in this town since before my soldiers were, because wounded men do not walk forty miles on their own."

The wine in my glass had gone very still. So had I.

"Now," he continued, pleasantly, "here is what I don't know. I don't know where Riehl is. I don't have proof tying any particular Voss to the press. And I am, despite everything you may have heard, a man who prefers not to waste resources. Arrests are expensive. Trials are tedious. Martyrs are worse than either — you hang a printer and suddenly every schoolboy in the province can recite his last words." He set down his glass. "So I'd rather make a trade."

"And what would I have," I said, "that a colonel would want?"

"A name. A location. Nothing more. You give me those, and the matter closes quietly. No investigation into the foundry. No audit complications. Your father keeps his reputation, your sister keeps her hands, and—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the interesting part— "there remains the question of berths."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped type case. All of it at once. Every letter spilling.

"There are eleven berths left on the evacuation barge," Brandt said, as if reading from a schedule. "Nine are committed. One is reserved — I won't pretend otherwise; I'm not a hypocrite — for parties whose cooperation merits consideration. And one remains unassigned. Your father is ill. Everyone in this town knows it; the man coughs loud enough to be heard across the square. A dying man belongs with his family when they go, or—" and here his voice softened into something that might have passed for kindness if you'd never heard kindness used as a tool— "or he stays behind in a town that will be under garrison law indefinitely. Hospitals are crowded. Winter is coming. I don't need to draw you a picture, Voss. You're a printer. You deal in pictures made of words."

I looked at my wine. I thought about drinking it, purely to have something to do with my hands.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like your father, breathing easier. It arrives looking like your sister, asleep instead of standing watch over a press. It arrives holding out exactly the thing you cannot buy anywhere else, priced in the only currency you happen to be carrying.

Anselm Riehl had hidden in our cellar for five weeks. He'd taught me to set a page blindfolded. He'd argued politics with me until two in the morning while his wound seeped through its bandages, insisting that a man's convictions ought to cost him something or they weren't worth the paper they were printed on. He'd said that laughing, mostly. Mostly.

And my father was dying. Actually, measurably, cough-blood-into-a-rag dying. And Isandro was carrying the whole shop and the whole ledger and the whole town's names on her back, and she would carry them until they broke her, because she was Isandro, and breaking was simply what happened to things that held too much weight too long.

One name. One location. One berth.

"One berth," I said. "For a name."

"For a name and a door," Brandt said. "I want to walk through the door myself. No warning given. If your information is good, no one ever knows you gave it. If your information is bad—" he shrugged, a small economical movement— "then we're having a different conversation, and it won't be in this nice chair."

"And if I say no?"

He smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced, warm at the edges, hollow as a font box. "Then tomorrow I begin a routine inspection of commercial premises. Routine inspections turn up irregularities. Irregularities require documentation. Documentation requires your sister's attendance at my office, daily, for questioning. Your father's medical care becomes subject to garrison scheduling. Small things, Voss. Individually small. But small things have a way of accumulating, like interest."

I understood then that he had done this arithmetic long before he sent the cream-colored card. He knew the exact weight of every item on both sides of the scale. He'd probably rehearsed my refusal, too, and prepared his answer to it, and his answer to that. A man like Brandt doesn't leave a conversation to chance. He leaves it to gravity.

And yet.

And yet there is a thing in me — I've never managed to locate it precisely, somewhere behind the sternum, near whatever organ handles stubbornness — that has never once in my life done what it was told when what it was told was wrong. It got me thrashed twice at school. It got me disinherited from my aunt's affections. It was the reason a fugitive printer was sleeping under my family's floor, and I suspect it would eventually be the reason I died, wherever and whenever that occurred.

It chose that moment to speak.

"No," I said.

Brandt raised his eyebrows slightly. "No?"

"No. Not because I have anything to hide — although I do — and not because I think you'll keep your word anyway, though I'm quite sure you wouldn't. I'm saying no because you've made me an offer where the price is a man's life and the goods are my father's, and I've watched my father price type his entire career, Colonel, and I know a bad exchange rate when I'm quoted one."

The pleasantness drained out of his face by degrees, the way water leaves a lock. What remained underneath was not rage. It was worse. It was professional disappointment, the look of a clerk finding an error in a column that should have balanced.

"You understand," he said quietly, "what happens now."

"I understand that tomorrow you'll start your inspections."

"I understand nothing of the sort. Tomorrow I do nothing. That's rather the point." He stood, and I stood, because some habits of deference are drilled deeper than principle. "I'm going to forget this conversation happened. You're going to go home, and sleep on it, and discover — I find most men do — that courage looks different at three in the morning than it does at four in the afternoon. And when you come back to me — and you will come back, Voss, they always come back — I will still have one berth, and you will still have one name, and the exchange rate will not have improved."

At the door he stopped me with a hand on my shoulder. His grip was light. It didn't need to be heavy; it was a grip that knew where everything in town was buried.

"Your father," he said. "How long does the doctor give him?"

"Longer than you'll be in Aldenmoor," I said.

"Everyone says that," said Colonel Brandt, "about everyone," and he closed the door behind me with a click soft as a full stop.

* * *

I didn't go home. Not immediately. I walked down to the river and stood on the landing where the barge was moored, that great iron-bellied thing that had become, in six weeks, the only object in Aldenmoor anyone looked at directly. People passed it on their errands the way you pass a grave — quickly, eyes averted, aware of it always.

Eleven berths. Nine committed. One reserved for the cooperative. One for sale.

I tried to imagine telling Isandro. Tried to compose it the way I'd compose a line of type: *Dear Sister, today a man offered me Father's life for Riehl's, and I refused, and now the price of my refusal will be paid by you.* She would not thank me. That was the hell of it. She would stand there with her ink-stained hands and her terrible clear eyes and she would not thank me, because Isandro had never once confused a clean conscience with a safe household, and from where she stood — carrying Father up the stairs twice a day, carrying the ledger's secrets, carrying the town's begging faces home with her at night — my conscience probably looked less like a virtue and more like another weight she'd have to haul.

Maybe she'd be right. Maybe love was just duty with the arithmetic removed.

But then I thought of Riehl in the cellar, feverish, arguing philosophy between coughs, and I thought of what he'd said the night I asked him whether he was afraid. *"Every day," he'd said. "That's how I know it matters. Cheap convictions don't scare anyone, least of all the man who holds them."*

And I thought of Father, who had spent fifty years cutting letters, who believed — actually believed, the way other men believed in God — that names outlive us only if they're true.

One berth. One name. I had stood in Mrs. Harrow's dead house and held the scales myself, and I had watched the pan with my father in it sink, and I had still said no, and I still couldn't tell you whether that was righteousness or vanity, and I suspected I'd die without knowing, and I suspected that not knowing was the whole condition of being alive in the year 1848.

When I finally came home, the lamps were lit and the shop smelled of ink and lamp oil, and Isandro met me in the doorway before I'd touched the latch. She read my face the way she read a galley proof — fast, thorough, missing nothing.

"What did he offer?" she said.

"Nothing," I said. "A conversation. About the weather."

"Liar," she said, not unkindly, and stepped aside to let me in, and behind her, on the composing stone, lay a fresh pull of the ledger page — and beside it, wrapped in sacking where no sacking should be, a smear of blood.

Riehl's wound had opened. And from the look on my sister's face, downstairs in our cellar, right now, the man whose life I had just refused to sell was asking for a pen.

He wanted to write. Of course he wanted to write. Men like Anselm Riehl always wanted to write, right up until the moment the writing killed them — and the question, the question that had followed me out of the colonel's quarters and down the whole length of the river road, was whether it would kill him alone, or whether his last pamphlet would take my father's berth, my sister's freedom, and every honest name in the ledger down with it.

Isandro held the door. I stepped inside. Somewhere below us, faint as a heartbeat, I could hear him moving.
