## 13. Ink Under the Nails

I have said before that I know my brother's hands better than my own. I cut his first matrices when he was too small to hold a graver; I taught him which letters bite and which forgive. So believe me when I tell you that a man cannot lie with his hands, not to a sister who has watched them for twenty years, and Oscar's hands had been lying all week.

It was small things at first. He stopped wearing his apron in the composing room — Oscar, who had been scolded since boyhood for wearing it to supper. He washed up twice as often, and longer, scrubbing at his nails with the pumice stone we kept for cleaning brass until the skin around them went raw and pink. A man who is hiding pamphlets hides paper. A man who is hiding *money* — or what passes for money now, which is favor — hides nothing so carefully as his hands.

And there was the matter of the berth.

Colonel Brandt kept his promises the way a river keeps its banks: loosely, and only where convenient, but he did keep them. The Voss family's names had appeared on the corrected list — Father's name, mine, Oscar's — inked in an officer's tidy hand above the widow Marta Kessel's struck-through line, which still haunted me every time I passed the posting board. Guaranteed passage. Our reward for a ledger that had been, by then, three entries deep in my own quiet surgery. I had told myself each alteration was a test, then a necessity, then a debt owed to someone whose name I would never speak aloud. That is how corruption works in a town of any size: not as a landslide but as erosion, grain by grain, until one morning you look at your hands and find you no longer recognize the shape of the work they do.

But Oscar's berth came with something extra. I learned this from Frau Hessler, who learns everything, who has learned things about Aldenmoor that Aldenmoor itself does not know. She came in for lamp oil she did not need and lingered by the case of nonpareil, lowering her voice the way women of her generation lower voices — audibly, so that the whole room may benefit.

"They're saying young Voss dined at the commandant's table Tuesday last," she said, examining a piece of type with tremendous false interest. "Sat down with Colonel Brandt himself, and the quartermaster, and that pale captain who smiles like a knife being drawn slowly from a boot."

"Oscar doesn't dine out," I said.

"Apparently he does now." She set the type down with a click. "They're saying he was asked questions. And that he answered some of them."

She left, taking her oil and her detonation with her, and I stood in the composing room with my heart going like a press run gone wrong, slamming and jamming, sheet after ruined sheet.

Because here is what I knew, and what I had known since the night I found Riehl bleeding in our cellar: Anselm Riehl was still down there. Weaker now — the wound had festered and broken and festered again despite Dr. Lamm's midnight visits — but alive, and hidden under forty hundredweight of lead pigs, behind a wall my grandfather had built thick enough to stop a cannonball, in the one room in Aldenmoor the army had never thought to search because they believed the foundry already theirs. Every day Brandt's men walked through my shop, handled my type, breathed my air, and every day the most wanted printer in the province lay twelve feet beneath their boots, listening to the sound of us through the floorboards.

And my brother had sat at Brandt's table.

I did not confront him that day. I want that recorded somewhere, if anything of me is ever recorded: I waited. I did my work. I set a funeral notice for old Pieter Lund and a birth announcement for the Dekker girl and I did both beautifully, because craft is the only prayer I trust, and I waited for Oscar to come to me. He had always come to me before. When he broke the galley of fourteen-point at eleven years old he had come to me crying before he even fetched Father. Whatever he was carrying, I believed he would bring it home like a stray and set it down between us.

He did not come. Three days, and he did not come, and on the third night I found him in the type room after closing, past midnight, standing at the California job case with a candle stub burning beside him, sorting type.

Now, sorting type is what Oscar did when his soul needed mending. It is the foundry equivalent of pacing — the hands busy, the mind elsewhere. But no one sorts type at midnight by candlelight unless they are avoiding sleep, and no one sorts *that* case — the bastard case, the one where we throw odds and ends and broken sorts — with that expression, which was not mending. It was drowning.

"You were seen," I said from the doorway.

He did not startle. That frightened me more than anything else could have. He simply kept sorting, his back half-turned, shoulders curved over the case like a man sheltering a flame from wind. "Seen doing what?"

"Dining with Colonel Brandt. At his own table. With the quartermaster." I stepped into the room and shut the door, and the click of the latch sounded enormous. "Frau Hessler knows. Which means everyone knows, which means *Brandt* knows everyone knows, which means whatever you two arranged, it isn't secret anymore, it's theater. He wants it seen."

"Maybe it was just dinner."

"Oscar."

"It was just dinner, Isandro." He turned then, finally, and the candle caught his face, and I saw that he had not been sleeping either, and that underneath the exhaustion there was something I had never seen in my brother before and hoped never to see again: guilt being worn like a coat buttoned wrong, all askew, hiding nothing.

"Then say it plainly," I said. "Say, *I ate dinner with the man who arrested the Kessel family for a crime I committed.* Say it and mean it, and then explain to me why you went back Wednesday, because Frau Hessler tells me there was a Wednesday too."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below us, or perhaps inside the walls, the house ticked and settled. And Oscar looked down at his hands — those clean-scrubbed, raw-knuckled, guilty hands — and said, very quietly:

"He offered me the last berth."

The words landed in the room and lay there between us like dropped lead.

"Not for me," he went on, fast now, the words tumbling the way they did when he'd rehearsed a composition too long and lost the argument for it. "That's not — Isandro, listen. He doesn't know about Riehl. He suspects, he *fishes*, but he doesn't know. He offered me the berth and immunity — full immunity, papers, no questions at the dock — in exchange for a name. One name. The fugitive printer. That's all he wants, it's the only loose thread he can't pull from his uniform, and he thinks I'm the thread's tailor—"

"And what did you tell him?"

"I didn't tell him anything yet."

"*Yet.*"

"I went back Wednesday to hear the rest of it!" His voice cracked upward and he gripped the edge of the case as though the room were tilting. "There are conditions. Deadlines. He gives me until the evacuation — six days — and if I give him Riehl, I get the berth, and Papa gets — Isandro, *Papa is dying*. Do you understand what a guaranteed berth means? It means Papa dies in his bed instead of on a dock or in a ditch. It means you go somewhere the war hasn't chewed up. It means—"

"It means you sell a man's life who is lying under our floor right now," I said. "A man who trusted you. Who gave you his plates, his words, his *cause*, and who bled on our cellar stones because he believed the Voss family was worth trusting."

"You think I don't know that?" He came around the case toward me and I saw his eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once. "You think I haven't lain awake doing the arithmetic? One life against three. Against four — against the whole family, against Papa, against *you*. Riehl would make the trade himself if he could stand up. He told me once that a printer is worth exactly what his last proof is worth, no more, and his last proof is done, Isandro. It's printed. It's *out*. Everything he has to say, he has already said. What's left of him is just a body in a cellar costing us bread and risking Papa's neck every single hour Brandt's men walk over his head!"

"That body wrote the pamphlet that put the truth about the berths into nine hundred hands," I said. "That body is the reason Brandt is auditing the ledger instead of simply burning it and shooting anyone who objects. You want to hand him over to save our skins, fine. But understand what you'd actually be selling: the only living witness to what Brandt has done. The moment they take him, every word he printed becomes deniable. *Anonymous agitation,* they'll call it. Foreign lies. And the ledger — my corrections, the widow's son, all of it — becomes the crime, and Brandt becomes the law that punished it."

"So we burn instead?" His voice rose to fill the little room, and the candle guttered. "We hold onto our beautiful principles while Papa coughs his lungs into his handkerchief, and we go down together, nobly, all three of us, and the ledger burns and the truth burns and nobody is saved by any of it? You've altered the ledger yourself, Isandro! Don't look at me like that — you think I don't know? You think I don't watch your hands the way you watch mine? The widow's entry moved up the list, the ferryman's tax struck quietly, and now the audit has found the discrepancies and the Kessels are sitting in a cell for *your arithmetic*—"

I hit him.

I am not proud of it, and I will not pretend it was justified, and I record it anyway, because this is a true account and truth does not get to curate itself. My palm caught his cheekbone and his head snapped sideways and the candle jumped in its pool of wax, and for one heartbeat neither of us moved, both of us shocked, both of us hearing the sound of it — the flat crack of it — as though someone else had made it in another room.

Then Oscar swung back and shoved me, hard, with both hands, and I staggered into the type rack, and the world became noise.

I have heard presses fail. I have heard a form of solid type collapse under the platen — the particular wet avalanche sound of a month's work going down at once. What happened in that type room was worse, because we did it ourselves. Oscar grabbed my arm, I wrenched free, my hip caught the corner of the job case, and the whole case — the bastard case, forty years of orphaned sorts, my grandfather's hoard of odd sizes and foreign faces and broken treasures — tipped, and slid, and went over with a crash that I felt in my teeth. Type sprayed across the floorboards like shot. The tray split along its old seam. Somewhere in the spray, glass cracked — the little frame of Father's first master punch, hung over the case since before I was born, and it came down and the glass starred white and hung, and did not fall.

We stood in the wreckage, breathing hard. Type everywhere, thousands of pieces, mixed beyond any hope of sorting — pi, printers call it, a case thrown to pi, the oldest catastrophe in our trade. Under the candle's shivering light the letters glittered on the floor like a spilled alphabet, every word that could ever be said lying jumbled and meaningless, waiting for someone patient enough to kneel down and make sense of it again.

Neither of us knelt.

"Get out of my shop," I said. My voice came out low and steady and strange, a stranger's voice.

"Your shop," Oscar repeated, and laughed, and it was the worst sound I had ever heard him make, worse than crying. "Yes. Your shop. Your ledger. Your corrections. Your *arithmetic*. You've been making trades since the day the requisition order came, Isandro, you've just been doing yours alone in the dark where you can pretend they aren't trades. At least I had the honesty to sit across a table from Brandt and let him see my face while I decided."

"And what have you decided?"

He looked at me for a long moment. The candle had burned down nearly to the board; the light climbed his face from below and made him look older, and younger, and like our father, and like a stranger.

"I don't know yet," he said. "That's the honest answer, sister. I don't know. Six days."

He picked his way through the spilled type to the door — I heard the crunch of letters under his boots and winced without meaning to — opened it, paused with his hand on the latch.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the glass didn't break. Look."

And he was gone, and I was alone with the wrecked case and the dying candle and the little frame hanging crooked on its nail, its glass starred white as frost, holding — intact, unbroken, held fast in its web of cracks — the first letter our father ever cut.

I knelt down then, among the pi. That much I could do. I picked up a piece of type at random, and turned it over, and it was a small *e*, worn nearly smooth, one of ten thousand such in the world and irreplaceable in this one, and I sat there on the cold floor holding it in the dark long after the candle drowned in its own wax.

Six days. One berth. A name for a life, or a life for a name.

And downstairs, faint through the floorboards, so faint I might have imagined it, came the sound of a man shifting his weight against a cellar wall, and settling again into silence — waiting, the way the buried wait, for news from the world above.

* * *

I did not sleep. Toward dawn I took the lamp down to the cellar, meaning — I told myself — only to check the stores, count the bread, see whether Dr. Lamm's bandages wanted changing. All true things. None of them the true thing.

Riehl was awake. They are always awake, the wounded, in the hour before dawn; pain keeps its own schedule.

"You've been fighting," he said, before I'd cleared the last step. In the lamplight his face was gray and hollowed, cheekbones like a drawing in charcoal, but the eyes were still quick, still amused at the world's expense. "Or you've been grieving. With siblings it's often the same evening."

"We argued about you," I said, because lying to a dying printer seemed a poor use of the hours left to any of us. "My brother has been offered a trade. Your name for a berth on the barge. For my father's life, maybe."

He absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain — without visible change, and completely.

"And what did you argue?" he asked.

"That selling you buys nothing. That the moment they have you, everything you printed becomes anonymous, deniable, foreign poison. That the truth needs a witness breathing."

"Mm." He shifted against the wall, and I saw the effort cost him, and saw him refuse to let it show, and understood suddenly where Oscar had learned it. "And what did he argue?"

"That you'd trade yourself willingly. That your work is finished. That a body in a cellar is just a debt accruing interest."

"Yes," Riehl said softly. "That sounds like something I'd say. I said it to him myself, months ago." He closed his eyes. "The trouble with arguments, Fräulein Voss, is that both sides are usually right, and the choosing is left to people who love each other, which is the cruelest part of the design." He opened his eyes and looked at me, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before — not pleading, not fear. An accounting. "How many days?"

"Six."

"Then you should know what I know, before anyone chooses anything." He beckoned me closer with two fingers, and his voice dropped to the register of men who have spent their lives printing secrets and know precisely how far sound carries. "Before Brandt offered your brother his bargain, he made another arrangement. Through the quartermaster. There is a buyer — a loyalist shipping family out of the capital — and they have purchased not one berth, Isandro. They have purchased the *manifest*. The whole list. Every name on that corrected ledger is already sold; the barge leaves heavy with the highest bidder, and the families whose names appear on it are cargo insurance, nothing more. Your brother's berth is real. Yours may be. Your father's—" He shook his head, slowly. "Brandt is not trading a berth. He is buying silence, with goods that were never his to sell, and when the barge clears the harbor he means to fire the records and let the river carry the story downstream as ash."

I crouched there in the cold dark, the lamp trembling in my hand, and thought of my father upstairs sleeping — actually sleeping, for once, the laudanum merciful — with his name on a manifest that had already been sold out from under him.

"Why are you telling me this?" I whispered. "Why now?"

"Because your brother has six days to decide what he'll trade," Riehl said. "And I judged you should decide first — knowing that whatever he decides, it won't save your father. Nothing he can sell will." He laid his hand, light as paper, over mine on the lamp handle. "There is only one thing left in this town worth buying, Fräulein Voss, and it isn't passage. It's testimony. Ask yourself what your father would print."

I climbed back up the stairs as the sky went gray, and stood in the wrecked type room among the spilled alphabet, and through the window I saw the posting board on the square, where a clerk in army blue was already at work with paste and brush, putting up fresh notices for the evacuation.

And beneath the new notices, half-hidden, someone had pasted a broadside — one of ours, one of Riehl's, rain-blurred but legible, the old accusation standing in bold black type for anyone who cared to read it: THE BERTHS ARE FOR SALE.

The clerk pasted his notice directly over it, smooth and careful, and smoothed it flat with his hand, and walked away.

But paper remembers. Paper always remembers what's printed under it.

Six days, I thought. And I went to find my father's proofing press.
