## 14. Father's Last Proof

My father had not left his bed in nine days, and in that time I had learned the geography of dying the way a compositor learns a case — by touch, by repetition, without wanting to.

The bedroom above the foundry smelled of camphor and hot metal. The camphor was for him; the metal was for me. I had taken to setting type at the little hand press we kept upstairs, the one Father had used for proofs before my arms were long enough to reach the lever, because I could not bear to be more than twelve steps from his door and could not bear to sit beside his bed doing nothing, either. So I compromised, as one does with grief: I worked within earshot of it.

He coughed at dawn. He slept through midmorning. In the afternoon the doctor came and charged us an obscene sum to tell us what we already knew, which is that the lungs go first in men who have spent forty years breathing the fumes of molten lead, and that there was nothing to be done but keep him comfortable, which is medicine's polite way of saying *we surrender*.

I had grown fluent in surrender lately. It seemed the whole town had.

Downstairs, the requisitioned shop sat under its new masters like a dog that had been kicked too often to bark. Two soldiers at the door, a sergeant who liked our coffee, Colonel Brandt's audit officers arriving each morning with their ruled paper and their little brass-bound boxes of instruments, going through the ledger line by line like men searching a haystack for a specific needle they intended to charge someone else for losing. My forgery was still hidden — the widow Marta Kellner's name lifted three places up the berth list, the interlineations disguised in an old hand, mine imitating Father's imitating his own father — but "hidden" is a word that means less every day Brandt's men sit in your front room.

Oscar had barely spoken to me since the night we broke a full case of fourteen-point across the floor of the type room. We set it back together afterward, in silence, sorting letters into their compartments by lamplight like two penitents doing penance in the same confessional, and neither of us said what we were thinking, which was that he was half-sold to the colonel and I was half-drowned in my own cleverness, and that between us we had made a ruin of everything Father built.

Father, of course, knew none of this officially. Unofficially, he knew everything. Dying men develop a hearing like the deaf develop sight.

On the ninth day, near evening, he called for me.

Not Oscar. Not the doctor. Me. And though some petty, exhausted corner of my heart wanted to send Oscar up instead — let *him* hold the wet cloth, let *him* hear whatever was coming — I went, because I was the eldest, and because I had been chosen, and being chosen by a dying man is not an honor you may decline.

* * *

He was propped against pillows that had been rearranged so many times they had lost all opinion about comfort. His hands lay on the coverlet, and I looked at them the way I always did now: those hands that had cut punches finer than anything in three counties, that had taught me to strike a letterform true when I was seven years old and all elbows. They looked smaller than I remembered. Hands shrink toward the end, I think, gathering themselves in, the way a tide goes out before it gives something back.

"You've been printing," he said.

"Proofs," I said. "Upstairs. Nothing important."

"Nothing is ever important until it is." He patted the bed beside him, an invitation and a command in one gesture, which was very like him. I sat. The mattress dipped and I slid slightly toward him, and he put one shrunken hand over mine and held on with a grip that surprised me. There was still iron in it. There would be iron in it at the very end; I'd have bet the foundry on it.

"I know about the ledger," he said.

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed at something, the sound floating up through the floorboards like a bubble through dark water.

"Which part?" I asked, because denial seemed pointless and because I am my father's daughter and we are a family that meets trouble with questions.

"All of it." His eyes, sunken as they were, had lost none of their ability to pin a person to the wall. "Marta Kellner. Three places up. You cut the amendment yourself — I could see the strokes from the proof you pulled last Tuesday. You hold your 'k' too tall at the shoulder when you're frightened. You've done it since you were nine."

I opened my mouth to explain — the widow's son, the conscription lists, the arithmetic of one saved boy against one suspicious colonel — and he stopped me with two fingers pressed lightly against my lips, the way he used to when I was small and interrupting.

"I'm not asking you to defend it," he said. "I'm telling you I know. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"Does it?" My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Because Brandt doesn't seem to care much about differences. His officers measure things."

"Yes." A ghost of a smile. "And you've been measuring them back. I raised two children. One of them thinks words can outrun armies, and one of them thinks she can forge her way to justice with a graver and a steady hand. Between you, you'll either save this town or get everyone in it shot." He coughed then, deep and wet, and I held the basin and hated how practiced I'd become at holding basins, and when it passed he lay back gray-faced and continued as if the interruption had been merely a change of weather. "I've thought a great deal, these nine days, about which."

"And?"

"And I've decided it isn't mine to decide anymore." He turned his head on the pillow, slowly, the way heavy things turn. "It's yours. Both of yours. But first —"

He tried to rise. I put my arm behind his shoulders without being asked, and together we got him upright, and he nodded toward the wardrobe in the corner, the old walnut one that had come with Mother from her mother and that I had never once seen open beyond the shelf of folded shirts.

"The false back," he said. "Bottom hinge. There's a key in my coat pocket. The grey coat, not the black one — the black one's key opens my strongbox and there's nothing in the strongbox but receipts, because I never trusted banks and I never trusted strongboxes either."

I found the key. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be, which struck me as its own kind of betrayal — shouldn't they shake? Shouldn't something?

The false back came away with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with printer's twine, lay a flat bundle the size of a folio page.

I carried it to the bed and unwrapped it there, under his eyes, because he wanted to watch, and what I found inside made me forget, for one full moment, that soldiers were downstairs and a colonel was auditing my soul and my brother was carrying a bargain around in his chest like a swallowed coal.

It was a proof. A complete pull of the ledger — every name, every claim, every entry, from the founding families down to the ferryman's widow — pulled on good rag stock in Father's own hand-press setup, inked dark and even, the margins wide. Every family in Aldenmoor. Every legal passage-right, documented exactly as forty years of honest bookkeeping had recorded it. Including, I noticed with a lurch, the entries Brandt had already begun quietly crossing out in the official copy upstairs — the names of families whose berths had been sold out from under them, whose claims existed now only here, on this sheet, in this ink.

"When?" was all I managed.

"Three weeks ago, after the first requisition notice came. I told you I was tired. I wasn't tired. I was printing." He said it with the smallest satisfaction, the kind a man allows himself once, near the end, when the account is nearly closed. "Pulled six copies. This one is the master. The others are where they need to be, and no, don't ask me where, because you'd only worry, and worrying looks terrible on you. Very pinched around the eyes."

"Father—"

"Listen to me now." He took my chin in his hand, and his fingers trembled, but his voice didn't. "Brandt will find your alteration. Men like him always find the one thing, because they aren't looking for truth — they're looking for leverage. When he finds it, he will use it to break the Kellners, and then he will use it to break you, and he will feel perfectly lawful about both, because that is what law becomes when lawless men hold it. Everything honest in this town is about to become evidence against it."

"So what do I do?" I heard myself ask, and hated the childishness of it, the nakedness. I was twenty-six years old and I was asking my father to tell me how to save everyone, the way I'd asked him at nine how to make the letterforms sit square.

"You do what a printer does." He pressed the proof into my hands and closed my fingers over it, both of his hands around both of mine, so that we held it together, four hands and one document, like a christening in reverse. "You print the truth. All of it. Not the ledger locked in a drawer where a colonel can seize it — the ledger loose in the world, in a hundred hands, in ten hundred, copied and recopied until seizing it means nothing. Names outlive us only if they're true, Isandro. Paper burns. Ink fades. But a name, truly given, passes from mouth to mouth forever. That's the only immortality our trade offers, and it is enough. It has always been enough."

The light through the window had gone the color of brass. Downstairs, boots crossed the shop floor; the day shift of auditors leaving, or the night shift of guards arriving; it hardly mattered which anymore. The army had become the weather.

"They'll arrest whoever prints it," I said. "They'll burn the shop. They might—" I stopped. I couldn't say it. *They might take Oscar.* The bargain I half-knew about hung between us, unspoken, the way it hung between Oscar and me at every meal we ate in silence.

"Then they'll arrest a printer and burn a building," Father said. "Both of which have happened before in the history of the world, and neither of which has ever once stopped a true sentence from getting where it was going." His breath caught; I felt the whole cage of his ribs shift under my arm. "Your brother will want to carry it himself. Let him. It's what he's for. And you —" he looked at me then with an expression I will keep until my own last hour, an expression of such specific, unblinking confidence that I understood suddenly it had been aimed at me my whole life and I had simply never stood in its path long enough to receive it — "you stay. Someone must keep the type. Words outrun armies, but only if somebody keeps casting them."

We sat like that while the light failed. I don't know how long. Long enough that my legs went numb under me and I didn't move, because moving would end it, and I have rarely in my life wanted so badly for a moment not to end.

At last he said, quieter, almost amused: "You're crying on the master proof. Mind the margins."

I laughed — a broken, hiccuping sound, entirely undignified — and lifted the sheet clear, and wiped my face with my sleeve like a child, and he watched me do it with an expression of vast paternal approval, as though I had finally, at twenty-six, shown promise.

* * *

I went downstairs after midnight, when the house was asleep except for me and the dying and whichever soldier drew the unlucky watch. I took the master proof into the type room, where the wrecked-and-mended case of fourteen-point still bore the scars of our fight, one compartment replaced from salvage, the letters in it a shade brighter than their neighbors, young metal among old.

I laid the proof on the stone and read it entire, top to bottom, by candlelight. Four hundred and eleven names. I counted twice; counting is a prayer, in our trade.

Then I did what I imagine every heir does at some point, standing in a dead man's — no. Not dead. Standing in a dying man's workshop. What every heir does: I asked myself whether I was equal to the inheritance.

And then, because I am also my father's daughter and we do not meet trouble with tears any longer than strictly necessary, I pulled a composing stick from the rack, and I began to set the title page of a free edition of the Aldenmoor ledger.

I was three lines in when I heard the stair creak, and turned, and found Oscar standing in the doorway in his coat, pale as tallow, with snow melting on his shoulders and an expression I had last seen on him the night Riehl's hiding place came out of the lead pigs.

"They've taken the Kellner boy," he said. "Tonight. Off the quay. And Isandro — Brandt's men pulled the official ledger an hour ago. All of it. Every page."
