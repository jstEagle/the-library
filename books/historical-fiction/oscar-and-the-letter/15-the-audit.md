## 15. The Audit

The trouble with a forgery done well is that nobody congratulates you.

I had spent three days waiting for the sky to fall — listening for boots on the cobbles that didn't belong to our own footsteps, watching the window for the particular way soldiers move through a town they've decided to own. Nothing came. Tuesday passed like any other Tuesday, which in occupied Aldenmoor meant gray, rationed, and smelling faintly of horse. Wednesday too. By Thursday I had almost persuaded myself that the widow Grell's name sat undisturbed on its new line, four entries higher than it had any right to be, and that the universe had simply failed to notice.

The universe notices. That's the first thing a typesetter learns and the last thing she believes.

It noticed on Friday morning, in the person of Lieutenant Halloran, who arrived at the foundry with two corporals, a canvas satchel, and the cheerful manner of a man come to collect a debt he knows you can't pay.

"Fraulein Voss." He removed his cap, which I suppose was meant as courtesy and landed instead as theater. "Colonel Brandt sends his regards. And his compliments." He patted the satchel. "He'd like a look at your ledger."

"The colonel has already seen the ledger," I said. "Twice. He held it. He turned its pages with his own hands, which I believe he wiped afterward."

Halloran smiled. It was a good smile, well-cut, the sort of face we set type for and never trust. "The colonel finds that memory improves with review. You'll understand — there are families in this town with very strong feelings about their positions on that list. The army prefers to be certain. Certainty requires comparison."

"Comparison against what?"

"Against itself," he said pleasantly, which made no sense until later, when it made terrible sense.

My father was upstairs, propped in the bed we'd moved beside the window so he could watch the river. The cough had gone from dry to wet over the past week, and the handkerchiefs told us things none of us said aloud. Oscar was out — of course he was out; my brother had developed a genius for being elsewhere precisely when elsewhere would have been useful — and so it was I alone who fetched the ledger from its locked drawer and laid it on the composing stone like a body on a slab.

Halloran didn't grab it. That was the unnerving part. A soldier seizes; an auditor receives. He opened to the first page with both hands flat, as if opening a hymnal, and began to read aloud in a low, even voice, the way a priest reads names of the dead.

"Aldenmoor. Registry of passage claims, established under charter of 1819. Entry the first: Brenner, Tomas, ferrywright..." He went on. Page by page. He read every line, and while he read, one of the corporals copied it into a duplicate book with a clerk's fast, bloodless hand. They were building a second ledger inside ours, a ghost ledger, and somewhere in a drawer in the garrison headquarters there was presumably another ghost still — the one Brandt had studied on his earlier visits, when he was being courteous.

Three ledgers. One truth. Arithmetic was never my weakness; I could see where this was going the way you see a line about to overrun its measure.

"You're transcribing it," I said.

"We're *confirming* it," Halloran corrected gently. "There's a difference. Transcription is copying. Confirmation is love." He turned a page. "You people and your ink. Do you know what the colonel says about printers? He says a printer is the only tradesman whose product can lie about him after he's dead. He finds that very funny. He tells it at dinner."

"I'm delighted to have contributed to his repertoire."

Page nine. The Grell entry lived on page eleven now — had lived on page seven before my needlework — and I stood at the composing stone with my hands folded like a widow herself and watched Halloran read toward it the way you watch water rise toward a doorstep. I considered three interventions: a fainting fit, a fire, and the truth. Two were theatrical. The third was fatal. I did what any sensible woman does when all her options are bad.

I made tea.

* * *

They were still there at noon. Still there at two. Halloran read slowly, deliberately, letting each page sit in the light long enough for the corporal's pen to finish, and between pages he asked me questions with the air of a man making conversation: Was the shop busy this season? Had we lost much custom since the requisition? Did I find the work lonely, a woman in a foundry?

"It keeps me humble," I said. "Lead doesn't flatter anyone."

"No," he agreed. "I imagine it doesn't." He turned page ten.

Here is what I had done, so that you may judge me fairly, or at least judge me with the facts in hand. Widow Grell — Marta Grell, seventy-one, whose son Piet had been taken for the labor conscription in October and whose name sat seventh on the passage list because her husband had paid the harbor levy for nineteen years without missing one — was dying of a heart that was quitting on her the way everything else in Aldenmoor was quitting. Her claim, honestly entered, put her berth far enough down the list that she'd have boarded the barge never, and Piet, once released from the works, would have been conscripted again within a month. So I had moved her. Four lines. I cut a fresh entry in a close imitation of my father's hand — not mine, never mine, my hand has a slant like a fence in a wind — altered the sequence numbers above and below, re-inked the register marks, and aged the paper over the stove smoke until even I flinched at how good it looked.

It looked so good that for three days I believed in it. That was the sin, if you want one. Not the alteration. The belief.

Halloran reached page eleven at half past three.

I was carrying the tea tray. I remember that with humiliating precision — the tray, the cups, the small domestic theater of hospitality performed over the corpse of my honesty. He read the Grell line. His eyes didn't change. His voice didn't change. He read the next line, and the next, and turned page twelve, and I thought — for the space of one breath, one full breath —

"There's an irregularity here," he said, on page fourteen.

Not the Grell page. Page fourteen. I set the tray down because my hands had opinions of their own.

"Oh?"

"Entry forty-one." He tapped it. "Voss, Isandro. Your own name, Fraulein. The sequence number is written twice. Once faintly, once firm. As though someone changed their mind about where you belonged on this list." He looked up at me with those pleasant eyes. "Did you change your mind?"

I hadn't touched my own entry. I would sooner forge a stranger's future than my own — no, that's not honesty either. I hadn't needed to. But the double-struck number was real; I remembered my father writing it years ago, pressing hard, then easing off mid-stroke when the nib caught. An accident of ink. A flaw in the original, faithfully preserved by everyone including the forger.

"That's my father's penmanship," I said. "He presses hard when his lungs hurt. It varies."

"Mm," said Halloran, and wrote something in his ghost ledger, and I understood that I had just handed him the shape of the thing he'd come to find. Not the crime. The *excuse*. Auditors don't need crimes; crimes are rare and inconvenient. What they need is variation. Any honest record varies. Variation proves nothing and damns everything, depending on what the man holding the pen wants proved.

They stayed until dark. When Halloran finally closed the ledger, he did it softly, reverently, and thanked me for the tea, and said the colonel would be in touch, and took the ledger with him.

"Our ledger," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Requisitioned property," he said, apologetic as weather. "You'll get it back. After."

After. The word the occupation hangs everything on.

* * *

Oscar came home at nine and knew before I finished the second sentence. I could tell by how still he went — my brother, who fidgets like a case of loose sorts, going absolutely quiet, which in Oscar is the equivalent of another man screaming.

"They'll compare it against what?" he asked. "You said comparison. Against what?"

"I don't know. Another copy. Something from the garrison's files. Something Brandt had made during one of his visits." I was pacing the type room, which is a bad place to pace; there are corners everywhere and each one holds a stone that wants your hip. "Or nothing at all. Maybe the second ledger is a prop. Maybe the whole audit is a play with one ending and they're just enjoying the scenery."

"Isa." He caught my arm. "The Grell entry. How clean?"

"Clean. Stove-smoked, matched hand, matched ink batch, register marks redone. Clean."

"How clean is clean? Because I've seen Riehl's work, Isa, plates that fooled garrison inspectors, and you know what fooled them in the end? Not the printing. The *paper*. Someone always remembers the paper."

"It's the shop's own stock," I snapped. "Same batch, same watermark, same everything, because it came out of the same drawer. There is no cleaner paper in Aldenmoor than paper that was always there."

He let go of my arm. In the lamp light his face had the particular misery of a man watching a machine he built run away with itself. "Then they won't find it," he said slowly. "That's the problem. If they find it, it's a mistake, mistakes get punished and forgotten. But if they *can't* find it —"

"— then they have to find something," I finished. Yes. I had gotten there myself around hour six of pacing. An audit that returns empty isn't an audit; it's an insult to the auditor. Brandt had announced an investigation into the sacred ledger, and the sacred ledger would therefore be found imperfect, because the alternative was admitting the colonel had wasted a week. Somewhere in that arithmetic, a family was going to be subtracted. The only question left was which one, and men like Brandt never do that sum blindly.

We stood there among the cases, brother and sister, both thinking the same thought and neither willing to say it, because saying it makes it load-bearing.

It was my father who said it. We heard the cough from upstairs, then his stick on the stairs, which he'd been forbidden to use alone, and then Father himself in the doorway, gray as unfixed ink, holding the banister with one hand and looking at us with eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively.

"They came for the ledger today," he said. Not a question; the whole street had watched them carry it out.

"Yes."

"And they will not find what they're looking for." He lowered himself onto the proofing stool like a man docking a boat. "Because one of my children has been cutting letters that were never cast."

Oscar opened his mouth. I stepped on his foot, hard enough to mean it.

"Papa —"

"Don't." He raised one hand, and the tremor in it was worse than the cough. "Don't tell me whether it was mercy or fear or pride. It doesn't matter. What matters is what happens Thursday, when the garrison announces its findings, and they announce *something*, because a colonel does not audit a ledger and discover it clean — it would be like a priest opening a reliquary and finding it empty. He'd sooner invent a relic." His breath caught; he rode through it. "And the relic will be a family. Some family with no friends in the garrison and no money behind it, and they will be marched out of their house as thieves, and the town will be shown the punishment so the town learns what its names cost."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Nobody moved to fix it.

"Which family?" Oscar asked quietly.

Father looked at me. And I knew — the way you know a misprint the instant the sheet comes off the press, before your eye has even found it — and I said it so it wouldn't have to live in the room unsaid.

"The Grells. It has to be the Grells. They moved up the list; therefore they cheated; therefore the widow bought her berth with a forged line and her son is a conspirator. It writes itself." My voice came out level, which I despised it for. "Piet is still in the labor works. They haven't released him yet. If they arrest the mother —"

"If they arrest the mother," my father said, "the son signs whatever confession they put in front of him to get her out. And the confession names whoever taught her to hold a pen." He looked at his hands then, those great blunt hands that had cut punches for forty years, hands that had never once lied in lead. "Names outlive us, children. I have told you that since you were small enough to stand on this stool. But a name can also hang us. The same letters. It depends on who sets them."

He got up, refused Oscar's elbow on principle, and climbed back toward his bed one stair at a time, and the sound of that climb was the worst thing I heard all week, and I include what came after.

* * *

What came after was Thursday.

The garrison posted the findings on the customs house door at noon, read aloud by a sergeant with a parade-ground voice, to a crowd assembled under the usual arrangement whereby attendance was voluntary the way drowning is voluntary. I stood at the edge of it with flour on my sleeves from pretending to bake bread, which is the oldest alibi in the world and fools no one, and I listened to the machinery do its work.

The audit, the sergeant read, had detected *irregularities in the registry of passage*. Irregularities of sequence, of ink, of hand. These irregularities, occurring in the vicinity of certain entries, indicated *tampering by persons unknown* with intent to defraud the lawful evacuation process. Pending full investigation, the following persons were remanded for questioning —

Marta Grell. Name read out flat, like freight.

— and her household, including her son, Piet Grell, recalled from labor detail for interview. Their passage claims suspended. Their house sealed pending examination. Any citizen harboring correspondence with said persons, or possessing documents relating to the registry, would be subject to the same.

The crowd did the crowd's work: shuffled, looked at its shoes, parted around the Grells' corner of the square as if the cobblestones there were hot. Old Marta wasn't even present to hear it — she'd been taken that morning, quietly, two corporals and a cart, before the announcement, so the announcement would confirm rather than cause. Efficient. Everything about it was efficient. That's the thing they never tell you about injustice done by paperwork: it runs smoother than justice ever manages to. No shouting, no broken glass. Just a sergeant, a list, and a town that suddenly couldn't meet its own eyes.

I walked home the long way, past the harbor, where the barge sat at its moorings with its berths half-fitted, and I stood looking at it and doing the sum I'd been avoiding for a week. Four lines. I had moved her four lines to save her, and the moving had killed her claim entire. If I had done nothing, she'd have waited years for a berth she'd never board, but she'd have waited in her own kitchen, with her own door unlocked. The forgery hadn't failed. It had succeeded exactly as designed, and success was the trap: a discrepancy too clean to prove became an accusation too convenient to drop.

Anselm Riehl would have called it dialectics. I called it what it was: I had loaded the gun and Brandt had merely chosen the target, and both of us had used both hands.

By the time I reached the foundry the light was going and Oscar was in the yard, talking low and fast to a figure in the shadow of the loading arch — a lean, unshaved figure with a bound shoulder and a voice I recognized from pamphlets I'd pretended not to recognize. They stopped when they saw me. Riehl looked at me the way men look at a press that's jammed: assessing damage, calculating leverage.

"They took the widow," he said. Not asking.

"They took the widow."

"Then you have tonight," he said, "before they come back for the rest of the evidence. And there's only one piece of evidence left in this town worth seizing, and you're standing on it." He nodded past me, toward the cellar door, toward the hidden frame, toward the thing Oscar had been feeding all autumn. "So the question, Fraulein Voss — the only question — is whether you print your testimony before they take your hands, or after."

Behind him, Oscar wouldn't look at me. Or wouldn't look away. With my brother the two have always been difficult to distinguish.

Inside, on the composing stone where the ledger used to lie, the lamp burned steady, and I went to it, because a compositor's hands go to the case the way a drowning man's go to anything — and because I already knew, had known since noon, since the sergeant's flat voice, since Marta Grell's name read out like freight:

I had one edition left in me, and this time it was going to be true.
