## 16. Oscar's Choice

They gave me a chair. That was the first thing I noticed — not the two soldiers flanking the door of the requisitioned customs house, not the map of the river valley pinned behind Brandt's desk with little black flags like flies, but the chair. A real chair, padded, angled toward the colonel's desk at the angle of a confessional. In Aldenmoor we did not waste padding on furniture meant for guests.

"Sit, Voss," Brandt said, without looking up from his papers. He had a way of saying your surname as though it were an accusation you'd been carrying for years and he'd only just now decided to arrest it.

I sat. The chair received me like a bribe.

He let the silence do some work first. I'll grant him this: he understood silence the way my father understands lead — as a material with weight, to be measured and spent deliberately. The clock on the mantel ticked with the smugness of a clock that has never once been wrong about anything that mattered.

"You're wondering why you're here," he said at last.

"Not especially," I said. "I assumed it was my charm."

His pen paused. He looked up then, and I saw what I'd seen in him since the day he marched into our foundry and called our ledger *a document of record* in a voice that made it sound like something you could be hanged by. He was a man who found impertinence interesting the way a chemist finds an unstable compound interesting. Not offended. Curious. Taking notes on how much heat before the reaction.

"Your charm," he repeated. "Yes. I imagine the foundry taught you patience. Pouring metal, waiting for it to cool. A trade for men who can wait."

"A trade for people," I said. "My sister cuts finer matrices than any man in the valley."

"I'm aware of your sister." He set down the pen. "That is rather the point of this conversation."

And there it was — the turn in the road, the one I'd been walking toward for three weeks without admitting the road existed. I thought of Isandro that morning, ink to her second knuckle, arguing with a crooked line of fourteen-point type as if it could argue back. I thought of Riehl in the cellar, feverish, his shoulder wound gone the color of bad tallow, whispering that words outrun armies. Words had not outrun this army. This army had arrived on schedule, eaten breakfast, and requisitioned a chair.

"Colonel," I said, "if this is about the ledger—"

"It is always about the ledger now." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood with his hands clasped behind him in the posture of men about to offer you something terrible with excellent manners. "The audit has been tedious. Pages of names, pages of claims, pages of small dishonesties — a date altered here, an heir omitted there. The usual rot of provincial bookkeeping." A pause, delicately calibrated. "And one entry moved. A widow. Frau Maren Kessler. Promoted four places on the berth list by a hand that knew the ledger very well indeed."

The room got very quiet, or I did. Somewhere outside, a horse stamped on cobblestones with the indifference of horses.

"The Kessler boy was conscripted last week," Brandt continued. "Then un-conscripted. Then the family was arrested for fraud they did not commit, because the discrepancy was found and someone had to wear it." He turned. "You look surprised, Voss. Don't. Your sister's handiwork is competent. Her *guilt* is irrelevant. What matters is that I know the ledger can be touched. Which means whoever touches it can be touched back. Which brings me to you."

"To me."

"To you." He returned to the desk, opened a drawer, and produced a single sheet of paper — heavy stock, watermarked, official enough to make my palms sweat on principle. He did not hand it over. He let it lie there between us like a card played face-down. "There is one berth remaining on the evacuation barge. One. And a father in your house who will not see spring regardless of where he sleeps. Your sister cannot take it — she is implicated in the forgery now, and I find it useful to keep implicated persons where I can watch them work. But you—" he tapped the paper once, lightly, almost fondly, "—you are clean. No record. No debts. No seditious associations whatsoever."

The word sat in the air between us, wearing its little uniform.

*Whatsoever.*

"That's generous," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"It's practical. I don't want your berth, Voss; berths are cheap and I have more loyalists than boat. I want Anselm Riehl." He said the name plainly, no theatrical whisper, which frightened me more than a whisper would have. "He is wounded. Wounded men are carried by someone. Fed by someone. Supplied with ink and paper by someone with access to a press." His eyes went, briefly, to the window — and I understood that the window faced the river, and beyond the river, in the geometry of his mind, sat our foundry with its cellar. "Give me a name and a place, and this paper becomes yours. Berth, immunity, and your sister's forgery quietly misfiled into oblivion. Everyone walks away. Well," he amended, with the ghost of a smile, "*someone* walks away."

I looked at the paper. Heavy stock. Watermark. It would print beautifully, I caught myself thinking — the traitor part of my mind that appraised all paper on sight. You could set an entire confession on that stock and it would look like scripture.

"And if I say I've never heard the name?"

"Then I'll believe you're lying, and I'll stop being courteous." He sat again, folding his hands. "But you won't say that. Because I've watched you, Voss. You burn letters in the yard at midnight like a man who's never learned that smoke leaves evidence. You have ink under your nails in colors your shop's books can't account for. You are a terrible conspirator." He leaned forward. "You are also, I think, a decent one — a man who loves his family. Decent men always come around. They simply need the choice arranged so that decency points the right direction."

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me in that moment, because history — even the kind I used to print in secret — deserves honest accounting. There was a voice in me that said *yes*. Clear as a bell. Yes, and Father sleeps his last months above ground instead of in a cell corridor; yes, and Isandro's one mistake dissolves like sugar; yes, and you live, Oscar, you foolish boy who believed pamphlets could outrun cavalry — you live, and isn't that the whole point of every cause? To be alive at the end of it?

Riehl would have understood the temptation. He'd told me once, coughing blood into a rag in our cellar: *"Anselm Riehl is a name, boy. Names are the cheapest thing a printer makes. Don't you dare spend your sister buying mine."*

But my father had said something else, years ago, teaching me to justify a line of type. He'd held up a composed page to the light — backwards letters, mirror-world — and said: "Every proof tells you the truth once. After that, it only tells you what you already decided." And I looked at Brandt's arrangement — the chair, the paper, the mercy — and I saw it plainly at last for what it was: not a proof. A decision, pre-set, waiting for me to pull the lever.

He wanted Riehl. But more than Riehl, he wanted the *pattern* — the confirmation that everyone breaks, that every name on every page in every town folds when the pressure is applied correctly. If I broke, then the ledger was a fiction, the resistance a rumor, and his stolen berths merely administration.

If I didn't—

Well. If I didn't, he would still get Riehl eventually. Fevered men in cellars are not difficult to find. Unless the cellar emptied first. Unless the searchers were sent looking in a direction where there was nothing to find except time.

Time. That was the only commodity left in Aldenmoor that nobody had requisitioned yet.

* * *

I asked for a night to consider it. Brandt granted it with the magnanimity of a man who believes he has already won, which was the first piece of good luck I'd had since winter. He even walked me to the door himself, past the two soldiers, and said, quietly, in the tone of a chess player commenting on the board:

"Your sister works late, I'm told. Tell her the lamp in the type room shows through the shutters. Careless, in occupied towns."

A threat wrapped in neighborly advice. I hated how well he'd learned us in three weeks. Hated more that he was right about the lamp.

I did not go home. Not immediately. I went the long way along the levee, where the river ran black and swollen with meltwater, and I stood there letting the cold work its way through my coat while I assembled myself. Below the customs house, moored to the old pilings, sat the evacuation barge — flat-bottomed, ugly, riding high and half-empty because most of the berths it was built to hold had been sold, over Brandt's signature, to wool merchants and quartermasters' cousins from three provinces away. Our town's names, sold off a page. I had helped print pamphlets saying exactly that. Nobody had needed to believe me. They'd only needed to read the manifest.

Somewhere downriver was the capital, and beyond it whatever government would rise after this one finished drowning. Riehl's whole creed fit in one sentence: *make the record outlive the regime.* I had believed it the way you believe in weather — generally, comfortably, until it rains on you specifically.

Now the rain was on me specifically, and the question was simple and monstrous: what was Anselm Riehl worth? What was a name worth, weighed against a berth, a father's comfort, a sister's safety?

I stood at the rail of the empty barge — they let me, the sentry too bored or too cold to care — and I did the arithmetic printers do. Lead is heavy. Paper is light. But a name set in lead weighs exactly as much as the truth it carries, no more, no less, forever. My father's voice again: *Names outlive us only if they're true.*

All right, then. If a name was the currency, I would pay in counterfeit.

* * *

Isandro was awake when I finally came home, of course. She was sitting at the kitchen table with Father's last proof spread out before her and a candle burned down to a stub, and she looked up at me with that expression she'd had since we were children — the one that said she'd already counted the ways this day could go wrong and was waiting to hear which one I'd chosen.

"They offered me the berth," I said.

She closed her eyes. Opened them. "And the price?"

"My silence costs nothing. The price is Riehl." I told her all of it — the chair, the paper, the audit, the widow's family arrested to wear my sister's cleverness like a borrowed coat. She took it standing, arms folded, jaw set, and when I reached the end she said:

"You can't give him up."

"No," I said. "But I can't save him either. He's dying in our cellar, Isandro, slowly, and every hour he's there is an hour closer to Brandt finding him. What I can do—" and here my voice did something humiliating, wavered, and I pushed through it, "—what I can do is spend the only thing I have that's worth anything. Me."

"Oscar—"

"He wants a name and a place. I'll give him both. Just not true ones." I pulled out a chair — a kitchen chair, unpadded, honest — and sat across from her. "There's a printer in Grellstadt. Old Halloran. He printed for Riehl's circle years ago; Riehl told me himself the man went tame after the crackdown, pays his taxes, prints wedding announcements. Dead two years now — Riehl mentioned it in a letter, said the movement buried another one of its ghosts. But Brandt's officers weren't at that funeral. To them, Halloran is alive, plausible, and exactly the kind of broken old man who'd shelter a fugitive for old loyalty and old coin."

Isandro stared at me. "You're going to send them chasing a dead man through a town two days' march away."

"I'm going to send them chasing him *slowly*. Brandt can't move openly — a raid across the district needs authorization, riders, verification. Verification takes time. By the time they've kicked in Halloran's empty door and found a workshop full of wedding invitations and dust, the barge will have sailed and Riehl will be—" I stopped. "Gone. Moved. Somewhere. Isandro, I don't have a plan for after. I only have a plan for *now*, and the plan for now is: buy days. Days are what we need to set the true edition."

Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down as though the table might otherwise leave without her. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done at the river, and arrive somewhere colder.

"They'll know you lied," she said. "Eventually. When Halloran turns up dead-and-buried, when they trace the false trail back to the mouth that fed it. They'll know, Oscar, and you'll have handed them the berth *and* the rope."

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

"And Father—"

"Father is the one who taught me that a proof only tells the truth once." I reached across the table and put my hand over hers, ink-stained, lead-scarred, the finest hand in the valley. "You heard him. Names outlive us only if they're true. So we make sure they outlive us, Isandro. All of them. Even mine — especially mine, written down as what I actually was: a fool who chose badly and on purpose, and would choose it again."

For a long moment she said nothing. The candle guttered. Outside, the river kept moving the way rivers do, indifferent to manifests and berths and the whole paper tragedy of Aldenmoor.

"If you do this," she said finally, and her voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet before she locked the shop door and worked through the night, "then you don't get to be noble about it afterward. No speeches. No dying with a smile so the rest of us have something pretty to remember. You take the berth they'll never give you back, and you lose it, and that's the whole transaction. Do you understand me? You don't get to be a martyr. Martyrs are easy. *Staying* is hard, and you're choosing not to stay."

"I understand you," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and all either of us had.

* * *

Brandt received me the next morning in the same chair, in the same silence, with the same watermarked paper lying on the desk like a trap that had been baited and re-baited.

"Well, Voss?"

I gave him Halloran. I gave him the street, the number, the cellar door behind the woodshed, the habit of taking payment in silver and printing at night. I gave him details polished smooth with use — details Riehl had trusted to a letter, God forgive us both, because a fugitive's memory is a warehouse and everything in it gets shipped eventually. I delivered it all in the flat voice of a man paying a debt, and I watched Brandt write it down, and I felt nothing like triumph. I felt like a counterfeiter watching his first bad coin pass: relief and shame in equal measure, and underneath both, the cold knowledge that the mint never stops.

Brandt finished writing, blotted the page, and looked up with something almost like respect.

"You made the sensible choice."

"I made a choice," I said.

"Same thing, in my experience." He slid the paper across the desk — berth, immunity, the widow's file marked for quiet destruction. "Collect your things. The barge loads in three days. And Voss—" I paused at the door. "If this proves accurate, I may even consider it the beginning of a useful relationship. Men who can weigh a decision are rare."

I walked out past the soldiers, past the bored sentry, down the steps of the customs house into a morning so ordinary it felt obscene — gulls working the shallows, a fishmonger crying her prices, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys of a town that did not yet know its own names were being set in type somewhere by lamplight.

Three days. I had bought three days, maybe four, with a dead man's address and my own neck as collateral.

Behind me, in the customs house, a rider was already being saddled. Ahead of me lay the foundry, the cellar, the fevered ghost of the free press, and my sister — who had heard every word of my plan and still looked at me as though I were the one thing in Aldenmoor she hadn't figured out how to save.

I started walking. There was an edition to set, and for the first time in my life, I knew exactly what every letter of it would cost.
