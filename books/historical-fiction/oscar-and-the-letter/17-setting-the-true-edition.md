## 17. Setting the True Edition

### Isandro

There is a particular hour — somewhere past midnight, when the candles have burned down to stubs and the oil in the lamps has gone the color of weak tea — when your hands stop belonging to you. They simply work. You stop being a person with opinions and become a machine with a heartbeat.

I had been that machine for six hours.

The foundry's back room smelled of lead and lampblack and my brother's unwashed collar, which was its own category of offense. We had barred the door, shuttered the windows, and hung blankets over every gap that might leak light toward the street, where Sergeant Hessler's patrol made its rounds with the punctuality of a bad conscience. Outside, Aldenmoor slept badly, if it slept at all. Inside, Oscar and I were committing the largest crime of our lives, and we were doing it in eleven-point Caslon.

"You've set 'Maren Kolb' with a swash on the K," Oscar said, peering over my shoulder.

"It's a ledger of testimony, not an invitation to a wedding."

"A swash implies dignity."

"A swash implies we had time to spare, which we don't." I pulled the stick from his hands and broke the line myself. "Names straight. Dates straight. No flourishes. If Brandt's men ever see this page, I want it so plain it looks like it was printed by God's own accountant."

Oscar held up both palms in surrender, which he had learned from watching me, which annoyed me more than the swash had.

We worked at two cases set side by side, the old composing stones dragged from the storeroom where they'd gathered dust since Father stopped taking outside jobs. Between us lay the proof sheet — the true ledger, the one Father had pulled with his ruined hands three days before he took to his bed for good. Every name in Aldenmoor. Every claim to a berth on the evacuation barge. Every date and signature that proved which families had legal passage and which had been quietly struck from the rolls by men who preferred their inheritances pre-stolen.

Four hundred and twelve names. And by dawn, if our backs and our luck held, four hundred and twelve lines of type, locked in chases, ready for the press.

My fingers ached. My eyes burned. Somewhere around the two-hundredth name I'd stopped feeling like a criminal and started feeling like something else — something I didn't have a word for yet. A witness, maybe. Witnesses usually got to just watch.

"Thora Lindqvist," Oscar read aloud, setting as he spoke. "Widow. Berth claim dated the eleventh of March, witnessed by E. Voss." He paused, his hand hovering over the case. "Isandro. Her entry. The one you altered in the colonel's copy — do we print it true here?"

"True," I said. "That's rather the entire point."

"I know. I'm asking because—" He set the type as he talked, quick and sure, the way he did everything when he'd stopped being afraid of it. "Because in the copy Brandt holds, her name is moved up. Because of you. Because of what it cost."

The widow's son was in the army now. That was the arithmetic nobody in this room liked: my one small forgery had saved him from conscription, and then Brandt had found the discrepancy, and then the widow's family had been arrested as scapegoats for sins they hadn't committed — sins *I* had committed, dressed up in lawful clothing. They were sitting in the guardhouse tonight while I set their names in type.

"They're in here," I said. "All of them. The widow, her son, the dates, the witnesses. When this edition gets out, Brandt can't pretend her family was ever anything but what the ledger says. It won't free them tomorrow. But it makes them un-erasable." I locked another line into the chase. "That's the most I can give them tonight. Un-erasability."

"That's a horrible word."

"I'm tired. Everything I say is a horrible word."

Oscar laughed — a real laugh, short and startled out of him, the first in days. It did something to the room. For a moment we were just two people who had grown up in this shop, arguing about typography at an indecent hour, exactly as we'd argued about everything since we could talk. Then the laugh faded and we went back to work, but the room stayed warmer somehow. Shared labor does that. It's the only argument-settler I've ever trusted.

### Oscar

Here is what nobody tells you about setting four hundred names by hand through one night: it stops being letters after a while and becomes people. You set "Aldous Brenner, cooper" and you remember him buying you cider at the autumn fair and cheating you at cards with the same hand that now existed only as forty pieces of metal in your palm. You set "Greta Voss, née Halvard" — Mother, ten years gone — and your throat closes around the em quad.

By three in the morning I understood what Anselm had been trying to tell me for a year, back when I thought his pamphlets were the weapon and the words inside them the ammunition. It was backwards. Words are cheap. Any fool can write *down with tyrants*. But a name, set in lead, with a date and a witness — that's a body. That's a person the regime cannot digest. You can burn paper. You cannot burn a chase of type without melting it first, and even melted, lead remembers.

I glanced across the stones at Isandro. She was bent over her case, candlelight catching the ink under her nails — she always had ink under her nails, always had, since we were children stealing impressions off Father's proofs. Her jaw was set in the way it set when she was winning an argument with herself.

Three days ago I had hated her. Or near enough — I had stood in the wrecked type room with a scattered case of fourteen-point between us and told her she was a coward's accomplice, and she had told me I was an idealist with other people's blood on his pamphlets, and we had both meant every word. Father had ended it the way he ended everything: by nearly dying, and pulling his last proof, and pressing it into her hands with those few words about names outliving us only if they're true. After that there was nothing left to argue. There was only the work.

"Riehl asked me once," I said, mostly to keep myself awake, "what I'd do if the cause asked for something I couldn't give."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the cause wouldn't ask. That's what believing in something means."

"And what do you believe now?" She didn't look up from her case. Her voice was careful, the way it is when someone is testing ice.

I set a line before answering. Brenner's berth date. Witnessed. True.

"Now? I believe the cause is whoever's asleep in those houses out there," I said. "I believe it's Thora Lindqvist in a cell for a crime she didn't commit. I believe Anselm was right about the press and wrong about almost everything else, including how much one person can carry." I looked at my hands, gray to the second knuckle. "And I believe my sister was right in the type room, and I'd rather set type all night than say it twice."

She did look up then. She didn't say anything — Isandro has never once in her life let a gracious moment pass without making it slightly worse — but she slid her candle an inch toward mine, so our light pooled together, and went back to work.

That was the apology. I took it.

* * *

### Isandro

Toward dawn the work turned strange. This happens in any long labor — the moment when craft becomes trance — but I had never felt it like this.

It was the repetition. Name, trade, date, witness. Name, trade, date, witness. Four hundred times, the same small architecture, and somewhere past the third hundred my mind began laying the town itself into the forme. I wasn't setting entries anymore; I was setting Aldenmoor. The tannery row and the ferry rights and the mill families and the fisher-folk whose claims went back further than anyone's paperwork. All of it arranged, justified, locked tight. A town in a wooden frame, ready to be pressed onto paper and handed back to itself.

Brandt thought the ledger was a list. That was his error, and it would be his ruin. A list you can shorten. A testimony you have to answer.

Oscar fell asleep standing up at half past five. I mean that literally: mid-line, mid-word, his body simply declared bankruptcy and he sagged against the stone, and I caught the stick before it spilled. I looked at him — twenty-three years old, hollow-eyed, hands wrecked, wanted by an army — and I felt such a stupid, enormous tenderness that I nearly woke him just to make it stop.

Instead I finished his line for him. Then the next one. I worked alone through the coldest hour, the one just before the sky considers itself, feeding names into the chases while my brother breathed slow against the composing stone, and I forgave him fully and finally for every secret he'd kept, because I understood at last that he hadn't been choosing Riehl over family. He'd been a boy who believed words could outrun soldiers, and someone had to be that boy, or no one would ever be the woman who set the true edition.

At six, I woke him with a knuckle between his shoulder blades.

"We're short," I said. "Two pages' worth of type. We need to distribute and reset the earliest formes before the press run, or we melt down the scrap bin."

"The scrap bin." He rubbed his face. "Father's old rejects."

"Our inheritance," I said. "Every mistake he ever caught, going back thirty years. He'd have loved this. All his errors pressed into service."

Oscar smiled, and for a second he looked like a child again, the one who used to sort type into rainbow piles while Father pretended not to notice. Then he rolled up his sleeves.

* * *

### Oscar

We melted the scrap at seven, working the little crucible Father used for repairs, and the smell of hot lead filled the shop like a memory. While the metal ran, Isandro checked every forme twice, then a third time, reading each page backward and forward the way Father taught us — because errors hide from familiarity, he said, and truth has no tolerance for a compositor in love with his own speed.

Outside, the town woke. Smoke from cookfires. Boots on the quay. Somewhere a soldier shouted and a dog answered, and we froze at the sound of the patrol passing the shutters — close enough that I could hear the man whistling, some parade tune, off-key and content — and then the footsteps faded and Isandro exhaled and said, "Page nine is crooked," because terror in our family always comes out as criticism.

At half past eight the formes were done. Fourteen pages. Four hundred and twelve names, plus the front matter Isandro had composed herself, which I read standing behind her shoulder and will carry to whatever grave I get:

*To the people of Aldenmoor: these are your names. No officer, order, or army may strike them. Read, copy, remember, pass on.*

No sedition in it. Not one word a magistrate could hang her for. Just a town, testifying.

She locked the final quoins and stepped back, and we looked at the chases laid out on the stones like a field of very small, very orderly graves — or seeds, depending on the day you were having. Today I chose seeds.

"The press," she said.

"The press is the problem. The army garrisons the square, but the old Washington hand press is still in the main room, and Hessler's men walk past it twice an hour. We can't run it quiet. You know what it sounds like."

"I know exactly what it sounds like." She was already moving toward the door, checking the bar. "It sounds like the truth being born, which is why they'll come running. So we don't run it at dawn, and we don't run it at dusk." She looked at me, and there was something in her face I'd seen only once before — the night she showed me the forged entry, the vertigo and the resolve wearing the same expression. "We run it at midnight. Tomorrow night. The same hour the army burns the records office — Brandt posted the order today, everyone saw it. Every soldier in Aldenmoor will be watching smoke, not listening for a press."

"And the copies?"

"Already arranged. The fisher families move at night anyway. By morning it's on every doorstep and in every boat heading downriver." She put her hand flat on the top chase, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "One night, Oscar. We rest today, we eat something, we sleep if our nerves allow it. And tomorrow at midnight—"

The knock came at the front door. Three raps. Measured. Unhurried. The knock of a man who knew exactly who was inside and wanted us to know he knew.

Isandro's hand closed over the chase as if she could shield four hundred names with one palm.

Through the shutters, in the slanted morning light, we watched Colonel Brandt's shadow settle against the boards — patient as type, waiting to be set.
