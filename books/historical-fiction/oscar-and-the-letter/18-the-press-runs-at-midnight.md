## 18. The Press Runs at Midnight

My father used to say that a press is the most honest machine ever built. It does exactly what you tell it, he said, no more, no less. He said this the way other men say a thing about God — with reverence and a certain relief that it wasn't his problem anymore.

At half past eleven that night, feeding paper into our own hand-press by the light of two shuttered lamps, I thought: Papa, you were wrong. The press is honest the way a knife is honest. It will cut bread or a throat, whichever your hand decides, and it will not lose one second of sleep over it.

We had been at work since dusk. Oscar stood at the composing stone, setting the last pages of the true ledger — every name, every berth claim, every date of registration, set in ten-point Caslon because it was what we had in quantity. Two hundred copies. That was the number we had agreed on, back when agreement still felt possible, before my hands began to shake in a way I pretended was the cold.

Two hundred copies for four hundred households. We would have to be generous with our definition of a copy.

"Hand me the quoins," Oscar said. His voice was steady. It had been steady for three days, ever since we stopped fighting and started working, which is its own kind of truce — the kind armies sign so they can get on with killing.

I handed him the quoins. My brother looked terrible. He hadn't slept properly since Riehl left the cellar, and there was a cut on his knuckle from the chase he wouldn't explain, and when he thought I wasn't looking he touched his breast pocket, where the false name he'd given Brandt's officers lived like a stone he couldn't cough up.

"You're bleeding on my forme," I said.

"I'm not."

"You are. There's blood in the margin. Very patriotic. The consul will think we fought the colonel hand to hand."

He wiped his hand on his apron without looking at it. "Isandro."

"Hm."

"If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"—if it goes wrong," he repeated, because my brother has never once let me win an argument by refusing to have it, "I want you to know the plan was mine. The distribution, the timing, all of it. You were only printing what Father told you to print."

I fed another sheet. The platen came down with its soft iron sigh. "Father is dying, Oscar. He's not telling anyone anything anymore. This was yours and mine." I pulled the sheet, held it to the lamp. Clean impression, good ink coverage, the widow Marta Kessel's name sitting where it belonged instead of where Brandt's money had put it. "And if it goes wrong, we'll both be hanged, so let's not spend our last quiet hour dividing credit like heirs."

He almost smiled. In the lamplight he looked fifteen again, the year he inked his own hair trying to clean the rollers, and I laughed at him for a week. Then the lamp guttered, and he looked twenty-six and doomed again, which was more accurate.

* * *

You should understand what we were racing against, because I did not fully understand it myself until that night.

Brandt had announced it at the market square with the courtesy of a man reading a menu: all civic records in Aldenmoor would be consolidated for "secure transport" to the capital. Secure transport meaning fire, though officially nothing had been said about fire. Officially, the records were being protected from us. The foundry's archives were scheduled for collection at dawn — the ledgers, the registers, the proof files, everything with a name in it. After dawn, Aldenmoor's memory would exist only in the capital's version, which was to say it would not exist at all.

They had already taken the great ledger itself, days ago, after the audit. I had watched them carry it out under an officer's arm like a stolen child. What remained in the shop was our proof pull — the one my father made with his ruined hands in the last week of his life — and our own memories, and type. Type was ours. No requisition order could take the alphabet out of our heads.

So we were resetting it. All of it. Every family, every claim, every date, plus — this was Riehl's addition, smuggled out through Oscar before he went north — the list of berths Brandt had sold, with prices, to loyalists who had never lived a day in Aldenmoor. Names bought. Names sold. The whole ugly arithmetic of it, set in metal.

Two hundred copies by dawn. A hand press does perhaps two hundred sheets in a night if the pressman is good and the ink behaves and God is distracted elsewhere.

"We'll make the first fifty ourselves," Oscar had said. "The rest goes out in galleys. Half a dozen printers' friends around town — Frau Behrend with her little jobbing press, the schoolmaster, the two men at the tannery who owe Father money. They set their own pages from ours. Nobody knows the whole edition except us."

"Nobody knows the whole edition," I said, "except everyone who prints a page."

"That's the point. You can't hang a town."

I remember thinking: you can try. But I didn't say it. There is a time for wryness and a time for pulling sheets, and I have always known the difference, mostly.

* * *

Around one o'clock, we heard the boots.

Oscar blew out both lamps so fast the room went black, and we stood pressed against the cold wall of the type room listening to a patrol pass along the cobbles outside — six men, maybe eight, unhurried, laughing about something. Laughing. At one in the morning, with fires planned for dawn, they were laughing about whatever soldiers laugh about, which is generally nothing, loudly.

My heart was going like a devil's drum. And here is the thing I am ashamed of, or proud of, I've never decided: underneath the fear there was something else. Something bright and disgraceful. We were committing treason in the dark with ink under our fingernails, and some traitorous chamber of my heart was singing about it.

When the boots faded, Oscar relit the lamps and looked at me.

"You're smiling," he said.

"I'm not."

"There's blood in the margin and you're smiling."

"Feed the press, Oscar."

But I was smiling. I admit it here, to no one, in the privacy of a page that may never be read: for the first time since Brandt's troops marched down our street, I was not afraid in the way I had been afraid. Before, fear had been a thing done to me — requisitions, audits, arrests, my father's lungs filling with blood while the army politely waited for him to die. Tonight fear was a thing I carried, chose, aimed. There is all the difference in the world between weather and a weapon.

* * *

By three we had ninety-one copies and a problem: the ink.

The ink cake was wearing thin. I had stretched what we had with the lampblack-and-linseed mixture Father used for cheap jobbing work, but the mixture ran greasy and the impressions were going soft. On copy eighty-eight, the name Hessler came out ghostly, half-swallowed. On ninety, Brandt's own purchased berths — the evidence that mattered most — printed pale as watermarks.

Evidence that prints faintly might as well not print. A court reads bold type as truth and gray type as rumor.

"Stop?" Oscar asked.

I considered the ink stone, the dregs in the can, the two hours of darkness left. "No," I said. "Change tactics. We run the berth-sale page twice as dark and let the family names go light. People will know their own names even half-printed. They don't know the colonel's crimes at all yet. Ink follows importance."

My brother stared at me. "That's — actually clever."

"Don't sound surprised. I've been clever for years. You were too busy being righteous to notice."

He laughed — a short, cracked laugh, the first in days — and went to re-ink the rollers, and for a moment, turning the crank together in the lamplight, sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow, we looked exactly like what Father had always said we'd be: two halves of one printer, arguing over one page.

Ninety-one became one hundred and forty. One hundred and forty became one hundred and seventy. Somewhere past midnight the work stopped feeling like labor and started feeling like prayer, the repetitive kind, the kind monks do — pull, turn, feed, pull — each sheet a small act of defiance so ordinary-seeming that no one hearing it would guess. The press doesn't shout. That's its genius. It whispers, two hundred times, in two hundred kitchens, until the whisper is a fact.

At half past four, Oscar took the first bundle and went out the cellar door into the blue-black cold, and I kept printing alone.

* * *

I want to record what happened next precisely, because someday someone may ask, and because precision is the only inheritance our family has ever trusted.

Frau Behrend came herself. Sixty-three years old, arthritic, carrying her little platen press parts in a laundry basket under a false bottom of shirts, and she stood in my doorway dripping snowmelt and said, "Voss girl. Where's my pages?"

I gave her formes nine through fourteen, locked and wrapped. She tucked them into the basket among the shirts.

"They'll come for this shop at dawn," I said.

"I know." She adjusted her shawl. "Your father set my wedding announcement in 1811. Charged me nothing. Said names should start free and stay free." She hefted the basket. "I've got thirty years of grudges against the tax office, girl, and now I've got a use for them."

Then the schoolmaster's boy, twelve years old, running through the snow with his collar up, taking the galleys for the schoolroom press like a courier of state. Then the tannery men, separately, an hour apart, neither knowing the other had come. By five o'clock the edition was loose in Aldenmoor like spilled mercury — impossible to gather, finding every crack, pooling in every kitchen.

And the army prepared its bonfires.

We heard them through the walls: wagons in the square, the scrape of sledge runners, officers calling to one another in the cheerful voices men use for chores. They were collecting the records from the church registry, the magistrate's office, the guild hall. Ours would be collected at first light. By noon, Aldenmoor's official past would be ash, and the official future would be whatever Colonel Brandt wrote in its place.

What they did not know — what no one knew yet, not even fully me — was that the past had already left the building. It was under shirts in a laundry basket. It was in a schoolboy's satchel. It was folded into coat linings and flour sacks and hymnals, moving house to house faster than any wagon could chase it.

You cannot arrest a town that agrees on its own name.

* * *

Oscar came back at quarter to six, snow in his hair, breath ragged, and counted the remaining stack.

"One hundred and ninety-eight," he said.

"The last two are drying." I pulled them, laid them flat. Two hundred. Exactly. I had promised myself we'd stop at two hundred and I am enough my father's daughter that we stopped at two hundred, though I confess I looked at the ink stone and wondered whether a few more might save someone.

"Rumeltz Street already has pages posted on the church door," Oscar said. "Behrend works fast. Someone's read them aloud twice. Old Halversen — the one who can't walk — sent his grandson to memorize the berth list in case the papers get confiscated." He sat down heavily on the composing stone's edge, suddenly looking like a man who had been awake for a century. "It's working, Isandro. People are reading it. Standing in the snow, reading it."

I cleaned the press in silence for a moment. Wiping the rollers, breaking down the forme, putting the letters back in their cases — E to its compartment, R to its compartment, the whole alphabet going home to bed. There is a ritual quality to it. Distributing type is called distributing, but it feels like forgiving.

"Whatever happens at dawn," I said, "it's out. They can burn this building to the foundation and it changes nothing. The names are gone. Out. Loose." I set the last quoin in the tray. "Do you understand? For the first time since the requisition, they cannot unprint us."

Oscar nodded slowly. Then he said, quietly, "There's one more thing. Riehl's last instruction. If the edition lands — truly lands, if the town takes it up — there's a man at the river office, a clerk, neutral papers, who'll carry a full set to the consul downstream. First barge out. Today."

"Which barge?"

He hesitated. I knew the hesitation. I had learned to read my brother's silences the way I read bad impressions — by what was missing.

"Oscar. Which barge?"

"The prisoners' transfer," he said. "Riehl's transfer." And then, not meeting my eyes: "I'm going to be on it."

The lamp flame stood very still between us. Outside, faintly, the army's wagons creaked in the square, loading the town's memory for burning, and somewhere across the rooftops a church bell that had no permission from anyone rang the hour anyway — six o'clock, first light coming up gray behind the shutters, and the smell of smoke already on the air, though whose smoke, whose fire, no one could yet say.
