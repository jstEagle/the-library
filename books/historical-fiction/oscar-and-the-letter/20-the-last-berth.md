## 20. The Last Berth

My father had spent sixty years making letters that could not be argued with, and in his final week he discovered the one argument no one could answer: he simply refused to die on schedule.

"You're hovering," he said, without opening his eyes. He lay propped in the bed behind the shop, in the little room where he'd slept since before I was born, beneath a window that looked out on the yard where the cooling racks stood. The army had left those alone. Even Brandt's quartermasters had no use for lead cooling in the open air.

"I'm checking your pulse," I said. "It's called medicine."

"It's called hovering." He opened his eyes then — grey as type metal, my father's eyes, the same colour as everything he'd ever made — and looked at me the way he used to look at a badly justified line of type. "Where is your brother?"

"Asleep. Or pretending to be. It's past two."

"Fetch him."

"Papa—"

"Isandro." His voice was quiet and it was iron. Sixty years of casting words had taught him exactly how much weight a single word could carry, and he put all of it into my name. "Fetch your brother. We are going to have the family meeting we should have had three weeks ago, while I can still sit up for it."

I went. In the corridor I stopped and pressed my forehead against the cool plaster and breathed, because I already knew what the meeting would be about, and knowing did not make it lighter to carry. There was one berth left on the evacuation barge. One. And three of us who had a legal claim to it under our own ledger — the true ledger, the one now sitting in a consul's strongbox across the river, copied in ten thousand hands and beyond anyone's power to unprint.

Brandt's world had come apart the way a poorly locked form comes apart when you lift it too fast: piecemeal, clattering, irrecoverable. Pages had reached the neutral consulate; the berth-selling had been read aloud in rooms where Brandt could not reach with his courtesy or his guns; the evacuation schedule had collapsed into something between a rout and an auction. The army's answer, as far as anyone could tell from the troop movements in the streets, was to prepare to burn whatever records remained — including ours — and pretend the paper trail had died of natural causes.

Which meant time was short, my father was shorter, and the family meeting was going to be the hardest piece of composition I'd ever set.

* * *

Oscar came down the stairs with his shirt buttoned wrong and his eyes already braced. He knew too. We had both known for days. Neither of us had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would require deciding, and deciding meant one of us losing.

Father sat up against his pillows with a shawl over his shoulders like a foreman inspecting the floor. He looked terrible. He also looked more awake than he had in a week, which frightened me more than the coughing had.

"Sit," he said. We sat, on either side of the bed, like children summoned after breaking a case of sorts — which, incidentally, we had also done, but that was not the crime on the agenda tonight.

"The barge leaves tomorrow at noon," Father said. "The last one. There is one berth with the Voss name on it. Mine." He held up a hand before either of us could draw breath to argue. "I have not lost my mind. Listen. Then argue, if you still want to."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, boots on cobbles — a patrol passing, unhurried, the way men walk when they own the hour.

"I am dying," Father said, in the tone he used for stating ink coverage. "Not tomorrow, perhaps, but soon, and certainly before any barge could reach anywhere worth reaching. If I take that berth, I will spend my last days being rocked on a cold river by strangers, and I will spend them *uselessly*. I have never in my life done anything uselessly and I do not intend to begin at the end." He looked from me to Oscar and back. "The berth goes to your brother."

"No," I said.

"Isandro—"

"No." My voice cracked and I hated it. "You don't get to decide this from a deathbed like it's a job allocation. Papa, he's your son. You're *our father*—"

"And precisely because I am your father," he said, "I know what I am leaving you, and what I am leaving him, and they are not the same thing." He turned to Oscar. "Tell her what you told me yesterday. While she was setting the widow Halversen's affidavit. Tell her what you've arranged."

Oscar went very still. That was answer enough, but I made him say it anyway, because if I had learned anything that autumn it was that unsaid things fester like type dust in a wound.

"Riehl is in the prisoners' transfer," Oscar said quietly. "They moved him out of the cellar two days ago — Brandt's officers swept the district, and the safe houses burned one after another. He's in the column going north. Prison barges, work camps, the usual machinery." His jaw worked. "There's a muster point at the mill quay tomorrow morning. The transfer lists are chaos since the consul story broke — half the officers are packing their own bags, nobody's checking names properly. A man with the right papers and the right face can step into that column in Riehl's place. And a man with a copy of the true ledger in his coat gets treated differently once he's across the border. Evidence travels better than testimony. Riehl taught me that."

I stared at him. "You're going to hand yourself to them."

"I'm going to hand them *me* instead of *him*," Oscar said. "And I'm going to carry the ledger into a courtroom somewhere that isn't run by Brandt, which is more than it would ever accomplish sitting in a cellar under a burning town."

"You'll be in irons within a week."

"Probably."

"They'll hang you."

"Probably not," he said, and for one absurd moment he smiled — my brother, twenty-two years old, smiling at me across our father's deathbed. "Sedition trials need witnesses. Witnesses need to stay alive. Anselm always said the regime's greatest weakness is that it loves paperwork. I intend to become extremely well-documented."

"This isn't funny."

"No," he agreed, and the smile went away, and what was underneath it was worse. "It isn't."

* * *

We argued until the lamp burned low. I argued that I should go — I had set every page of the true edition, I knew its arguments letter by letter, I could defend it better than anyone. Father said gently that the ledger needed its compositor alive and free, in a town where the story had to keep being told after the barge was gone. Oscar said nothing during this part, which told me he agreed with Father, which told me they had already settled it between themselves, which made me angrier than any argument could have.

Then I argued that no one should go — that we could hide the copy again, wait for spring, wait for the war to turn, wait for something. And my father, who had been silent through this whole stretch with his eyes closed, opened them and delivered the sentence I will carry to my own grave, wherever that turns out to be.

"Isandro. Names outlive us only if they're true. But they only *travel* if someone carries them." He reached out and took my hand, and his grip was still strong, which felt like a personal betrayal of physics. "I have carried mine for sixty years in lead. Your brother will carry them in paper. You will carry them in memory, and then in metal again, because you are the one who stays. That is not a lesser share. Do not let anyone — least of all yourself — tell you it is."

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own telling: I did not cry. I sat very straight and I said, "He takes my good coat, then. Not his. His buttons are wrong for a printer's man and the transfer sergeants notice things."

Oscar laughed — a wet, broken laugh — and Father nodded as though coats were the natural conclusion of the evening's business, and just like that it was decided, the way the biggest decisions in a foundry always get decided: not with ceremony, but with someone checking the practical details while their heart tears down the middle.

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night, because that was the only language we had ever all spoken fluently.

Father directed from bed, his voice thinner by the hour but exact. Oscar packed: the ledger copy wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of my good coat — the one with the mended cuff, the one I'd worn to every funeral Aldenmoor had held these ten years, so that it already knew how to behave at sad occasions. I sat at the stone sink and cleaned type, which is what I do when my hands need to be busy so the rest of me doesn't shatter, and I cleaned enough type for three presses and then started on cases that didn't need it at all.

At some point near dawn, Oscar came and stood beside me at the sink. He didn't touch anything. He just rolled up his sleeve and showed me his forearm, where the type dust still settled into the lines of his skin the way it had since he was nine years old.

"Do you remember," he said, "when you taught me to distribute a case? I was seven. I kept putting the e's in the a box."

"You were hopeless."

"You made me sort the entire case twice." He turned his arm over, looking at the dust in the creases of his wrist. "I hated you for a whole afternoon. Best afternoon of my life, probably. I've thought about it every day this month."

"Oscar—"

"If it goes badly," he said, fast, before I could build any walls, "the consul has the full edition. Ten thousand copies. They can't kill it, Isandro. Whatever happens to me, they can't kill it. That's the whole point of everything we did. So don't you dare stand in the ashes of this shop feeling sorry for yourself, because the work survived, and the work was always the point."

"That is a horrible speech."

"I know. Anselm wrote most of it."

That got a sound out of me that was halfway to a laugh, which I think was its purpose.

Then Father called us in, and there was no more cleverness available to any of us.

* * *

He gave Oscar the berth papers himself. He had signed them the night before, in a hand that shook on the signature only — the rest of the document was as firm as a punchcut stroke, dates and claims and the Voss name written out in full, the way he'd written it ten thousand times in the ledger, the way it would now travel north in a prisoner's coat lining toward whatever court might still be bothered with truth.

"There," he said, when Oscar took the papers. "Legal, witnessed, and honest. The last honest document this family produces for some months, I expect. Try to deserve it."

"Yes, Papa."

"And you—" He beckoned me close, and when I bent over him he smelled of camphor and lead and the inside of the shop, and I understood that I was memorizing him without meaning to, the way you memorize a page you know you'll never pull again. "You will rebuild. Not the shop. The shop is wood and iron; the army will burn it and good riddance, it was always too damp. You will rebuild the *press*. Wherever there's a bench and a case and one stubborn woman, there's a press. I've seen you make letters out of nothing since you were four years old. Don't stop now because the times have gotten dramatic."

"I won't."

"Say it properly."

"I won't," I said, "stop."

"Good girl." His eyes were closing. "Terrible daughter. Worst hoverer in the history of medicine. Go and see your brother off, and don't cry at the quay, it embarrasses the cause."

* * *

The mill quay at eight in the morning was grey water, grey sky, grey-faced men in a column, and soldiers with lists who checked almost nothing, because the empire that had demanded perfect ledgers from everyone else could no longer produce one itself. There was a poetry in that. I had no appetite for it.

Riehl was easy to find in the column — a big man gone gaunt, a bandage at his temple, standing with the particular stillness of someone conserving everything for later. When Oscar walked up to him in my good coat and murmured the arranged words, Riehl stared at him for a long moment, and then did something I didn't expect: he bowed. Formally. A printer's bow, to a colleague.

Then the sergeant was counting heads, and there was no time for anything, and my brother turned around and looked at me across fifteen feet of trampled mud, and neither of us cried, because we had both been raised by a man who believed emotion should be justified flush to the margin.

He touched two fingers to his chest — the pocket where the ledger rode — and then raised the same two fingers in the compositor's signal for *copy clear*, the sign we used across the shop to say the page is right, send it on.

Send it on.

I gave him back the sign. The column swallowed him. The barge whistle blew, long and low, and the prisoners went one way and the evacuees another, and the last civilian barge cast off at noon with one empty berth that had my father's name on the manifest and my brother's future in its keeping, sliding down the grey river until the mist took it.

I stood on the quay until everyone had gone, because someone from Aldenmoor ought to watch the water, and then I walked home through occupied streets to a house where my father was dying on schedule after all, and a shop that would burn within the week, and one case of type I intended to save.

Behind me the river carried my whole heart north.

Ahead of me, at the end of the lane, two soldiers were already stacking barrels of lamp oil against the foundry wall, and one of them was whistling.
