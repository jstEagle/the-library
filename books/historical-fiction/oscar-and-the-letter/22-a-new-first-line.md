## 22. A New First Line

There is a particular sound a type case makes when it has been carried through fire and survived — a rattle, like teeth chattering in a cold room, like someone trying to speak and having forgotten the words. My case of Baskerville speaks that way every morning when I unlock the drawer where I keep it. It is missing its lowercase e's, most of them, because e is the hungriest letter in any alphabet and mine went into pamphlets and proclamations and one very long ledger that no longer exists anywhere except in four hundred heads and one printed page.

I have learned to make do without e's. There is a lesson in that, though I would not recommend the tuition.

The shop is not my father's foundry. That building is ash and a foundation, and the army put up a notice on the blackened lot explaining, in official language, that the fire had been regrettable and spontaneous and certainly not ordered by anyone whose signature might be legally pursued. The notice is still there. Children have thrown stones at it until the word *regrettable* is unreadable, which I consider the most honest editing the occupation has produced.

My new press lives in the back of Frau Hessler's dye house, which smells of madder and urine and, lately, of ink, to her loud and theatrical disgust. "You are ruining my livelihood," she tells me, hands on hips.

"Your livelihood smells worse than I do," I tell her.

"Impossible."

But she leaves the back door unlatched for me at dawn and pretends, with tremendous commitment, not to know what I print there. In Aldenmoor we have all become experts at pretending not to know things. It is practically a trade now, like coopering or tanning. The difference is that some of us pretend not to know things while doing them anyway, in secret, at night, and this is called resistance, whereas before it was simply called being a Voss.

* * *

It is March again, or nearly. Ten months since the barge went downriver carrying my brother and my father's proof and four hundred true names, and nine months since I buried my father under the willow behind what used to be the churchyard wall, because the churchyard itself was full of men the colonel's audit had arrested, and the ground there belongs to the state now, and the state charges rent even for grief.

Father died the way he cut punches: slowly, precisely, leaving no stroke wasted. His last week he could not lift his arms, so he directed me by voice, teaching me the old strokes from his bed — the angle of the graver, the patience of the counterpunch, the way you must cut toward yourself and trust your own hand more than your fear. He talked about lead as if lead were a congregation. He said, "Metal remembers what flesh is asked to forget," which I wrote down afterward on the inside of my wrist in pencil because I was afraid of losing it, and which stayed on my wrist for eleven days until the bathwater took it.

He never said goodbye. What he said was: "Set the line straight." And then, an hour later, quieter, as if correcting himself: "No. Set it *true*. Straight lines can lie if the letters are wrong."

That is the epitaph I gave him, carved small, in slate, because marble was requisitioned too. *Names outlive us only if they're true.* The stonecutter charged me double for the asterisk. I paid it gladly. Somewhere my father is laughing at the markup.

* * *

This morning I am setting the first page of the first edition of the *Aldenmoor Free Gazette*, and the first page is not news. It is not an editorial. It is not even, strictly speaking, writing. It is a list.

Four hundred and six names. Every family that held claim to passage on the last evacuation barge. Every name from my father's ledger, set in type, in full, in the order they were entered, with the dates their claims were made and — this was Oscar's addition, sent to me in his careful hand across three hundred miles of occupied country — with the names of those who bought berths out of turn, printed beside the prices they paid, because a list that hides its thieves is only half a list.

The army burned the ledger in October. They burned it in the market square, with trumpets, as though burning paper were a victory rather than an admission. By then it did not matter. By then the true edition had been out of their reach for five months, folded into coats and hymnals and sacks of flour, copied by hand by schoolteachers and priests and one very determined ferryman who reproduced the whole thing from memory onto tar paper because he claimed paper made him sneeze. Copies reached the neutral consul in Braven. Copies reached newspapers in cities whose names I only knew from shipping crates. Colonel Brandt's tidy commerce in other people's survival became, over one autumn, a matter of international embarrassment, then international correspondence, then — and here the story goes sideways, the way justice does, limping in late and smelling faintly of other people's politics — international pressure. Brandt was recalled in January. Not tried. Not hanged. Recalled, with honors, to some desk in the capital where men who sell berths go to write memos about why the river towns are ungrateful.

Oscar writes to me that justice in our country arrives the way freight does: eventually, damaged, and billed to the wrong party.

* * *

Setting four hundred and six names takes patience of a kind I did not have at twenty-two. I have it now, at twenty-three, the way you acquire a scar: involuntarily, permanently, with a story attached.

I work in the gray light before Frau Hessler's fires are lit, because the dye house is hers by day and mine by the grace of early hours. My composing stick fits my hand the way my father's graver fit his. I set the names two at a time, quoining each line tight, and I say them under my breath as I go, because Father said a compositor should never set a name she won't speak aloud. *Aldermann. Anselm-Riehl, docketed under alias, see annotation. Baumann. Brandt, Col. H., entry falsified, berth 9, price 400 crowns — see annotation.* Yes. His name is in it too. Oscar insisted, and I agreed, and then I insisted on something further: that the annotations be set in smaller type but not italicized, because italics apologize, and I am done apologizing.

The widow Marta Kellner's name sits near the top now, where my forgery moved it, except that it is no forgery anymore; it is simply true, ratified by print and distance and the fact that her son Emil came home in February with a limp and a discharge and a habit of standing very still when doors slam. Emil works for me now, two mornings a week, distributing what I print. He is seventeen and handles the army patrols with a serenity I find frankly unnerving. Yesterday a sergeant asked him what he was carrying and Emil said, "Bread," and showed him bread, and underneath the bread, in the basket's false bottom, were forty copies of the gazette's second sheet, and the sergeant waved him through because the bread was real and warm and soldiers are hungry, and this, Emil told me afterward with a perfectly straight face, is called logistics.

I have come to believe the revolution will be carried out entirely by teenagers and grandmothers, and that history books will get all the details wrong.

* * *

The press itself is a Riehl. Of course it is. Anselm Riehl — fugitive, seditionist, wounded guest of our cellar for one terrible fortnight — built presses in his youth, before words became his weapon instead of his trade, and when he slipped out of Aldenmoor ahead of Brandt's search, going downriver hidden in a coal barge with a fever and a satchel of plates, he left behind drawings. Oscar smuggled them to me inside a consignment of hymnals, which I consider the single funniest thing either of my brother's causes ever produced. It took the town's remaining honest carpenter, old Piet Vandt, four months to build it, working nights, swearing continuously in two languages. It is ugly. It prints crookedly on the left margin. I love it the way you love a stubborn animal that has carried you through weather.

Riehl himself is alive. This I know from Oscar's letters, which arrive irregularly, disguised as invoices from a fictional button merchant named Herr Fassbinder, whose buttons are always described in terms that would strike a casual reader as deranged. ("Shipment of 12 dozen horn buttons delayed; the road past Marn is watched; expect delivery by the feast of St. Alban.") I have developed genuine affection for Herr Fassbinder. I worry about his inventory. I hope his buttons arrive by St. Alban's day.

* * *

The letter comes today.

I hear the knock — three quick, two slow, our code, older than the press, older than the war — and open the back door to find Emil holding an envelope with the button-merchant's unmistakable hand. My fingers know before my mind does. Something in the weight of it. Letters have weights the way type does; a compositor learns to read them blind.

I wait until evening to open it. I don't know why. Habit, perhaps — the old discipline of the cellar months, when joy and terror had to be rationed in equal portions lest either one give you away. I sit in the dye house among the cooling vats with a candle stub, and I break the seal, and I read it twice before I let myself believe it.

*Dear Sandro,*

*Herr Fassbinder regrets to inform you that his warehouse in the east has been seized by persons unknown, and that he must relocate his entire inventory north, to a city with a free port and insufficient censors. He has secured a position there as, imagine this, a printer. The authorities in that city have heard of a certain river town whose names traveled farther than its barges, and they wish to reprint the list in an edition of ten thousand. Herr Fassbinder requires a compositor who can set four hundred and six names accurately, quickly, and without italics. He knows of exactly one.*

*The berth is not free — nothing ever is — but the road is open now past Marn, and the watching has grown lazy, and I am told the bread in the north is excellent.*

*I will not presume. You have a press. You have a town. You have a father's craft in your hands and a widow's son carrying baskets for you. If you stay, stay knowing that everything you built here travels ahead of me like a herald, and that in every city where our names are read, Aldenmoor is not a place they burned but a place that spoke.*

*But if you come —*

*Bring the case of Baskerville. It owes me an e.*

*Your brother,*
*Oscar*
*(Fassbinder, per the customs men)*

*P.S. Riehl sends his regards and a correction for page three, annotation eleven, which he swears he flagged in the original and I swear he did not.*

I laugh. Out loud, alone, in a dye house at night, which is the sort of behavior that gets a woman spoken about in a town like this, and I find I no longer care what the town says about me. The town and I have an understanding. I print what it is not allowed to say; it lets me be eccentric.

* * *

I do not decide that night. Father taught me never to set type tired, and decisions, I have found, are typeset too — they need clean hands and a steady stick or the errors compound down the whole column of your life.

But I will tell you what I did instead, that night, because it turned out to be the answer arriving ahead of the question, the way it always does.

I sat down at the case, and I pulled the first galley of the gazette's opening page, and I looked at it in the candlelight: four hundred and six names, true and entire, thieves and victims and widows and ferryman all set side by side in metal that remembered. And beneath the final name — beneath *Voss, Isandro, compositor, entry retained, no berth taken* — beneath my own name, which I had set last and held longest —

I unlocked the drawer and took out the battered case of Baskerville, teeth chattering, e-less, fire-marked, and I began, carefully, stroke by patient stroke, to cut a new e on the graver Father left me. Cutting toward myself. Trusting my hand more than my fear.

Whether I set that letter into a page here in Aldenmoor or carry it north in my pocket, I cannot yet say. But I know this much, and I set it now in the steadiest type I own:

History stopped happening to other people the night the press ran at midnight. Since then, it happens in my hands, line by line, name by name — and whoever reads this page next, in whatever city, in whatever year, should understand that the letters were cut by someone who stayed, and that staying was also a departure, and that the first line of any free press is always the same:

*We were here. We are here. Read us.*
