## 1. The Weight of Glass

The lens had to be polished before the sun came up, because that was when the salt did its worst work. Lior Ashgrove had learned this from his father, who had learned it from his, and in twenty-two years of keeping the light at Orchard's End he had never once broken the rule. Salt was patient. Salt crept up the tower stairs on the wind and settled on the great curved faces of the Fresnel lens like a man waiting outside a door he intended to enter eventually. You could not argue with it. You could only arrive earlier.

So he arrived earlier. He always did.

He climbed the ninety-one steps with the lamp still burning above him, its flame a soft orange eye that had watched over the bay all night without his help. At the top he set down his kit—chamois cloths, a bottle of spirits, a small brass brush for the prism edges—and began. The work was simple and it was not easy. Each facet wanted its own attention. You breathed on the glass, wiped in slow circles, held the cloth up to the flame to check for streaks, and if you found one you started again. A smudge no bigger than a thumbnail could throw a shadow across a mile of dark water, and somewhere out there a fisherman's boy might be steering home by the light you'd let go dirty.

That was the whole philosophy of it, Lior thought, working the chamois along the curve. Do the thing properly. Let the doing speak. Nobody needed to know your name to be saved by your light.

He finished the last facet as the sky went from iron to pewter, and stood a moment with his hand flat against the glass, feeling the cold of it seep into his palm. From the gallery he could see the whole town laid out below him: the crooked lane, the chapel with its slate roof, the harbour wall where the boats knocked together like old friends gossiping. Smoke rising from chimneys. Gulls arguing over something on the shingle. Orchard's End, two hundred souls, give or take, most of whom knew him only as *the keeper*—a function, not a face. He had spent his life making sure it stayed that way.

His wife Elowen used to tease him about it. *You're a ghost, Lior Ashgrove,* she'd say, laughing, her hands full of windfall apples. *You haunt your own house.* She had been the visible one—the loud one, the one who sang in the market and argued with the fishmonger about prices and once, memorably, told the harbormaster's wife exactly what she thought of her new hat. When she died, two winters ago now, half the town came to the funeral just to feel the absence of her voice.

Lior had gone back to polishing glass the next morning. It was what he knew how to do with grief: contain it, tend it, keep it fed on silence so it wouldn't spill onto anyone else.

He descended the stairs, doused nothing—for the lamp would burn until full daylight—and stepped out into the grey morning to walk the cliff path down toward the orchard.

* * *

The orchard had been Elowen's kingdom, and it showed. She had planted it against everyone's advice—apple trees on exposed coastal soil, forty feet above a sea that threw salt at anything green—but she had chosen sheltered varieties and mulched them with seaweed and kelp, and by some stubborn alchemy they had grown. Fourteen trees in rows that followed no logic but hers. She used to say each one had a name and a temperament, though she refused to tell anyone which was which, so that Lior spent years wondering which tree was the one that sulked.

Since her death he had let it run wild. Not out of neglect exactly—or not only. Pruning meant decisions, and decisions meant admitting the orchard was his now, and he wasn't ready to own anything that large. So the trees grew as they pleased, branches crossing, suckers shooting up from the roots, and every autumn the fruit fell and rotted sweet into the grass and the gulls got fat and nobody minded much except Maren, who said it was a sin against good land.

This morning he walked the rows anyway, boots wet with dew, checking the boughs for winter damage. One limb on the oldest tree had split in November's gales, hanging by a sliver of bark. He fetched the saw from the shed and cut it clean, and the wound showed pale wood, fresh as bone. He stood looking at it longer than the job required.

"You'd have done this in October," he said aloud, to no one. To her. The wind took the words and gave nothing back.

He gathered a basket of the last keeping apples—small, hard, ugly things that would soften into something decent by February—and carried them up the path to the cottage where Maren and Oscar slept.

* * *

The cough woke him before dawn these days, which meant it had been waking Maren long before that. He heard it through the wall as he banked the fire: a dry ratcheting sound, like a pump drawing on a well that was running low. By the time he had the kettle on she appeared in the kitchen doorway, wrapped in a shawl, trying to arrange her face into something reassuring.

"You're up early," she said, and then coughed into her sleeve, and pretended she hadn't.

"The lens wanted doing."

"The lens always wants doing." She lowered herself into her chair by the window—the same chair she'd sat in since she came to live with them after Elowen died, bringing Oscar and her widow's poverty and a laugh that sounded, these days, thinner than it used to. "You'll wear yourself to a shadow, Lior."

"Shadows are useful," he said. "They show you where the light is."

She smiled at that, or made the shape of a smile, and he set tea and bread and a boiled egg before her, and pretended not to count how much of the egg she left. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken and load-bearing: he managed, she endured, neither named the thing growing in her chest. Dr. Penhale had used the word *consumption* once, quietly, in Lior's hearing, and then looked at the floor, and Lior had thanked him and paid him and never mentioned it again. Naming things gave them weight. He preferred to carry them unlabelled.

Oscar came down at half past seven like weather—all elbows and noise, hair sticking up, ten years old and hungry in the way only ten-year-olds are hungry, which is to say cosmically, permanently, as an affront to the universe. He dropped into his chair and took the bread before anyone spoke.

"There's a ship," he announced, mouth full. "Out past the point. Big one. Grey."

"Fishing boat," Lior said.

"It's too big to be a fishing boat. And there were two more behind it, I saw them from the headland on my way back from the well." Oscar said this with the particular satisfaction of a boy delivering intelligence. "Mr. Tregose says it's the army."

Lior kept pouring the tea. His hand was steady. He noticed himself noticing that—checking his own steadiness the way he checked the lens for streaks—and filed the observation away without examining it.

"Armies have better things to do than visit us," Maren said lightly.

"Not according to Mr. Tregose." Oscar swallowed. "He says there's a war over the water and somebody's losing it, and losing people come here. He said—" the boy hesitated, catching something in the room's temperature, "—he said we'd all know soon enough."

"Well," said Maren. "Mr. Tregose does love knowing soon enough."

But Lior saw her hand go, briefly, to her chest, as if the cough had moved deeper at the news. He saw it and said nothing, because saying nothing was what he did. He packed Oscar's satchel with bread and cheese and the battered copy of *Robinson Crusoe* they were reading aloud at night, and sent him down the lane to the schoolhouse, and watched from the door until the boy turned the corner by the chapel.

Then he stood in the kitchen with his sister and listened to the sea, which sounded, this morning, no different than any other morning. That was the trouble with the sea. It gave you no warning worth trusting.

* * *

The rest of the day he spent on accounts he did not need to keep—oil consumed, wicks trimmed, weather logged in his neat clerk's hand, the hand that would matter later, though he couldn't know it yet. He walked into town for flour and lamp oil and heard the talk in the shop: three ships, maybe four, anchored past the point, dark hulls with no colours flying. The harbormaster had gone out in a punt and been waved off. Nobody liked being waved off. Orchard's End was a place where you knew every boat by the sound of its engine, and these ships sat silent as stones, and silence on the water is worse than shouting.

"They'll want provisions," the shopkeeper said, weighing sugar for Lior with a nervous hand. "Water. That's all. War's a long way off yet."

"Aye," said Lior, because agreeing cost nothing.

But walking home along the harbour wall in the failing light, he counted things the way he counted everything—steps, facets, coins, eggs left uneaten. Two hundred souls, give or take. Boats that fed them. A school with one teacher. A doctor already stretched thin. A sister with a sickness that had a name nobody spoke. A nephew who deserved more orchard than war.

He thought: whatever comes ashore from those ships, my work is the light. Keep the glass clean. Keep the flame lit. Sign nothing, want nothing, stand where the shadows fall, and the world will pass over you like weather.

It had worked for forty-nine years. He had no reason to think it would stop working now.

That night he read to Oscar by the fire while Maren dozed in her chair, her breathing whistling faintly at the bottom of each breath. Crusoe had just found the footprint in the sand, the single print that ruined his whole understanding of his island, and Oscar sat bolt upright and demanded to know what happened next, and Lior closed the book and said, "Tomorrow," because that was the ritual, and rituals were the walls he built his life inside.

After the boy slept, Lior climbed the tower one last time and stood in the lantern room, watching the beam sweep out over the black water, around and around, patient as breathing. Far past the point, where the grey ships rode at anchor, he could just make out their shapes—darker than the dark, motionless, waiting.

The light touched them once each time it swung round. They didn't answer it. Nothing on the water answered anything; that was the bargain of the sea.

Lior went down the ninety-one steps and to bed, and slept the sleep of a man who believes that routine is the same thing as safety, and that a clean lens and a full logbook can hold back whatever is coming.

In the morning, the soldiers came ashore.
