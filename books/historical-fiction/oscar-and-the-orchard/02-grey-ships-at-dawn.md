## 2. Grey Ships at Dawn

Lior saw the ships before anyone in Orchard's End did, because seeing was his profession.

Three of them, grey as unpolished pewter, riding low in the dawn haze off the point. He had been trimming the wick when their shapes resolved out of the fog, and he stood a long moment with the brass key still in his hand, watching. Troopships—he knew them now from the illustrated papers Maren kept, from the war news she read aloud in her cracked voice while Oscar built forts out of bread crusts. Three transports meant three thousand men or more, and three thousand men did not anchor off a fishing village by accident.

He logged the sighting at 5:47, wind southwest, visibility improving. His hand was steady. It always was. But he noticed he wrote *transports* and then crossed it out and wrote *vessels*, because *transports* was an admission, and admissions had consequences.

By six o'clock the whole town knew. Word ran up the hill faster than the light ran round the water—Mrs. Trelawney banging on doors, the Cobb boys sprinting barefoot down to the seawall, old Petherick climbing his roof in his nightshirt with a spyglass like a general surveying a field. Lior came down the ninety-one steps and found the lane already full, everyone looking out to sea the way people look at weather they cannot stop.

"They'll want provisions," said Petherick from his rooftop. "Water. Coal. That's all."

Nobody answered him. Everybody thought it.

* * *

They came ashore at nine.

Lior watched from the seawall with Oscar perched on the bollard beside him, and what struck him first was not the rifles or the grey uniforms but the orderliness of it—the boats grounding in sequence, the men forming ranks on the shingle without shouting, the whole operation moving like something already rehearsed. A town being taken the way you take a receipt from a shopkeeper's hand: quickly, politely, without discussion.

"Uncle." Oscar tugged his sleeve. "Are those our soldiers?"

"No."

"Whose, then?"

"The ones who lost where they came from," Lior said, "and are looking for somewhere else to stand."

Oscar considered this with the serious frown he'd inherited from his mother, God keep her, and decided it was satisfactory. "Will they stay long?"

"L longer than we'd like," Lior said, "shorter than we'll remember."

A lieutenant with a red band on his arm walked the length of the seawall hammering papers to posts and doorframes and the noticeboard outside the chandlery, four nails each, efficient as a man hanging curtains. The crowd pressed in to read what most of them could not, so Lior read it aloud, because that was the other thing he was for—useful, unseen, a voice for other people's business.

BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPYING FORCE, it began. ALL INHABITANTS OF ORCHARD'S END SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR ENUMERATION AT THE CUSTOMS HOUSE WITHIN FORTY-EIGHT HOURS. REGISTRATION IS COMPULSORY. RATION CHITS WILL BE ISSUED ONLY TO PERSONS OF RECORD. ANY HOUSEHOLD NOT REGISTERED SHALL BE DEEMED VACANT AND SUBJECT TO REQUISITION.

*Deemed vacant.* Lior read that line twice. It had a way of making a home sound like an empty jug.

"What's enumeration?" Oscar asked.

"It means they're going to write us down."

"Write down what?"

"Our names. Our ages. What we do."

"That's all? That doesn't sound bad."

Lior looked at the proclamation again—at the neat official hand of it, the wax seal, the promise of chits—and felt the prickle start at the back of his neck, small and cold, like the first drop before rain. "No," he said. "I suppose it doesn't."

* * *

The Customs House had not opened its shutters in eleven years, and now it flew a flag. By noon there was a queue along Harbour Street, and by two o'clock it reached the chapel gate, and the people of Orchard's End stood in it in the drizzle with their hats off and their documents in their fists, shuffling forward one pace at a time toward a table where a clerk sat writing.

Lior watched from the doorway of the chandlery, ostensibly buying lamp oil, actually standing very still, which was how he did most things.

It was the quietness that unsettled him. Not one voice raised. Mrs. Trelawney, who had once held off a customs seizure single-handedly with a broom and a vocabulary that made fishermen blush, handed over her papers like a schoolgirl. Petherick climbed down off his dignity and gave his age, his trade, the number of rooms in his house, and thanked the clerk. Thanked him. The whole town filing past that table like sheep through a hurdle, patient and dumb, trading their names for tickets.

*Sign nothing,* his father had told him forty years ago, a fisherman's wisdom about contracts and press-gangs and every paper that ever promised fair dealing. *Do your work, sign nothing, want nothing publicly, and no one can spend you.*

But here was the trouble: not signing was no longer among the choices. The proclamation had closed that door with four nails apiece. No chit, no flour. No flour, no bread. And no record, no house—the word *vacant* doing its quiet work, making absence itself a crime against the requisition.

"You'll register, of course."

Voss was suddenly there—not suddenly really, Lior simply hadn't marked him coming, which he would later think about a great deal—a young officer in a clean coat, hands clasped behind him, studying the queue with the mild interest of a man inspecting livestock.

"I hadn't decided," Lior said.

"It isn't a decision. That's rather the point of it." The officer turned. He was perhaps thirty, with the kind of face that had been handsome and was now merely precise. "Captain Voss. You're the lighthouse keeper."

"I am."

"Ashgrove. Yes, I have your name already." He let that sit a moment. "The Admiralty lists are thorough even if the towns aren't. Ninety-one steps, is it? They say you count everything."

"Somebody has to know the numbers," Lior said, "or nobody knows anything."

Something flickered across Voss's face—approval, possibly, or the recognition of it. "Then you'll appreciate the ledger better than most. A place for every name, every name in its place. Order is mercy, Mr. Ashgrove. People forget that. Chaos is what kills them."

On the table behind them, the clerk called the next family forward. Lior watched old Widow Cade state her name and her age and her occupation—*none*, she said, and the clerk's pen scratched *none*—and he watched the man enter it into a book bound in black cloth, and he could not have said why the sight of that book made the prickle at his neck turn to something colder.

"Chits at the end of the week," Voss said pleasantly. "Don't be last in the queue, keeper. Last is remembered, and remembered is examined." He touched two fingers to his cap, almost courteous, and moved off down the street to where a sergeant was measuring the width of the harbour road with a tape.

*Last is remembered.* Lior turned it over as he carried his oil can home. There were worse things than being remembered. He had spent fifty-one years proving otherwise, building a life the exact size of his duties, wanting nothing publicly, and it had worked—it had kept grief private and hunger honest and Oscar safe—but the word *enumeration* kept turning over in him like a stone in a boot. Because a census was never only names. Somewhere behind that black-clothed book there would be columns, and columns meant sorting, and sorting meant somebody, some captain with a precise face, deciding which names mattered.

Maren was coughing when he got home. The wet rattle underneath, the one she thought he couldn't hear over the fire. She was at the stove waving away steam, pretending to be well, and Oscar was showing her how he'd counted the soldiers' boats—eleven boats, forty men to a boat, he'd done the sum twice—and she laughed, and the laugh caught on the cough, and Lior stood in the doorway with the oil can in his hand and understood, with the plain clarity of a lens brought into focus, that whatever those columns sorted, his family would not sort well.

"We'll go tomorrow," he said. "First thing. Be near the front of the line."

"Why?" Maren asked. "What difference does it make?"

He didn't have an answer he wanted to give a sick woman in her own kitchen. *Last is remembered, and remembered is examined.* "None," he lied. "But I hate queues."

She looked at him a moment too long—she had always been able to tell his lies from his silences, it was the one trick she'd never lost to illness—and then nodded and went back to the stove, and Oscar went on counting soldiers, and outside the window the grey ships rode at anchor in the bay like a verdict waiting to be read aloud.

That night Lior climbed the tower and lit the lamp and opened his logbook, and for the first time in nineteen years of keeping the light he wrote an entry that had nothing to do with the sea:

*Ships in roads, 3, troop transports, anchored 0547. Soldiers ashore approx. 1,200. Town occupied without resistance. Proclamation posted re: enumeration. All residents to be entered in ledger.*

He stopped. Then, beneath it, in the same steady hand:

*Maren coughing worse. Oscar fed and schooled. Both must be registered. Both must be recorded well.*

Recorded *well*. As though ink were a current you could swim with instead of drown in. He read the entry over, struck it through with a single line—routine, safety, the old bargain—and left only the facts, and took the lamp up its stairs, and swept the beam out over the water toward the ships.

It touched them once each time it swung round. This time, he noticed, someone aboard answered: a brief blink of signal-lamp, sharp white, three flashes and a pause, three flashes and a pause. Asking or telling, he couldn't read it, and didn't try.

But he lay awake after, listening to Maren breathe through the wall—each breath arriving a little late, like a tide running out further than it should—and he thought about the black book, and the clerk's pen scratching *none* beside a widow's name, and a young captain saying *order is mercy* in a voice that believed it.

In the morning, the queue formed early. And at its head, beside the flagpole, they had set up a second table—one chair, one inkwell, one sign in that same neat official hand:

*Persons unable to present in person may be registered by proxy. One literate volunteer required.*
