## 3. Names in Ink

Maren had dressed for the queue as if it were church, because that was what it felt like—a judgment day conducted by clerks. Her good grey wool dress, the one with the high collar that hid the hollows above her collarbone, and her mother's garnet brooch at the throat like a small red argument against everything else the morning was saying.

She'd left before Oscar woke, leaving bread and dripping on the table with a note in her careful schoolteacher's hand: *Stay inside until I come back. Mind the stove.* Lior would be at the light—he was always at the light—but she'd told him last night she would do the registering herself. She wanted to see the ledger for what it was before anyone she loved touched it.

The queue outside the customs house ran down Fishergate and bent at the corner like a sentence that wouldn't end. It moved slowly. The soldiers at the door were younger than their coats, most of them, boys with chapped faces and rifles held the way you'd hold a tool you hadn't yet learned to trust. One of them sneezed and no one said anything, but a ripple went down the line anyway, the whole town flinching together.

Maren counted heads to keep her cough down. That was the trick she'd learned these two years: occupy the lungs with something else. Numbers worked better than prayers. She got to two hundred and eleven before the door took her.

Inside, the customs house smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and something sharper underneath—ink, gallons of it. Three long tables had been set up where the fish auction benches used to sit, and behind each table sat an army clerk with a ledger open before him like a mouth waiting to be fed. On the wall, where the portrait of the old harbormaster used to hang, there was now a printed proclamation, still curling at its corners:

*ALL PERSONS RESIDENT WITHIN THE BOUNDARY OF ORCHARD'S END SHALL BE ENTERED UPON THE CENSUS LEDGER AND SHALL RECEIVE RATION CHITS ACCORDING TO ENTRY. NO PERSON NOT SO ENTERED SHALL RECEIVE PROVISIONS, LODGING ALLOCATION, OR PASSAGE.*

*Passage.* Maren read that word twice. Passage to where? The word sat in the proclamation like a bone in bread.

"Name," said the clerk at the middle table without looking up.

"Ashgrove."

He wrote it. She watched his pen move—quick, competent, indifferent—and understood suddenly why they'd chosen clerks for this and not officers. An officer would have looked at her. A clerk only needed the name to exist on paper; the woman attached to it could be dying for all he cared, so long as the ink dried straight.

"Maren Ashgrove. Widow's household—I mean—" She corrected herself before he could look up. "Household of four. Myself, my brother Lior Ashgrove, lighthouse keeper. His son, Oscar Ashgrove, aged ten. And myself."

"You said yourself twice."

"I said myself once and myself once more. It's a household of three." Heat climbed her neck. Her voice had always been her good instrument—clear, patient, made for reading aloud to children—and it was failing her now over a counting error. "Three. Write three."

He wrote three. "Occupation?"

"For me, none. Formerly governess. My brother keeps the light. The boy is schooled at home."

The clerk's pen stopped. He looked up then, briefly, and she saw him actually see her—the thinness the collar couldn't quite hide, the way she stood too carefully, as though her ribs were borrowed. His eyes did the thing she'd seen doctors' eyes do. They measured.

"Cough?" he said.

"No."

He waited. That was the cruelty of it—no threat, just patience, letting the truth walk out on its own.

"A winter cough," she amended. "It clears in spring. Every year it clears in spring."

"Come around the table."

* * *

The examination was not called an examination. It was called a *classification*, and it happened in the side room where smuggled goods used to be weighed. A medical officer with cold hands listened to her chest through a brass instrument and asked her to breathe while he counted under his breath, and Maren thought, absurdly, of all the times she'd counted Oscar's breaths when he was small and feverish, standing guard over the small warm bellows of him, bargaining with God in the currency of numbers.

"Breathe again."

She breathed. Somewhere in the other room a clerk called out a name—Pell, Josiah, fisherman—and another voice answered with a word she didn't catch, and the clerk repeated it back in a flat recitation: *"Tier Two, Tier Two,"* like a bellman announcing arrivals.

"What does the tier mean?" she asked, when the medical officer finally straightened.

"It means nothing to worry about."

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"Lieutenant," he said, mildly, correcting her the way she'd once corrected pupils. "It's a classification of usefulness. Tier One is skilled or essential trades—keepers, smiths, surgeons, engineers. Tier Two is labor fit for garrison work. Tier Three—" He paused, and she hated him for the pause, because pauses were how teachers delivered bad marks, and she knew every kind of pause there was.

"Tier Three is transported," she finished for him. "That's the passage in the proclamation. Laborers for the colonies."

"The Empire requires hands, madam. There are plantations, docks, roads. The climate is difficult but the terms are—"

"How many tiers are there?"

"Three."

"And which am I?"

He didn't say it. He turned back to the ledger on the weighing-table and wrote something, and the scratch of the nib was the loudest sound in the room. But Maren had spent twenty years reading children's faces for the answer they were afraid to give aloud, and she read this man's silence fluently.

Tier Three. The bottom of the page, bound for the bottom of the world.

"My brother keeps your light," she said, and heard her own voice go quiet and level in a way that frightened her slightly, as if someone else had stepped into her throat. "Without him, every ship in your convoy strikes the point in fog. Does the ledger know that?"

"The keeper will be classified separately."

"He'll be Tier One."

"Almost certainly."

"Then hear me." She leaned forward over the weighing-table, over his neat columns, close enough to see the ink shining wet on the page, and lowered her voice to the register she'd used for ten thousand children in trouble: firm, warm, immovable. "Whatever you write beside my name, write nothing beside my nephew's. He is ten years old. He eats like a horse, he learns like fire takes paper, and he has never been ill a day in his life except chickenpox. Whatever arithmetic you're doing on this family, put all of it on my line and none of it on his."

The lieutenant looked at her with something that might have been respect, in another life, in another war.

"Madam, the classification follows the person, not the preference."

"Then follow the person," Maren said. "And leave the boy out of it."

* * *

They gave her ration chits at the door—one blue, one blue, one white. She stood in Fishergate in the grey drizzle and stared at the white one, turning it over. On the back, stamped small and red: *CLASS 3 — TRANSPORT PENDING.*

Transport pending. Four words that weighed more than the ship they'd send her away in.

She should tell Lior. The thought arrived and she set it down immediately, the way you set down something hot. Because she knew exactly what Lior would do. Lior, who had spent fifty-one years being useful and unseen, who polished glass nobody thanked him for polishing and trimmed orchard trees nobody watched him trim—Lior would go to the officers and offer himself. He would trade his Tier One, his essential hands, his irreplaceable post at the light, for her white chit. He would stand in front of some captain and say *take me instead* in that mild reasonable voice of his, and some clerk with a straight pen would let him, because the ledger loves a volunteer, the ledger *feeds* on volunteers.

And then Oscar would lose them both. The boy would go to the colonies alone, or worse—worse, he'd stay here, ward of a garrison, raised on rations and bugle calls, and everything Edith had left him, every story and every seed and every summer, would be administered to him by strangers in a book.

No.

Maren walked home through the occupied town slowly, cataloguing it. Soldiers on the quay, soldiers at the well, a wagon of requisitioned flour under guard outside the bakery. Mrs. Pell weeping quietly near the customs house steps—Tier Three, presumably; Josiah Pell had been called out in there, and now his wife stood wringing her apron like the world's laundry. A corporal was nailing a new notice to the chapel door, and Maren read it without stopping: convoy assembling for New Year's tide, berths assigned by classification, appeals to be submitted in writing.

Appeals in writing. Everything in writing. This army ruled by ink the way older armies had ruled by fire, and the ink was crueler, because fire only burned what it touched and the ledger reached into every house and rearranged the people inside it.

At home, Oscar had ignored her note completely, of course. He met her at the gate with apple cores on his breath and mud to his knees, holding up a jar with a beetle in it as though presenting credentials.

"It's a stag beetle. Look at the jaws. Uncle says they can lift fifty times their own weight."

"Can they lift themselves out of that jar by supper?" Maren said. "Because that's their deadline."

He laughed and fell into step beside her, and she put her hand on the warm rough crown of his head, and the fear that had been sitting in her chest since the weighing-room rose up and changed shape entirely. It wasn't fear for herself anymore. It had been transmuted, in the space of one walk down Fishergate, into something harder and more dangerous: fear wearing the face of purpose. She would be shipped across the world to a plantation if the paper demanded it. Fine. Paper could demand whatever it liked. But her boy—Edith's boy, the last living piece of her sister, the child she'd helped raise through fevers and night terrors and the long grey first year after his mother died—that boy would board no transport, would fill no column in any clerk's arithmetic, not while Maren Ashgrove had a voice and a mind and whatever time was left written into her lungs.

"Are you crying?" Oscar asked, peering up.

"It's the rain."

"It's not raining."

"Then it's the wind." She squeezed his hand. "Go wash. Tell your uncle I'm home safe and the registration's done. Tell him it was simple. Tell him it was nothing."

Oscar ran ahead with his jar, and Maren stood a moment in the gate of the house where her sister had died, looking down the hill toward the lighthouse, white and steady against the grey sea, keeping its faithful pointless watch.

*Simple,* she thought. *Nothing.* She was already lying to them, and the lies were easier than breathing, and breathing itself was becoming the hard part.

In the kitchen she took the white chit from her pocket, and instead of laying it on the table where Lior might find it, she hid it inside her sewing box, beneath the spools, beneath the dress pattern she'd been cutting for Oscar before the ships came—his winter coat, half-finished, sleeves pinned and waiting.

Then she sat down, took out her good pen and her best ink, and began, in the steadiest hand she owned, to draft an appeal.
