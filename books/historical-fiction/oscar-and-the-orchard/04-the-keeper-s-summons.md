## 4. The Keeper's Summons

They came for him on the ninth day of the occupation, when the frost had learned to live indoors.

Lior heard the boots on the gravel path before he saw them—two pairs, unhurried, the walk of men who had nowhere to be refused. He was in the lamp room with a cloth and a jar of whiting, polishing the great lens the way his father had polished it, and his father before him, each facet wiped in the same order, seaward first, always seaward, because that was the face the light showed to the world. He did not stop working when the door below banged open. Stopping would have meant acknowledging them, and acknowledging men like these was how you became interesting to them.

"Keeper Ashgrove." Captain Voss's voice carried up the stairwell without effort, the way certain voices do—flat, precise, a voice that had never needed to shout because shouting was for men who could not be believed. "Come down. Bring your coat."

Lior brought his coat. It was the sensible thing to do, and he had built his whole life out of sensible things: the lens polished, the wicks trimmed, Maren's cough syrup measured to the drop, Oscar fed and schooled and lied to gently about the ships in the bay. Do your work. Sign nothing. Want nothing publicly. His wife Elowen used to tease him about it—"You move through rooms like weather, Lior. Everyone feels you, nobody sees you"—and she'd meant it kindly, mostly. He had thought it a kind of armor. He would think so for another hour yet.

Outside, the morning was the color of dishwater. Three grey transports still sat at anchor where they'd been for over a week now, their funnels breathing thin smoke into a sky that couldn't decide between snow and rain. The soldiers had taken the customs house, the schoolhouse, the two best rooms of the Anchor Inn, and—this still made something twist in Lior's chest—the orchard, Elowen's orchard, where sentries now walked the rows between the bare apple trees as if fruit might grow in December if guarded well enough.

The younger soldier said nothing at all. Voss fell into step beside him, hands clasped behind his back, and together they went down the cliff road toward the town like two men out for constitutional exercise.

"You have good handwriting," Voss said.

It was such an absurd sentence that Lior almost laughed. "I keep the station log."

"I've read it." A pause long enough to mean something. "Eleven years. Not one correction, not one blot, not one entry out of sequence. Your columns align like masonry, Keeper." Voss looked at him sidelong, and there was something in it that was not admiration exactly—it was closer to what a carpenter feels examining a length of timber. "You are the steadiest hand in this town. I checked."

*He checked.* The words settled somewhere cold under Lior's ribs. He had spent forty-one years arranging himself to be overlooked, and here was proof that invisibility, done thoroughly enough, was simply another way of being watched.

"The quartermaster requires a fair copy of the census ledger," Voss went on. "The original is—well. Originals suffer. Salt air, damp, careless handling. The New Year convoy will sail on documents, and documents must be exact." They passed the baker's, shuttered; passed old Tam Prynne shoveling his step too slowly and being barked at by a private half his age. "You'll work in the requisitioned schoolhouse. Eight hours a day. Rations doubled for your household."

"I didn't ask for rations."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "That's what makes you useful."

* * *

The schoolhouse smelled of chalk and cold iron. Someone had cleared the children's desks into a corner and installed in their place a long table, a green-shaded lamp, an inkwell of army-black, and a ledger so thick it took both of Voss's men to carry it in—a ledger bound in grey board, its pages ruled in columns finer than any schoolbook's, designed, Lior thought later, by someone who understood that a page can be a cage if you draw the bars close enough.

Voss set the original beside the blank copy. "Transcribe every name, every classification, every annotation. Exactly. I will compare them myself."

"And then?"

"Then you go home to your lighthouse, Keeper, and you are nobody again. I give you my word." Voss said *my word* the way other men said *cash in hand*, and turned to go, then paused at the door with his hand on the frame. "A word of advice. Don't read what you copy. Clerks who read begin to think, and clerks who think make errors." The door shut behind him. The latch was broken; it didn't quite catch, and hung a finger's width open all day, breathing hallway cold into the room, as if even the building wanted to keep an eye on him.

Lior dipped the pen. He told himself it was simple work, honest work, the kind he knew: lines, and numbers, and names.

The first name was Tam Prynne. *Prynne, Tamas. Cooper. Class II. Fit.* Then his wife, then his sons. Lior copied them in black ink, each letter placed with the care he gave the log, and felt nothing—or believed he felt nothing, which is nearly the same trick, and he had been practicing it all his life.

By mid-morning he had filled four pages. By noon he understood why Voss had advised him not to read.

There were three classes. That much he'd guessed from the talk at the chit line. But the annotations—the small marginal marks in Voss's own hand, a different ink, a colder hand—those were new. Next to certain Class III names ran a single letter: *T*. Beside others: *C*. And beside some, no mark at all but a faint horizontal stroke, like a man drawing a line through something he hadn't yet decided to erase.

Lior copied a *T*. Copied a *C*. Told himself they were abbreviations, trade codes, anything. Then he reached the Hollis family—Nathan the fisherman, his wife Bess, their girl with the crooked spine—and found *Hollis, N., Class III. T. Berth 41, Transport *Corvus*.* And beneath it, in smaller script: *berth confirmed against debt.*

Debt. Nathan Hollis owed money to no one Lior knew. Unless the army itself was the creditor, unless the chits they'd been paid in were themselves the trap—rations advanced, passage owed, freedom mortgaged before it was lost. Lior sat back. The green lamp hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a soldier laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the ordinariness of it made it worse.

He kept copying. That was the horror of it, he would remember afterward—he kept copying, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping was a thing he did not know how to do. Do your work. The work was the hiding place. The work had always been the hiding place.

Page after page. Names he'd known since boyhood rendered in his own beautiful, blameless hand. Widow Curnow, Class III, *T*. The Ferrier brothers, Class III, one marked *T*, one unmarked—and Lior stared a long time at that asymmetry, at the arithmetic of a family split across a column like a fence through a field. He was copying people into boxes, and the boxes had doors on only one side.

Near dusk his pen found the page that held his own household, transcribed from Maren's registration nine days ago. *Ashgrove, Lior. Keeper of Light. Class II. Essential—retained.* Of course. A lighthouse was infrastructure; the army needed the harbor lit for the convoy. He had been classified the way a jetty or a water pump is classified. Useful. Retained. It should have been comforting. It read instead like a description of furniture.

Then: *Ashgrove, Maren. Class III. Medical annotation: pulmonary. T.* And beneath it, in Voss's colder hand: *transport value low. Review.*

And: *Ashgrove, Oscar. Age 10. Dependent of Class III. T.*

His sister. His nephew. Marked with the same letter as Nathan Hollis, as widow Curnow—as the people the convoy would carry away in January to labor in colonies whose names the ledger didn't bother to record, because to the ledger they were not destinations but disposals.

Lior set down the pen. His hand, the steadiest hand in Orchard's End, was shaking, and he watched it shake with a strange detachment, as though it belonged to some other, more honest man.

He began, without deciding to, to count. It was the keeper in him—years of counting oil, wicks, rotations, seconds between flashes. The convoy list ran to berth 214. He tallied the assigned berths on his fingers, twice, three times, the way he checked a light calculation he already trusted. Two hundred thirteen assigned.

One berth remaining. Unassigned. A blank space at the bottom of the final page, ruled and waiting, the size of a name.

One berth for a town full of *T*s. One berth, and two Ashgroves marked for transport, and review pending on a sick woman's "value." Lior looked at that empty ruled box until the lamp guttered and the cold came up through the floorboards like a tide, and he understood at last what Voss had bought with doubled rations—not his handwriting. His silence. Because a clerk who copies everything sees everything, and a man who sees everything can be managed, or can manage.

Invisibility, he thought, gathering his coat in the darkening schoolroom, had been a story he told himself. There was no such thing. There was only being unseen by those who loved you while being perfectly visible to those who owned the paper.

He walked home along the cliff with the wind off the water and the three grey ships riding black against the last light. In the kitchen Maren had soup on, and Oscar was doing sums by lamplight, tongue between his teeth, and they looked up when he came in—looked straight at him, warm, unguarded, seeing him—and Lior realized he could not remember the last time anyone truly had.

"Maren," he said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped. "When you registered. What exactly did the officer write?"

She went very still, ladle in hand, and in that stillness—quick as a bird, gone in a breath—he saw something flicker that he recognized from his own mirror that morning.

"Soup's getting cold," she said.

Behind her, on the shelf, sat her coat, and folded in the pocket where she thought no one ever looked was a paper she had not shown him.
