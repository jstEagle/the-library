## 5. What the Orchard Remembers

The soldiers had put a fence around the orchard.

Not a good fence. Not the kind Uncle Lior would have built, with posts set deep and rails planed smooth. This was a fence of stakes and rope, the kind you'd use for sheep, or for things that couldn't be trusted not to wander off. Oscar stood at the gap in the sea-wall where the path met the orchard gate and looked at it for a long time, the way you look at something that has changed overnight in a dream.

There was a sign on the gate too, painted on a board by someone in a hurry: *RESTRICTED — MILITARY STORES*. The letters dripped at the bottom, as if the paint had been crying.

Nobody was guarding it. That was the strange part. Two soldiers walked the harbor road with rifles slung backward like schoolbags, and a third was asleep against the well-house wall with his cap over his eyes, but nobody stood at the orchard gate, because who would steal apples in December? Who would want anything from a field of bare trees?

Oscar wanted everything from a field of bare trees. It was his mother's orchard before it was anyone's, and then it was Uncle Lior's, and now it was the army's, which meant it was nobody's, which meant—by the strange arithmetic of occupation—it might as well still be his.

He slipped through the gap where the rope sagged. He'd practiced the move in his head all the way down the cliff path, one hand on the stake, one foot through, quiet as smoke. Maren said he was getting tall. Tall boys could do things small boys couldn't, like reach the second rail of a fence and slide under rope without leaving a mark on it.

Inside, the orchard smelled different. That was the first thing he noticed, before he even saw them. It used to smell of wet grass and rotting windfalls and the particular sweetness of the old Bramley by the wall, the one his mother had called Grandmother Tree because it was older than everyone's grandmother put together. Now it smelled of woodsmoke and leather and something oily, like the lamps they lit on the ships.

They had made a camp of it. Between the pear rows there were tents the color of pond water, and crates stacked into walls, and a cook-fire burning in a ring of stones that had been stolen, Oscar realized with a jolt, from the cairn on the headland—the cairn he and Uncle Lior had added a stone to every year on his birthday. Eleven years, eleven stones. Somebody's fire was burning his birthday stones.

A man sat alone near the fire, apart from the others, mending a boot with a curved needle. He wasn't wearing his uniform properly. His tunic was buttoned wrong, one hole off, so the whole front pulled sideways like a face looking askance. His rifle leaned against a crate three arm-lengths away, which Oscar knew—from a book about soldiers, which was nearly the same as knowing—was a very long way for a soldier to sit from his rifle.

"Oi," the man said, without looking up. "You're the little ghost."

"I'm not a ghost."

"Ghost of the orchard." He looked up then. He was young, younger than Uncle Lior by a lot, with sandy hair cut short and a healing split in his lip. "Been seeing you at the wall for three days. Thought I'd invented you."

"You're sitting in my mother's orchard."

The man considered this. He turned the boot over in his hands. "Is she about?"

"She's dead."

Something moved across the man's face, quick as a fish under ice. "Then I expect she won't mind," he said quietly. "Or maybe she will. Maybe dead people mind more than anyone. Nobody ever asks them."

That was such an odd thing to say that Oscar forgot to be angry. He came closer, staying just out of arm's reach, because Maren said never let a stranger get hold of your sleeve. "What's your name?"

"Hale. Private Hale, except—" He stopped himself, smiled with the split lip, and started again. "Hale. Just Hale is fine."

"Just Hale."

"That's me." He patted the ground beside him. Oscar didn't sit. He stood with his hands in his pockets and studied the camp instead: the crates stenciled with words he couldn't read yet, the tent flaps tied back, the sleeping man with the cap over his eyes, the way nothing here was arranged the way soldiers arranged things in books—in straight lines, everything squared away. Everything here was slightly crooked. The tents were crooked. The sentry-walk was crooked. Even the flag on its pole drooped like a wet sock.

Nothing here had been squared away since the day they arrived.

"You're not real soldiers," Oscar said.

Hale's needle stopped mid-stitch. "Careful, little ghost."

"The books say soldiers make everything straight. You're all crooked." He pointed at the rifle, far away. "And your gun's too far. Real soldiers keep it close."

For a long moment Hale said nothing. Then he laughed—not loudly, but really, from somewhere deep, clapping a hand over his mouth because of the sleeping man. When he took the hand away his eyes were wet at the corners, and Oscar understood suddenly that the laughing was a kind of crying that had found a better way out.

"Straight lines," Hale said, wiping his eyes. "God, yes. Straight lines and bugles and men shouting *left, left* until your feet forget they belong to you." He held up the boot. "I'm making everything crooked, see. One stitch at a time. It's the only rebellion I can afford."

"What's a rebellion?"

"It's when you say no." Hale threaded the needle again. "Very expensive word. Costs different amounts depending on who says it. For a boy it costs a hiding. For a private it costs—" He glanced toward the harbor road, where the grey ships sat at anchor like closed fists. "It can cost everything."

* * *

He came back the next day, and the next. It was easy; the sentries rotated at odd hours and none of them ever looked at the orchard, because the orchard was beneath notice, and Oscar had learned that being beneath notice was a kind of cloak. Uncle Lior wore the same cloak every day of his life—did his work, signed nothing, wanted nothing where anyone could see him wanting—and Oscar had inherited it without being taught, the way you inherit your mother's color of hair.

Hale taught him things. How to fold a letter so it fits in a boot heel. Which stars were which, though Oscar already knew those from the lighthouse gallery. How to whistle through cupped hands so it carried across water. In exchange Oscar told him about the orchard—which tree gave early, which gave late, which one his mother had planted the year he was born so they'd grow up together, both of them crooked at first and then straightening out.

"That one," Hale said, looking at the young apple by the south wall. "She planted it for you?"

"For both of us."

"Good answer." Hale ran his thumb along the sapling's bark, gentle, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking. "My mum had roses. Killed half of them every year pruning wrong, and every spring she'd swear she'd learn, and every winter she'd do it again." He smiled at the memory and then the smile went out like a pinched wick. "I wrote her I was somewhere safe."

"Are you?"

"No," said Hale. "But it's true I'm somewhere."

* * *

On the fourth day, Oscar came earlier than usual, before the light was properly up, because Maren had slept late and he'd fed himself bread and dripping and let himself out the door like smoke. The fog lay over the orchard in ropes. And voices carried in fog—that was another thing he knew without knowing how he knew, the way lighthouse children know weather in their teeth.

Two men stood by the cook-fire, and neither of them was Hale. They wore better coats than the others, longer, with red tabs at the collar, and one of them was Captain Voss, whom Oscar had seen once from the sea-wall, posting the proclamation with its wax seal like a scab.

"—the convoy sails on the first, no later," Voss was saying. His voice was smooth and cold, like water over stone. "The transport manifest goes aboard sealed. I will not have irregularities."

"There are already irregularities, sir." The other man had a whiny voice, a clerk's voice. "Three names entered after the original census. All ranked essential. All with—forgive me—remarkably similar handwriting."

"A clerical error."

"Three times, sir?"

"Captain Ferro." Voss's voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Every army runs on paper. Paper is how we feed ten thousand men, move ten thousand men, tell ten thousand men where to die. And paper—" he paused, and Oscar, crouched behind the Grandmother Tree with his heart going like a gull in a storm, heard something in the pause that he had no name for yet but would carry all his life, "—paper is also how certain men find their way onto a ship bound for safety instead of a trench bound for nowhere. Do you understand me? The ledger is not a list. The ledger is a price list. Everything on it has a buyer."

"And the berths, sir? The labor transports? There's talk in the town—"

"There's talk everywhere. Talk is free. Berths are not." A match scraped; tobacco smell drifted through the fog. "The last berth on the *Meridian* hasn't been assigned. It won't be assigned by any census. It'll be assigned the way everything worth having gets assigned. Someone will pay for it. Someone always pays."

"Pay with what, sir?"

"With whatever they have." Voss exhaled, and the smoke curled up through the bare branches and was gone. "Coin. Silence. A daughter's hand. A kept mouth. Everyone owns something, Ferro. My job is to run the inventory."

Their boots crunched away toward the harbor road. Oscar stayed crouched behind the tree for a long time after, long enough for his knees to ache and the fog to start lifting in rags. He thought about the ledger—he'd seen it once through the customs house window, great brown book, columns of names in beautiful straight handwriting, the steadiest hand in town, Maren said, that's your uncle's work, the army pays him to copy it because his letters never tremble.

Uncle Lior wrote the names. The captain sold the berths.

Oscar didn't understand all of it. He was ten. But he understood that the orchard where his mother grew up and planted a tree for him had become a place where people were bought, and that the man who whistled through cupped hands and folded letters into boot heels was one of the things being hidden, the way he himself was hidden behind the Grandmother Tree, the whole town hidden behind its ordinary mornings.

Hale appeared at the tent flap, saw him, and crossed the wet grass quickly. "Little ghost. You're white as the fog. What is it?"

"They were talking about selling," Oscar whispered. "Selling places on the ships. And my uncle writes the book, Hale. My uncle writes the book they sell out of."

Hale went very still. Over his shoulder, the fog was thinning, and through it Oscar could see the grey ships at anchor, and beyond them the white finger of the lighthouse where his uncle stood every night keeping the light for ships that never said thank you.

"Listen to me," Hale said quietly, gripping Oscar's shoulders, his split lip pressed white. "Whatever you heard—you take it home, and you don't say a word. Not to your sister. Not even to your uncle. Especially not to your uncle. Can you do that?"

"Why especially not my uncle?"

But Hale was already looking past him, toward the harbor road, where a figure in a long coat with red tabs had stopped at the orchard gate and was standing perfectly still, facing in, as if the fog itself had been counting heads and had found one too many.
