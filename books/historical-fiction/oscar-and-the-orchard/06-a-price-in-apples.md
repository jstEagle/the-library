## 6. A Price in Apples

The knock came at half past nine, when the lamp was lit and the lens was turning and Lior had just sat down to a supper of boiled potatoes that would go cold before he touched them.

He opened the door to Captain Voss alone — no escort, no rifle, no proclamation. Just the officer's grey coat buttoned to the throat and his breath steaming in the cold, and behind him the night so black it seemed the sea had swallowed the sky whole.

"Keeper Ashgrove." Voss did not wait to be invited. He stepped past Lior into the small kitchen, his boots leaving wet crescents on the flagstones, and stopped at the table where the potatoes sat in their dented tin. "You keep late hours."

"The light keeps its own hours. I only follow it."

"Yes." Voss turned. His eyes went around the room the way a customs man's hands go through luggage — unhurried, thorough, missing nothing. The mended chair. Maren's shawl over the second seat, still warm from her. Oscar's slate propped against the wall with its chalk sums half-erased. "You have a gift for following things. It's why your hand is the steadiest I've ever employed."

"You didn't come here at this hour to compliment my penmanship."

Voss smiled. It was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a man who has found the exact weight of a lever and knows where to set it. "No. I came because one of my soldiers is missing. Private Hale. Fourth company. He signed himself onto the garrison rolls in November, and tonight he is not in his billet, and the sergeant who covered for him has confessed everything under very little persuasion." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "The interesting thing, Keeper, is what the sergeant confessed *to*. Because it seems certain entries in my ledger were falsified. Names written in that were never registered. Deserters given clean paper and shipped out as labourers before anyone thought to count noses twice."

Lior kept his face still. Two years of widowhood will teach a man stillness if nothing else will.

"That's an army matter," he said. "I only copy what I'm given."

"You copy beautifully what you're given." Voss took a step closer. Outside, far below, the sea worked at the rocks with its patient, indifferent hands. "And you copy nothing else. You say nothing else. You are the most silent man in Orchard's End, Ashgrove — I've watched you. You walk through the occupied town like a ghost through a wedding. Nobody sees you. You like it that way." He tilted his head. "But ghosts see *everything*, don't they? And you've been sitting in that clerk's chair every afternoon with your eyes open. So I'm going to ask you plainly, once: have you seen anything in that ledger worth reporting?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs the clockwork of the lens turned over, steady as a heartbeat.

"No," Lior said.

It cost him something to say it. He felt the word leave him like a coin paid out of a nearly empty purse.

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, as if Lior had passed some examination whose results had never been in doubt. "Good," he said softly. "That was the correct answer. Because here is what happens next, Keeper, and I want you to hear all of it before you speak again. Hale will be found by morning. He always is — there is nowhere on this coast for a man to go in December. When he is found, he'll stand against a wall at dawn, and the garrison will watch, and the example will be made. That is the ordinary arithmetic of armies." He let that settle. "*Unless* the ledger's irregularities are never examined. Unless the clerk who copied every page swears they were clean from the first line to the last. A quiet word from you, and no audit ever happens, and no firing squad ever forms, and Private Hale sails away with the New Year convoy under whatever false name we've given him."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" Voss drew off one glove, finger by finger, taking his time, "—you name the holder of the last berth. The unassigned one. The one that's been sitting empty since the December revision while I've waited to see which way you'd jump." He smiled again, thinner now. "Name whoever you like, Ashgrove. Your sister. Your nephew. Anyone. One name, spoken honestly, and both our problems disappear. You keep your silence, I keep my secret, and a boy or a woman boards a ship instead of standing on this quay watching everyone else sail."

Lior heard his own breathing. He heard the lens turning overhead, that faint mechanical whisper, sweeping its light across water full of nothing.

"You're asking me to choose," he said.

"I'm asking you to stop pretending you haven't already chosen every day since the requisition began. Every man who registers, every man who queues, every man who takes his ration chit and thanks me politely — they've all chosen. They've chosen to survive. You're only being asked to choose *out loud*." Voss pulled his glove back on. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "I'll want the name by tomorrow evening. Write it on the chit and bring it to me yourself. And Ashgrove—" now he did look back, and his eyes were not cruel, which was somehow worse, "—don't imagine that not choosing spares either of them. An unassigned berth isn't mercy. It's just a vacancy waiting for whoever bids highest. I've had three offers this week alone."

Then he was gone, and the door closed, and the cold came in after him like a thing invited.

* * *

Maren was asleep. Oscar was asleep. Lior put on his coat and went down into the orchard.

The trees stood bare and black against the last grey of the sky, rows of them marching down the slope toward the sea wall, each one planted by Elowen's hands in the years when her lungs were still strong enough for digging. He walked the rows without a lantern. He knew this ground the way he knew the inside of his own eyelids.

*Love doesn't divide,* she had told him once, here, under the plum tree that no longer bore. It was their third summer. Oscar wasn't even a rumour yet. She'd been arguing — laughing-arguing — that he loved her more than he loved the light, and he'd said something careful and accountant-like about how a heart only held so much, and she'd thrown an unripe apple at his chest. *You great fool. Love isn't bread, Lior. It doesn't get smaller when you cut it. It multiplies. Give it away and you have more, not less.* She'd been so certain. She'd been certain about everything except the cough that came later, quiet as fog, and took her in nineteen months.

He stood under the plum tree now and tried to make the arithmetic work.

One berth. Two names.

If he wrote **Oscar** — and God help him, the pen was already moving in his mind, the ink already flowing — then the boy sailed on the first of January, ten years old, alone, to a colony he could not find on a map, among strangers who would feed him and school him and never once tell him why his uncle hadn't come. Oscar, who still left a chalk mark on the lighthouse door every night so the light would know someone was home. Oscar, who slept with his boots by the bed in case the ships came back for him too. He would live. That was the argument for it, the whole iron argument: *he would live*. Maren would not. Her lungs were failing; everyone in town knew it though nobody said it; the transport's hold would be a coffin with a porthole. Writing Oscar's name meant Maren died here, in her own bed, in her own town, warm, tended, forgiven — and Lior would spend the rest of his life being the man who chose the child and buried the sister, and every apple he ever ate afterward would taste of it.

If he wrote **Maren** —

But that was not writing a name. That was signing a death warrant in a kinder hand. She would board the ship feverish, and the sea would finish what winter started, and Oscar would stay behind with an uncle who had traded his aunt's life for his own and would never, not once, be able to look the boy in the face without both of them knowing. What kind of inheritance was that? *Your aunt died so you could keep my surname.*

And if he wrote neither — if he tore up the chit, if he told Voss to give the berth to whoever bid highest, if he retreated into his old doctrine, *do your work, sign nothing, want nothing publicly* —

But that doctrine was dead. He understood that now, standing in the dark among his wife's trees. Invisibility had been a luxury of peacetime, a superstition, the belief that a man could stand beside the fire and not be burned. The ledger had ended all that. There was no longer any such thing as standing outside the arithmetic. Every soul in Orchard's End had been entered into ink, ranked, weighed, priced — and the only question left was whether Lior Ashgrove would be a number in someone else's column or a hand holding the pen.

He walked the rows until his feet were numb. Frost silvered the grass between the trees, and above the headland the lighthouse beam swung round and round, faithful, stupid, illuminating nothing but the same old sea.

Near midnight he found himself at the tree Elowen had called the stubborn one — the pear that fruited every other year, that froze in spring frosts and rallied anyway. She used to say it was the only tree in the orchard with principles. He laid his palm flat against its bark, rough and cold as a wharf post.

"Tell me what to do," he said aloud, and hated how small his voice sounded. "You always knew. Multiply, you said. But it doesn't multiply tonight, Elowen. Tonight it divides, and whichever half I keep, I bury the other."

The tree said nothing. Trees are honest that way.

What he kept returning to — the thought that circled like the beam overhead — was Voss's parting words. *Three offers this week alone.* Berths sold. Names bought. Somewhere in that ledger, in his own copied hand, men had purchased passages out of other men's sentences, and the whole rotten market ran on exactly one fuel: that decent people would rather buy their own salvation than burn the shop down.

He climbed back up to the cottage as the sky went from black to the colour of dishwater. Maren was awake — he could tell by the lamplight under her door and the suppressed rhythm of her cough, that terrible discipline she'd taught herself, smothering each fit in the pillow so as not to wake anyone, as if her illness were a discourtesy. He stood in the corridor with his hand raised to knock, and did not knock.

Because he saw it then, suddenly, horribly clearly: whatever he decided, he could not decide it *for* her. She had spent two years hiding the depth of her sickness from him precisely so that he would never be forced into a room like this one. If he wrote a name in secret, he stole the choice from her twice — once from her life, and once from her dignity.

The chit lay on the kitchen table where he'd left it, blank, white as a held breath. Beside it, his pen. Beside the pen, an apple from the cellar stores, the last of Elowen's harvest, going soft at the stem.

Lior picked up the apple instead of the pen. He turned it over once in his hands, feeling its small dense weight — a year of sun folded into something that fit in a palm — and set it down in front of the door to Maren's room, where she would find it in the morning and know, without being told, that her brother had spent the whole night walking among her trees and choosing nothing.

Then he blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark until the convoy's anchor lights came up in the bay, one, two, three, strung along the black water like a sentence waiting for its final word.

Tomorrow evening, Voss wanted a name.

Tonight, someone was coughing behind a thin wooden door, and Lior Ashgrove rose from the dark and went to sit outside it, listening, until he heard — beneath the coughing — footsteps on the stairs that were too light for Maren, and too quick for a sleeping boy.

Oscar's door was open. Oscar's bed was empty.
