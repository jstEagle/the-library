## 7. Maren's Bargain

Maren knew her brother was dying of something long before she understood what it was.

Not the cough—that was hers, a wet rattle that had taken up residence in her chest sometime in November and refused to pay rent. No, what she watched now across the kitchen table was a different sickness altogether. Lior sat with his hands flat on the wood, staring at nothing, and he had been staring at nothing for three days. He ate because she put food in front of him. He slept—or lay down—in the narrow room off the kitchen, and she could hear him turning over and over like bread dough that wouldn't rise.

He had stopped polishing the lens.

That was how she knew. In twenty-two years of knowing Lior Ashgrove, through their parents' deaths and her widowhood-that-never-was and the burial of Elise under the plum tree, he had never once missed the lens. The light came first, before food, before grief, before sense. Now the lamp room stood dark for whole minutes at dusk while he stood in the yard looking at the orchard as if it had spoken to him.

"You're going to wear a hole in the floor," she said.

Lior blinked, surfacing. "The glass needs cleaning."

"The glass has been clean since Tuesday." She pushed the bowl of barley soup toward him. It had gone cold an hour ago; she'd reheated it twice. "Sit. Eat."

He sat. He did not eat. Ten-year-old Oscar, sprawled on the settle by the fire with a book of ship illustrations he'd been given by a soldier, watched them both with the particular stillness children develop when the weather inside a house turns bad.

"Uncle," Oscar said carefully, "do ships really have names like people do?"

"They have names," Lior said. "It doesn't save them."

Oscar looked down at his book. Maren looked at her brother, and the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all week forced itself upon her.

She had seen the ledger. Not the army's copy—she'd never get near that—but Lior's transcription, left open on this very table one night when he thought everyone asleep. She had read only a moment before shame closed her throat: columns of names, columns of trades, columns of numbers that were not numbers but verdicts. *Usefulness.* The word the young officers used in the street, casual as weather. And near the bottom of the page, in her own name, the entry she had made herself at the schoolhouse two weeks ago:

*Ashgrove, Maren. Widow's sister. Occupation: none. Health: —*

She had coughed during her registration. She remembered the clerk's pen pausing, the small dry sound it made, and then moving on. A pause was all it took. A pause was a door closing.

And there was only one berth left. Lior had said nothing about berths, but Maren had grown up in this town, and towns talk even under occupation. One transport before New Year. One seat on a grey ship to somewhere hot and far away, where laborers were wanted and widows' sisters with bad lungs were apparently counted among them.

Her brother was trying to choose between her son and herself.

She saw it now in everything—the cold soup, the unwashed glass, the way he flinched when Oscar laughed. He was carrying the choice alone because carrying things alone was the only prayer he knew.

Well. She had buried a husband and raised a boy on half rations and stubbornness. She would not be carried like luggage.

* * *

Captain Voss received petitions in the requisitioned customs house between nine and eleven each morning, and Maren joined the queue at half past eight with her shawl pulled tight and a lie prepared in advance.

The line moved slowly. Farmers, fishermen, a woman from the chandlery who wept quietly and continuously without drawing attention to herself. When Maren reached the desk, Voss looked up with the weary courtesy of a man who had heard every kind of begging and expected hers to be no different.

"Name?"

"Maren Ashgrove. I'm the lighthouse keeper's sister."

Something flickered behind his eyes—recognition, perhaps, or calculation. "You're listed for transport," he said. Not cruelly. As a matter of inventory.

"I've come to confirm it." Her voice held steadier than she felt. "I want to go. Voluntarily. Today, if the paperwork allows."

Voss set down his pen. "Voluntary transport is uncommon."

"My lungs are bad. I'm told the climate south is drier." The lie came out smooth, which surprised her; she had never been much of a liar until this winter taught her that honesty was a luxury good. "I have a condition."

"Everyone has conditions."

"Not this one." She leaned forward. "My nephew. Oscar Ashgrove, age ten. He goes in my place—my berth transfers to him—and my brother keeps the light and bothers no one. You write it however you need to write it. I'll sign anything."

For a long moment the captain studied her the way Lior studied weather coming off the water. Then he opened the ledger, ran one finger down a column, and stopped.

"There are complications," he said. "Berths aren't assigned by sentiment, Mrs.—"

"Ashgrove."

"—Ashgrove. They're assigned by the convoy manifest. Which is—" a fractional hesitation, so slight anyone else would have missed it, "—under review."

Maren did not miss it. Grief had sharpened her senses to the texture of other people's evasions; she'd had two years of practice reading doctors who softened news into wool. The captain was hiding something in that ledger. Something with ink still wet on it.

"I don't care about your review," she said. "I care about a boy who thinks his uncle can fix anything. Someone has to be the adult here. Apparently it's me."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. "Return tomorrow. If the manifest permits, I'll consider your petition."

"That's not yes."

"It's not no."

In this town, this winter, that was the best currency on offer. Maren took it and walked out into the grey morning, her chest burning, her heart strangely light—the lightness of a woman who has finally put down something heavy, not noticing yet the weight of what she has picked up in its place.

* * *

Lior was waiting for her at the corner of Harbour Street, and she knew at once that it was over—not the petition, but the peace.

He had the look of a man who had run. Actually run, his keeper's coat unbuttoned, his breath ragged in the cold. Behind him, at the far end of the street, Oscar stood outside the baker's holding a paper bag of rolls, watching them with wide eyes, too far off to hear but close enough to see that something was wrong.

"You went to him," Lior said.

"I went to the customs house. There's a difference."

"Don't." His voice cracked on the word. "Don't do this, Maren."

"Do what? Say it plainly, Lior. Say what you've been doing every night this week while you let the light go dark and starve yourself over a ledger." She stepped closer, lowering her voice though the street was empty enough. "Say that you were choosing. Between me and your nephew. Go on."

His jaw worked. "It wasn't—it isn't like that."

"It's exactly like that. You've been sitting in that kitchen playing God with two lives, weighing us like—" her breath caught, the cough rising; she crushed it down, furious at her own body for interrupting her one great speech. "Like apples at market. This one's bruised, throw it out. Keep the good one."

"That's not—" He pressed his knuckles against his mouth. "You don't know what he offered me."

"I know exactly what he offered you, because I just offered him the same thing, only I didn't take three days and a sleepless week about it." Her voice rose despite herself. "One berth, Lior. One. And you sat in our kitchen doing sums with our lives instead of telling me. Did you think I wouldn't see? Did you think I was too sick to notice my own brother drowning next to me?"

"You're sick," he said, raw. "That's the whole point. That's why it can't be you."

"Why? Because I'm damaged goods? Because the ledger says?" She laughed, and it came out bitter as brine. "You've been copying their book so long you've started believing it. Their columns. Their arithmetic. A person is not a column, Lior."

"And a boy is not a motherless orphan sent across the ocean alone!" Now he was shouting, and heads turned at the end of the street, and Oscar had started walking toward them, quick, frightened steps. Lior mastered himself with visible effort, dragging his voice down. "He's ten years old, Maren. He's ten. You cannot put a child on a convict ship because your pride won't accept help."

"My pride?" She felt the words land somewhere deep and true and terrible. "My pride kept that boy fed through two winters after his father died. My pride is the only reason he knows what love looks like on a bad day. Don't you dare call it pride."

They stood chest to chest in the middle of Harbour Street, brother and sister, two people shaped from the same stubborn stock, and around them the occupied town went on its careful way, and the grey ships rode at anchor in the bay, and somewhere in the customs house a ledger lay open with all their futures ranked in a steady hand.

"You'd die on that ship," Lior said quietly. "Or soon after. You know it. I know it. And Oscar would learn what your sacrifice bought him standing at a rail in a country that doesn't know his name."

"And you'd stay here keeping a light for a town that sold us," she returned, "growing old with a grave and an orchard and a guilt you'll polish every night like glass. At least mine buys something. Yours buys nothing but company for the dead."

The blow landed. She saw it land. And worse—she saw, in the flinch of his face, that part of him agreed.

"Uncle? Mum?" Oscar had reached them, holding out the paper bag like an offering. Rolls, still warm. "Is—is everything all right?"

Neither of them answered fast enough. The silence answered for them.

Oscar looked from his uncle to his mother and back, and Maren watched her son—her bright, sharp-eyed son—understand, in the way children understand catastrophes, that whatever was broken was about him. His chin began to tremble. He hid it by hugging the bag of rolls tighter.

Lior crouched down to the boy's height, and when he spoke his voice was gentle again, unbearably gentle. "Nothing's wrong, Oscar. Your mum and I were just discussing the orchard."

"Liar," Oscar said softly.

And then he turned and ran—not toward home, but toward the water, toward the occupied orchard below the headland where he'd been spending his afternoons, and neither of them was quick enough to stop him.

Maren and Lior stood in the street, the argument still hanging unfinished between them, both watching the small figure shrink down the hill.

"The storm's coming early," Lior said at last, eyes on the bruised horizon. "Voss told me. The convoy sails before it hits. Tomorrow night."

"Then whatever we're going to do," Maren said, "we do it today."

Above the harbour, the first wind off the sea bent the bare apple trees, and far out on the water, the grey ships began to make ready.
