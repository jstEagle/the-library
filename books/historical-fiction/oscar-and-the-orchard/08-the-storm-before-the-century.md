## 8. The Storm Before the Century

The storm came a day early, the way grief does.

Lior felt it in his teeth before he felt it in the wind—a pressure behind his eyes that had nothing to do with the barometer falling all through the morning of December 30th. By noon the gulls had gone silent and inland. By two o'clock the sea had turned the color of gunmetal dragged across wet slate, and by three the first squall hit the tower so hard the glass hummed in its brass cradle like a struck bell.

He climbed anyway. He always climbed.

The lens turned above him in its slow, patient revolution, throwing its blade of light out over water that no longer resembled water. He checked the clockwork, oiled the carriage wheels, wiped salt spray from the storm panes with a rag gone grey from twenty years of the same motion. His hands knew this work the way Maren's lungs knew the sea air: without being asked.

*Do your work,* he thought, and heard his own voice say it aloud, absurd against the roar. *Sign nothing. Want nothing.*

Except he had signed everything now. His hand was on every page of that ledger, copying names in his steadiest ink—names ranked, names priced, one name left unassigned at the bottom like an empty chair at a funeral table. And he had wanted something, publicly, terribly: he had named Oscar for the last berth, and Voss had smiled the way a man smiles when a debt is booked.

The wind slammed the gallery rail. Below, in the harbor, the convoy rode at anchor—three transports black against the dying light, riding high and empty, waiting to be filled with the town's unwanted come New Year's morning. The harbor lights were already failing. He could see it from up here: the red buoy lamp at the channel mouth guttering, drowning, going dark. The pier lights followed, one after another, snuffed like candles at a wake.

By nightfall every guiding light in Orchard's End would be dead except his own.

Lior stood in the lantern room with the storm trying to peel the roof off over his head and understood, with terrible clarity, that the army's whole machinery—the ledger, the berths, the arithmetic of human worth—was about to be blind. Ships couldn't sail a mined channel in a gale with no lights. Convoy orders would stall. Officers would panic. And somewhere in that panic, men would make decisions they'd otherwise have made slowly.

He went down to trim the wicks and feed the stove, and that was when the knocking began.

* * *

Three blows, then silence, then three more—desperate, arrhythmic, nothing like a patrol's measured fist. Lior opened the door a hand's width and the wind took it the rest of the way, slamming it against the stone, and Private Hale fell half into the lighthouse with the rain.

The boy-soldier was soaked to the skin, lips blue, one eye swollen shut where something had caught him. His uniform coat was gone. Underneath, where the regimental insignia should have been, there was only torn cloth—he'd cut them off himself, or someone had done it for him roughly.

"They know," Hale said. His teeth chattered between words. "Voss knows I've been writing letters home under a false regiment. He found the orchard—the boy, is the boy—"

"Oscar's home," Lior said. "Safe."

"Then you know." Hale grabbed Lior's sleeve with fingers too cold to grip properly. "They're not sending me back for trial. There's no trial. Voss told me himself, tonight, before I ran—he said desertion in the face of the convoy was *contagious*, and contagions get *cauterized*. They're going to shoot me on the pier tomorrow morning as an example, and everyone in town will be marched down to watch, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You're the only door in Orchard's End that isn't watched."

It wasn't true. Nothing was unwatched anymore. But Lior looked at the boy—nineteen, maybe twenty, younger than Oscar would ever know how to be—and saw what he supposed everyone else had stopped seeing months ago: not a deserter, not a falsified ledger line, just a child who had walked into a war and wanted out of it.

His wife's voice, two years dead and still clear as a bell in this kind of weather: *Love doesn't divide, Lior. It multiplies.*

"Cellar," Lior said. "Under the oil store. Don't come up until I call you, even if the building comes down around you."

Hale went. Lior barred the door behind him and stood in the dark entry hall listening to the storm try to take the tower apart plank by plank, and he did not mistake this for safety. He had learned that lesson. Safety was a story people told themselves while the ledger filled in.

He put another log on the fire and sat down to wait, because he knew—had known since the moment the harbor lights died—that Voss would come. Men like Voss always came to the last lit place. It was the only thing left they trusted.

* * *

They came at half past eleven, six of them, lanterns swinging, boots hammering the causeway stones through the rain. Lior heard them coming and did not bar the door again. Barred doors were accusations. Open doors were invitations, and he meant to control which thing this was.

Captain Voss entered shaking rain from his greatcoat like a dog, his eyes already moving past Lior into the interior of the tower with the flat professional sweep of a man counting exits.

"Keeper." Even shouted conversationally, over the storm, Voss's voice stayed level. That was the most frightening thing about him. "We've lost a soldier. A private who deserted his post during blackout preparations. You'll have seen nothing, of course. Keepers see nothing. It's practically doctrine."

"I've seen plenty," Lior said.

Voss paused. Something shifted behind his eyes—not surprise exactly; recalculation. "Have you."

"He's in my cellar."

The soldiers' rifles came up in the same instant, six clicks almost musical beneath the wind. Voss didn't move. He studied Lior the way he'd studied him across the clerk's desk for weeks: as an instrument that had finally produced an unexpected reading.

"You're going to give him to me," Voss said.

"No," Lior said. "I'm going to trade him to you."

He crossed to the desk where he did the light's accounts, took out the ledger—his own fair copy, the one Voss had ordered made, the one with every name ranked and every berth assigned—and laid it on the desk between them. Then he took from the drawer beside it a sheaf of loose papers. Notes. Weeks of notes, in his small precise hand.

"Do you know what happens when a man copies a thousand names?" Lior said. "He starts to notice things. Names that appear twice. Names of soldiers who supposedly shipped out in October but whose rations are still drawn here. Berths sold for cash and recorded as *laborer, infirm, priority transport*. Your falsifications, Captain. Every one. Dates, amounts, initials. I have them all."

Voss's face did not change. That was the discipline in him. But his hand drifted, very slightly, toward the papers, and Lior moved them back beyond reach.

"Here is the trade," Lior said, and heard his own voice come out steady—not the steady of the invisible man, the do-your-work sign-nothing want-nothing steady, but something older and hotter, forged tonight in the cellar-warm dark with a terrified boy below him and a killer in front of him. "The private sails free on the convoy. No pier, no firing squad, no example. My sister Maren Ashgrove receives a medical discharge—her lungs are ruined, your own surgeon wrote it in October, page ninety-one—and she stays ashore. And my nephew Oscar takes the last transport berth, as agreed, with the private's protection aboard."

"And if I refuse?"

"Tomorrow morning, when the convoy can't sail for the storm and your superiors arrive to ask why their harbor went dark, these pages find their way onto the colonel's desk instead of yours." Lior met his eyes. "You built your machine out of paper, Captain. Paper burns."

For a long moment there was only the storm, battering the tower like a fist on a door, and the six rifles, and the fire ticking in the grate.

Then Voss laughed—one short breath of it, more exhalation than sound—and something in his face loosened by a degree, the way ice loosens before it breaks.

"You know what I was going to do with the last berth originally?" he said. "Before you named the boy. I was going to sell it. I've been selling everything since October—berths, discharges, silence. Do you know why?" He stepped closer, close enough that Lior could smell the wet wool and the brandy beneath it. "Because the war doesn't care who it eats. Because the ledgers don't either. Because everyone in this town lined up like sheep to be counted, and not one of them—not one—ever asked what the counting was *for*. Except you." He looked at the papers in Lior's hand with something that might, in a better man, have been respect. "The invisible man. Who saw everything."

"The trade," Lior said.

"The trade," Voss agreed. He pulled off one glove finger by finger, produced a pen from inside his coat, and scrawled three lines on the back of Lior's oil-account sheet: *Hale—transported, conditionally pardoned. Ashgrove, M.—medical discharge. Ashgrove, O.—berth confirmed.* He signed it, folded it once, and held it out.

Lior reached for it—

—and Voss didn't let go.

"One condition," Voss said quietly, under the wind. "The original ledger. The master copy. When we're done tomorrow, when the convoy sails—you burn it. Not because I fear your notes. Because as long as that book exists, everyone in this town is worth what's written next to their name, including you. And I think you've finally decided you're worth more than ink." He released the paper. "Burn it, Keeper. Prove me right."

Lior stared at him. Of all the bargains he had imagined, all the threats, all the violence—he had never imagined being handed the match.

Below them, faintly, from the cellar, came the sound of Private Hale beginning to cough—or perhaps to cry; in a storm, at this distance, the two sounded alike.

And outside, through the streaming glass, the beam swept round once more over the drowned harbor, over the three black ships, over the last hours of a century—and Lior took the paper, and said:

"At dawn, then. All of us. Bring your soldiers."

He meant it as surrender. Voss, turning up his collar and stepping back out into the roaring dark, seemed to understand it as something else entirely—and the look he gave Lior over his shoulder, just before the door closed, was the look of a man watching a fuse he himself had lit.
