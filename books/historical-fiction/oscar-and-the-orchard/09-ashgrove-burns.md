## 9. Ashgrove Burns

The storm had not finished with the coast; it was only catching its breath.

Lior stood at the top of the tower with the ledger under his arm and the wind trying to take it from him, and below in the lantern room Private Hale sat on the iron floor with his back against the lens housing, bleeding through a bandage Maren had tied with the same hands she used to knead bread. Oscar crouched beside him, holding a candle that guttered with every gust through the shattered ventilator glass. The boy's face was pale but set, and Lior thought: he looks like his mother did the night she decided to marry me into this family. That look of someone who has already chosen and is waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

The soldiers came up the stairwell two minutes later—Lior counted their boots on the iron treads, six pairs—and then Voss came through the hatchway with rain running off the brim of his cap, and behind him two men with rifles leveled, and behind them the whole grey machinery of the army pressing up into the light like water into a breach.

"Step away from him," Voss said.

Hale tried to stand. His leg folded. Oscar caught his arm and held it, ten years old and not letting go.

"No," Lior said.

It was such a small word for what it cost him. He had spent fifty-one years making himself a man made of small useful motions—trimming wicks, polishing brass, signing nothing, wanting nothing—and now he heard his own voice come out of him like something breaking out of a locked room.

Voss looked at him the way a man looks at furniture that has spoken. "Keeper. You are harbouring a deserter of the Third Coastal Regiment. The penalty is—"

"I know what the penalty is." Lior lifted the ledger. Water had spotted its cover; his own hand had written every page inside it twice over, once in Voss's office and once again in his memory, in the long sleepless hours while the storm came down the coast. "And I know what's in this book better than you do."

The wind screamed through the ventilator. The beam swept around above them, once, twice, laying its long white arm across the sea where three grey ships rode at anchor, loading for the last convoy of the century.

"You will put down the rifle," Lior said to the nearer soldier, "and then Captain Voss and I are going to talk."

* * *

They talked in the watch room below, because Voss would not negotiate in front of his men, and because Lior asked him not to, and there was still enough of the officer left in Voss to be grateful for the mercy of appearances. The room was eight feet across. Rain lashed the seaward glass. The little stove ticked and breathed heat, and Lior set the ledger on the table between them like a card laid face down.

"You copied it," Voss said. "That's all you did. You copied what I gave you."

"I copied it four hundred and eleven times." Lior opened it. The pages were damp at the edges; the ink had begun to feather. "I have a steady hand and nothing else to occupy my mind at night, Captain. So I read as I wrote. Do you want me to tell you what I found?"

Voss said nothing. Rain ran down his collar.

"Page thirty-one. Aldous Wren, cooper, transported for insufficiency—except Wren shipped out in October on the *Meridian*, alive and willing, because I loaded his crates myself. Someone used his name to move a man who didn't exist. Page forty-four, the Kessler brothers, listed as transported when they're both still sleeping in their mother's house above the chandlery—their berths were sold, and other names were written in their place. Names of soldiers. Names of men who deserted your regiment before the ships ever anchored, and whom you have been smuggling out one berth at a time, dressed as fishermen, marked in my handwriting as labourers bound for the colonies."

"Stop."

"Page sixty-eight. Page seventy-three. Page ninety. There are nineteen of them, Captain. Nineteen deserters you've bought out of this town using the ledger like a door, and every one of those entries is forged, and every forgery would end your career if it reached an admiral, and several of them would end more than your career." Lior closed the book. His hands were shaking now, finally, now that it no longer mattered whether anyone saw them shake. "You have spent three months teaching this town that a name in ink decides what a person is worth. I am telling you that I know exactly which names are lies."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, Hale coughed, and Oscar's voice murmured to him, low and steady, the way Lior himself murmured to the lamp.

Voss sat down slowly on the stool by the chart table, and for a moment he looked very young, younger than his rank, a wet tired man of perhaps thirty with the war sitting on his shoulders like ballast. When he spoke again the command had gone out of his voice and something rawer had come in.

"They'd have shot them," he said. "Every one. Hale, and Brandt, and the Ostrava boys—they were seventeen. A court-martial on the dock and a bullet, as an example, because the war is lost and lost wars need examples. I had berths. I had paper. It was the only door open." He looked up. "What do you want?"

Three things. He had rehearsed them all night walking the orchard rows with the wind tearing at him, and now they came out of him plainly, without ornament, because ornament was a luxury for men whose families were not priced by the berth.

"Hale sails on the morning convoy. Free passage, clean record, no charges waiting at the far end. You write it tonight, in your own hand, and you give me the paper."

"Maren Ashgrove receives a medical discharge from the transport rolls. She is ill. Ill women don't survive the holds of troop ships, and you know it, and putting her name on that manifest was murder with extra steps. Discharged on my testimony as her brother and the crown's own census clerk. In writing."

"And the last berth. The one unassigned berth before the new year." Lior's throat tightened. "Oscar Ashgrove. Age ten. He goes."

Voss stared at him. "And you?"

"I stay. I keep the light. Somebody has to keep the light."

"That's not—" Voss stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, some arithmetic of his own, and Lior watched him arrive at the sum: a clerk who knew nineteen falsifications, staying behind, free, with a grudge and a memory. "You'll report me."

"I could have reported you any day for two months." Lior shook his head slowly. "I'm not going to hang you with your own book, Captain. I'm going to make sure nobody ever hangs anybody with it again."

He picked up the ledger, carried it three steps to the stove, and opened the firebox door. The coals glowed orange in the draft, hungry, patient.

"What are you doing?" Voss was on his feet. "That's a military document—that's—"

"The only copy of the transport rolls. The only record of who was ranked, and sold, and shipped, and erased." Lior held the book over the open door and felt the heat climb his wrists. Behind him, in the doorway, he heard Maren's breath catch, and Oscar go very still. "You taught us that whoever holds the paper owns the people in it. So here is my last act as your clerk, Captain. I resign."

He let it fall.

The ledger turned over once in the air, pages fanning like a gull's wings, and then it hit the coals and the cover curled and the fire took it—not all at once, but greedily, page by page, the ink flaring blue-green where the copper salts burned. Four hundred and eleven names. The Kesslers. Aldous Wren. Maren. Oscar. His own hand, his whole winter of careful complicit work, going up in a smell of hot glue and scorching linen.

Voss lunged forward and Lior stepped into his path—not a big man, not a brave-seeming man, just a body placed squarely between a captain and a stove—and Voss pulled up short. For a long moment they stood chest to chest in the firelight, and Lior saw that the officer was not reaching for the book at all. He was watching it burn with an expression Lior had last seen on his own face at his wife's graveside: grief and relief arriving together, inseparable, like twins.

"It's done," Voss said quietly. "There'll be inquiries. Convoy manifests, muster rolls—I can't forge those too. When the fleet asks why the census doesn't match—"

"Tell them the truth. Tell them the keeper burned it." Lior almost smiled. "No one will question it. Everyone knows what lighthouse men are like about their fires."

Above them the great lamp turned, and turned, and turned.

* * *

They came down from the tower at first grey light, and the storm had blown itself out to sea overnight, leaving the bay swept clean and glittering like the inside of a shell. The convoy would sail on the morning tide. Soldiers were already moving along the quay with lanterns, and the smell of coal smoke lay over everything, and somewhere a bosun was calling numbers in a voice like a gull.

Maren walked down the cliff path leaning on Lior's arm, breathing the cold air in careful sips. At the bottom she stopped and faced him, and her cheeks were wet, though whether from wind or weeping she would never admit.

"You should have told me," she said. "About the berth. About any of it. I'm not made of glass, Lior."

"No," he agreed. "You're made of something considerably harder to break and much more dangerous."

She laughed—a real laugh, ruined at the edges—and gripped his sleeve. "Take care of the orchard. Don't let it go wild again. And write to him. Every week. He'll pretend he's too old for letters and he'll read every one twenty times."

"I know," Lior said. "I did the same at his age."

At the foot of the quay, Private Hale waited with a kit bag and a crutch, and Oscar waited beside him with his one suitcase and his grandmother's Bible and the apple—dried, wrinkled, saved since autumn—from the tree his aunt had planted the year he was born. He had asked to carry it himself and refused all offers otherwise.

Lior knelt down on the wet stones so they were eye to eye. Up close his nephew smelled of woodsmoke and salt and childhood, and Lior committed the smell to memory the way he committed the falsified pages, line by line, never to be lost.

"You'll get off at Cape Harbour. Hale's cousin has a farm there—Hale has written ahead, it's all arranged, it's on paper, proper paper." He tucked the boy's collar straight. "You mind your aunt's trees until she can follow. You do your sums. And when the light reaches you at night, all the way across the water—you'll see it, it carries eleven miles on a clear night—when you see it, you think of me, and you'll know I'm thinking of you."

"You could come," Oscar said. His voice cracked on it. "There's room. There's always room."

"There isn't, lad. Not on the ship." Lior pressed his forehead briefly against the boy's forehead, the old greeting, the orchard greeting, the one his wife had invented and named after the way branches touch. "But there's room everywhere else. Go on now. Before your uncle does something undignified in front of the entire army."

Oscar hugged him hard enough to hurt, and then he was walking up the gangway with his hand in Hale's, small and straight-backed, not looking back until the very top—and when he did look back, Lior raised his hand, and kept it raised, and kept it raised, as the lines were cast off and the great grey ship slid its length away from the quay and turned its bow toward the open sea.

The convoy went out on the morning tide, three ships in line ahead, their smoke bending east on the land breeze. Lior stood on the gallery of the tower as noon passed and the shapes shrank and vanished, and Maren stood beside him with her hand in the crook of his elbow, neither of them speaking, because there was nothing left to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

Then the sun went down behind Orchard's End, and the sky over the water went the colour of a fresh bruise clearing, and Lior climbed inside and lit the lamp.

The new century was coming up out of the dark whether anyone was ready for it or not. He trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork, checked the mercury bath with a steadiness that surprised him, and took his place at the rail of the lantern room as the first stars came out. Far off to the southeast, invisible now, a ship was carrying his nephew toward a life Lior would never see—but would hear about, in letters, in years, in the slow accumulating mail of a family scattered on purpose rather than by accident.

The beam swung out over the water, eleven miles, touching everything.

He had spent a whole life learning how not to be seen. It had taken one burning book to teach him the other lesson: that being seen was the price of mattering, and mattering was the price of love, and love, as his wife had told him once among the apple trees, does not divide—it multiplies.

Below him, in the dark orchard, the pruned trees stood in their rows with their wounds sealed in cold sap, holding next spring inside themselves like a secret.

Lior Ashgrove turned up the flame one notch brighter, and waited for the year to turn.
