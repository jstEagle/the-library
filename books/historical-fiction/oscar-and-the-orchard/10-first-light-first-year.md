## 10. First Light, First Year

The ship's horn woke me before the gulls did.

I lay in my berth in the dark, listening to it moan across the harbor like something alive and grieving, and for a moment I didn't know where I was. The bed was too narrow. The blanket smelled of tar and other boys, dozens of other boys who had slept here on the crossing out and would sleep here again on the crossing back—if there was a crossing back. Then I remembered. The transport. The last day of the old year. Uncle Lior standing at the gangway with the cold coming off the water and his hand on my shoulder, heavier than it had ever been.

*You'll be my eyes,* he'd said. *Somebody has to tell me what the world looks like from out there.*

The horn sounded again, closer now, and I got up.

* * *

The deck was grey and moving. Grey water, grey sky, grey men stamping their boots against the cold, and above all of it the white wake churning behind us like a seam being ripped open. Private Hale found me at the rail before I'd been standing there five minutes. He had a way of appearing like that, quiet as weather.

"You're up early," he said.

"I wanted to see it."

"See what?"

"The light."

Hale didn't laugh at me, which was one of the things I liked about him. Most soldiers laughed easily and meanly, quick as striking a match. Hale just leaned his forearms on the rail beside mine and followed where I was looking—back, always back, toward the coast we were leaving.

Orchard's End was a smudge in the dawn, low hills and darker trees and the town tucked between them like a coin lost in grass. And there, on the point where the land gave up and the sea began, stood the lighthouse. Small from here. A white finger, no bigger than my thumb at arm's length.

But its beam was burning.

Even in daylight, even through the thin morning fog, you could see it sweep—the great slow arm of it turning, turning, reaching out over the water to touch us and pull away and reach again. It caught the mist and lit it gold for half a second each time, then let it go dark.

"He kept it lit," Hale said. "Through everything."

"He never misses," I said. "Not once. Not when Mum was sick. Not after." I hesitated. "He says the light isn't for him. He says it doesn't matter if anyone sees him doing it. The ships only need to see the light."

Hale was quiet awhile. When he spoke again his voice had changed, gone rough at the edges.

"Your uncle is the bravest man I ever met, Oscar. And I've met men who charged guns."

"He's not brave," I said, because that was true and everyone knew it. "He hides. He always hid. He used to say—sign nothing, want nothing, let them look through you."

"And then?"

I watched the beam swing out over the water, touch us, release us.

"Then they made him write down every name in town," I said. "And he couldn't hide inside the writing anymore. You can't be invisible when your own hand is the thing deciding who gets sold."

* * *

We had left in chaos, because everything about the convoy was chaos. The storm had broken the harbor lights and half the pier and Captain Voss's certainty along with them, and in the end it had happened fast, faster than I could hold onto. Hale walking up the gangway free, no falsified ledger to hang him. Maren staying ashore, her name struck from the transport lists by Voss's own reluctant pen—a medical discharge, wrung out of him like blood from a stone. And me, with the last berth, the one that had sat empty on the page while grown men did arithmetic with human lives.

Uncle Lior hadn't cried. I don't think Uncle Lior has ever cried where anyone could see, though I caught him once, the night before, in the orchard, standing among the stumps the soldiers' axes had made. His shoulders were shaking and I pretended I hadn't seen, because pretending not to see things is a family trade.

What he did instead was give me things. My mother's Bible, small enough to fit in a coat pocket. A paper packet of apple seeds, folded into quarters, tied with string. And the brass whistle from the lighthouse door, worn smooth by his thumb.

"When the buds come," he said, kneeling so our faces were level, "you count them for me. Every spring. Write to me how many. That's your job now. That's how you'll still be useful."

"You always said useful was a trap."

"It is," he said. "It's also the only thing worth being. Both are true. You'll learn to carry both."

Then Maren hugged me until my ribs hurt and told me she'd be well, she'd be strong, she'd plant the seeds herself if the letters stopped coming, and Uncle Lior put his hand on my head the way he does when he's saying something too big for words, and then the sergeant was shouting and I was on the deck of a ship watching the whole world shrink.

* * *

By midmorning Orchard's End was gone under the horizon, but the light stayed longer than the land did. That's the trick of a lighthouse—it sits higher than everything, so it says goodbye last. All morning I stayed at the rail, and all morning the beam turned behind us, sweeping, sweeping, patient as breathing, and I understood something I couldn't have said out loud:

He wasn't keeping the light for the ships.

Or—he was, yes, that was the work, that was always the work. But he was keeping it *lit*, burning in broad daylight where no ship needed it, because for two years my uncle had lived invisible, and invisible men keep lights for other people. Now everyone could see him. The whole town knew his name now. Knew what he'd done, what he'd burned, what he'd traded and refused to trade. He had stepped into the open the way you step off a cliff, and this was him standing at the bottom of it, visible to everyone, alone, and unashamed.

Maren had told me, the night before we sailed, holding my face in her cracked hands: "Your uncle spent his whole life making sure nobody looked at him. Do you understand what it cost him to make them look? To stand in front of soldiers and say *look at me, I'm the one you want*?" She'd kissed my forehead. "That's the bravest kind of love. The kind that stops hiding."

The beam swept. I counted the turns. Eleven sweeps between one bell and the next. I decided I would remember that number forever.

* * *

In the afternoon the light finally sank away, and the ocean became all there was, enormous and indifferent, and I went below and ate hard biscuit and salt meat with forty strangers and found it wasn't so bad, because Hale sat with me and taught me knots and told me about his mother's farm inland, wheat fields, a dog named Biscuit, a brother who'd be twenty next summer.

"Do you regret it?" I asked him. "Coming aboard instead of running?"

Hale turned the knot over in his big fingers.

"A man can run his whole life and never arrive anywhere," he said. "This way I'm going somewhere honest. Your uncle bought me that. I won't waste it."

"Voss could have shot you."

"Voss could have shot all of us. Instead a clerk with steady hands burned the book that owned us." Hale smiled, crooked. "Never trust a man with a ledger, Oscar. And never underestimate one either."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I went up on deck with the blanket around my shoulders and stood where the wind couldn't quite reach me behind the deckhouse. The stars over the ocean were more than over land—more of them, closer somehow, like the sky had bent down to see what we were doing.

And far behind us, at the very edge of the dark, a smudge of gold rose and fell and rose again. The light. Still turning. Still sweeping. Reaching all the way out here, to me, or seeming to.

I took out the paper packet and untied the string with numb fingers. Apple seeds, seven of them, small and brown and ordinary as gravel. My mother had planted that orchard. Uncle Lior had tended it without ever once sitting under the trees. And the soldiers had cut it down in a single afternoon because armies cannot abide anything that takes years to grow.

But seeds don't read ledgers. Seeds don't care whose name is written where. Seven seeds, and I pressed them into my palm and thought: *somewhere there is ground. Somewhere I will dig seven holes.*

Belonging, I thought, watching the far light rise and fall. Maybe it isn't a place. Maybe it's the people who keep a light on for you, whether you can see them or not.

* * *

They say the buds came early that year. That's what Maren wrote, in the first letter that reached me in April, her handwriting steadier than it had been in two years: *The orchard shows green at the tips. Your uncle swears it's the burning that fed the roots. He counts them every morning. He says he's saving the count for you.*

And beneath her words, in a different hand, careful and unhurried, the steadiest hand in Orchard's End:

*Eleven sweeps between bells. Tell me what the world looks like from out there. — Uncle*

I sat down right there on the dock with the letter in my lap and the gulls shouting overhead, and I began my reply.
