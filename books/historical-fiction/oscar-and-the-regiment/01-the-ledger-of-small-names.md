## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The river froze in November and stayed frozen, and the workshop hospital sat on its bank like a barnacle on a stone.

Jude Kavanagh had been there eleven weeks when the year turned. She knew this not from any calendar—nobody gave nurses calendars—but from her ledger, which she ruled into months herself, in pencil first, ink over top when she was sure. Eleven weeks of frostbite cases and lung-rot and boys who had stepped wrong off a wagon and shattered a leg that Ferro would later replace with pine. Eleven weeks of the same four stoves, the same smell of lye and woodsmoke and gangrene kept mostly at bay, the same view through the east window: the river lying white and dead under ice a foot thick, and beyond it the world where history was happening without her.

She did not mind being left out of history. That was the thing nobody understood about her, and the thing she never said aloud. The officers thought a woman who wanted to be forgotten was a woman with no ambitions. They were wrong, but she saw no profit in correcting them.

Her shift ended at nine. Mags took the midnight watch, because Mags slept like a cat and woke like one too, and because Mags was seventy-one years old and had decided long ago which hours belonged to her. So at nine Jude climbed the narrow stair to the room above the ward that served as her lodging, her office, her church, and she lit the candle stub she was allowed, and she took down the ledger.

It was a warehouse book originally. She'd bought it off Ferro for three sous when he was clearing out dead stock—a big square thing with a canvas spine and pages the color of weak tea, ruled for quantities of nails and planks. In the top right corner of the cover, faded, someone had written *HALLIDAY & SONS, TIMBER*. Jude had crossed it out. Above it, in her own hand, small and level, she had written: *Names.*

Not much of a title. But titles told people what you were doing, and she did not want anyone to know what she was doing.

* * *

The ritual went like this.

First she reviewed the day's admission list—the official one, the one that went up to the adjutant's office every evening in Sergeant Brannigan's careful hand. Brannigan wrote a fair round fist, better than most officers; he'd learned his letters as a boy in County Down before the militia took him at nineteen. His lists were complete. That was the trouble. They were complete, and then they arrived back at the workshop two days later, copied fair into the regimental record, and they were not complete anymore.

Names went missing between Brannigan's desk and the adjutant's.

Not all names. Never the officers'. Lieutenant Halloran's broken wrist appeared in the record with full particulars, including the fact that the injury was sustained "in the performance of duty," though everyone in the workshop knew he'd done it falling drunk off the loading dock. No—the names that vanished were private soldiers'. And not even all of those. Just enough. Just the ones whose absence made somebody look better or worse than the truth allowed.

Tonight there were three.

Jude laid the returned copy beside her ledger and read it through twice, the way she always did, because the trick was to be certain. A mistake in the other direction—recording a name the record already held—would make her ledger a mess, and a mess could be dismissed. Her ledger's whole power lay in being exact.

The first missing name was easy: Private Doherty, Michael, 3rd Company, admitted Tuesday with a fever, died Thursday morning. She had closed his eyes herself. He'd been nineteen. The official record now showed an admission but no death, which meant somewhere in the regimental arithmetic Michael Doherty was still alive, still drawing rations, still available to be marched another twenty miles next summer. She entered him in the ledger: name, company, age if known, ailment, date of admission, date of death, and then the line she added for every man, the line that was the whole point of the book:

*Died of fever. Not of cowardice, desertion, or any cause requiring inquiry.*

That phrasing had taken her some time to settle on. Early entries just said *died.* But words mattered in a book like this. Someday, if anyone ever read it, she wanted each line to say plainly what the official record implied otherwise—that these men had died ordinary deaths, deaths that needed no explaining away, no court-martial, no blot on their character. The record erased them by omission; she would restore them by insisting their deaths had been simple.

The second name was harder. Private Okafor, listed in Brannigan's original list as admitted with a hand wound, absent from the return copy entirely—not dead, just gone from the page, as though he'd never come through the door. Jude remembered him. Tall fellow, quiet, from some island she couldn't pronounce properly and he didn't correct her. The wound was bad; Mags had taken two fingers. He'd been transferred upriver ten days ago to the general hospital. Transfers got lost in the record constantly—they passed out of the regiment's responsibility and into no one's. She entered him: transferred, date, destination, condition at departure. Then, after a moment's thought: *If he dies there, no one will write it down. So I am writing it down now, in advance, that he lived until the tenth of December and left this place alive.*

It wasn't strictly a death ledger. She'd stopped pretending it was around week six.

The third name she sat with longest.

Private Willets, Josiah. Admitted eight days ago, delirious, a head wound from a fall—or that was the story. Jude had seen head wounds from falls, and she'd seen head wounds from fists and musket butts, and Willets' was the second kind. He'd died on the fifth day without ever waking fully, and in his lucid hours he'd said things. Fragments. About a night march he shouldn't have been on. About an order given and countermanded and given again. About a lieutenant's name, spoken twice, with something in his voice that hadn't been fear exactly—more like the particular distress of a man who has discovered the ground is not where he thought it was.

Willets' name was not merely missing from the return copy. It had been struck. There was a line through it in the adjutant's own hand, and beside the strike, in the margin, a notation: *entered in error.*

Entered in error. As though the man had never existed. As though eight days in her ward, the fever, the death, the burial detail—Ferro and two convalescents digging the grave in ground so hard they'd had to build a fire on it first—as though all of that were the error, and the clean page were the truth.

Jude dipped her pen. Wrote: *Willets, Josiah. Head wound, cause disputed. Died the fifth day after admission. Buried north wall, third row.* And then, because her hand seemed to want it:

*He said the lieutenant's name twice. I will learn why.*

She looked at that last sentence for a while. It was not like her other entries. Her other entries were records; this was a thread, and threads led places, and places were dangerous. She almost struck it out.

She left it. She told herself it was because a record ought to be honest about what was known, and what was known was that Willets had said the name. That was true. It was also true that some part of her, some part she kept in the dark even from herself, was tired of only recording endings. Eight years she had spent in hospitals and workshops, mending the wreckage that history left behind it, writing down what history chose not to carry. History happened elsewhere—on fields she'd never see, in councils she'd never enter—and arrived here as meat and fever and silence. She had accepted that. She had built a life on accepting it.

But Willets had talked in his dying, and the talk had gone into her ears, and now it was in the book, and the book did not let go of things. That was its nature. That was why she'd made it.

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat a moment with her hand flat on the cover, feeling the cold coming up through the floorboards from the river side of the building.

* * *

Below her, the ward settled into its night noises. Someone coughing in the long rhythm that meant the lungs were going. The stoves ticking as they cooled. Mags' footsteps making their slow circuit, pausing here and there—Mags checked breathing the way other people checked locks, twice a night, unhurried, thorough. Through the floor Jude could feel the whole structure of the workshop: the ward below her, the carpentry shop beyond it where Ferro worked late, lamplight under his door even now, the wash-house where Tilda boiled linen until midnight and sang to herself in a language none of the rest of them knew. Four people, more or less, holding a building together against the winter, while across the river and up the road and over the horizon the regiment assembled itself for whatever the spring would demand of it.

She thought sometimes about how many men passed through this place and were never counted anywhere. Not in the returns, not in the gazettes, not in the letters home, because half of them had no one to write to or couldn't write themselves. A soldier wounded here and shipped upriver was a rumor. A soldier who died here was a hole in a list. A soldier who recovered and marched on was a number in a strength return, and numbers had no mothers.

Someone should count them. That was all. Not for glory—God knew there was no glory in a warehouse ledger. For arithmetic. For the plain fact that a thing unwritten can be denied, and a thing written cannot. She had learned that young, in her father's house, where nothing that happened indoors was ever spoken of outdoors, and so officially nothing had ever happened at all, and she had grown up inside the lie like a fish grows up inside water. Writing was the way out of the water. She'd known that since she was seven years old and had understood almost nothing else since.

The candle guttered. She trimmed it, meaning to close up for the night.

Instead she found herself turning back to the beginning of the book, to the first entries, October's dead, and reading down the column of names the way you'd read a rosary. Byrne. Kowalczyk. Petit. Anselm. Names she had gathered like a gleaner follows a harvest, taking what the reapers left in the field. Forty-three names so far. Small against the war the way a candle is small against the winter—but the candle is not trying to be the sun. The candle is only refusing the dark, one inch at a time, and it turns out the dark minds that.

She smiled at her own fancy, blew it out along with the candle, and went to bed.

* * *

She woke once in the deep of the night—not to a sound exactly, but to a change in the sound, the way you wake when snow stops falling. The wind had dropped. In the sudden stillness the river made itself heard: a long groan under the ice, far out toward mid-channel, the sound of freezing water settling and straining, the sound the river made every clear night and that newcomers found terrifying and that she found, after eleven weeks, almost companionable. The ice talking in its sleep.

Then, nearer—a different sound. Boots in the yard. More than one pair, moving fast, and the low voice of the gate sentry challenged, and answered, and then boots on the porch stairs, and a fist on the ward door below, urgent but disciplined, the knock of a man carrying orders rather than news.

Jude was already out of bed, pulling her shawl on, her heart going hard. In her experience, knocks at this hour meant casualties—a wagon coming in from the road, a sledge from the picket line. She was down the stairs before Mags reached the door, and together they opened it onto the blowing cold.

There was a horse steaming in the yard and an officer's aide on the step, snow to his knees in his hurry, and at his feet—

At his feet, half-buried in the drift against the doorframe, was a boy.
