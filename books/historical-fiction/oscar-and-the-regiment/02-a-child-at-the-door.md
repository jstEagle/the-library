## 2. A Child at the Door

The blizzard came up the river valley in the last hour before dark, the way bad news always comes—suddenly and from a direction you weren't watching. One moment Jude Kavanagh could see the far bank, the black teeth of the winter camp's pickets against a gray sky; the next, the world was a white wall pressing against the workshop windows like something trying to get in.

She had seen three winters on this river. The first she had spent learning that cold kills more soldiers than muskets do, quietly, without glory, one frostbitten foot at a time. The second she had spent learning that the army knows this and budgets for it anyway, because feet are cheaper than boots. This third winter she had stopped learning anything new and started writing things down instead.

The workshop hospital sat on pilings over the frozen river, a long low building of timber and tar paper that had been a boatwright's shed before the war found it. Forty cots in two rows. A stove at each end that never quite met in the middle. The smell of carbolic and wet wool and, underneath it all, the river itself—a vast cold presence under the floorboards, patient as debt.

Jude made her evening rounds with a lantern and a stub of pencil behind her ear. Fever cases first: Private Doyle, whose cough had turned wet; a sapper named Hult who'd taken shrapnel through the shoulder and refused, on principle, to admit it hurt. She counted pulses, changed dressings, noted everything in her head for later, when the lamps were out and no one could ask what a nurse wanted with ink.

The official ledger of the workshop lived on a lectern by the door, kept in a fair hand by the adjutant's clerk, who came twice a week and recorded what the officers thought worth recording. *Admitted. Discharged. Died.* Three words for whole lives, and even those three words were too many for some of them.

Her own ledger lived under the third floorboard from the stove, wrapped in oilcloth. It was nothing grand—a school exercise book she'd bought from a peddler for two eggs—but it held what the other one didn't. Not just *died*, but *of what*, and *whose son he was*, and *what he said near the end*, which was usually a woman's name, or a village, or nothing at all, which she also wrote down, because nothing was a fact too. When the clerk wrote *discharged*, she wrote where they'd been sent, and whether anyone had come for them. When the clerk skipped a man entirely—as happened more often than coincidence allowed—she gave him his full name, his regiment, his company, the date, and a line about his hands or his voice or the joke he'd told, so that whoever read it later would know he had been a person and not a clerical error.

History, she had decided, happened elsewhere. It happened in headquarters tents and capital cities, among men whose names got printed. Here on the river there was only weather and work and the small machinery of dying. But someone ought to keep the count. If no one else would, then a nurse with a stolen hour and a twopenny exercise book would have to do.

She finished the rounds, checked the stove, and was reaching for the bar on the door to bring in the night's firewood when the knocking started.

Not knocking, quite. Pounding—the flat desperate rhythm of someone whose hands are too cold to feel what they're doing. She unbarred the door and the storm shoved her back a step, snow swirling into the entryway like it had been waiting all day for exactly this invitation.

A man stood on the step, bent double against the wind. He wore an officer's staff coat, good cloth, though the snow had already crusted the shoulders white. Behind him, half-lost in the whirling dark, stood a horse—and beside the horse, holding its bridle, something small.

"Kavanagh." The aide had to shout over the wind. She knew him distantly—Lieutenant Vasseur, or Vasquez, some smooth foreign syllable the officers wore like their gloves. He was young under the snow on his face. Younger than she'd expected, and unhappy about whatever he'd been sent here to do. That registered somewhere beneath her ribs before any of his actual words did.

"You're the head nurse." Not a question. He pushed past her into the entryway without waiting for leave, and the small thing beside the horse came stumbling after him through the drifts.

It was a boy. Six years old, perhaps seven—small for either, bundled in a child's wool coat that had been expensive once and was now soaked through, a fur cap pulled down so far she could see only a pale chin and a mouth pressed into a thin line. He did not cry. That struck her before anything else did. Children in storms cry, or scream, or go rigid with terror. This one simply stood where he was put, the way furniture stands, and let the snow land on him.

"There's a fee." Vasseur dug in his coat and produced a purse. Coins, real silver, enough to keep the workshop in flour for a month. He set them on her shelf with a clink that sounded obscenely loud against the wind. "For his board. Through the winter."

"His—" Jude looked at the boy again. "Whose boy is this?"

"It doesn't matter."

"Sir, this is a military hospital. I've no authority to take in—"

"You've authority to take in anyone who'll die otherwise, I should think." His voice cracked on the last word, and he steadied it with visible effort. Snow was melting down his face and he didn't wipe it away. He crouched suddenly, to the boy's level, and said something low and quick—not English. The boy didn't answer. Didn't nod. Just stared at the middle button of the aide's coat as if memorizing it.

Vasseur straightened. When he turned back to Jude, his face had closed like a ledger being shut.

"Nurse. Look at me." She did. His eyes were bloodshot and very tired and very afraid, and none of those things were for himself. "You will forget my face. You will forget this conversation. The boy doesn't speak—he can't, and if he ever does, you'll wish he hadn't. No one is to know how he came here. Invent a story. Say he's a refugee off the road, say you bought him from a peddler, I don't care. But no one asks questions about him, and above all—" he glanced once, quickly, toward the road, toward the town, toward the direction of Headquarters as though the buildings themselves might be listening— "no one writes anything down."

The last words landed strangely, like a hand pressed flat over her heart. Her ledger sat under its floorboard not four feet from where she stood, and she felt it there the way you feel a bruise.

"I don't know what you imagine I—"

"Forget his face." He was already backing out into the storm, pulling his collar up. "Forget mine. That's the whole of it, and the coins make it honest, and honest arrangements are the only kind that hold."

He swung onto the horse and was gone—two shapes swallowed by the white, the hoofbeats erased almost instantly by the wind, as if the storm had been hired to tidy up after him.

The boy remained on the doorstep, snow gathering on his shoulders, making a small pale monument of him.

* * *

Jude stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the cold decide things for her, because she couldn't.

Regulation said report him. Regulation said an unaccompanied child of unknown provenance appearing at a military facility was a matter for the provost, for paperwork, for the adjutant's clerk and his fair hand and his three-word lives. Regulation said, in essence, hand him to the same machine that had just tried to lose him, and watch which drawer he disappeared into.

The coins sat on her shelf. She counted them without touching: enough for a month, as promised, and a month was how long until the thaw, until the regiment moved on, until whatever this was could resolve itself one way or another without her ever having chosen a side. That was the seduction of it, and she recognized the seduction because she'd spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of not choosing sides. Invisible women survived. She had built her whole life on that arithmetic.

But the boy was shaking now. The stillness had been shock, she realized—shock, or obedience trained so deep it looked like stillness—and under the soaked coat the trembling had gone past shivering into something rhythmic and wrong. Hypothermia didn't care about regulations. Neither, apparently, did she.

"Inside," she said. "Now. Before I come to my senses."

He didn't move. Of course he didn't. Why would a mute child obey a stranger's word in a language that might not even be his?

She took his sleeve—wet wool, freezing—and drew him gently over the threshold, and he came the way water comes, without resistance and without trust. She barred the door behind them and the wind threw itself against the boards once, twice, testing, then moved on to find weaker houses.

Up close, in the lamplight, she took inventory the way she'd take inventory of any casualty. Frost-nipped cheeks going pink again already—good. Hands blue at the fingertips but moving. Boots two sizes too big, stuffed with rags. No wounds she could find, no lice yet, no marks of the strap that she could see, though she knew better than to assume. And the eyes, when the fur cap came off: dark brown, enormous, and aimed at a point six inches to the left of her face, fixed on nothing, guarded like a strongbox.

He hadn't made a sound. Not a whimper through all of it. Most children his age, snatched from wherever he'd been snatched from, dumped at a strangers' door in a blizzard—they'd have cried themselves inside out by now.

"What did that man do to you?" she asked softly.

Nothing. The eyes stayed on their empty point. But something moved behind them, deep down, like a fish turning under ice.

* * *

The workshop noticed, because the workshop noticed everything.

She stripped him by the stove in the far corner—the corner she'd claimed two winters ago as unofficially hers, with Mags' old chair and a screen and a trunk that served as a desk—and wrapped him in a blanket warmed on the rail. She fed him broth in small spoonfuls. He ate mechanically, thoroughly, the way soldiers eat, cleaning the bowl and then sitting with his hands in his lap waiting to be told whether there would be more. That frightened her worse than crying would have. Crying meant a child who still expected the world to answer him.

Mags appeared at the edge of the screen around eight o'clock, drawn by the gravity of unusual events, her gray braid coming loose and her spectacles fogged from the cold ward. She looked at the boy. She looked at Jude. She was seventy years old and had assisted at amputations since before Jude was born, and she required approximately four seconds to arrive at the correct question.

"Whose is he?"

"A refugee," Jude said. "Off the road. The storm drove him to the door."

Mags considered this. Mags had buried two husbands, outlived three surgeons, and once told a visiting inspector general, to his face, that his mustache was a lie. She was not a woman easily lied to, which was why lying to her worked only when she agreed to permit it.

"Refugees," she said, "don't arrive with that coat. That coat cost more than Ferro makes in a year." She lowered herself into the chair by the stove, joints cracking, and studied the boy with clinical tenderness. "And refugees generally cry."

"He can't speak. Or won't."

"Mm." Mags extended one knotted finger toward the blanket's edge, offering rather than demanding, and after a long moment the boy's small hand emerged and touched it, briefly, like a bird testing a branch. Something in Mags' face rearranged itself. "Well," she said. "Whatever he is, he's frozen, starved, and silent, and I've been all three myself in my time. The stew's on. Fetch him a bowl before Tilda drinks the pot dry."

That was all. No questions about the coins Jude had swept into her pocket, no speculation about staff coats or midnight visitors. Just an old woman deciding, on insufficient evidence, to stand between a child and whatever was chasing him—which was, Jude thought later, the entire history of decent people in one gesture, repeated endlessly and never once recorded in any dispatch.

* * *

By eleven the ward was asleep, the fevers were holding steady, and Jude sat alone at her corner trunk with the lamp turned low, doing sums she didn't want to do.

The coins in her pocket. An officer's aide, off his proper duties, riding into a blizzard to deposit a child he plainly did not want to be depositing. *You'll wish he hadn't,* Vasseur had said—if the boy ever spoke. Which meant the boy had heard something. Which meant the boy was carrying something, and what children carry, always, is what adults did.

And: *no one writes anything down.*

She looked at the floorboard. Third from the stove. Under it, wrapped in oilcloth, forty pages of names that existed nowhere else in the world. She had always understood the ledger as a private devotion, a small stubborn candle—harmless precisely because no one powerful would ever care about the deaths of privates and pioneers and drummer boys. Powerful men didn't read lists of the poor.

Unless the list contained something they needed unread.

The boy had fallen asleep sitting upright, mid-refusal, and she'd eased him down onto the pallet she'd made behind the screen. Now she crouched beside him in the lamplight and looked properly at his face for the first time—the fine bones under the baby fat, the chapped lips, a fading bruise along the jaw shaped like a large thumb. Somebody had gripped this child's face recently, hard, and recently enough that the mark was still yellow-green.

She thought of Vasseur's bloodshot eyes. *Forget his face.*

The trouble was that forgetting faces was, in a sense, her profession's opposite. She spent her days memorizing them—the slack mask of the dying, the gray dawn expression of men waking from fever, the thousand small ways suffering arranged and rearranged a human countenance. She had learned to read faces the way clerks read columns. And the face of this sleeping child was not blank. It was a locked room, and locked rooms, in her experience, were locked by somebody, for reasons.

Somewhere across the river, in the lit windows of Headquarters, men were awake tonight wondering whether their arrangement had held. Whether the nurse had taken the coins and asked nothing. Whether the storm had covered the tracks.

Jude pried up the third floorboard, unwrapped the oilcloth, and opened her ledger to a fresh page. Her pen hovered.

If she wrote nothing, she was safe. If she wrote nothing, the boy was a refugee off the road, the coins were charity, and in the spring everyone would scatter on the wind and the ice would melt and it would be as though he had never been anyone's evidence at all.

If she wrote—

She wrote: *Boy, approx. 6 yrs, arrived night of the 14th in a blizzard. Does not speak. Bruise on jaw, right side, adult-sized grip. Brought by a staff officer whom I am instructed to forget.*

Then, after a moment, because a record without a name was just another clerk's three words:

*Name unknown. Yet.*

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the river groaned under the ice—a vast slow shifting, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and she lay listening to it, with the coins in one pocket and the boy's breath audible behind the screen, and understood that she had already made her choice, sometime between the door and the floorboard, and that from here on, every quiet day of routine she managed to keep would be a lie maintained on purpose.

Across the compound, a light was burning in the colonel's quarters. She couldn't see it from her corner. But she found she could feel it anyway, the way you feel weather coming in an old wound.
