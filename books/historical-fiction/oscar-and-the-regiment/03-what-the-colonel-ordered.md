## 3. What the Colonel Ordered

The rumor arrived the way everything important arrived at the workshop: not through the officers' channel but through the laundry.

Tilda came in from the washing sheds with her hands red to the wrist and her mouth set in a particular way that Jude had learned to read over three winters. It was the look of a woman carrying news she hadn't yet decided whom to feed it to. She dumped her basket by the stove and began pegging sheets with more force than linen required.

"Well?" Jude said, without looking up from the splint she was binding.

"Well what?"

"You've been rehearsing it since you crossed the yard. Out with it before you wear a groove in the floor."

Tilda glanced toward the door. The ward was quiet—four men sleeping off fever, one groaning softly over a frostbitten heel that Mags would likely take off him by week's end—and the corridor beyond was empty. Even so, she lowered her voice as though the walls had been commissioned by the colonel personally.

"They're saying Lieutenant Vane is dead."

Jude kept her hands moving. Binding a splint was good work for moments when your face needed somewhere else to be. "Half the officers are dead. That's what we're here for."

"Not like this." Tilda pegged another sheet, hard enough to snap the wooden toggle against the line. "Not shot in the back of the head behind his own lines. They found him past the pickets, past where any man had orders to be. And no dispatch about it. Nothing read out. Nothing written down. Just gone, and the colonel telling everyone who asks that inquiries are proceeding and they'd proceed faster if people stopped asking."

"Inquiries," Jude repeated.

"Closed," Tilda said. "That's the word Ferro heard from the quartermaster's clerk. Closed quickly. Quietly. Before the thaw, if they can manage it." She straightened up and looked at Jude directly for the first time. "And here's the other thing. You'll want to sit for this part, maybe."

"I'm standing."

"The aide who brought the boy. Captain's man, name of Pell. He's been asking around too—not about the boy, mind, he'd never be so stupid—but about whether anyone saw anything the night of the blizzard. Which road the sleigh took. Whether the nurse at the workshop keeps late hours." Tilda's voice flattened. "He's asking whether you can be trusted to forget things."

The splint binding went taut under Jude's fingers. She made herself loosen it, finish the knot, tuck the end flat the way Mags had taught her, because a bad knot on a frostbite splint could cost a man two toes and because her hands needed an honest job while her head did dishonest arithmetic.

Lieutenant Vane. She turned the name over. She had never written it in the ledger—the ledger held common soldiers, the ones whose deaths were filed as wastage, whose funerals were a sergeant and a prayer—but she knew the shape of every officer's name that passed through the town's talk, the way she knew the shape of weather coming off the river. Vane had been talked about. Young, well-connected, the sort of officer whose father's letters arrived under seal and got answered within the day. The sort of officer who did not get shot in the back of the head behind his own lines and vanish from the record like spilled water on a hot stove.

A child left at her door during the worst blizzard of the winter. Coins pressed into the aide's hand. Forget his face.

She thought of the boy asleep upstairs in the mending loft, curled around the rag horse Tilda had given him, breathing small and even. He had been there nine days now. Nobody had come for him. Nobody had asked after him. In nine days, not one officer, not one orderly, not one person with braid on their sleeve had walked into her workshop and said, where is the child?

Children were asked after. Children were screamed over, searched for, ransomed, mourned. Unless the child was not missing at all. Unless the child had been delivered exactly where someone wanted him delivered, into the hands of a woman paid to forget him, in a town full of dying men where one more small death would go into the ground unrecorded.

Her ledger sat in its drawer beneath the spare bandages, forty pages of names in her tight, even hand. For three years it had been a private act, almost a devotional one—a prayer said nightly over men the army had already stopped counting. She had never once believed it mattered to anyone but her.

Now it felt like something a man could hang for.

"Tilda," she said. "When Pell asks his questions—who answers them?"

"The laundresses answer first. Laundresses always answer first." Tilda wrung out a cloth and did not meet her eye. "I told him you keep regular hours and see nobody. I told him you're a dull woman with no curiosity and a bad hip."

"My hip is fine."

"That's why I said it. Nobody checks the ailments of a boring woman." She finally looked up. "Jude. What did that man say to you, the night he left the boy? Exactly?"

"He said—" Jude heard her own voice come out level, which cost her something. "He said forget his face. He counted coins into my hand like I was a tollgate. And then he stood in the snow for a moment, and he looked at me, and I want to say it was shame, but I've seen shame on men's faces and this was different. It was the look of a man checking that a thing is done. Like bolting a door."

"And then he rode back toward the colonel's quarters."

"So Ferro says."

"So Ferro says," Tilda agreed grimly, and picked up her empty basket, and paused at the door long enough to add, quietly, "Whatever that boy is, he isn't nobody. And whatever happened to that lieutenant, somebody means for it to stay buried. Mind which side of the spade you're standing on."

She went out. The sheets snapped on the line in the wind off the river.

Jude finished the splint, checked the fevers, wrote the evening doses in her dispensary book—all the lawful records, the ones any inspecting officer might read—and only then, when the ward lamp was turned low and the men slept, did she open the drawer and take out the ledger and sit with it unopened on her knees for a long time.

Then she dipped her pen and, beneath the night's names—Private Aldous Reeve, died of fever; Private Sam Hodge, taken the foot—she wrote, in the smallest hand she could manage without disguise:

*Lt. ——— V., d. behind own lines, no inquiry.*

She had never written an officer before. Her pen hovered over the blank where the Christian name should go, and stayed hovering, because she realized she did not know it. No one in the workshop knew it. The lieutenant had been talked about for a year and no one had ever once used his first name, because officers' first names belonged to a country the rest of them didn't have papers for.

*Find out*, she wrote instead, beside it, as a note to herself. Then she closed the book, put it back beneath the bandages, and climbed the stairs to check on Oscar, who was asleep, and alive, and breathing, and—for how much longer?—hers.

Sergeant Brannigan had learned, across twenty-two years of service, that the truth of any regiment lived in three places: the pay book, the punishment book, and the mouths of sergeants. The first two could be doctored. The third had to be killed one mouth at a time, and that was slower work, and lately someone had been doing it fast.

He stood at the window of his quarters above the guardroom—he was permitted a window, an old courtesy from a colonel long dead, and he guarded it like a commission—and watched the light die over the frozen river. Below him, two of Ashworth's chosen men crossed the yard toward the officers' mess, walking with the particular ease of soldiers who had been told their duties were flexible. Corporal Dunne. Private Sallow. Brannigan had signed both their conduct sheets himself, years ago, for small cruelties. A wise colonel kept such men close. Ashworth was a wise colonel, in the way a knife is wise.

Behind him, his batman, young Ollerenshaw, was laying out the supper Brannigan would eat half of, as he ate half of everything now, because the wound in his gut had opinions about quantity. Three years since the failed charge at the river crossing, and the ball still lodged where no surgeon on either side of the war would risk going after it, doing its slow work like a tenant who meant to inherit the house.

"Sergeant," Ollerenshaw said, in the tone he used when he'd been holding a thing in all day. "They're saying Lieutenant Vane's death is to be entered as fell in action against irregulars. Patrol contact, beyond the lines. Honorable. Closed."

"Who's saying?"

"The clerk's boy. He copied it himself. Dated tomorrow." The boy hesitated. "Dated before the inquiry meets, Sergeant. If you follow me."

Brannigan followed him. He followed him all the way to a cold place in his chest that wasn't the wound.

An inquiry dated before it met was not an inquiry. It was a signature waiting for ink. He had seen it done twice before in his service—once for a major's son who'd beaten a corporal senseless, once for a supply contract that had rotted five hundred men's boots in a wet autumn—and both times the machinery had worked smooth as a well-oiled lock, because the machinery was built by the same smiths as the locks.

But Vane. Vane was different. Whatever else could be said of the lieutenant—and Brannigan had said plenty of it, silently, watching the young man ride past with his fine coat and his fine horse—he had been the son of a man with a seat and a name, the kind of father whose grief could not be managed with a closed file and a polite letter. Unless the father had been managed first. Unless there were letters traveling south right now, under seal, explaining matters in terms an anxious father would accept because accepting them was easier than the alternative.

Or unless the father already knew, and had ordered it.

Brannigan turned from the window. His gut gave its usual slow twist, and he breathed through it the way he breathed through everything now—in through the nose, slow, patient, a man negotiating daily with his own body and losing by inches.

"Ollerenshaw. The nurse at the workshop. Kavanagh. What's the talk about her?"

The boy blinked. "Talk? There's no talk, Sergeant. She keeps herself quiet. Good name among the men—she writes to the families of the dead, does her. Proper letters, not the official rubbish. My mother got one when my brother died of the fever. She says it was the only paper that ever told her true."

"She writes to the families," Brannigan said slowly.

"Every one she can find an address for. The officers' letters go through channels and arrive six months late saying nothing. Hers go direct."

Brannigan sat down heavily on his chair, and for a moment he let himself feel the thing he felt, before he folded it away again. A woman who wrote true letters to the families of dead men. A woman keeping her own record. A woman who had been handed a child by Captain Pell on the worst night of the winter, with money and an order to forget.

Pell. He should have known. Pell was Ashworth's creature from the old campaigns—the kind of officer who did the tasks that didn't get written down, and did them without being told twice. If Pell had carried a child through a blizzard to a nurse's door, then the child was not abandoned. Abandoned children were left at convents, at farmhouses, at church steps in towns where nobody knew anybody. Not carried thirty versts through a killing storm to the one woman in the regiment known for writing down truths.

Unless the woman was known for something else too. Known for being invisible. Known for taking what was left at her door and asking nothing, because asking was a habit invisible women couldn't afford.

Someone had chosen her the way you choose a grave: deep, quiet, unlikely to be disturbed.

"Sergeant?" Ollerenshaw said. "You've gone greyer than usual."

"Eat your supper." Brannigan reached for his stick and levered himself upright, because sitting with a thought was how thoughts became regrets. "I'm going to walk as far as the workshop. Doctor's orders, if anyone asks. The cold is good for the wound."

"The cold will kill you, Sergeant."

"The cold has been trying for three years. It'll have to join a queue." He settled his greatcoat on his shoulders and paused at the door. "Ollerenshaw. If anyone asks what errand I'm on—you don't know. You carried my stick to the cobbler last week and I've been limping badly ever since. Let me be a fool about my boots."

The boy grinned despite himself. "Yes, Sergeant."

The yard was blue with dusk and the wind off the river cut like the memory of the charge, and Brannigan walked through it slowly, leaning on his stick, an old sergeant out airing his wound, entirely unremarkable, while inside him a thought grew colder and harder with every step:

There was a lieutenant dead behind his own lines, and an inquiry pre-dated and pre-closed, and a child erased, and a woman paid to forget. Four facts. Any one of them alone was the ordinary filth of army life, survivable, ignorable, the sort of thing a man learned to step around like ordure on a road.

But together they made a shape. And Brannigan had been a soldier too long not to know that when four filthy facts make a clean shape, the shape is a conspiracy, and conspiracies of that size do not stop at one body. They stop when everyone who could testify is dead, or bought, or marching somewhere far away under new orders.

His brother's name sat in a ledger in that workshop. He had not asked her to write it—hadn't the words for it yet, hadn't earned them—but he knew it was there. He had seen her ledger once, by accident, a glimpse of columns of small names in a small neat hand, and he had thought then: *someone is counting*. Someone in this whole rotten machine is counting what the machine throws away.

If the machine found out it was being counted—

At the workshop door he stopped, breath smoking, and knocked.

It opened on lamplight and the smell of carbolic, and Nurse Kavanagh looked up at him with eyes that had already heard the rumors—he could see it in her face, the same dawning unease he carried—and she stepped back to let him in, and said quietly:

"Sergeant. I think you'd better tell me what you know. Because I think I know what you know."

"No, ma'am," Brannigan said, stamping the snow from his boots. "You know a piece of it. So do I. And I've just understood the piece we're both missing, and God forgive me, I hope I'm wrong." He let the door close behind him. "Show me where you keep that ledger of yours. And then show me the boy."
