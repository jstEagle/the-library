## 5. Visitors

Colonel Ashworth had a theory about inspections, and it was this: a building told you everything before anyone in it opened their mouth. The state of the floors told you about discipline. The state of the stores told you about honesty. And the state of small, unconsidered things—a bucket left where a bucket should not be, a coat hung on the wrong nail—told you about fear.

So he arrived at the workshop hospital without warning, at half past eight on a gray Tuesday morning, with only his aide Captain Pell and two orderlies, and he stood for a moment in the yard and let the place speak to him.

It spoke of competence. That was unusual, and he noted it. The woodpile was stacked to the eaves and covered against snow. The drainage channel along the south wall had been cleared of ice, which meant someone had thought about meltwater in January, weeks before any sensible person would need to. Smoke rose from three chimneys, thin and even, banked fires rather than wasteful ones. A laundry line ran between two posts, frozen shirts stiff as boards, hung with a spacing that said *order* even through the frost.

"Who commands here?" he asked Pell.

"No one commands, sir. It's civilian contract. There's a matron of sorts. Kavanagh. Irish."

"A woman runs my workshop."

"The army's woman, sir. She draws regimental pay."

Ashworth smiled at that, because it was the correct thing to smile at, and because it was useful to know how Pell phrased things when he thought no one was listening. *My* workshop. He had let the possessive slip naturally enough. Good. A man who owned his errors aloud was easier to steer than one who hid them.

The lieutenant's affair had been closed—or was closing—and closing affairs required tidiness, and tidiness required knowing what lay in every corner of every room within a day's ride of the inquiry's paper trail. The workshop was a corner. He intended to look into it personally.

He crossed the yard. The door opened before he reached it.

* * *

Jude Kavanagh was not what he expected, which annoyed him mildly, since he prided himself on expecting correctly. He had pictured a stout widow, floury and flustered. Instead: a tall, spare woman of perhaps thirty-five, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred with old scalds, eyes the flat gray of the river ice. She did not curtsy. She inclined her head the way one does to weather.

"Colonel. You'll want the ward first. It's warmest."

"You know why I've come?"

"To inspect, sir." She stepped back to admit them. "We're proud of what we do here. I'd show you whether you'd come or not."

He liked that answer and distrusted it equally, which he had learned over thirty years of service was the correct posture toward most things people said with their chins level.

The ward was long and low, built into the riverbank so that half its wall was stone and the stone sweated cold. Two rows of cots, twenty-two occupied by his count, men from the winter fevers and the frostbitten and the two or three mangled by the sawmill upriver. The air smelled of carbolic and clean linen and, beneath it, woodsmoke. Every cot had a slate at its head. He stopped at the nearest and read it.

*Pvt. R. Osei, 4th Foot. Fever, day 9. Taking broth.*

A name. A regiment. A condition. A day count. He moved down the row and every slate bore the same careful hand, upright and unhurried.

"You keep records," he said.

"I keep records."

"Official returns come through the surgeon-major's office. They are often—" he chose the word gently "—incomplete."

"Returns go where they're ordered to go, Colonel. My slates stay on my cots." A beat. "Unless you're ordering otherwise."

There it was. Not insolence—she had kept her voice level, almost bored—but a door held open just wide enough to see the wall behind it. She was telling him she knew there were orders in the world that could reach into her ward, and she was daring him to issue one in front of witnesses.

"Not today," he said pleasantly. "Show me your stores."

She showed him the stores. Flour, salt pork, lint, splints, a locked cabinet of laudanum whose key she wore on a cord at her neck. The counts matched the book. Of course they matched the book. A woman who ran a workshop like this did not make arithmetic errors; she made other kinds.

He was turning from the shelf when he heard it—a sound too small for soldiers to register but precisely calibrated for a man who had spent decades listening for what did not belong. A wooden clatter. Then a hush that was itself a sound: the hush of twenty-two men pretending very hard to be ill.

At the far end of the ward, behind a screen of stretched canvas, something moved.

"What's behind there?"

"The dying, usually." She said it without inflection. "This week, nobody. It's the quiet corner. Fever cases sleep better away from the coughing."

"Then it won't disturb you to have it looked at."

For one instant—half a heartbeat, no more—something crossed her face. Not guilt. He knew guilt; guilt was cheap and common and he could smell it through a brick wall. This was calculation. She was measuring him. Then it was gone, smoothed under a nurse's tired courtesy.

"It's your workshop, Colonel."

He walked the length of the ward, boots loud on the boards, and put back the canvas screen himself.

* * *

The boy sat cross-legged on a stool beside the last cot, where an old private slept with his bandaged foot propped on a pillow. The child was maybe six, dark-haired, thin in the way of children who had been fed recently but not for long. In his hands was a piece of charcoal. On the floor around him, on every available surface—the boards, the leg of the cot, a flattened crate—were drawings.

Faces. Dozens of faces. Some crude, some startlingly not. Soldiers' faces mostly, heavy-jawed and tired. But near his knee, drawn and redrawn until the paper—no, the board itself—was gray with repetition, was another figure. Taller. Fine-coated. A face with careful lines around a mouth that might have been smiling.

The boy looked up at Ashworth with the flat, unblinking stare of an animal deciding whether to bolt.

"And who is this?" Ashworth asked, still pleasantly.

"A foundling," Kavanagh said, behind him. "Came in with the last supply convoy from the west. Lost, we think. Or abandoned. There's been no one to claim him."

"Foundlings are a matter for the parish authorities."

"The nearest parish authority is forty miles and a frozen river away. Until the thaw, he's mine."

"Yours." He let the word sit in the air. Then, to the boy, in a tone of grandfatherly warmth that he could produce like a coin from behind an ear: "And what is your name, lad?"

The boy said nothing. He looked at Ashworth the way he had been looking, unblinking, and then—this was the moment Ashworth felt the first true cold along his spine—he looked down at the drawing at his knee. At the fine-coated man. Then back up.

As if comparing them.

"He doesn't speak," Kavanagh said. "Trauma of the road, Mags thinks. The surgeon's assistant. Children go silent sometimes. It comes back."

"Does it." He crouched, slowly, joints cracking, and studied the drawings. The fine-coated man appeared eleven times that he could count without moving. Eleven times, and each time the mouth was different—smiling, then not smiling, then smiling again, as if the boy were arguing with the dead about something. "These are very good," he said to the child. "Did you know this man?"

Nothing. The charcoal turned in small fingers.

"Where did you learn to draw like this?"

Nothing.

"He doesn't understand English well yet," Kavanagh offered. "Foreign, possibly. The convoy came through the border towns."

"Mm." Ashworth stood. His knees complained. He ignored them. "Captain Pell will enter the child in the workshop rolls as a dependent. Provisional. Pending identification." He watched her face as he said it. Nothing moved in it. Excellent discipline. "We can't have unregistered persons drifting about a military installation, Mrs.—it is Mrs.?"

"Miss."

"Miss Kavanagh. We'll need particulars for the roll. Age, origin, how exactly he came to your door. Full particulars."

"I wasn't at the door when he was set down, sir. It was a blizzard night. Whoever left him didn't wait for particulars, and neither did I. He'd have frozen by dawn. I imagine the roll can say that."

"I imagine it can say many things." He smiled at her, warmly, and let the smile do its work. "You'll forgive an old soldier's curiosity. We had a loss recently. A lieutenant. Well regarded. There was an inquiry—it concluded—and in such inquiries one develops a habit of noticing children. Sons visit camps, you know. Fathers write home about showing them the regiment. Sentimental nonsense, but there it is."

The ward was very quiet. Twenty-two men had achieved a silence beyond illness, beyond death even—a silence with sweat in it.

"I'm sorry for the officer's family," Kavanagh said.

"Yes. So was I." He straightened his gloves, finger by finger, taking his time. "Well. The stores are in good order. The ward is in good order. Your slates are—thorough." He paused over the word. "Thoroughness is a virtue, Miss Kavanagh. Like all virtues, it has its season. In wartime, thoroughness about the wrong particulars can look like—" he searched, delicately, "—ambition. And ambition in persons outside the chain of command makes staff officers nervous. Nervous staff officers write memoranda. Memoranda become reassignments."

"Reassignments."

"There is a general hospital at the front. Forward of the front, really. Tents. They lose nurses there at nearly the rate they lose orderlies—not to enemy action, mostly. To the work. It grinds them." He said it as a man remarks on weather. "A woman of your abilities would be wasted there. Which is why I mention it only in the abstract. As a category of thing that happens."

"To other people."

"To inattentive people." He let that land, then softened everything above it. "But you are attentive. Anyone can see it. The drains alone tell me so." He gestured vaguely toward the south wall. "Attend carefully, then, to your workshop, your patients, and your—foundling—and I see no reason the winter shouldn't pass quietly for everyone."

"Everyone," she repeated.

"Everyone I care about," he said, and made it sound like a kindness.

* * *

In the yard, pulling on his second glove, he said to Pell, low: "The boy."

"Yes, sir?"

"When the thaw comes and the regiment moves, dependents are mustered out to the rear depots. See that the entry in the rolls gives no description. No age. No distinguishing marks. 'One dependent female child'—no." He considered. "'One dependent minor.' Let it be vague. Vagueness is a kind of kindness, Pell. It leaves everyone room."

"Yes, sir. And the nurse?"

Ashworth looked back at the workshop. Smoke rising thin and even from three chimneys. The frozen shirts hanging in their disciplined rows. Somewhere inside, a woman was writing names on slates with an upright, unhurried hand, and somewhere in that same building, he would have wagered a month's pay, there was a book or a page or a habit of mind that wrote other names besides—the names officers omitted, the names that cluttered tidy conclusions. He had closed the lieutenant's inquiry with testimony that would hold unless someone, somewhere, kept a fuller account than the army kept. People who kept fuller accounts existed. He had shot two of them, in a manner of speaking, over the years. Shot was dramatic. Corrected. Corrected thoroughly.

"She stays," he said. "Watched. Quietly. If she writes letters, I read them. If she talks in her sleep, I want to know to whom. And find out who left that boy on her doorstep. Someone carried him through a blizzard, and someone paid them to do it, and whoever gave the order—" he settled his hat brim against the wind coming off the river "—was trying to be kind to me. I intend to thank them properly."

Pell went pale in the way subordinates did, which Ashworth always found restful.

"One more thing. The drawings. The fine-coated gentleman. Have someone copy one, discreetly, before the child destroys them. Children burn their drawings, Pell, when they're frightened. And I have a suspicion our young friend is going to be frightened soon."

"By whom, sir?"

Ashworth smiled out across the ice, at the far shore, at the gray sky, at the whole obedient winter.

"That depends," he said, "entirely on Miss Kavanagh's memory. Women like her remember everything, Captain. It's their single flaw. Fortunately—" he drew the glove tight "—the cure is simple. All we ever have to do is give them something better to remember."
