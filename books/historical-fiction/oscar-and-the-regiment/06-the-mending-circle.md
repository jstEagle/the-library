## 6. The Mending Circle

Tilda found out about the boy before I told her, which was inevitable. A washerwoman knows everything that passes through a building the way a river knows every stone in its bed.

She cornered me at the boiling coppers on Thursday morning, steam rising around her like a curtain, and said without preamble: "There's a child sleeping in your storeroom."

"There's a child mending shirts in my storeroom," I said. "He earns his keep."

"He eats your keep too. I counted the bread."

"You count everything, Tilda."

"I do." She wrung a sheet with hands like knotted rope and looked at me over the steam. "And I'm telling you that whatever you've done, you'd best not have done it alone."

That was Tilda's whole philosophy in one sentence. Nothing survived alone. Not laundry, not winter, not war. She had buried a husband at Salamanca and two sons of fever since, and she had come through it by the simple method of never letting anyone else come through anything by themselves. She was sixty if she was a day and could carry a full copper of scalding water up the stairs without pausing, and she had decided, apparently, that the boy at my door was hers now as much as mine.

I hadn't asked her to decide anything. That was the trouble with Tilda. She decided for you, and then she was right, and then you were grateful, which was the most infuriating sequence in the world.

"He's to be kept quiet," I said. "If anyone asks—"

"Nobody will ask me. Nobody asks the washerwoman anything. That's the one gift of being invisible." She smiled without much humor. "You'd know about that, Jude."

* * *

Mags came next, though Mags required no telling at all, because Mags missed nothing and forgot less.

She was seventy-three years old and had served three surgeons in forty years, and she moved through the ward now like a slow tide, doing the small work the young orderlies disdained: turning men who couldn't turn themselves, spooning broth into mouths too weak to lift their heads, holding hands while the surgeons did what they did. The soldiers called her Mother Winter behind her back and to her face, when they were well enough to joke, and she answered to both.

I found her sitting beside Oscar on the third evening, on the low stool by the storeroom door, watching him draw.

He drew constantly now. He had worn the charcoal down to a stub working on the same face over and over—a man in a fine coat, tall, with careful hands—and he would not be drawn away from it. When Mags sat down he tensed, but she simply folded her hands in her lap and waited, the way she waited with dying men, as though time were something she owned plenty of.

"That's his father," she said to me quietly, when we stood aside together. "Or someone he's lost. Children draw what they're grieving."

"You can't know that."

"I know it the way you know weather." She patted my arm with a hand like dry paper. "You took him in knowing there was a reason he needed taking in. You don't need me to tell you the reason is still out there, walking about in a red coat."

"I know it, Mags."

"Then hear the rest." Her eyes, pale and hooded as a hawk's, held mine. "Whatever comes for him will come through this workshop, and it'll come asking questions of people like us, because nobody thinks to guard their tongues in front of the old and the invisible. So we'd best decide now what our tongues say."

"And what do they say?"

"They say there's been a boy here all winter, sister's son to the washerwoman's late husband's family, sent down from the north because his mother's dead and there was nowhere else." She said it smoothly, as though she had rehearsed it, and perhaps she had; perhaps she had worked it out days ago while turning soldiers and watching him draw. "A lie is a bandage, Jude. You put them on wounds so they can close underneath. You've been putting one on this child since the night you found him."

I looked across the room at Oscar, bent over his paper, charcoal moving in small precise strokes, and felt the truth of it settle over me like snowfall. Somewhere between the blizzard and the morning, between fear and responsibility, I had stopped thinking of him as evidence and started thinking of him as a boy. And boys needed stories wrapped around them the same way they needed blankets.

"Sister's son to Tilda's late husband's family," I repeated.

"Mags's memory confirms it. Old women are never doubted. It's the last advantage of age."

* * *

Ferro was the hardest, because Ferro noticed things the way an axe notices grain.

The carpenter had a shop at the far end of the workshop where he made legs. That was the trade of the place, really—the hospital was the front of the house and the leg-shop was the heart of it, because this war fed men into its mill and spat them back shortened, and somebody had to measure the stumps and carve the willow and fit the leather sockets and teach men to walk again on wood. Ferro did it all with a patience that seemed geological. He was maybe fifty, broad and silent, with forearms like the beams he worked, and he spoke perhaps forty words a day and meant thirty-nine of them.

Oscar drifted into the shop the way children drift toward any room with interesting smells—wood shavings and glue and lamp oil—and Ferro let him stand and watch, and then let him hold things, and then let him sand blocks of willow smooth under a big guiding hand.

I saw it happen over a week. I came in with dressings or messages and found the boy on the carpenter's bench stool, sanding, while Ferro worked in the companionable silence of two people who both preferred not talking.

But on the ninth day I came in and Ferro was standing very still, and Oscar had gone white as paper, and there was a finished leg lying on the bench between them—a fine one, willow and leather, polished like furniture—and the boy was looking at it as though it were a corpse.

"He asked whose it was for," Ferro said to me later, while Oscar slept in the storeroom. "I told him. A sergeant. Lost it above the knee at Leipzig. He's walking on the other one already, learning." Ferro turned a chisel over in his huge hands. "Boy went grey. Ran behind the woodpile and was sick."

"The lieutenant," I said. "His father may have lost more than life."

"Aye." Ferro set the chisel down. "Jude. Who is he?"

And I told him. All of it—the aide, the coins, the orders to forget a face, the fine-coated man in the drawings, the inquiry rumor drifting down from the colonel's quarters like smoke through a floorboard. Ferro listened without interrupting, which was his habit, and when I finished he was quiet for a long moment.

"You understand," he said finally, "that if they come looking, they won't only look at you. They'll ask everyone. Washerwomen, carpenters, old women. People talk when they're frightened, and frightened is easy to arrange."

"I know."

"So." He picked the chisel back up and began, absently, to square an edge that was already square. "Then we'd best not be frightened. And we'd best all tell the same story. Sister's son to Tilda's people. Mother dead. Come down from the north." He glanced at the storeroom door. "I'll carve him some toys. Boys with toys are ordinary. Ordinary is armor."

That was Ferro's whole speech for the month, and it covered everything.

* * *

It happened without anyone naming it, the way ice forms without anyone deciding.

By mid-January Oscar ate breakfast with Tilda at the wash-house table while she taught him to fold linen into squares sharp enough to cut. He spent afternoons in Ferro's shop sanding wood and learning the names of tools, and Ferro carved him a horse from a knot of birch, mane and all, and the boy carried it everywhere and slept with it under his cheek. In the evenings Mags sat with him and taught him the herbs in her basket by smell—chamomile, yarrow, comfrey—because, she said, a child who knew useful things was a child worth protecting twice.

Nobody said the word *protect*. Nobody said *ours*. There was no meeting, no pact sworn over candles. What happened instead was smaller and stronger: Tilda started washing his clothes without being asked. Mags adjusted the ward rounds so someone's eye was always on the storeroom corridor. Ferro moved his glue pot to block the view of the storeroom door from the yard. Small rearrangements of the day, each innocent alone, and together a wall.

One night I came down past midnight with a fever case needing quiet, and found Mags asleep in a chair outside the storeroom, blanket over her knees, and Tilda's spare shawl folded on a second chair where Brannigan sometimes sat when his wound kept him from his bunk. The boy's candle was out. The workshop breathed around us, the frozen river groaning under the ice below the windows, and I stood there a moment in the dark and understood that I had stopped keeping a secret and started keeping a child—that the ledger in my trunk and the boy behind the door had become two halves of the same work, the recording of what powerful men wanted unwritten.

Mags opened one eye. "Go to bed, girl. I'm awake anyway."

"You were asleep."

"Old women sleep with one ear open. It's how we've survived everyone." She resettled herself. "He laughed today. At Ferro's horse. Did you hear it?"

I had. A small rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years.

"It'll bring trouble," Mags said, not unkindly. "Laughter travels. But you can't raise a child in silence and expect him to live. So we'll take the trouble when it comes." She closed the eye again. "We're four old women and a carpenter against a colonel, if it comes to counting. Poor odds."

"We have a sergeant too."

"Aye. Brannigan sits with him sometimes. Teaches him dice with three buttons and a cup." A pause. "That Irishman knows more about the lieutenant's death than he says, by the way. Watch him. Men like Brannigan carry guilt like a stone in the boot—they walk crooked until they set it down somewhere. Your ledger might be where he sets his down."

I filed that away, as I filed everything, in the place where names waited to be written.

* * *

On the last Sunday of January, Tilda brought Oscar to the wash-house table and gave him his first real task: sorting the regimental shirts by company, by the marks stitched inside the collars. He worked an hour, solemn as a clerk, and earned a heel of bread with honey, and Tilda watched him eat with an expression she would have denied under oath.

"He should learn his letters," she said to me, low. "Mags has a primer left over from her surgeon's boy. Every name must be written somewhere, or it never happened. That's your gospel, isn't it, Jude? Then give the boy the alphabet. Give him the means to write himself down."

I thought of my ledger in its trunk, the pages filling nightly with the small names the dispatches left out. I thought of a boy who would one day want to know his own name written large, in ink, witnessed.

"All in good time," I said. "First he has to be safe enough to learn."

"Safe," Tilda snorted, wringing out a sheet. "No such thing. There's only together and alone, and together is safer." She nodded toward the yard, and I followed her glance through the frosted window.

Sergeant Brannigan stood in the snow by the woodpile, letting Oscar show him the birch horse, bending down to examine it with enormous seriousness, saying something that made the boy smile. Behind them, the river lay locked under a foot of ice, patient and silent, holding everything beneath it exactly where it had been all winter.

I remember thinking: how long can a river stay frozen?

I remember thinking: not forever.

Three days later, a runner came up from the colonel's quarters with a message sealed in black wax, and the message said that Colonel Ashworth would inspect the workshop hospital on Thursday morning, personally, and that all personnel and dependents would be presented for review.

*Dependents.*

Someone had used the word. Someone had talked.
