## 7. Names in the Ice

The cold came up through the floor of the workshop like a debt being collected, and Sergeant Declan Brannigan had learned to read it the way other men read weather. It started in his hip — the old wound, the one from Talavera that the surgeons had probed twice and cursed over three times — and it climbed his spine in the small hours, joint by joint, until he lay awake on his pallet behind the carpenter's shop counting the cracks in the ceiling beams and listening to the river.

The river talked at night. That was something no one told you about a frozen river: it was not silent. The ice groaned and ticked and shifted against its banks like an old man turning in bed, and Brannigan had come to think of it as conversation. The river remembering what was under it. What had been put there.

He knew what was under it.

Not bodies — the dead of the charge had been brought back, most of them, laid out on trestles in this very workshop while their officers wrote tidy letters to tidy widows. But things. A drum. A color sergeant's sash. A boy's boot, one boot only, that Ferro had fished out with a grappling hook because it kept snagging the nets and Ferro could not stand a thing that snagged. And further down, where the current ran faster than any of them liked to admit beneath all that ice, whatever else the winter had taken and would give back in April when the thaw came, floating up like accusations.

Brannigan swung his legs off the pallet and sat in the dark, breathing through it. Sixty-one years old. Thirty-eight years a soldier. He could still rise without groaning if he bit down on it, and he did, because groaning was a habit and habits were how men died before they died.

There was a lamp burning in the main room of the hospital. He could see its light under the door, thin as a knife blade. She was awake again. She was always awake again.

* * *

Jude Kavanagh wrote at night because the nights were the only hours that belonged to her.

That was what Brannigan had decided, watching her from across the workshop these past months, in the way a man watches anything when he has too much time and too little future. By day she belonged to the surgeons and the stench and the endless laundry of the wounded, moving through the wards with her sleeves rolled and her face set like a woman crossing a yard full of dogs. By day she was regulation and lamp oil and the colonel's requisitions. But at night, when the lamps were trimmed to embers and even Mags had gone to her cot, Jude sat at the little table by the window with her ledger open, and she wrote.

Brannigan had seen what she wrote. Not by sneaking — he was past sneaking, and anyway she'd have caught him; the woman missed nothing — but once, in November, she'd left the book open while she went to fetch more ink, and he'd looked. Names. Ranks. Regiments. Dates. Little notes in a hand so small and neat it looked printed: *died of fever, 3rd of November, no kin listed.* *Private Halloran, shot for desertion though he was going for water; note filed by Ensign Pryce who was not present.*

Names that weren't anywhere else. He'd known that in his gut before he knew it in his head, because Brannigan had spent thirty-eight years learning which paper mattered and which paper was decoration. The dispatches went south in the colonel's leather case with the gilt corners, and they were beautiful, and they were lies of omission — nothing forged, nothing you could point to, just whole fields of men quietly absent, as if the winter had eaten them and no one had fed them to it.

He stood in the doorway now, wrapped in his greatcoat, and watched her write. She didn't look up.

"You'll ruin your eyes," he said.

"You'll ruin your hip standing in a draft." She dipped her pen. "Sit down, Sergeant. The chair doesn't bite."

He crossed the room slowly. Not slow from pain — well, some from pain — but slow because he had been composing this visit for eleven days and he wanted the walking to do part of the talking. He lowered himself into the chair opposite her table. Up close, in the lamplight, she looked younger than she let herself look by day. That was a trick he'd seen women in hospitals learn early. You made yourself plain and hard during working hours or the work ate you alive.

"Can't sleep," he said.

"I know. Your boots squeak on the third floorboard. They've squeaked every night since December."

"And yet you never bar the door."

"No," she said, and looked at him then, level and unhurried. "I never do."

There it was again, that quality she had. Most people, when they were kind, wanted you to see it. Jude's kindness was like the ledger itself: done in private, shown to no one, deniable. Brannigan had decided months ago that he trusted her more than any officer he'd served under, and he had served under some good ones, and the deciding had cost him nothing because it had cost the officers everything first.

"I've a favor," he said. "And I'll thank you to hear the whole of it before you answer, because the whole of it is longer than the asking."

She capped the ink and folded her hands. Waiting. She was good at waiting. Another thing the ward taught you.

"It's my brother," Brannigan said.

Something moved in her face — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. Whatever story she'd built about him, it had just shifted, and she was honest enough to let him see it shift.

"You've a brother."

"Had. Michael. Two years younger than me, which made him the clever one, God help us both." His voice came out steady. He'd rehearsed that too. "Michael Kavanagh—"

"Kavanagh." Her eyebrows went up. "You're Kavanaghs."

"Brannigan by our mother. She married twice, and the second husband's name stuck harder than the first, the way names do. Michael stayed a Kavanagh because Michael stayed himself in all things, and it killed him, eventually, but not the way you'd think." Brannigan looked at the window, at the black pane with the frost ferns growing on it. Easier to say it to the frost. "He enlisted two years after me. Younger man, better soldier — don't make that face, Nurse, it's true, and I'll tell you why. Because he asked questions. A good soldier asks questions. He wants to know why a hill is worth taking before he takes it, and nine times in ten the answer is that some major needs it for his report, and a man who knows that walks slower up the hill and lives longer. Michael walked slow. Michael lived long."

"But not forever."

"Not forever." He worked his jaw. Outside, far off, the ice gave a long groan, like something turning over in its sleep. "Last October. The action at the ford — you'll not have it in any dispatch worth the paper. The colonel ordered the crossing at dawn. The ford was held. Everyone with eyes knew it was held; the scouts said it, Michael said it, I said it, and I was staff sergeant then and my saying it should have weighed something. It weighed nothing. We went in at first light in four feet of freezing water, and the fire came off the far bank like scythes through wheat, and we went in anyway, and we came out with forty-one fewer men and nothing gained. Nothing. The ground didn't change hands. The river didn't change course. Forty-one men paid for a morning."

Jude hadn't moved. But her hands had unclasped themselves, and her whole body had gone very still, the stillness of someone listening with more than her ears.

"The dispatch called it a reconnaissance in force," Brannigan said. "Slightly opposed. Three casualties." He laughed, short and ugly. "Three. As if the other thirty-eight had wandered off to pick berries."

"And Michael?"

"Michael got it in the water. Shot through the chest going in, second wave. He was carrying the sergeant-major's whistle because the sergeant-major had already gone down, and a man with a whistle is a target, and Michael knew it, and he blew it anyway the whole way across. Rallying. Calling the line forward." Brannigan's throat closed on it. He breathed. "They found him at the far shallows with the whistle still in his fist. Dented it, the ball did. Ferro has it. Wouldn't give it up to the officers, and they didn't press, because pressing Ferro about a dented whistle wasn't worth the trouble."

"Why are they not in your account?" Jude asked quietly. "The forty-one. Why aren't they in mine?"

"That," Brannigan said, "is the favor."

He leaned forward. The hip screamed and he let it.

"After the action, there was to be a return of casualties. Standard form. Names, ranks, next of kin, cause. I helped compile it — I knew the men, I knew the spellings, half of them couldn't spell themselves and trusted me to keep their mothers' names straight. Forty-one names in my hand, fair copy, signed. Captain Ellery took it. Captain Ellery brought it back an hour later with orders from the colonel to reduce it. The engagement was to be entered as a skirmish, and skirmishes carry light returns, and a light return carries light blame. So we struck it down. I struck it down. My own hand, striking my own brother's name off the list of the dead so that the list would flatter a man whose worst wound that morning was chilblains."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself.

"They struck twenty-six," Brannigan went on, when he could. "Kept fifteen because fifteen could be attributed to drowning and exposure, and weather kills neutrally, weather can't be blamed on anyone's judgment. But twenty-six died of shot, and shot means orders, and orders mean inquiry, so twenty-six names went into the drawer and the drawer went quiet. Fifteen families got letters. Twenty-six got silence." He looked at her. "Fifteen got widows. Twenty-six got nothing."

Jude opened the ledger.

She did it without a word, without ceremony — turned back through the pages, past the winter's dead, past November, to a blank space, and uncapped the ink, and wrote at the head of it, in her small print hand:

*Ford action, 14 October. Struck from the return by order of Col. Ashworth. Corrected here.*

Then she looked up. "Give them to me."

Brannigan had prepared arguments. He had prepared appeals, and logic, and if it came to it a sort of blackmail — the knowledge that she already sheltered secrets, that one more would weigh less than she feared. He had prepared all of it across eleven sleepless nights, and now none of it was needed, because the woman had simply turned to a fresh page and asked for the names as if he were handing her a marketing list.

His eyes burned. He blinked it away. There was no time in a soldier's life for weeping and he was not going to start in a warm room with ink in it, but God, the relief of it — thirty-eight years of carrying things for men who wouldn't carry them for themselves, and here at the end of it, a nurse with a notebook doing in ten seconds what the entire apparatus of the army would not do in a hundred years.

"Sergeant-Major Thomas Oduya," he began. "Drowned, or near enough — took a ball first, we think. Wife, Adaeze, in Canterbury. Then Corporal James Whitlock..."

He gave them one at a time. He gave them slow, because some of them he hadn't spoken aloud since October and the shapes of the names had gone stiff in his mouth, and because Jude wrote each one fully — rank, regiment, age where he knew it, kin where he knew it, and where he didn't know it she wrote *kin unknown, inquire of the parish,* and the *inquire* undid him worse than anything, because it meant the book intended to go on after tonight. It meant follow-up. It meant somebody, somewhere, someday, might actually knock on a door in Canterbury.

"Private Michael Kavanagh," he said at last. "Aged thirty-four. Killed in the water, shot through the chest, blowing the rally. Brother to the compiler of this entry."

Jude's pen stopped.

"He'd want that line?" she asked. "The last one?"

"He'd laugh himself sick at it," Brannigan said. "Put it down."

She put it down. Then she blotted the page, and sat back, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't file under any of the faces nurses wore.

"There were twenty-six," she said. "I count twenty-five."

"Twenty-five and Michael makes twenty-six."

"Twenty-five including Michael." She turned the page back and ran her finger down the column, lips moving. "Count them yourself if you doubt me. One through twenty-four, and Michael twenty-fifth. Someone's missing, Sergeant. Who did you leave out?"

He frowned. Went back through the roll in his memory, the way you'd check a kit before a march, item by item. Oduya. Whitlock. The Byrne twins — both of them, God, both in the same hour. Harkness. The drummer boy, Peter Lilly, fourteen years old, and Brannigan's stomach clenched the same way it clenched every time, because he had carried the boy's pack out of the water personally and it had weighed nothing at all...

And then it arrived. The gap. The shape of the hole where a name should be.

"Rooke," he whispered. "Lieutenant Rooke. William Rooke." He stared at her. "I left out Rooke."

"Who is Rooke?"

"A subaltern. Attached from another regiment, temporary — the kind of officer nobody knows well enough to grieve. He led the third wave. He went down in the shallows. And—" Brannigan stopped, and the cold that climbed out of him then had nothing to do with the floor. "And he was struck separately. Before the rest. The night after the action. Captain Ellery took the casualty return and Rooke's name was removed from the muster rolls entirely — not killed, not wounded, not missing. Just gone. As if he'd never been attached. I assumed it was the family. Some grand family wanting no scandal of a stupid death, buying the paperwork clean. It happens."

"Does it," Jude said. It wasn't a question.

But her face had changed. Something had come into it that Brannigan recognized from the wards — the look of a woman who has felt a fever spike under her hand and knows, before any symptom shows, that the sickness underneath is worse than the wound above.

"You know something," he said.

"I know a lieutenant died in October," Jude said slowly, "whose death was written up as an accident on the drill field. I know his effects were inventoried and sealed and shipped south under guard, which is not done for lieutenants. And I know—" she hesitated, and Brannigan watched her decide, deliberately, to trust him, the decision moving through her face like cloud-shadow over a field, "—I know a child was left at my door in January by an officer's aide, with money to keep me quiet and an order to forget his face. A boy of six who won't speak. Who draws."

"Draws what?"

"One face, mostly. Over and over. A man in a fine coat."

The lamp hissed. Out on the river, the ice boomed — a deep report, like a cannon under the world — and both of them turned toward the window, toward the black glass and the frozen dark, and neither of them spoke for a moment, because the river had said something and they had both heard it.

"Lieutenant Rooke had a son," Brannigan said. The words came out of him flat and certain, the way true things do. "He showed me a miniature once. Carried it in his breast pocket. He was young, hardly older than the lieutenant we lost in the summer, and he was proud of that boy the way green officers are proud of anything — loudly. Showed it around the mess until Ellery told him to put it away." He looked at Jude. "William Rooke was attached to this garrison for eleven days before the ford. Eleven days. And then he led the third wave into killing water against orders everyone knew were wrong, and afterward his name was scraped off the rolls like wax off a seal, and his son was delivered to a charity nurse with coin and a command to forget him."

Jude's hand had gone to the ledger. Not closing it. Resting on it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child's back.

"If the boy is Rooke's," Brannigan said, "then Rooke wasn't killed by French shot at the ford. Or he was, but not only — because you don't erase a man's whole existence over a skirmish. You do it over something he saw, or something he'd have said. Something the colonel needed buried deeper than the water." He shook his head slowly. "I'm an old man, Nurse, and I've got maybe a year of walking left in this hip, and I had planned to spend it on my brother's name and let the rest of it rot. But if there's a living witness sitting in your ward eating your bread—"

"There isn't a witness," Jude said. Too fast. Too flat.

And then, softer: "There's a child. Whatever he knows, he hasn't said, and I'll not have him dragged into it. Not for your inquiry. Not for the truth. Not for anything."

"Even if the truth is the only wall between him and the colonel?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. Brannigan had spent a career reading silences, and this one said: *I have already thought of that. I think of it every night. Do not make it louder.*

He rose, painfully, and gathered his greatcoat about him. At the door he stopped.

"The page stays," he said. "Whatever comes. Twenty-five names and a correction in the margin. Promise me that much, and promise me the book survives you — because you're braver than me, Nurse, but bravery's just the interest; the principal is the paper."

"It survives," Jude said. "One way or another."

He believed her. He believed her the way he'd believed few things in thirty-eight years, and he went back down the cold corridor to his pallet, and lay down, and listened to the river talk, and for the first time since October he slept without dreaming of the water.

Behind him, the lamp under her door burned on until nearly dawn. Writing.

And two floors above, in the loft where the boy slept curled against Tilda's spare blankets, Oscar dreamed — though no one saw it — of a fine coat, and a whistle, and a voice reading names aloud over cracking ice.
