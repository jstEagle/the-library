## 9. Oscar Speaks

The boy has a name before he has words.

He knows this because the woman with the ink-stained fingers says it to him every night, kneeling by his pallet, her breath fogging in the cold room. *Oscar.* She made it up, he thinks, or found it somewhere, like she finds buttons and thread and the little dried apples she cuts into pieces small enough for a mouth that has forgotten how to open.

Oscar. He tries it inside himself the way you try a step on ice. Put your weight down slow. See if it holds.

It holds.

* * *

Before, there was another house. A tall house with a green door and a dog named Sergeant who was not a sergeant at all, just a dog who liked to steal boots. There was a mother who smelled like bread and lavender water and who sang off-key on purpose because it made him laugh. And there was Papa, who wore a coat with gold on it, who lifted him onto one shoulder so high his head nearly touched the ceiling beams, who said, *My little man, my Oscar, when the war is done we'll have a garden.*

Papa's coat had gold braid at the cuffs. Oscar knows this because he drew it, over and over, on every scrap of paper the woman would give him. He draws the coat even now when his hands are restless. The coat is easy. The face is harder. Faces go away if you don't keep drawing them, that is what he believes, deep in the place where beliefs live, and so he draws: Papa, Mama, the cook, Sergeant the dog, the girl next door who once gave him half an apple. He draws them all, again and again, like counting them keeps them alive.

The woman never asks why. She just sharpens his pencil stubs with her knife and lays out the paper and lets him work. Sometimes she sits beside him and mends shirts, her needle going in and out, in and out, quiet as snowfall.

He likes her hands best of everything here. They are always moving, always fixing something. Hands like that don't leave you at doors with coins in your pocket. Hands like that stay.

* * *

He remembers the night they came for Papa. Not all of it — memory is a torn page — but some of it, the parts that won't smooth out no matter how many times he flattens them in his mind.

He had been supposed to be asleep. But there were voices downstairs, angry voices, and one of them was Papa's, and Papa never sounded angry, so Oscar crept out of bed and sat on the top stair where the banister hid him, in the dark gap behind the newel post where nobody ever looked.

Two men stood in the hall below. One was Papa. The other was a big man with a red face and a voice like a door slamming. Later — much later, after the workshop, after the woman — Oscar will learn to call that voice *the colonel*, but that night it was only the slamming-door voice, and it was saying things that didn't fit together right.

"—the inquiry will be closed, Lieutenant, and closed clean. You will sign what you are given to sign."

"I cannot sign it, sir. The men were ordered up the slope without support. Someone must say—"

"Someone *has* said. I have said. What has been said is what happened." The voice came closer, and Oscar shrank smaller behind the post. "You have a wife and a son, Lieutenant. Think carefully about which battles are worth fighting. This one is mine, not yours. Do you understand me?"

Papa was quiet a long time. When he spoke again his voice had gone strange and flat, like a coin laid on a table. "I understand you, Colonel."

"Good. You'll take the advance position at the river crossing. It's a posting of honor." A laugh then, short and ugly, nothing like Papa's laugh. "Of honor. Report at dawn."

Then boots on the floor, the front door, the cold rushing in, and Papa standing alone in the hall with his head bent, looking — this is the part Oscar can never make smooth — looking like Mama did the day they buried Grandfather. Like someone trying to hold up a roof by themselves.

Papa came upstairs. Oscar scrambled back to bed but his heart was banging too loud, and anyway Papa knew. Papas always know. He sat on the edge of the bed in the dark and stroked Oscar's hair and said, "Go back to sleep, my little man. Everything will be arranged."

Not *everything will be well*. *Everything will be arranged.* Oscar didn't understand the difference then.

Papa left at dawn. He wore the coat with the gold braid. He let Oscar wear his hat, which was much too big, and walked him to the end of the street on his shoulders, higher than the fences, higher than everyone, and at the corner he set him down and knelt and held his face in both big hands and said, "Be brave for your mama. I'll write when I can."

Then he walked away, and the gold braid caught the morning light, and that is the last picture Oscar has of him. That is the one he draws. That one, always that one.

Papa never wrote. Papa never came home. Men in uniform came instead, and Mama made a sound Oscar had never heard a person make, and after that the world broke apart piece by piece, the way ice breaks, slowly and then all at once, until there was a long road and a cart and a man who said *wait here* and never came back, and then a blizzard, and then a door, and then the woman with the ink-stained fingers.

* * *

The trouble with not talking is that it gets easier the longer you do it. At first he could not talk because the words were stuck, jammed in his throat like a swallowed stone. Then he would not talk, because talking meant saying it, and saying it might make it real in a way that drawing never did. Drawing was safe. You could draw the dead and they stayed where you put them, on the page, flat and quiet. If you said them out loud they got loose.

But the woman kept giving him apples and names and paper, and the old one called Mags kept humming songs with no words in them, as if she knew he needed music that didn't ask anything of him. And Tilda — the big loud one with arms like a blacksmith — Tilda held him once, when he dropped a cup, and said, "Never you mind, lamb, cups are cheap," and something in his chest cracked a little, the way ice cracks, making room.

And there was the ledger.

He saw it one evening when she thought he slept. She sat by the lamp writing in a book, and her lips moved as she wrote, whispering the names, and he understood — the way children understand enormous things suddenly and completely — that she was doing for her soldiers what he did with his drawings. Keeping them. Saying them over and over so they wouldn't go away.

She was keeping people alive with a pencil stub.

After that he watched her more closely. He saw her write names the officers' lists didn't have. He heard Mags tell Tilda, "Officers count victories; Jude counts men," and he turned that sentence over in his mind for days. Counting men. Counting Papa. Would anyone count Papa? Papa had been counted wrong already — counted as a man who signed what he was given to sign, when really he was a man who refused, and was sent up a slope, or to a river crossing, to die for it.

Nobody had written that down. Nobody except the boy hiding behind the banister post, holding the truth in his mouth like a hot coal, burning a hole in him year after month after week.

* * *

It happens on an ordinary evening, which is how enormous things happen. No thunder. Just soup.

Tilda has made barley soup, and Oscar is sitting on his stool between Mags and Jude, blowing on his spoon, when Ferro comes in stamping snow and says, "More orders posted today. They're listing the winter dead. Eleven names."

"Eleven?" Tilda snorts. "We buried nine here alone since Yule, and that's not counting the ones sent off to die at the front hospital."

"They count what suits them," Ferro says, hanging up his coat. "Always have."

And Oscar puts down his spoon.

Because the words come together in him all at once — the red-faced voice, the slammed door, the posting of honor, the eleven names, the ledger, Papa — and the coal in his throat is suddenly too heavy to hold anymore, and he hears his own voice come out rusty and cracked, a stranger's voice, saying:

"He wouldn't sign it."

The room goes still. The fire pops. Everyone is looking at him and he wants to crawl under the floor but the words keep coming now, tumbling, a dam broken, and he can't stop them and doesn't want to stop them, not anymore, not ever.

"The colonel. The big man with the loud voice. He came to our house and Papa argued with him about the battle, about the charge up the hill where the men died, and the colonel said Papa had to sign the paper that said it wasn't anybody's fault, and Papa said he couldn't, and the colonel said—" Oscar's breath hitches. Jude's hand covers his fist on the table, warm and steady. "The colonel said, think about your wife and son. And then he sent Papa away to the river crossing. He called it a posting of honor. But it wasn't. It was because Papa told the truth and the colonel didn't want the truth told."

Silence, wide as the frozen river.

Mags has gone very pale. Ferro stands motionless with one arm still in his coat sleeve. Tilda's big hands are pressed flat against the table as if holding it down in a wind.

Jude doesn't gasp. Doesn't cry out. She just turns to him slowly, and her eyes are bright and terrible, and she says, very quietly, "Oscar. The officer who came to your house. His name — do you know his name?"

"Ashworth," Oscar says. "Everyone said it. Colonel Ashworth." And then, because the last stone must come out too, because keeping it any longer would split him in two: "Papa was Lieutenant Verey. William Verey. I'm Oscar Verey. Papa used to carry me on his shoulders and he said when the war was done we'd have a garden, and he never came home, and Mama cried the crying sound, and I heard everything, I heard it from the stairs, I remember all of it."

His voice gives out. He has said it. It is loose in the world now, walking around in the lamplight where anyone can see it, and he is shaking, and Tilda gathers him up against her enormous soft chest and holds him while he sobs, really sobs, for the first time since the green door, since the dog, since the shoulders and the hat and the garden that will never be planted.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Tilda has finally carried him half-asleep to his pallet — Oscar surfaces briefly from sleep and sees the lamp still burning and Jude still writing. But she isn't writing in the ledger tonight. She is sitting with both hands flat on the closed cover of it, staring at nothing, and Mags is beside her, and he can't hear what they're saying, only the shape of it, low and urgent, like water under ice.

He should be afraid. Some far-off part of him knows that what he said tonight is the kind of thing that gets people erased — he has seen erasure, he has been its object, carried in a cart and set down at a door with coins in his pocket and an order to forget his own face.

But he isn't afraid. He is light. Emptied-out light, the way the sky looks after a storm has passed through and taken everything with it.

They asked him to forget. The man with the coins said forget your face. The whole winter he tried.

Now a woman with ink-stained fingers is writing down what actually happened, and tomorrow she will ask him to say it again, slowly, every word, and he will. Because Papa told the truth and was killed for it, and because somebody has to count Lieutenant William Verey, and because a boy behind a banister post is a witness too, even if he is only six years old, even if he is small enough to be carried out of the world on one man's shoulder.

In the dark, half-dreaming, Oscar whispers it one more time, practicing, the way he practices letters now with Tilda:

"My papa was Lieutenant Verey. I heard the order. I remember."

Across the room, the lamp gutters and steadies. Jude dips her pen.

And miles away, in a warm quarter where a fire burns behind curtained windows, a colonel is about to learn that a ledger exists.
