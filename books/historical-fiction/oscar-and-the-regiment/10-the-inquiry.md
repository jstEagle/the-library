## 10. The Inquiry

Colonel Ashworth had learned long ago that the best lies were the ones that arrived early and dressed plainly. He had Major Pell prepare the testimony three days before the inquiry was scheduled, so that by the time the officers assembled in the map room with their cold hands and their cold tea, the truth would already be an old thing, worn smooth, something everyone remembered hearing about.

The lieutenant's death was to be a misfortune of weather. That was all. A patrol sent out late in February, against the advice of its own sergeant, caught in a thaw that came too soon, ice failing under six men and one officer. The written account — signed by two witnesses who owed Ashworth more than they could ever repay — placed the order to cross at the feet of Lieutenant Vane himself. Ambition, it said. A young man eager to distinguish himself, unwilling to wait for the river to be properly tested.

It was elegant. It required only that no one look too hard at the counter-order, the one Ashworth had given on the fourteenth, the one that moved Vane's patrol from the safe southern bank to the northern crossing where the ice was rotten and where, not coincidentally, a supply wagon had gone missing with certain figures inside it that Ashworth could not afford to have counted.

He sat at the head of the table while Pell read the findings aloud. There were five officers present, plus the regimental surgeon, who kept his eyes on his notebook as though the pages might offer him a way out of the room. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the workshop hammering went on — Ferro's men, shaping wood for legs the army would owe these men forever and pay for never.

"The board finds," Pell concluded, "that Lieutenant Vane acted with initiative exceeding his instructions, and that his death resulted from this initiative. No further action is recommended."

"Any objection?" Ashworth asked.

Silence, of the kind he had spent eleven years purchasing. The surgeon coughed once and said nothing. Captain Reyes, young enough to still believe things, looked at his hands for a long moment and then looked up, and Ashworth met the look steadily, because that was the price of the trade: you did not blink when they watched you. You let them see that you were comfortable, and their discomfort became their own problem.

"Then we are concluded." Ashworth closed the folder. "Lieutenant Vane served with distinction. The record will reflect that."

*The record.* He almost smiled saying it. The record was whatever he said it was, and tomorrow morning the clerk would copy the findings into the regimental book in fair hand, and in April the whole workshop town would burn its papers and move north, and the river would open, and the water would carry away everything the winter had held.

He dismissed them and stood alone at the map table for a moment, looking down at the blue line of the frozen river. Six men dead. One lieutenant. One boy, somewhere, hopefully far away by now — the aide had sworn he'd left the child with a woman outside town, some nurse, paid her well, told her to forget his face. Ashworth had not liked the arrangement. Loose arrangements were how empires ended. But the alternative had been worse, and the aide had been loyal, or frightened enough to act like it.

There was a knock.

"Enter."

Sergeant Brannigan stood in the doorway, and Ashworth felt the small cold drop in his stomach that Brannigan always produced, like a stone into a well. The man should have died years ago — the wound in his hip had been killing him slowly since the autumn campaign, and every week he seemed grayer, more stooped, as though the earth were already pulling at him politely, patiently, the way it pulls at everything eventually. But he was still upright, and he was still the best-informed soldier in the regiment, because dying men listen better than living ones.

"Sergeant. I wasn't told you were attending the inquiry."

"Wasn't invited, sir." Brannigan's voice was gravel left out overnight. "Came on another matter."

"Then state it."

Brannigan came into the room without being asked, which was itself a small insolence, and stopped at the edge of the firelight. He smelled of the workshop — pitch and lamp oil and the particular sourness of the hospital ward.

"It's about the record, sir. Since you mentioned it."

Ashworth waited. He was good at waiting. It was most of what command was.

"There's a woman at the workshop hospital. Nurse Kavanagh. Keeps a book." Brannigan's eyes stayed level. "Names. Men the dispatches leave off. Fellows who die of fever instead of glory. She writes them down every night. Has done since she came."

"A ledger of the dead. How touching. The Church has kept such books for centuries, Sergeant, and no empire has fallen from it yet."

"Not the dead, sir. Not only." Brannigan shifted his weight off the bad hip, and Ashworth saw the pain move through him like weather. "She writes the names the officers strike. My brother's name is in that book. Struck from the charge of last June — official report says the company took twelve casualties. We took nineteen. Her book has all nineteen."

The fire popped. Downstairs the hammers went on, patient as tide.

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I've seen her write in it, sir. Every night. And because today I heard the findings of this inquiry read aloud, and I thought: there's a woman down the hill who keeps a true account of things, and one day someone will read it." He paused. "I'm telling you because I'm going to die before spring, Colonel, and when I go I'd like to know whether my brother died in a battle or in an arithmetic error. That's all. Thought you'd want to know your record wasn't the only one."

Ashworth dismissed him with a nod and stood listening to the boots fade down the corridor, and after the door shut he did not move for a long time.

A ledger.

His first instinct was contempt — some sentimental woman scribbling names by candlelight, the kind of piety that flourishes wherever real work is being done. But the contempt cooled quickly, as it always did, and underneath it the colder machinery began to turn. Nineteen names where the report said twelve. Which meant she had sources among the wounded, among the orderlies, among the sergeants. Which meant she listened. And if she wrote down struck casualties from June, what else had she written? What had been said near her? What had the aide said, standing at her door in the blizzard with a child and a purse?

Ashworth went to the window. Below, the workshop sprawled along the frozen bank, smoke rising from its chimneys, small figures moving between the sheds. Somewhere down there was a woman with a pen, and a book, and — possibly — a boy with a face he had ordered forgotten.

He summoned Pell back.

"The nurse at the workshop hospital. Kavanagh. What do we know of her?"

Pell blinked. "The quiet one? Nothing, sir. She keeps to herself. The women like her. Surgeon says her hands are the cleanest in the garrison."

"Find out where she came from. Who recommended her. Who she speaks to. Quietly."

"Yes, sir. May I ask why?"

"You may not."

Pell left. Ashworth remained at the window, watching the smoke rise straight up in the windless cold, and permitted himself, at last, to feel the thing he had been holding at arm's length since Brannigan walked out: fear.

It was a professional fear, precise and clarifying. He had survived on the principle that corruption must be tidy. A lie is safe when it stands alone; it becomes dangerous when it touches another lie, and lethal when it meets a true thing written down by a careful hand. Dispatches could be burned. Witnesses could be reassigned, promoted, buried. But a private ledger, kept nightly, copied nowhere, held by a woman no one owned — that was not a document. That was a memory with a spine. You could not edit it. You could only destroy it, or be destroyed by it.

And if the boy was in that house —

No. He made himself stop there. First the facts. Facts were the discipline that separated him from panicked men.

He sat and drafted orders in his own hand, in cipher, short enough to burn in a single match: *Inquire discreetly into the papers and correspondence of the nurse at the workshop hospital. Determine whether she holds records beyond her duties. Report by Thursday.*

Then he sat with the paper in his hand and found, to his irritation, that he hesitated over the flame.

Not conscience. He had sold his conscience years ago at a fair price and had never regretted the transaction. It was something else — the same instinct that had made him send Vane north across rotten ice rather than simply poison him, the instinct of a man who prefers problems to dissolve naturally. Burning the inquiry order meant admitting, even to himself, that a nurse's ledger was a threat worth ciphers. It gave her weight. It made her real.

He burned it anyway. The ash floated down onto the carpet like gray moths, and he ground it under his heel, and upstairs in the ward the hammers went on knocking wood into the shapes of missing limbs.

Thursday, he told himself. By Thursday he would know what she kept, and whom she loved, and where the seams of her life ran, so that when the time came — and it would come, before the thaw, before the burning — he could unpick her quietly, stitch by stitch, the way one unpicks anything: patiently, and in order, and without the whole cloth ever knowing it was coming apart.

Down the hill, in a workshop that smelled of pitch and lamp oil, a candle was surely burning over a page of names.

He would find it. He told himself he would find it, and that finding it would be the end of the matter.

It did not occur to him — it never occurred to him, until much later, when it was far too late — that the woman might not be keeping the ledger for herself at all. That she might be keeping it for everyone whose name was in it. And that such a book does not belong to the woman who writes it. It belongs to the dead, and the dead do not surrender their property to colonels.

The snow ticked against the shutters all night, soft as fingers, counting.
