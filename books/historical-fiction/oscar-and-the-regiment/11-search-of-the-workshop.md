## 11. Search of the Workshop

The knock came at first light, and it was not a knock. It was a fist against the workshop door, hard enough to shake frost from the lintel, and behind it a voice that did not ask.

"Open in the name of Colonel Ashworth."

Jude was already awake. She had been awake since before dawn, since Oscar had whimpered in his sleep and she had gone to him in the dark of the linen store, sitting on the cold floor beside his pallet until he settled again. She had been awake, in truth, for three days, ever since Mags had come back from delivering dressings to the officers' quarters with the news delivered flat as a folded bandage: *He knows about the ledger. The major told him. He's ordered a search.*

She had known it would come. Knowing did not stop her hands from shaking now.

Tilda was already at the door, arms crossed, jaw set like a woman who had spent thirty years wringing other people's filth out of their fine linens and would not be made to feel small by anyone's uniform. Ferro stood up from his bench slowly, deliberately, the way a large man moves when he wants you to understand exactly how large he is. Oscar was not in the main room. He was in the linen store, where Jude had put him twenty minutes ago, tucked between shelves of folded sheets with a heel of bread in one hand and instructions to be quiet as the dead.

"Open it," Tilda said to no one in particular, and pulled the bar herself.

They came in six strong, boots stamping snow off at the threshold like men who had been taught manners somewhere else entirely. A lieutenant led them—young, pink-cheeked, embarrassed in the way of a man doing a thing he knows will look ugly later—and behind him two sergeants Jude recognized from the hospital rounds, men whose splinters she had drawn and whose fevers she had broken. Neither met her eye.

"We're to search the premises," the lieutenant said. "Papers. Correspondence. Any written matter outside official stores."

"You're welcome to look," Jude said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Mind the ward. There are nine men in there recovering from amputation, and if your boots wake them, they'll tell you what they think of the colonel at volume."

The lieutenant flushed. "We'll be careful."

*Careful.* She almost laughed. Careful was what Ashworth had been too—careful, courteous, velvet over iron, standing in her workshop three weeks ago with his hat in his hand and his threat dressed as concern. *A nurse can be reassigned,* he had said. *The front lines are short of hands.* Now he had stopped being careful, which meant he was frightened, which meant the ledger mattered more than she had let herself believe.

The soldiers spread through the workshop with methodical rudeness. They went through the medicine chest, tipping bottles, reading labels aloud to each other like men illiterate in Latin. They shook out the mending basket. One of them flipped through Ferro's pattern book—the carpenter's careful drawings of cabinet joints and chair legs—and the lieutenant frowned at it, disappointed to find only furniture.

Jude stood by the stove and watched, and kept her breathing slow, and thought about the leg.

* * *

Ferro had been carving it for a month. A prosthetic leg, ash wood, for a private named Hollis who had lost his own below the knee to a French ball at the river crossing. Ferro carved the way some men prayed—slowly, completely, with an attention that made the thing being made seem less important than the making. The leg had a socket shaped to Hollis's stump, a knee joint Ferro had engineered with a leather hinge, a foot with toes, because Ferro insisted a man should have toes even if they were wood.

And it was hollow.

Not all of it. The shin bone of it—a length of it above the ankle, where no joiner's eye would look twice—was bored out, a cavity the width of her thumb running up into the calf, plugged at the foot with a dowel so fine you could not find the seam with your fingernail. Ferro had shown her ten days ago without saying why, turning the unfinished leg in his great hands and tapping the shin once, twice, and looking at her.

"I bored it out," he'd said, "because I was thinking about how a man carries things. Inside himself, mostly." Then he'd gone back to sanding, and she'd understood, and neither of them had said another word, because words were the one thing the workshop had learned to ration.

Last night, after Mags brought the warning, Jude had sat up by candlelight with the ledger open across her knees and done the arithmetic of it. Forty-one pages. Sixty-three names. Brannigan's brother among them, and the eleven men of the failed charge, and the fever dead of December whose names existed nowhere but her ink. It would not fit in a loaf. It would not fit in a bandage roll. It would fit—rolled tight, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with thread—in the hollow of a wooden leg waiting for a man who could not yet walk.

At midnight she had done it. Rolled, wrapped, seated home, dowel pressed flush, a smear of sawdust and glue rubbed into the seam by Ferro's own thumb. Then the leg went back on the bench where it had always been, half-finished, unremarkable, one project among many in a carpenter's cluttered corner. And Jude had gone to bed—or lain down, anyway—and listened to the wind, and waited for the fist on the door.

Now the fist had come, and the leg sat on the bench not four feet from where the lieutenant stood shaking out aprons.

* * *

They found her ledger shelf and emptied it. That was expected; she had left it stocked. Blank account books. Store inventories. The ration tallies, all proper, all dull, every line reconciled to the quartermaster's own figures because she had spent two nights copying them clean. The lieutenant paged through the ration tallies with growing frustration, as though hoping numbers might confess.

"What's this?" One of the sergeants held up the loose papers she'd planted: a half-finished letter to a supply clerk in town, complaining about linen prices, boring beyond endurance.

"Correspondence," Jude said. "As you see."

"Who's the supply clerk?"

"A man who charges me double for bandage cloth. Do you want to read it? It takes an hour and it never gets better."

Someone snorted. The sergeant put the letter down.

But they were thorough, she had to give them that. Thorough the way fear makes men thorough. Two of them went into the ward, boots soft now, and pulled the personal effects from under nine cots—letters from home, a hymnbook, a carved pipe—one of the convalescents swearing at them in a whisper so as not to tear his stitches. They went into the kitchen and dumped the flour bin. They went through Mags's instrument case and held her probes up to the light as if surgical steel might conceal a confession. Mags watched them with an expression Jude had seen on her face only once before, over an operation table, when a man was dying and everyone knew it and nobody said so: a stillness like held breath.

They did not go into the linen store.

Jude understood why a moment later, when she heard the lieutenant say, "Check the storerooms," and one of the sergeants opened the linen store door, looked at the shelves of folded sheets, and closed it again. Folded sheets. Nothing there but folded sheets, in a hospital. Nobody wanted to unfold sixty blankets on a February morning for a piece of paper. She watched the door close and felt something in her chest unlock by a single degree.

Oscar did not make a sound. Later she would learn that he had sat between those shelves with the bread untouched in his hand, listening to the boots come near and go away, and that he had understood, in whatever language lived inside him, exactly what was happening—that men had come to take something, and that his job was to be nothing. He was six years old and he had already learned to be nothing well. That fact settled into Jude like a swallowed stone and stayed there.

* * *

It took them an hour. At the end of it, the workshop looked like weather had been through indoors—drawers gaping, flour dust settling over everything, Mags's instruments laid out on a table in a gleaming row—but they had nothing. The lieutenant stood in the middle of the room holding the ration tallies against his hip and looking around at the wreckage with the expression of a man who has been sent to find a thing and must now go back and report that the thing declined to be found.

"That's everything?" Jude asked.

"That's everything." He hesitated. He was young enough that hesitation still reached his face. "Nurse Kavanagh. I'm to tell you—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a list. Of persons attached to this establishment whose conduct is under review. If any irregularities should come to light, it would go better for everyone if they came to light voluntarily."

"Is that your own advice, Lieutenant, or the colonel's?"

His ears went red. "It's the colonel's phrasing."

"Then you can tell the colonel that I keep accounts, I change dressings, and I have never in my life had an irregularity I didn't record honestly. Your men can vouch for my sewing if nothing else. Half of them are wearing it."

The sergeants looked at the floor. The lieutenant almost smiled, killed it, and got his men moving. They filed out past Tilda, who stood by the door with her arms still crossed, and she looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, the way she looked at laundry that had come back stained, until the last soldier was out in the snow and the bar dropped back into its bracket.

For a full minute nobody spoke. Then Ferro crossed to the bench, picked up the wooden leg, weighed it in his hands, and set it down again exactly where it had been.

"Hollis comes tomorrow for his fitting," he said.

"Yes," Jude said.

"Good." He began gathering his scattered drawings. That was all. That was the whole of the conversation, and it was enough, because everything that mattered had already been said in glue and sawdust and a bored-out shinbone.

Mags started restacking her instruments. Tilda righted the flour bin. And Jude went to the linen store door and opened it, and Oscar looked up at her from between the shelves with bread still clutched in his fist, gone stale now, and she knelt and gathered him in before he could decide whether to flinch.

"It's over," she said into his hair. "You were perfect."

He didn't cry. That was the worst part. He just held on, hard, the way small things hold on when they have learned that holding is not guaranteed.

* * *

They might have gotten away with it entire. That was the cruelty of it—later Jude would turn that over and over, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. The search had failed. The lieutenant had nothing. All Ashworth had was suspicion, and suspicion, unsupported, dies of embarrassment in a week.

But as the soldiers regrouped in the yard, stamping and blowing on their hands, one of them lingered. A private, young, one of the men whose dressings Jude changed on Tuesdays. He hung back by the gate, and then he turned and came halfway back to the door, and there was something working in his face, shame and calculation fighting for the same ground.

Jude saw it happen. Everyone saw it happen. The yard was silent except for snow-squeak under boots, and the private walked up to the lieutenant, spoke low and fast, and pointed—not at the workshop, not at the ward, but at Tilda.

At Tilda, who had opened the door for them. Who had stood in front of them all morning with her arms crossed and her chin up. Whose washing line ran along the south wall of the colonel's own quarters, and who therefore saw, carried, and heard everything that passed between the officers' wing and the rest of the garrison.

Jude couldn't hear what was said. But she saw the lieutenant's face change—saw reluctance lose to orders—and saw him turn and look at Tilda for a long moment, weighing her, the way a man weighs a sack before deciding it's worth carrying.

They took her an hour later. Not at the door with fists this time; they had learned manners, or wanted witnesses to think they had. Two military policemen came at mid-morning with a paper, and read it out in the yard where everyone could hear, and the charge was beautifully, perfectly nothing: *improper conveyance of correspondence*—a bundle of undelivered letters found, they claimed, among her effects during the search. Letters that had not been there when the search ended. Letters that had grown, somehow, in the interval between the workshop and the reporting of its failure, like mold in a dark drawer.

Tilda listened to the whole of it with her arms at her sides. Then she looked at Jude, and what passed across her face was not fear and not appeal. It was instruction. *Say nothing. Hold fast. Mind the boy.* Thirty years of other people's linen, and she went with the military police the way she went everywhere, upright, unhurried, as though the cell they were taking her to were simply one more pile of washing that would have to be dealt with eventually.

The gate closed behind her.

Inside the workshop, the sounds resumed one by one—Ferro's plane, Mags sorting bandages, the ward's low mutter—as though the day meant to pretend nothing had happened. Jude stood in the yard a long time, cold working through her skirts, watching the gate. The cost had arrived, and it had a name, and the name was not hers.

Ferro came to stand beside her. He did not look at the gate. He looked at his own hands, enormous, scarred, gentle.

"They'll want her to give a reason," he said quietly. "For holding her. A reason that holds up. When they can't find one—" He stopped. "Three days. Maybe four. Then they'll have to let her go or admit what she is."

"And what is she?"

"The only person in this garrison who knows which of the colonel's officers writes letters to a woman in town he isn't married to." Ferro picked a curl of ash wood off his sleeve, considered it, let it fall. "They won't break her. But they'll try to teach us the price. That's what today was. Not a search. An invoice."

That night Jude lit no candle. She sat in the dark with Oscar asleep against her side and did the accounting she had promised herself she would never do: whether sixty-three names were worth what they cost, and who got to decide, and why the bill always came to someone else's door. She had no answer. She only knew that tomorrow she would sit down at her table in plain view of anyone who cared to look, and write the next name, and the one after that, because Tilda had gone upright through a gate so that the writing could continue, and stopping now would spend her for nothing.

In the linen store, rolled tight in oilcloth inside a dead man's future leg, sixty-three names slept on, waiting for spring, and for the water, and for whatever came after.

Two days later, word came down from the officers' quarters that Tilda would not be released after three days after all. Her interrogation, the order said, was ongoing.

And with the order came a second sheet, addressed to Nurse Kavanagh personally, in a hand she recognized: a summons to the colonel's quarters, at his convenience.

Which meant, she understood, at his pleasure. Which meant soon.
