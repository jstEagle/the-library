## 12. What Tilda Carried

Mags had watched men die in three wars and women survive worse, and she had learned that the body tells the truth before the mouth does. So when Tilda came back through the workshop gate on the third morning—escorted, of all things, by a corporal who touched his cap and walked away like a man delivering laundry—Mags read her at a glance.

The walk was wrong. Not limping. Tilda was too proud to limp. But she moved as if the ground were a question she no longer trusted, testing each step before committing her weight to it. Her hands were tucked into her armpits despite the thaw-warm air. And her eyes—her eyes went everywhere at once, cataloguing exits, counting faces, doing the arithmetic of danger that soldiers did and washerwomen should never have to learn.

"Well," Mags said, loud enough for the yard to hear. "Three days they kept you, and not so much as a thank-you note."

"I'd have written one," Tilda said, "but I can't spell."

The laugh that went around the drying yard was thin and grateful. It was the kind of laugh people made when they needed proof that the world still worked. Mags let it run its course, then took Tilda's elbow—gently, the way you handled a splinted limb—and steered her toward the wash-house.

Inside, with the door shut and the steam hissing, Mags sat her down on the bench and looked at her properly. There was a bruise on Tilda's jaw, yellowed already, older than three days. Her wrists, when the sleeves fell back, carried marks like a ledger's ruled lines. Her knuckles were split. But it was none of those things that made Mags's chest tighten. It was the way Tilda sat: perfectly straight, hands flat on her knees, a woman holding herself together with posture alone.

"Tell me," Mags said.

"No."

"Tilda—"

"No." The word came out level, without heat, which frightened Mags more than screaming would have. "You'll ask me every day, Mags, because you're kind and because it's your nature to poke at wounds. And every day I'll say no, and every day we'll waste a bit of each other's lives on it. So let's save the trouble." She looked up then, and her eyes were dry and very clear. "What they did is mine to carry. That's what carrying is. You don't hand it off because your arms ache."

Mags had prepared arguments. She had them ranked and ready, the way a surgeon ranks instruments. She put them away unused.

"Then tell me this," she said. "Can you work?"

"I can work."

"Can you eat?"

"I could eat a horse and insult its mother."

Mags nodded. "That'll do for now."

* * *

But it didn't do, not really, and Mags knew it, because that night she heard Tilda crying in the loft above the wash-house—not sobbing, nothing so honest as sobbing, but the long ragged breathing of a woman who had learned to weep silently, who had learned it recently, and who hated herself for needing to learn it. Mags lay in her cot below and listened and did not go up, because some griefs bite when reached for, and because she had learned in thirty years of army surgery that you do not dig out shrapnel before the flesh has softened around it.

What softened the flesh, in the end, was the boy.

Oscar had been quiet since the arrest—quiet in a new way, watchful, as though he had understood that the soldiers at the gate had come for something connected to him, and that the something had been taken instead from Tilda. He had stopped drawing faces. He hung about the drying lines where Tilda worked, or had worked, and when she was gone he stood in her empty place among the sheets like a bird returning to a robbed nest.

On the fourth morning after her return—the fifth since the gate—Tilda came down to the yard early. Mags watched from the wash-house window as the boy approached her, hesitant, hands working at each other.

"You came back," Oscar said.

"I said I would."

"They hurt you."

"That's my business," Tilda said. Then, and Mags saw the decision move across her face like weather: "Sit down."

He sat on the washing block. Tilda crouched in front of him, and she picked up a stick of laundry chalk from the block's edge—the blue kind, the kind that ruined good linen and was therefore always underfoot—and she drew a single letter on the flat stone.

"A," she said. "That's an A."

The boy looked at it. "Why?"

"Because everything starts somewhere," Tilda said, "and A is where writing starts. Every name ever written begins here." She tapped the stone. "Say it back."

"A."

"Again."

"A."

"Good. Now B."

Mags turned away from the window. Some things you watched and some things you witnessed, and there was a difference, and she had no right to the second.

* * *

It became a ritual within a week, and rituals, Mags knew, were how broken people rebuilt themselves—they took the shape of something steady and poured themselves into it until they hardened around it. Every morning after the first kettle, Tilda sat Oscar on the washing block and taught him letters. Chalk on stone, stone wiped clean, chalk again. A through Z by the second week, and then the letters began to marry.

"Ca," Tilda said. "C-a. What does that make?"

The boy frowned at it. "Ka."

"Cah. Like the beginning of—" She paused. Mags, folding bandages nearby, saw the pause cost her something. "Like the beginning of *captain*. C-a-p-t-a-i-n."

"My papa was a lieutenant," Oscar said. He said it simply, the way children state facts that adults have built fortifications around.

"Yes," Tilda said. "Well. We'll start smaller." She wrote *cat*. She wrote *hat*. She wrote *mud*, and the boy laughed for the first time in weeks, because mud was funny, mud was everywhere, mud was the great democratic truth of a regiment on a frozen river.

Mags kept her distance and kept her count. By the third week the boy could write his own name. He wrote it over and over—OSCAR, four letters marching across the washing block, up the side of a tub, once, dangerously, on the seat of Ferro's best sawhorse—and each time he wrote it he looked at it longer than the time before, as though checking that it was still true.

One evening Mags found him alone in the yard, chalk in hand, writing the letters slowly on the stones while the light failed.

"Why does Tilda make me practice?" he asked, without looking up. Children asked questions sideways; that was their genius.

"Because names are important," Mags said.

"Whose names?"

"Everyone's. Every name must be written somewhere, or else the person might as well have never been." She heard herself say it and realized it was Jude's creed, absorbed secondhand, worn smooth like a coin passed through many hands. "That's why she teaches you. Writing is how you keep someone."

"Keep them from what?"

"From being forgotten."

The boy considered this with the terrible thoroughness of six-year-olds. Then he wrote OSCAR again, pressing hard, digging the chalk into the stone as though the name might otherwise blow away.

"Mama said that," he said quietly. "Before. She said, *if anything happens, remember your name, because a boy who knows his name can be found.*"

Mags's throat closed. She knelt beside him, joints cracking like green wood, and helped him hold the chalk properly, thumb and two fingers, the way surgeons held scalpels.

"She was right," Mags said. "Your mama was a wise woman."

"Is," Oscar corrected. "Not was. Is."

"Is," Mags agreed. "A wise woman *is*."

She thought about that sentence all night. She thought about how the boy had said *before*, meaning before whatever before was—a flight, an abandonment, an order given in a fine coat—and she thought about Tilda on the washing block, teaching letters with split knuckles, and she thought about the ledger in Jude's keeping with its columns of small names, and she understood, with the clarity that comes only to the old and the tired, that all of them were doing the same work. The whole workshop was one long act of writing against erasure. Tilda taught letters because letters were armor. Jude wrote names because names were testimony. And the boy practiced his own name because a child who knew his name could be found, and finding, someday, meant reckoning.

* * *

Tilda never spoke of the three days. Not to Mags, not to Jude, not to Ferro, who left a carved wooden comb on her washboard one morning without a word and found it in use the next day and never mentioned that either. The workshop adjusted around her silence the way water adjusts around a stone—flowing past it, shaping itself to it, never pretending the stone wasn't there.

Only once did she come close. It was late, near the end of the month, and Mags was boiling lint in the surgery while Tilda folded clean linen across the room. The river had begun to mutter under the ice—that deep irregular groaning, like a giant turning in sleep—and the sound filled the silences between them.

"When I was young," Mags said, apropos of nothing, because she was old enough to know that apropos of nothing was sometimes the only door left open, "I worked a field hospital after a retreat. We had a girl, sixteen, brought in after the baggage train was raided. Wouldn't speak for a month. Everyone said she was ruined, simple, done for." She wrung out a handful of lint. "In the spring she started teaching the other girls to sing. That was all. Just singing. And by summer you couldn't shut any of them up."

Tilda's hands kept moving. Fold, turn, stack.

"What happened to her?" Mags asked.

"Went home. Married a farrier. Had seven children." Mags set the wet lint in the frame. "The singing wasn't recovery. It was the opposite. It was refusal. She couldn't stop what was done to her, but she could fill the world with noise afterward, and she did, and the noise won."

Tilda stopped folding. For a moment she stood absolutely still, and Mags braced for the door to open—for anger, for tears, for the whole buried thing to come up like floodwater.

Instead Tilda said, "Every name must be written somewhere."

It wasn't an answer to anything Mags had asked. It was the thing she told Oscar every morning, the catechism of the washing block. But hearing it now, in the steam and the river-groan, Mags understood that Tilda was telling her something else entirely: that the letters were not for the boy alone. That the chalk was not chalk. That every morning Tilda sat on that cold stone and taught a stolen child his alphabet, she was carving her answer to the three days into the world, letter by letter, where no officer could confiscate it.

"I understand," Mags said.

"You don't," Tilda said, without bitterness. "And I hope to God you never do." She picked up another sheet. "But thank you for asking anyway. Every day. Even though I say no."

"That's what carrying is," Mags said. "You don't hand it off because your arms ache."

Something moved across Tilda's face—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might one day be rebuilt. "Listen to you. Quoting me."

"I'm an old woman. I steal from everyone."

* * *

April came in like a debt collector. The ice darkened, rotted, went grey and honeycombed at the edges. Orders arrived—everyone felt them arrive before anyone announced them, the way a household feels a death coming—and the talk in the yard turned to departure dates and burning days, and the word *records* began to appear in conversations like a knife appearing at a table.

Jude wrote faster. Mags saw her at all hours, hunched over the ledger, ink-stained to the wrist, copying, cross-checking, feeding pages into places no search had yet thought to look. Brannigan coughed blood into his kerchief and pretended it was pipe tar. Ferro spent his evenings with his plane and his chisels, making sounds in the carpentry shed that Mags recognized as the sounds of a man building something with a false bottom, because she had heard such sounds before, in other wars, from other desperate joiners.

And Tilda taught letters.

Every morning. Cold stone, blue chalk, the boy's voice saying the alphabet back to her like a small stubborn echo. Other children of the yard drifted over eventually—a cooper's daughter, two drummer boys on errands—and Tilda absorbed them without comment, and soon the washing block looked less like a lesson than a school, and nobody called it a school, because calling it a school would have been asking permission, and permission was the one thing the workshop had stopped believing in.

On the last morning of April, Mags came out with her tea and found Tilda standing apart, watching Oscar write. He was doing something new. He had stopped copying her words and was writing words of his own, laboriously, tongue between his teeth: a list, a column of names, marching down the washing block in a child's crooked hand.

Mags came closer and read them upside down.

BRANNIGAN. FERRO. MAGS. TILDA. JUDE.

And beneath them, larger, pressed harder:

PAPA.

Tilda saw Mags reading. She didn't snatch the chalk away or wipe the stone. She just stood there, arms crossed, split knuckles white against her sleeves, watching her own student commit his family to stone.

"He asked me how to spell lieutenant," Tilda said. "I told him. Then he asked me how to spell the rest of you." A pause. "He wanted everyone in one place."

Mags looked at the names a long time. Papa last, largest, hardest-pressed—the name a boy digs deepest because he is afraid the world will take it.

"Every name must be written somewhere," Mags said softly.

"That's the whole of the law," Tilda said. "Everything else is commentary."

They stood together in the cold spring light while the river groaned beneath its rotten ice, and neither of them saw the figure at the gate—had no reason to look, no reason to fear a quartermaster's clerk with a list in his hand—until he was already gone, having counted heads, having lingered longest, having marked the washing block and the blue-chalk letters and the boy who wrote them.

Having seen exactly what a school looks like.
