## 13. The Thaw Begins

The river spoke first.

Jude heard it before she was fully awake, a long groan rolling up through the frozen ground and into the timbers of the workshop, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep. She lay still in her cot above the ward and listened to it come again—a crack, a shudder, then silence so complete she could hear the frost growing in the window glass.

Three winters on this river had taught her its language. In December it whispered. In January it went mute as stone. But when February loosened its grip and the days stretched past five o'clock of light, the ice began to talk, and everything it said was a countdown.

She dressed in the dark and went down to check the wards out of habit rather than need. The fever cases had emptied through January; what remained were the slow mendings—Ferro with his half-carved legs for men who'd left limbs at Borodino, Mags boiling bandages in the big copper, two frostbitten privates snoring in the corner cots. The stove held its heat. All was well.

All was well, and yet she could not go back to bed. She climbed instead to the loft window that faced the river, breath fogging the glass, and looked out at the grey expanse of it under a sky going from black to iron. Somewhere beneath that ice lay the channel current, patient, doing its work all winter long, eating the river from underneath while everyone above walked across it without thinking.

That was how she thought of the ledger now. Her small theft, hidden under the feet of the regiment all winter.

She had filled forty-one pages since November. Forty-one pages of names the dispatches would never carry: Private Aldous Reeve, died of putrid fever, who used to sing to himself while his hands were being lanced. Gunner's mate Halloran, drowned through the ice hauling timber, recorded by no one because he was a contractor's man and contractors' men did not count. The boy Vassili, twelve, a camp follower's son, dead of the coughing sickness in the same week three officers' horses received individual veterinary attention at a cost entered carefully in the commissary books.

Jude wrote them because no one else would. That was the whole of it, though she knew it was not the whole of it anymore. It had been the whole of it once, back when history happened elsewhere—in ministers' offices, in headquarters tents, in the columns of the Gazette—and her work was simply to keep men alive and note their passing honestly. Now history had walked up to her door in a blizzard, six years old and unable to speak, and stood there holding her hand.

Oscar slept in the box room off the laundry, in a proper cot Tilda had scrounged from somewhere and Ferro had planed smooth. He slept badly. He woke screaming some nights, wordless, and Jude learned to sit beside him until the shaking stopped, and in the mornings he drew faces. Always faces. Most often the one in the fine coat—the officer whose likeness he scratched into candle wax, flour dust, the fogged window glass—with a careful, terrible precision, drawing the collar, the braid, the hard mouth, over and over, like a boy learning his prayers.

Colonel Ashworth's face. Though Oscar didn't know the name, and Jude had never said it aloud where he could hear.

* * *

The orders came on a Thursday, carried up from the town by a dragoon who stamped snow off his boots in the ward doorway and handed Jude the packet meant for Surgeon-Major Quill with the air of a man delivering a fish.

Quill read it standing by the stove, his eyebrows climbing, and then he laughed—one short bark with nothing in it—and passed the pages to Jude because his eyes had been failing all winter and she did most of his close reading now.

"Go on, then."

She read it twice to be sure.

*By order of Colonel R. Ashworth, commanding: upon opening of the river to navigation, the regiment will march for the west. All records, registers, correspondence, and papers of whatsoever kind held at this station are to be burned prior to departure. No document is to be transported. No document is to survive.*

"No document is to survive," she repeated.

"Efficiency," said Quill. "The army's soul." He lowered himself onto a stool, joints cracking, and rubbed his face. "It's not unusual, you understand. Regiments burn their filth when they move. Half the paperwork of this war has gone up chimneys."

"Not the muster rolls."

"The muster rolls go to the Horse Guards. The rest—" he waved a hand, "—ash. And this colonel wants the ash swept too, I'd wager. Have you seen the annex to it?"

She hadn't. She turned the page. There it was, in the same round clerk's hand:

*A full inventory of all papers held is to be submitted within seven days, that nothing be overlooked in the burning. Any paper found unlisted after the burning shall be presumed suppressed deliberately, and treated accordingly.*

"Treated accordingly," Jude said. "Lovely. Treated how?"

"I shouldn't wonder." Quill took the orders back and set them on the stove, watching them rather than reading them, as if they might bite. "You'll want to make your inventory honest, Nurse Kavanagh."

There it was. Not an accusation. Worse—an understanding, delivered flatly, by a tired old man who had served thirty years and knew exactly what lived in the workshop besides patients.

"I keep a treatment register," she said. "Admissions, discharges, deaths. Standard."

"I know what you keep." He looked at her then, and his eyes behind the clouding lenses were sharp as lancets. "I've known since the Halloran business. You think nobody notices you writing at night? Woman, the walls are thinner than your discretion." He sighed. "I'm not telling you to stop. I'm telling you the arithmetic has changed. Seven days to declare every sheet of paper in this building. After that, whatever you've hidden is contraband, and contraband gets people hanged or worse."

"Worse than hanged?"

"In this regiment, lately? Yes."

He got up stiffly and went to see his frostbite cases, and Jude stood alone by the stove with the orders lying on the iron plate, warming, curling slightly at the corners, as if eager for the fire they promised.

* * *

She did the arithmetic that evening in the office, by lamplight, with the door barred and Oscar asleep against her shoulder because he would not settle unless she sat with him first.

Weeks until the thaw opened the river properly—she guessed four, perhaps five. Ice this year had been thick, but the sun climbed higher every day now, and the groaning had begun, and groaning became cracking and cracking became leads of black water, and then the barges would come and the regiment would leave and the fires would be lit. Call it thirty days. Thirty-five if the spring came grudging.

Against that: forty-one pages written, and how many names unwritten?

She made a list of debts. Sergeant Brannigan—she owed him hours of listening; his old wound was weeping again and men who wept like that, slowly, inwardly, were spending themselves faster than they knew, and she suspected there were things he wanted said before he stopped saying anything at all. The convalescents in the ward—eleven of them, each with a story the dispatches had flattened into *wounded, doing well*. The laundresses. The carpenter's apprentices. The dead of December and January she had noted but not finished—their mothers' names, their villages, the small particulars that made a name more than ink.

And the duplicate. Because a single ledger, however well hidden, was one fire away from nothing. If the workshop burned, the ledger burned. If the ledger were seized, everything died with it. Two copies, then, in two places, and maybe a third in pieces, sewn into bandage rolls or bread loaves—no, bread went stale and got eaten, bandages got used. She would think about the third copy later. First: two.

Thirty days. She calculated the copying at an hour a night, two if the light allowed—no, light was the problem, lamplight drew eyes, and her windows faced the yard. She would have to write in the linen store, behind the drying sheets, with the shuttered lantern Mags used for night bleeding. Twenty pages copied so far. Sixty to go, plus new entries arriving daily, plus Brannigan's testimony, which might run long.

It was not enough time. But it was almost enough time, and almost was the only currency she had ever traded in.

Oscar stirred against her shoulder. She felt him wake the way animals wake—all at once, silent, tense—and then relax as her thumb moved in a slow circle between his shoulder blades. He didn't speak still. Three months under her roof and not one word, though he had begun making sounds lately, small ones, a hum when Tilda sang, a grunt of satisfaction at food. The words were in there. She had watched them gathering behind his teeth like water behind ice.

She thought about the orders again. *No document is to survive.* A man did not write such a sentence over laundry lists. That sentence was written over a lieutenant who had died inconveniently, and over an inquiry closed too fast, and over a boy who had heard something in a tent one night and been carried through a blizzard to a nurse's door with coins and an instruction to forget his face.

Forget his face, the aide had said. As if faces were things a person could put down.

"You're a document," she murmured into Oscar's hair. "Did you know that? The only one he can't burn."

The boy said nothing. But his hand came up and closed around a fold of her sleeve and held it, and she sat in the lamplight doing sums she already knew the answer to, counting down days she could feel draining away like heat from a cooling stove, and outside, under the moonless dark, the river went on talking to itself in the deep voice of a thing that intended, this spring, to be free.

* * *

Brannigan found her at dawn on the ice road, of all places. She had gone down to the landing stage to watch the first lead open—it was superstition, she admitted it, wanting to see the enemy's timetable begin—and he came limping along the shore path with his stick, breath smoking, and stood beside her without greeting, because they had long ago spent their greetings.

"Orders came," he said.

"They did."

"Burning the papers." He spat, expertly, through the gap in his teeth, onto the ice. "Thirty years of service and they'll not leave so much as a laundry chit to say we were here. You know what'll be left of this winter? The colonel's dispatch. One page. *The regiment conducted itself with distinction.*" He said the words in a fair imitation of Ashworth's drawing-room voice, then dropped it. "Two hundred men in the ground along that stretch of shore and one page to show for it."

"There's more than one page," Jude said quietly.

Brannigan looked at her. On the river behind him, with a sound like a giant knuckle cracking, a seam split fifty yards out, and a thin line of black water showed and froze their breath and closed again.

"Aye," he said. "I know there is. I've known since autumn, girl, I'm old, not blind." His hand went to his side, to the wound under the coat, the old habitual gesture. "I've a thing to say to your ledger. More than a thing. It'll take nights."

"It can take all the nights there are."

"There aren't." He said it without drama, looking out at the water line. "I can feel the accounting coming, Nurse. Old wounds tell you. Like weather in a joint." He turned, and for a moment the hardness went out of his face and what was underneath was just a tired man of fifty with a brother buried in a field he couldn't reach. "Start tomorrow. Start tonight. Whatever nights I've got, I'll spend them on your pages, and when the burning comes, let them stand around the ashes wondering where the truth went."

Behind them, up the slope, the workshop bell rang the waking hour, and smoke rose straight from the chimney into windless air. The river cracked again, nearer now, and neither of them flinched. They had both spent too many winters listening to ice to fear the sound of something ending.

But Jude felt it in her chest all the same, cold and clear as the water showing through: the clock had started. Thirty days, maybe less. Enough nights to finish, if she stole every one of them.

And somewhere in the town behind them, she would learn later, a clerk was already drawing up the inventory list, seven days given, every sheet of paper in the station to be declared—or else.
