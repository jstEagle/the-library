## 14. Brannigan's Testimony

He had rehearsed it for three nights, lying on his cot above the tannery, going through it the way he'd once gone through drill orders—first light, second light, form line, advance. Except this was the reverse of an order. This was an un-ordering. A taking back.

Sergeant Declan Brannigan was fifty-one years old and had been dying since Talavera, slow as rot in wet timber, the French ball still lodged against his spine where no surgeon would go after it. He knew what the march south would cost him. Every man in the workshop town knew. They'd begun quietly not meeting his eye when the rations were counted, the way men do when they've already decided who won't be eating by midsummer. He didn't mind that. What he minded was the other thing—the thing that sat in his chest like a second wound, the one that had nothing to do with lead.

His brother's name wasn't anywhere it mattered.

* * *

He found her at dusk in the workshop's back room, where the lamplight came amber through frost-ferned windows and the smell of boiled linen hung over everything like weather. Nurse Kavanagh was at her table with the ledger open before her, and around her the small nightly machinery of the place: Mags grinding willow bark, Tilda folding shirts into squares sharp enough to cut. Oscar sat under the table with a stub of charcoal, drawing faces on a board, and looked up at Brannigan with those eyes that never spoke.

"Sergeant." Kavanagh didn't rise. She'd stopped rising for officers months ago; she wasn't about to start for sergeants. "You're grey. Sit down before you fall down."

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit," said Tilda, without looking up from her folding, "or I'll fetch the chair myself and be insulted either way."

He sat. That was the thing about this room—he'd understood it slowly, over the winter—that here you did as you were told by washerwomen and it cost you nothing. In the army obedience was currency. Here it was just how people were kind.

Kavanagh turned the ledger toward herself but kept two fingers resting on its edge, watching him. She had a way of waiting that could strip paint. He'd seen her use it on surgeons and quartermasters alike. Now she used it on him, and he felt the speech he'd rehearsed begin to come apart in his hands like wet paper.

"I want you to write something down," he said.

Her fingers stilled on the page.

"Not in your book of names. Not yet. First—" He stopped. Started again. "First I want to say it out loud, all of it, so I know I can."

The room had gone quiet in the particular way rooms went quiet when women decided to listen without admitting they were listening. Mags kept grinding, but slower. Tilda's hands had stopped moving over the shirts.

"The eleventh of September," Brannigan said. "Last year. The action at the mill ford."

Kavanagh didn't blink. "There's no action at the mill ford in the dispatches."

"No," he said. "There isn't."

* * *

It came out of him then not the way he'd rehearsed it—measured, soldierly—but the way water comes out of a cracked cask, all at once and sideways, and he let it come because stopping it now would mean never starting again.

"There were four companies," he said. "Two hundred and six men across the ford at first light, because Colonel Ashworth wanted the mill taken before the enemy could spike the guns they'd left there. Only they hadn't left the guns. They'd left the guns and two companies of infantry behind the mill wall, and we knew it—Lieutenant Vane knew it, he'd had a man across the river in the night, a local lad, and the lad told us plain as Sunday: there's troops behind the wall, sir, there's troops in the mill itself."

Brannigan's hands were shaking. He put them flat on his knees.

"Lieutenant Vane was twenty-three years old. Younger than my boots. But he wrote to the colonel—wrote it down, in ink, which was his mistake—and he asked for the attack to wait until the pontoon company came up, or until fog, or anything. And Ashworth sent back a verbal reply through Major Cleese. One sentence. I heard Cleese deliver it because I was standing right there holding the lieutenant's horse. The colonel's compliments, and the lieutenant would take the ford at once, and any officer who hesitated would answer for it."

"He ordered it knowing?" Kavanagh asked. Her voice was very even. Even the way ice is even.

"He'd been told there were troops there. He ordered it anyway. Why does a man do that, nurse? I've asked myself all winter." Brannigan looked at the window, at the dark coming down on the frozen river. "I'll tell you why. Because the colonel had promised the mill taken by noon in a letter to division, and because a dead lieutenant is cheaper than a colonel's embarrassed signature. Because the boy was nobody's nephew and nobody's friend and he'd put his objection in writing, and written objections have a way of surviving wars and turning up in inquiries. You don't send a man like that home to London. You send him across a ford."

Oscar had gone still under the table. Brannigan saw it—the boy's charcoal hand frozen mid-stroke, the whole small body listening the way animals listen, with their skin. His chest tightened. He'd guessed, weeks ago, whose child that was. The face in the fine coat, drawn over and over. The mouth that wouldn't open.

"We went across," he said. "Two hundred and six went across. We took the ford because soldiers take things, that's the whole cursed trade of it, and we held it four hours, and then we came back with sixty-one men fit to walk. Denny—" his voice caught, and he hated it, hated that fifty years hadn't taught his throat better manners— "my brother Denny was third over the ford and he didn't come back across it. They fished eleven of ours out of the shallows two days later. The rest stayed where they fell. There was no time given to bury them properly, because the regiment moved on the next morning, and the official record says—"

He recited it flat, like scripture learned for punishment:

"'A successful reconnaissance in force was conducted at the mill ford. Casualties: light.' Light. Sixty-one walking out of two hundred and six, and the word is *light.*"

Nobody in the room breathed. Somewhere out in the workshop a hammer rang twice against iron and stopped, as if the hammer too were listening.

"And the inquiry?" Kavanagh asked quietly.

"There was to be an inquiry. Division ordered one. Then it wasn't ordered anymore. Papers went missing—the lieutenant's letter went missing, I know because I asked the adjutant for a copy and watched his face do a small complicated thing. The witnesses were scattered. I was reassigned here, to the workshop, to count nails and mend cart harness, which is where you put a sergeant whose memory is inconvenient. And last month Cleese stood up in front of the assembled officers and gave testimony that the lieutenant had attacked on his own initiative, against orders, in disobedience." Brannigan laughed, one dry note with no humor in it whatsoever. "They made him the author of his own murder. Dead men can't contradict you. That's the whole art of command, right there. That's the secret of it."

* * *

Kavanagh had not touched the pen through any of it. Now she drew the ledger toward her, opened it—not to the current pages but back, back, to a page Brannigan had glimpsed only once before, the night she'd shown him his brother's name.

*Dennis Brannigan, private, 3rd Company. Felled at an unnamed action.*

She had crossed out *unnamed* and written *mill ford* in her own small fierce hand, months ago, when he'd first told her the little he could bear to tell. But the page beside it—the page for Lieutenant Vane—said only: *Lieutenant A. Vane. Died of wounds received in action. No further particulars recorded.*

"That's what history knows," she said. "That's everything the army will ever admit. Died of wounds received in action. It sounds almost peaceful, doesn't it. Like weather."

"I know what I'm asking," Brannigan said. "I've thought on it every night since the search of the workshop. Soldiers came through these rooms hunting papers, and Tilda paid for it, and I sat on my cot and did nothing because there was nothing yet worth doing. But if I carry this account to the grave unspoken—if it dies in my head the way Denny died in that water—then Ashworth wins twice. He wins the battle and he wins the story. And the story is the only part of it that ever belonged to us."

Mags had set down her mortar. Tilda had turned fully around, shirts forgotten. Even Ferro had come to the doorway, sawdust still on his sleeves, filling it with his bulk, saying nothing, which from Ferro was a speech.

"Dictate it to me," Kavanagh said. "All of it. Names, hours, distances, who said what and who heard them say it. Everything you remember, in the order you remember it, even if the order's wrong. Truth doesn't have to be tidy. It just has to be written down somewhere they can't burn."

"They burned the records at Salamanca," Brannigan said. "Whole wagonloads. Nobody blinked."

"They can burn paper," said Tilda. "They can't burn everyone who's read it."

Brannigan looked at her—at the washerwoman with knuckles like walnuts—and thought that she was more of a soldier than most captains he'd served under, and that the army would never know it, and that maybe that was the army's loss and not hers.

"All right," he said. "Then write this."

And he began at the beginning, the true beginning, which was not the eleventh of September at all but the ninth, when the local boy had swum the river with intelligence in his teeth and Lieutenant Vane had listened to him—actually listened, which was already unusual in an officer—and written his warning in a careful schoolboy hand. Brannigan gave her the wording of the letter as near as memory held it, and marked where memory failed. He gave her the hour the order came back. He gave her Major Cleese's face while delivering it—flushed, looking at the ground, a man doing a thing he was ashamed of and would spend a career being ashamed of, or wouldn't, depending on how it turned out. He gave her the depth of the water at the ford, the placement of the enemy behind the mill wall, the number of rounds fired, the number lost, name by name by name—sixty-one walking, one hundred and forty-five not, and of those he could give her thirty-eight Christian names and surnames from memory alone, because a sergeant carries his company in his skull the way a mother carries children, whether anyone asks him to or not.

Kavanaghan wrote without stopping. Her pen scratched like a small animal burrowing. Twice she stopped him—*say that again, slower*—and once she looked up and said, "You're certain Cleese used that exact phrase? *Answer for it?*" and he said yes, ma'am, certain as he was of his own baptism, and she wrote it down with a pressure that nearly tore the page.

Under the table, Oscar had come out and sat against Brannigan's boot. The boy didn't look at him. He looked at the ledger, at the names going down in rows, and his charcoal hand moved on his board, copying the shapes as best a child could, letterless, making marks that meant something only to him. Brannigan understood then that the boy was keeping the testimony too, in his own language, the one that needed no letters. Two ledgers in one room. The army should be afraid, he thought, and was surprised to find that he was smiling.

It took two hours. When it was done, Kavanagh read it back to him entire, every word, her voice low and level, and he corrected her twice, and then he said, "Read me the end of it again," and she read:

"*I, Declan Brannigan, sergeant, make this statement freely, knowing it may cost me whatever standing I have left and possibly my life. I heard the order given. I saw the ground. I carried my brother's body out of the water myself. Let any man who calls this account false come to me and say so to my face, while I am still alive to answer him.*"

She set down the pen. "Sign it."

"My name's already in your book. Denny's name."

"This isn't Denny's page," she said. "This is yours."

He took the pen. His hand shook worse now than it had all evening, and he pressed hard to steady it, and the signature came out deep-scored, the ink of a man carving rather than writing. Declan Brannigan. Underneath, the date, and underneath that, in Kavanagh's hand, small and deliberate: *Witnessed and recorded in the workshop hospital, on the frozen river, by the undersigned.*

But she didn't sign it. He watched her hover the pen over the line and then draw it back, and something in her face—a flinch, a refusal, old as she was old—made his heart go heavy in a new way.

"You'll witness it," he said. "Your own rule. Every name must be written somewhere."

"In a moment," she said, and closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Tonight it goes where tonight's work goes. Tomorrow it gets witnessed. I don't break my own rules, Sergeant. Not even the ones I keep for the wrong reasons."

* * *

He slept that night better than he had in a year, which he took as an omen, though of what kind he couldn't say. Men who have finished their accounts sleep well. It's the unfinished ones who pace.

He woke before dawn to voices below the tannery stairs—low, urgent, Tilda's voice among them, and another voice he knew and dreaded, the adjutant's, smooth as oiled leather. He hauled himself up on his elbows, breath rasping, and heard the words come through the floorboard gaps in fragments, each fragment worse than the last.

"—the barge pilot has been questioned—"

"—someone talked—"

"—the colonel wants the carpenter's chest opened at first light—"

And then Tilda's voice, hard as a hoof on stone: "And if it's empty?"

A pause. The adjutant's laugh, soft and unpleasant, drifting up through the boards like smoke.

"Then, madam, we shall have to ask where it isn't."

Brannigan lay in the dark with his heart slamming and his useless legs aching, and thought of the ledger, and the boy, and the chest, and the ice on the river beginning, very faintly, far off, to groan.
