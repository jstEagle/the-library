## 15. Ashworth's Bargain

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had learned long ago that men could be bought in one of two ways: with fear or with the thing they wanted most and had never dared ask for. Fear worked quickly but left a residue—resentment, caution, the habit of looking over one's shoulder. The other method was cleaner. A man handed his own dignity over willingly, and thanked you for taking it.

The trouble with Jude Kavanagh was that she did not appear to want anything.

He had observed her twice now, once across the ward during his inspection and once through Sergeant Brannigan's clumsy attempts at misdirection, and both times he had come away with the same unsettled feeling he got from an unfinished ledger entry—a column that refused to sum. She was not ambitious in any way he could read. She took no coin. She kept no company worth cultivating. She mended men and wrote her little lists and asked for nothing, which made her either the most honest person in the regiment or the most dangerous.

He suspected both.

The report from the inquiry clerk sat on his desk under a paperweight of river stone: *a ledger exists, kept by the workshop nurse, containing names omitted from official correspondence.* The clerk who'd brought it—a nervous captain named Pell with ink on his cuffs—had spoken the words as though they were trivial. Names omitted. As if the omission were a clerical courtesy rather than a policy. As if the ledger were a curiosity instead of what it actually was: a mirror held up to every decision Ashworth had made since the autumn, reflecting back the parts he had chosen not to see.

Lieutenant Harrow's death had been handled. The inquiry was closed, testimony arranged, the widow's pension approved at half rate with a letter expressing the regiment's condolences in language so polished it gleamed. That part of his house was swept. But a woman on the frozen river was writing down everything he'd swept under, in her own hand, in her own book, night after night like some parish clerk of the damned.

He could have her arrested. He had considered it for exactly four minutes before discarding the idea. Arrest created questions. Questions created witnesses. Witnesses created paper, and paper was the one enemy that outlived everyone. No—if Kavanagh was going to be neutralized, she would be neutralized in a way that produced no record at all. In a way that made her complicit.

In a way, if he was honest with himself—and he permitted himself honesty only in this room, alone, after the candles were trimmed—that made her grateful.

* * *

He came to the workshop at dusk, without escort, which was itself a message. A colonel who arrived alone was either trusting or confident, and the distinction rarely mattered. The thaw had begun its first tentative work; the ice still held, but there was water talking underneath it now, a sound like whispering in an empty church, and the light off the river had gone soft and grey as old linen.

Inside, the ward smelled of carbolic and woodsmoke and wet wool. Men lay in rows, missing this or that portion of themselves, breathing in ragged chorus. And there she was at the far table, sleeves rolled, writing. Not charting—he knew charting when he saw it—but writing in that book, unhurried, as if the world were a thing that happened elsewhere and she were merely its accountant.

She looked up when his shadow fell across the page. She did not stand. He noted that; filed it away.

"Colonel."

"Mrs.—no. Miss Kavanagh." He let the small error hang, a demonstration that he had looked into her, that he knew she was nobody's wife, nobody's daughter of consequence, a woman who had drifted to the edge of the army the way driftwood finds a shore. "May I?" He indicated the chair opposite without waiting for permission and sat.

"You can," she said, capping her inkwell anyway. "I've ten minutes before the fever rounds."

"I'll be brief, then." He folded his hands. Outside, a sledge runner squealed past on the frozen lane. "You keep a ledger."

It was not a question, and he watched her decide whether to pretend otherwise. To her credit, the pretense lasted less than a second.

"I do."

"Names of the dead. The wounded. Men the dispatches pass over."

"Some of them." Her voice was level, unremarkable, the voice of a woman discussing flour. "Officers are well recorded already. They have clerks."

"They have clerks," Ashworth agreed pleasantly. "What they don't have—" he paused, choosing the shape of it, "—is your industry. I'm told you've been at it all winter. Every man through this workshop, entered by name, regiment, wound, date. Some of them, I understand, entered contrary to the official record."

"Contrary is a strong word."

"It's the word I've chosen."

For a moment neither spoke. A man three beds down groaned in his sleep and settled. Somewhere behind the partition wall, Ferro's saw whispered through pine—the sound of a leg being built for someone, or a box being built for something.

"You should know," Ashworth said, "that I am not here to threaten you. I find threats vulgar, and worse, unreliable. A threatened person becomes a careful person, and careful people do unpredictable things at inconvenient hours." He smiled, and was aware of how the smile worked on most faces, how it promised reasonableness while reserving everything else. It did not appear to work on hers. "I'm here to make you an offer."

"I don't need money."

"Everyone needs money. But no, that isn't the offer—or rather, it's the smallest part of it." He leaned forward slightly, and lowered his voice as though the sleeping men might overhear their own futures being negotiated. "In six weeks, perhaps five, the ice opens. This regiment marches west and this workshop burns with everything else. Orders are orders. You will be given transport—you always were; you're on the rolls, however far down. You'd have gone on nursing somewhere new, in some other freezing annex of somebody else's war, invisible again, keeping your lists for no one."

Her expression did not change, but something behind it did. He had spent thirty years reading officers' faces across negotiating tables, and he recognized the flicker: she had not known about the burning. Or she had guessed, and hearing it said aloud was different. He pressed the advantage gently.

"Here is what I propose instead. When we move, you come with us—not as a follower, not as camp baggage. With a commission. Matron of the new divisional hospital at Veldenz. Your own wards. Your own staff, chosen by you. Authority over forty beds and every surgeon junior enough to resent it and obey anyway. A salary with figures in it you have probably never seen attached to your own name. Respectability, Miss Kavanagh—not the borrowed kind that lasts as long as a war, but the kind that survives into peacetime, that gets you a post in a city hospital when the guns go quiet. There are women who would commit crimes for such a thing. There are women who have."

He watched her hands. They were still on the table, scarred at the knuckles from lye and needlework, absolutely motionless. Stillness, in his experience, was what desire looked like on people who had trained themselves out of wanting.

"And in exchange," she said quietly.

"In exchange, the ledger comes to me. All of it. And the boy comes to the care appropriate to him—which is to say, to officers equipped to determine his disposition. You will have performed a service to the regiment. The service will be remembered. Officially, discreetly, in the way services to regiments are best remembered."

"The boy," she repeated. Not the ledger first. Interesting. He catalogued it: the book was principle; the child was something warmer and therefore more useful. "He's six years old and mute with grief, Colonel. What disposition does a six-year-old require?"

"That," Ashworth said, "is precisely the sort of question I am empowered to answer and you are not."

* * *

He gave her until morning, because urgency reads as weakness in a negotiator, and because he genuinely believed the answer would be yes. He had done the arithmetic on the walk back along the river path, boots crunching on snow gone granular with the thaw, breath smoking in the blue dusk. What did Jude Kavanagh have? A drafty workshop, a dying sergeant, a washerwoman, a carpenter, an old man who smelled of laudanum. A book of names that history would never read, because history did not live on frozen rivers—it lived in ministries, in marble corridors, in the offices of men like him. Against that he offered her the door into the marble. People did not refuse that door. He had watched hardened men weep with gratitude for lesser versions of it.

There was one variable he had not fully weighted, and it irritated him that evening at dinner, between the soup and the meat, when he found himself calculating it despite himself: the boy. Oscar, they called him now. He had sent Pell to confirm the name, and Pell had confirmed it with the particular blankness of a man who suspects his errand matters and hopes to God it doesn't.

A child who had heard things. A child whose face certain people had ordered forgotten. Ashworth turned his wineglass by the stem and considered the boy the way he considered terrain: what could be occupied, what had to be crossed, what was better destroyed than defended. The lieutenant had been dealt with honorably, more or less; honor was a currency he spent carefully. But the boy was not honorable business. The boy was loose ends walking around in a coat two sizes too big.

And the woman wanted to keep him. That was the flaw in his arithmetic. He had priced her ambition correctly, but he had assumed the ambition stood alone. Ambition plus attachment was a different equation entirely—one he could still solve, but messier, requiring more force than he preferred to spend.

Unless the offer itself solved it. Unless, given a night with the future spread out before her like clean linen, she chose it. Chose warmth, light, permanence, her name spoken with respect in rooms where names mattered. People told themselves stories to make surrender bearable, and the best story was always the same one: *I did it for the greater good; I did it because I had no choice; I did it and no one truly suffered.* He had built a career on the reliability of that story.

He slept well.

* * *

She came to him at dawn. He had expected a message, a summons accepted, the whole transaction conducted through intermediaries like civilized commerce—but no, she walked to headquarters herself through the grey slush of first light, alone, and stood in his office with her shawl still stiff with frost, and he understood that she intended this to be a meeting between two people, which was either respect or insolence, and with her it was impossible to say.

"Well?" he said.

"I thought about it." Her voice was steady. "All night. I want you to know that. I didn't dismiss it. I sat with it like a coal in my hand and I let it burn."

Something in her tone made him sit very still.

"My own hospital," she went on. "Forty beds. Staff I choose. A salary. My name said aloud by men like you as though it were worth saying." She looked at the window, where the river showed pale between the buildings, ice still holding but thinner now, darker at the edges. "Do you know what I was before this war? Nothing anyone recorded. A daughter who kept accounts for a father who forgot them. A widow of eleven months who inherited debts. I have spent my whole life being the person who writes down other people's names, Colonel. So believe me when I tell you I understand exactly what you're offering me. It isn't a hospital. It's a self. One that counts."

"And?"

"And I sat with it until nearly three in the morning." She turned back to him, and her eyes were red-rimmed—from cold, or from the sitting-with-it, he couldn't tell, and for the first time in many years he found he genuinely could not predict a human being standing in front of him. "I'm not giving you my answer today. I'm telling you I'll give it honestly, which is more than you'd get from most people in this regiment."

Ashworth felt something cold slide beneath his ribs. Delay was not refusal—but delay was not acceptance either, and he had offered her a door and she had responded by making him wait in his own hallway.

"Miss Kavanagh—"

"Three days," she said. "When the ice starts moving, you'll have your answer. And whatever it is, you'll know it was true." She paused at the door, and for one moment her composure thinned and he glimpsed what was underneath: not defiance, but exhaustion, deep as the river. "You should also know that last night I copied nothing and hid nothing and planned nothing. I just sat with it. That's the honest truth, and I'm giving it to you free, because I suspect you've had very little truth lately and might not recognize it."

Then she was gone, boots fading down the corridor, and Ashworth sat alone with his papers and the sound of the river whispering under its thinning shell.

Three days. He poured himself the first brandy of the morning and told himself that patience was simply force applied slowly. But in the ledger of his mind, where he recorded everything and admitted little, he opened a fresh column and entered, in his own precise hand, a single line:

*Kavanagh — undecided — contingency required.*

Outside, far downriver, the ice groaned like a ship changing its mind.
