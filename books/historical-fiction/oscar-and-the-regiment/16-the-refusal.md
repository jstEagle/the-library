## 16. The Refusal

He came at dusk, when the workshop smelled of tallow and wet wool and the light through the east windows had gone the color of old bone. Colonel Ashworth arrived without his aide this time, without even a driver — just himself and a hired sledge that waited in the lane with its horse steaming, as if he expected the visit to be short.

I knew before he spoke that it would be the offer. Men like Ashworth always arrive at the same door eventually: first menace, then money. He'd done the menace in the winter, standing over my mending table with his gloves on, telling me how easily a nurse could be reassigned to the lines. He had done the searching through his soldiers in February. What remained was the coin.

"Mrs. Kavanagh." He never used my right name — Kavanagh wasn't mine by marriage or anything else; I'd chosen it the way you choose a coat off a dead man's back — but he pronounced it carefully, like a man handling something he didn't want to touch bare-handed.

"Colonel."

"You'll forgive the hour." He removed his hat, which was more courtesy than he'd shown me all winter, and looked around the ward as if taking inventory. Twenty-two cots. Nine men well enough to sleep in the loft above. The great stove Ferro had rebuilt twice because the army wouldn't send proper iron. "I've come to make you a proposal."

"I don't gamble, sir."

"This isn't gambling. It's arithmetic." He set his hat on my mending table, precisely centered, and I understood it was meant to look like ease. It looked like a man marking territory. "The regiment marches when the ice breaks. Three weeks, perhaps four. You will be left behind — I have arranged for your discharge papers to include a commendation. A hospital of your own in York. Staff, supplies, a salary that a woman in your position cannot otherwise expect in ten lifetimes. Respectability, Mrs. Kavanagh. Real respectability, not this—" he gestured at the ward, at Tilda's washing lines strung across the rafters, at the smell of lye and gangrene — "this garrison charity."

"And in exchange."

"The ledger. And the boy."

There it was. He said *the boy* the way he said everything, evenly, without heat, and somehow that was worse than if he'd snarled. Oscar was asleep in the corner cot they'd let him keep, curled against Mags' spare shawl, one fist under his chin. He'd slept badly since the search — since the soldiers turned out our stores and took Tilda away in irons for three days for nothing, for a pretext so thin it insulted us all. He slept now only because exhaustion had finally outrun fear.

"He is six years old," I said.

"He is evidence."

"He is a child whose father is dead."

"Whose father," Ashworth said, "was an officer who disobeyed a direct order and got eleven men killed doing it, and whose death broke his mother's heart, God rest her, and whose son has been cared for at considerable regimental expense by men who owe him nothing." He let that sit a moment. "That is the record, Mrs. Kavanagh. That is what history will say. Unless you prefer another version."

"And yours is kinder?"

"Mine is finished." For the first time something moved under the smoothness — not anger exactly. Impatience. The impatience of a man who has already written the ending and finds the world slow to copy it out. "You are a practical woman. You've kept this place running through a winter that should have closed it. You count things. So count this: one woman's pride against a boy's whole future. With me he goes to his mother's people in Kent. School, land, a name restored. Without me—" he spread his hands, and the gesture included the frozen river, the burning records, the war itself — "without me he is a nameless child smuggled out of a garrison town by persons unknown, and persons unknown are hanged when they're caught. Or transported. There are degrees of mercy even in discipline."

"You'd hang Ferro."

"Ferro's choices are Ferro's."

"They'd be your choices. You'd see to it."

"Mrs. Kavanagh." He picked up his hat again, turned it once in his gloved hands. "I have commanded men for nineteen years. I have sent them across ground I knew would kill them, because the order came from above me and the order above that came from a man who needed the ground taken before Tuesday. Lieutenant Harrow was given such an order. He questioned it — loudly, in front of the wrong people — and he was sent anyway, and he died, and the inquiry found what inquiries find. I did not write that system. I am merely the last pair of hands it passes through. And those hands," he said, "are offering you York."

I thought about it. That's the thing I've never told anyone, and I'll tell it here because this book is where true things go: I thought about it. Not long — the length of a breath, maybe two — but honestly. I saw the hospital. Clean floors that weren't river-ice. A bed I didn't share with the laudanum stores. My own key to my own door, after nine years of sleeping in rooms that belonged to the army, the parish, the surgeon, anyone but me. I am forty-one years old and I have been invisible so long that visibility felt like a foreign country, and Ashworth was offering me papers of passage to it.

And Oscar, safe, in Kent, learning Latin.

It would have been so easy to believe that was the same as saving him.

"No," I said.

Ashworth's face did not change. That was the terrible skill of him. "You should consider—"

"I have considered. The answer is no." I heard my own voice and was surprised by it — level, unhurried, a voice I didn't know I owned until that moment. "You asked me once how easily a nurse could be reassigned. Let me tell you what I know instead. Eleven men died at Harrow's charge, and your dispatch lists seven. Four names went into nothing. Sergeant Brannigan's brother went into nothing. Private Osei went into nothing, though he crawled half a mile on his elbows toward our line before he froze. I know because he reached the picket rope. I cut him loose from it myself." My hands were steady. I watched them be steady, almost with curiosity. "Those names exist because I wrote them down, and they will continue to exist whether I am in York or in the ground, because I have made arrangements. You can burn this workshop, Colonel. You can burn every paper in it. You cannot burn four copies."

A lie. There were not yet four copies. There was one ledger and my memory and God's, and between those three things stood a woman with chilblains. But Ashworth couldn't know that, and I watched him decide he couldn't afford to assume otherwise, and I learned in that instant what power actually looks like from the underside: it isn't strength. It's the other man's uncertainty, borrowed and spent like coin.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that when we go, the orders include the destruction of all records. All of them. The workshop with its contents."

"The workshop with its contents. Yes. I read the standing order when it was posted."

"Then you know I'm not threatening you. I'm describing the weather."

"You're describing a fire you intend to light." I walked to the door and opened it, and the cold came in and stood with us. Down the lane his hired sledge waited, and beyond it the river lay white and silent under its lid of ice, holding back the spring the way it held back everything — patiently, and not forever. "Names are not yours to spend, Colonel. You spend soldiers like they're yours, and then you burn the receipts. I'm keeping the receipts."

For a moment — just a moment — the smoothness went out of his face entirely, and what was underneath was small and cold and mortal, a man of fifty-three with a career balanced on a dead lieutenant and a mute child, and I thought: there you are. Then it sealed over again, polished as a button.

"When the water opens," he said, settling his hat, "this building burns. Everything in it burns. I want you to carry that with you tonight, Mrs. Kavanagh. Not as a threat. As a courtesy. So that whatever you choose to save, you choose knowing the cost."

"Thank you for the courtesy."

He paused at the threshold. Behind him the dusk had thickened; somewhere downriver the ice groaned, that long deep complaint it made at nightfall, like a giant turning in its sleep. "Harrow's boy talks now, I'm told," he said. "Whatever he told you, children confabulate. They stitch dreams into what they overhear and hand it back as fact. No court on earth weighs a six-year-old's testimony."

"No court on earth," I agreed. "But courts aren't the only place testimony gets weighed."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the first time all winter I'd seen him actually calculate rather than simply prevail — and then he stepped past me into the dark and the sledge took him away, runners hissing on the packed snow, and I stood in the doorway until the sound was gone and the cold had gotten into my shoulders and I still didn't move, because moving would mean beginning, and beginning meant there was no version of the future anymore except the hard one.

Oscar was awake. I saw it in the way the shawl moved — the too-stillness of a child pretending. I went and sat on the edge of his cot.

"He offered to send you to school in Kent," I said. "With your mother's family."

His eyes were enormous in the lamplight. He didn't ask what I'd said. Children always know what was said; it's the adults who pretend otherwise.

"I said no," I told him. "You're staying with us. With me. Until the water opens, and after it, if I can manage it."

He reached up and took a fold of my sleeve between his fingers, the way he'd done the first week, when he couldn't speak and couldn't stop touching things to prove they were real. Then he let go, and rolled over, and was asleep within minutes, because children can do that — close the day like a book — while I sat there a long while with the lamp burning low, listening to the ice talk to itself in the dark, planning.

* * *

I started copying that same night, and I did not stop, in any real sense, for nineteen days.

The problem was never ink or paper — the workshop had both, more paper than sense, requisition forms and sick-lists and the endless bureaucratic sediment the army leaves behind everywhere it pools. The problem was time and eyes. I nursed by day. I copied by night, by the stove, with the wick trimmed to its lowest honest flame, and I learned to write smaller than I'd ever written, crowding names into columns fine as stitching. Brannigan's dictated testimony alone ran to eleven pages. The failed charge, the order, the ground, the weather, who hesitated, who didn't, what Harrow said aloud and to whom — I wrote it all in a hand so cramped that only Mags and I could read it, and Mags checked every page against the original, her clouded eyes an inch from the paper, because she was the only one of us whose sight was better close-up than far away.

Then the hiding, which became its own craft, and here Ferro earned his keep a second time, because a carpenter thinks in cavities.

Bread loaves first — Tilda's idea, delivered flatly the evening she came back to us, thinner, quieter, refusing to say what the three days had held, and nobody pressed her because pressing her would have been a second violence. She baked for the garrison kitchen as she always had, and into certain loaves — the misshapen ones, the ones destined for the picket post downriver where the sentries were cold, bored, and honest — went oilcloth packets, flattened to the thickness of a knife blade and curved to fit the crumb. A loaf leaves the kitchen. A loaf arrives hungry at a guardhouse. No one audits bread.

Bandage rolls next, which was mine. The workshop shipped dressings to the forward posts weekly, and no quartermaster in the King's army unrolls clean linen looking for sedition. I learned to roll a page into the core of a bandage so smoothly that the roll weighed the same, looked the same, snapped open the same. Mags sewed the outer layer with a slightly different stitch — her stitch, not mine — so that we'd know our own when they came home to us, if they ever did.

And the master ledger stayed where it had always lived, inside the prosthetic leg Ferro had carved for Corporal Dunn before Dunn marched south — a leg of birch and leather, hollowed through the shin, socketed, strapped, and hung on the workshop wall among the other fittings like any piece of stock. The searchers had put their hands on it in February and found wood. Wood it remained. But now, each night, I added the day's copying to it, and each morning Ferro dusted the wall around it and remarked, loudly, that no one wanted the ugly thing, and each night it held four months of the dead against its hollow heart.

We lost pages and we gained them. One bread packet came back mold-eaten; I rewrote it from the master. Two bandage rolls vanished entirely into the army's digestive tract, destination unknown, and I chose — deliberately, lying awake choosing — to believe they'd arrived somewhere. Faith, for a practical woman, is mostly bookkeeping done in the dark.

On the twelfth night Brannigan sat with me while I copied, wrapped in three blankets in the chair nearest the stove, his old wound eating him by inches, watching my pen move.

"You write small," he said.

"So no one reads it over my shoulder."

"My brother had a hand like that." He was quiet a while. The fire ticked. "He'd have liked you, Jude. He liked stubborn women and he liked ledgers. Kept accounts of everything — debts, letters, the weight of his kit. Said a thing unwritten was a thing unfinished."

"Then he'd have approved of this."

"He'd have called it madness and then helped." Brannigan smiled, and it cost him, and he paid anyway. "Nineteen days, you said. Is that truly what's left?"

"The engineers say the ice goes mid-April. Orders say the burning happens the morning the barges can run. So yes. Nineteen days, give or take a thaw."

"And the chest? Ferro's cabinet?"

"Done. Hidden. The pilot's paid half." I didn't look up from the page. "The rest happens the last night, and the fewer who know the shape of it, the longer we all stay unhanged."

Brannigan nodded slowly, the nod of a soldier hearing a plan he's not cleared for and approving of it anyway. Then he said, "Jude. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong," he repeated, with the patience of a dying man who has no use for comfort, "you get the boy on that barge before you get the ledger on it. Promise me. Paper can be rewritten. He can't."

I put down my pen. Outside, far down the valley, the river spoke again — that long, splitting groan, deeper than it had been even a week ago, closer together now, the ice confessing in its sleep that its reign was ending. Nineteen days of white silence left. Then the water, and the fire, and whatever we could carry out between them.

"All right," I said. "The boy first. I promise."

"Good girl." He closed his eyes. "Now write. We've four names owed from the fever ward and I mean to hear them read out someday, in a warm room, by someone who never knew their faces — and I mean to know they were read."

So I wrote. Page after page, name after name, small and cramped and stubborn, while the stove burned down and the workshop breathed around me and the river counted down in the dark — and on the nineteenth morning, when Tilda pulled the first loaves of the day from the oven and slid the last oiled packet into the crumb of a misshapen loaf bound for the downriver picket, the ice gave a crack so loud it stopped every hand in the kitchen, and we all stood listening, and none of us said what we were all thinking:

It was starting early.
