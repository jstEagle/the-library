## 17. Ferro's Last Cabinet

Ferro had built coffins for officers and gun carriages for cannon, and he knew the difference between the two was mostly varnish. Both were boxes meant to hold something precious until it reached its destination. The trick of a good box was not in the wood. It was in knowing what would be asked of it, and answering before anyone thought to ask.

He had known for six days that the chest would need a false bottom.

Nobody told him. Nobody had to. He watched Jude copying pages by candlelight after the others slept, two copies now instead of one, her pen moving like something hunted. He watched Tilda folding duplicate sheets into bandage rolls and then taking them back out again, unsatisfied, because cloth could be shaken out by any corporal with a bad temper. He watched Mags counting the laudanum bottles twice and saying nothing about why.

And he watched Oscar, who sat on the floor of the workshop with his slate and drew the river. Over and over, the river. A long gray shape with a boat on it, and beside the boat, always, a small figure standing alone. The boy did not know he was drawing his own departure. Children never do. But Ferro had built enough things for people to recognize a plan when it was being made without words.

So on the sixth day, when the workshop was empty and the light through the frosted windows had gone blue and thin, Ferro took down his good oak — the stock he'd been saving for no purpose except that good wood deserved better than splints — and began measuring for a medicine chest.

* * *

The chest itself was honest work. Dovetailed corners, cut by hand because joints done by machine could be read like a signature. A hinged lid with a hasp. Inside, shallow drawers sized for phials, a lift-out tray for instruments, a compartment lined with tin for anything that leaked. A traveling apothecary's chest, the kind a surgeon's assistant might carry onto a barge without a second glance from anyone. Mags would pack it with real medicines, real bandages, real instruments, because the first rule of hiding a thing was to make everything around it true.

The false bottom went in last.

Ferro cut it from pine — lighter than the oak, less likely to betray itself by weight if some soldier hefted the chest and frowned. Two inches of clearance, running the full length. Enough space for a ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and room beside it for a boy of six, folded up small, breathing through a gap Ferro would drill along the rear rail where the molding would cover it.

He worked the joinery so the panel sat flush against runners glued and pegged inside the walls. From above, looking into the drawers, a man would see solid wood. From below, if a man turned the chest over and studied the base — and soldiers did study bases, Ferro knew, because he'd been a soldier once and had searched enough houses — there would be screws. So he used no screws. He used dowels, oak pins driven in and trimmed flush, and he left three of them loose in their holes, seated but free, so the panel could be dropped away from underneath in ten seconds by someone who knew which pins to push. He taught himself the sequence until his fingers found them blind: port side aft, starboard forward, center. Tap, tap, tap. The panel fell into his palm quiet as snow off a roof.

Then he drilled the air holes and fitted them with brass grommets scavenged from an old saddle bag, so they'd read as ventilation for the tin-lined compartment, which every proper medicine chest had anyway.

He sanded everything until his fingerprints were gone from the wood, and then he oiled it, and then he sat on his stool and looked at what he'd made, and allowed himself, for exactly the length of time it took the kettle on the stove to come to a boil, to think about the fact that he was building a coffin-shaped thing for a living child.

The kettle whistled. The thought ended. There was tea to make.

* * *

The pilot's name was Havel, and Ferro went to find him at the ice-stairs where the barges lay locked in the frozen river like animals asleep in a barn.

Havel was a Bohemian with hands like tree roots and a face that gave away nothing except, occasionally, contempt. He ran cargo downstream when the water was open and drank and mended nets when it wasn't, and he owed Ferro for a leg brace Ferro had carved him two winters past, after the ice took a barge and nearly took Havel's leg with it. Ferro had never mentioned the debt. Debts mentioned were debts halved.

Havel saw him coming and didn't get up from his net-mending. "Carpenter."

"Havel."

"You want another brace. Your knee is worse than mine, everyone knows this."

"I want passage. When the water opens."

Havel's hands kept working the net. Only his eyes came up, flat and gray as the ice. "Passage for what?"

"A chest. Medicine chest. And a passenger."

"Medicine doesn't breathe."

"This one does."

For a while there was only the sound of the net cord pulling through Havel's fingers and, far off under their feet, the deep slow groan of the river thinking about spring.

"The regiment burns its papers before it marches," Havel said at last. "Everyone knows this. Everyone knows what the colonel keeps in those papers too. You are asking me to carry something out of a burning house while the man who lit the fire is still watching the door."

"I'm asking you to carry a chest. What's in the chest is between you and your conscience, and I've seen your conscience, Havel. It drinks."

Something moved at the corner of Havel's mouth that on another man would have been a smile. "And the passenger. This breathing medicine. How old?"

"Six."

The net stopped moving.

"He'll lie still," Ferro said. "I've built it so he can. Air holes, padded boards, a lamp signal if he needs out. He'll be frightened and he'll do it anyway, because the woman who's sending him has already taught him how to be brave in small spaces, and she's very good at teaching things nobody wants to learn."

Havel set down the net. He stood, slowly, favoring the leg Ferro had saved, and looked down the frozen river toward the town, toward the workshop smoke rising straight and thin in the cold air.

"There is a toll station at the weir below the mill bend," he said. "Corporal and two men. They open every crate going down."

"Not every crate. Not a marked medical chest consigned to a surgeon's assistant, with papers signed by the workshop matron, on a day when half the garrison is loading barges and the other half is burning records. They'll wave it through because opening it is work and waving is not." Ferro held out his hand. "But if they do open it, you'll be standing there with a signed manifest and a face that says you're as bored as they are. That part I can't build for you. That part is yours."

"And what do I say the passenger is?"

"You don't say anything. If they open it, we were wrong, and it's finished, and none of us will have time to be sorry."

Havel looked at the offered hand a long moment. Then he took it, his grip crushing and warm, and said, "When the water opens. Not before. Ice this year is rotten in the middle — I can hear it lying to me every night."

"It'll be ready before the ice is honest."

"One more thing, carpenter." Havel picked his net back up. "You speak of the woman, the matron, the boy. You do not speak of yourself. On my barge there is room for a carpenter who wants to be elsewhere. Every boat going down this river this spring will carry men escaping something."

Ferro shook his head before Havel had finished. "If I'm gone, they'll know the others helped me. Brannigan can barely walk. Tilda's been in their cells once already. Mags is seventy if she's a day. Someone stays, someone visible, someone whose absence would shout. My staying is quiet. Their staying would be a bell."

"That is a stupid arithmetic."

"It's joinery," Ferro said. "You fit the piece where it holds, not where it wants to go."

Havel spat into the snow, which among Bohemians was a signature on a contract.

* * *

He built the lid's secret last, because a false bottom guards the cargo but a false seam guards the guard. Along the front rail, under the lip where a man's fingers would naturally grip to lift the chest, he routed a channel and set into it a strip of wood that matched the grain so perfectly it disappeared. Under the strip: a folded square of oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, written in his own blunt carpenter's hand because he trusted no one else's pen, the sequence of pins, the weight limits, the instruction that the boy must be given water every four hours and must never, whatever he heard outside the chest, make a sound.

He wrote it like a bill of materials. He had learned long ago that grief written plainly was unbearable, but grief itemized could be carried. *One panel. Three pins. One child. Four hours.*

Mags found him at dusk, when the stove had burned down to red coals and the chest stood finished on the bench, smelling of oak and linseed.

She put her hand flat on the lid, the way she laid a hand on a patient's forehead, reading it.

"Heavier at one end," she said.

"Ledger goes at the rear. Ballast for the waterline. Havel likes a boat trimmed stern-heavy in current."

"Mm." She opened the lid, lifted the instrument tray, looked at the seamless drawer bottoms, and did not pretend to look for the false panel, because pretending would have insulted them both. She closed it again gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person.

"Ferro."

"Mags."

"You've measured the interior against the boy?"

"I measured him with my eyes while he ate soup. Twice. Add half a finger each way for bread and fear."

"And yourself."

"What about myself."

"You know what I'm asking."

He wiped his chisels, one by one, and laid them in their roll. "Brannigan dies before the march is a week old. We both know it; he knows it best of all. Somebody has to be the one still standing here when the soldiers come asking questions, or the questions turn into searching, and searching turns into Tilda in a cell again, or worse. A workshop full of innocent women needs a guilty man to point at. I'm the one with sawdust on his hands and a history. I stay. It fits."

"Things that fit can still break."

"Everything breaks," Ferro said. "The craft is choosing what breaks last."

Mags stood there in the coal-light, old and straight-backed, and for a moment her composure slipped the way ice slips in thaw, all at once, showing the black water underneath. She put her hand on his forearm — the first time in three winters she had touched him without a reason of bandages or splints.

"She won't allow it," she said. "Jude. She'll order you onto that barge herself."

"Jude doesn't give orders. She asks. And I'll tell her the same thing I told Havel." He smiled, and it cost him something, so he spent it quickly before it got expensive. "It's joinery, Mags. Don't let her argue with the joinery."

* * *

That night he carried the chest down to the workshop cellar and set it behind the timber rack, under the tarpaulin that covered the winter stock, and he came back up to find the workshop quiet, the candles out, only the stove ticking as it cooled.

Oscar was awake. Of course he was. The boy slept like a cat — lightly, anywhere, ready. He was sitting up in his blanket by the warming shelf, watching Ferro with those dark serious eyes, and he pointed at the sawdust on Ferro's sleeves, and then he pointed at the stairs leading down to the cellar, and then he looked at Ferro with a question so plain it needed no words.

Ferro crouched down to the boy's level. Up close, Oscar smelled of soap and wood smoke, and there was a smear of charcoal on his cheek from the day's drawing, the river again, Ferro would wager, the river and the boat.

"In the cellar," Ferro said quietly, "there's a box. In the box there's a place just your size. I made it myself, and I make things well — ask anyone. When the time comes, you'll climb in, and it will be dark, and you'll hear water, and you might be afraid. Being afraid is fine. I've been afraid every day since I was six years older than you, and it hasn't killed me yet."

Oscar listened the way children listen, entirely, with his whole body. Then he held up his slate. On it, in his careful childish letters that Tilda had been teaching him, he had written three words:

*I WONT CRY*

Ferro looked at it a long time. Something in his chest shifted the way timber shifts when the season turns — a settling, a giving-way, the sound a strong structure makes when it accepts a load it will carry for a long time.

"No," he said. "Cry if you need to. Just cry quiet, and cry moving downriver."

Oscar nodded, solemn as a foreman accepting a contract, and erased the slate with his sleeve, and lay down, and closed his eyes.

Ferro banked the stove and stood a moment in the dark workshop among the smells of his whole life — glue and shavings and linseed — listening to the river groan beneath the ice, closer tonight, more impatient, the sound of a door beginning to open somewhere far under the world.

Three days later, at dawn, a knock came at the workshop door that nobody liked: quick, official, more fist than knuckle. Tilda answered it, and Ferro, halfway down the cellar stairs with a load of kindling, heard her go still.

At the gate, the informer was already talking to the sergeant of the guard, and pointing.
