## 18. Betrayal at the Gate

The morning of the escape, the river spoke first.

Jude stood at the workshop window with Oscar's coat folded over her arm and listened to the ice groan along the far bank, a long low sound like a door opening in a house where no one was awake. The thaw had come three days early. Water showed in silver threads along the channel, and the barge pilot Ferro trusted—a man named Ossler with a face like wet rope—had sent word he would hold at the willow bend until the chest came down.

Everything was ready. That was the thing Jude could not stop turning over afterward, in the days when turning things over was all she had. Everything had been ready.

The ledger lay inside the false bottom of the medicine chest, wrapped in oilcloth beside the duplicate pages she had spent eleven nights copying, hiding by day in bandage rolls and bread loaves until there were enough to move. Oscar wore his new coat, the one Tilda had cut down from a dead man's greatcoat because nothing smaller existed in the whole regiment. He had eaten his breakfast without being asked. He had even spoken that morning, two words, *good morning*, said to Mags as though he had been saying it all his life, and Mags had gripped the edge of the table so hard her knuckles went white and then pretended she had burned her hand on the kettle.

Ferro came for the chest just after dawn. He carried it out to the cart himself, refusing help with a look, and he had sawdust on his sleeves and a splinter in his thumb he kept worrying with his other hand, which was how Jude knew he was frightened. Ferro did not worry splinters. He pulled them or he ignored them.

"You'll follow at noon," he said. "Market crowd. Nobody looks twice at a nurse going to buy onions."

"And if somebody does?"

"Then you'll be a nurse buying onions." He set his hat on. "Ossler waits until the water opens. Could be tomorrow, could be three days. You don't go near the willow bend until I send Tilda with the signal."

"What's the signal?"

"Tilda," Ferro said, and almost smiled. "Tilda is the signal."

They put Oscar in the cart among the medicine crates, under sacking, with instructions to stay silent no matter what he heard, and he looked up at Jude from under the brim of the too-big hat and did not say anything at all, because he understood silence better than any child she had ever known. She touched his cheek once. She wanted to say something enormous and true, but what came out was, "Mind your elbows against the crate," and he nodded gravely, as though elbows were the whole of it.

She watched the cart go through the gate. She watched it turn onto the river road. And then she went back inside and began, absurdly, to sort bandages, because her hands needed occupation or they would shake, and the shaking would show, and showing was not allowed. Not today.

At noon she tied her shawl and took her basket.

* * *

The market square stank of thaw—wet straw, mud, horse dung, the sweet rot of last autumn's cabbage unearthed from the snowmelt. The crowd was good and thick, exactly as Ferro had promised: women arguing over flour, troopers buying sausages off a cart, a fiddler playing badly near the well. Jude bought onions from the first stall. She bought nothing else. She turned for home along the lane behind the chandlery, walking at the pace of a woman with nowhere particular to be.

She heard the hooves before she understood them.

Two riders came up the lane at a trot, and one of them was Sergeant Hale, who ran the gate detail, and the other was a man she did not know—a civilian in a gray coat, soft-bodied, comfortable, the kind of man whose hands had never done anything worse than count money. He reined in beside her and looked down with an expression of mild apology.

"Nurse Kavanagh."

"I'm expected back at the workshop."

"I'm sure you are." He swung down, unhurried, and stood close enough that she could smell pomade on him. "You'll forgive the intrusion. There's been a report of stolen regimental property moving through the town this morning. A medicine chest, unlisted. We're stopping carts at every gate."

Her heart did not leap; it dropped, slow and cold, like a stone lowered into a well. She kept her face still. "I mend bandages. I know nothing about chests."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I don't imagine you do."

He tipped his hat and rode past her toward the river road, and Jude stood in the lane holding a basket of onions and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what she was looking at. Not a soldier. A paid man. A man bought to walk the streets and point—and someone had pointed.

She began to run. Onions scattered behind her across the cobbles and she did not stop for them.

* * *

She was too late by four minutes. She learned the number later from Tilda, who had counted everything, because counting was how Tilda survived things.

Four minutes. In that time the cart had reached the north gate, where Hale and two troopers waited with fixed bayonets and a paper signed by the colonel's own hand authorizing search of all departing goods. Ferro had argued—Tilda said his voice carried all the way to the chandler's yard, calm and reasonable and deadly, citing regulations about medical stores, offering the manifest, offering to open every crate himself, one by one, slowly, while the afternoon died. He was magnificent, Tilda said. He was magnificent for six minutes.

Then a trooper drove his bayonet point into the sacking over the medicine chest—not searching, just probing, lazy, bored—and felt the give of the false bottom, shallow as it was, hollow as it was, wrong.

They lifted the lid in front of everyone. They took out the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and they took out the duplicate pages, and they took out Oscar.

He did not cry. That was the part Tilda could not get past, telling it, her voice cracking like the ice on the river. They hauled him up by the arms out of the cart and set him on the tailboard in front of the whole assembled crowd, a small boy in a dead man's coat and a too-big hat, and he looked around at the soldiers and the gawking market people and he did not make a sound. He looked for Jude. Tilda was certain of it. He turned his head slowly, side to side, searching faces the way he used to search them when he first came, before he had anyone to find.

And then they put him in a wagon, and the wagon went toward the colonel's quarters, and the ledger went into a dispatch case held shut by a sergeant's fist, and Ferro was left standing in the road with his empty cart and his splintered thumb and a look on his face that Tilda said she hoped never to see again on a living man.

By the time Jude arrived, running, breathless, the gate was closed and the street was already returning to itself, the way streets do—flour argued over, sausages sold, the fiddler scraping on. Nobody had stopped anything. Nobody ever stopped anything. That was the whole lesson of the world, delivered at a gate on a thawing morning: history happened elsewhere, and it rolled right over the small names.

Mags met her at the workshop door and caught her by both shoulders before she could speak. "Don't. Don't say it here. Walls."

"They have him." Her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "They have both of them. The ledger and—" She couldn't finish it.

"Inside. Now."

* * *

They confined her within the hour. It was almost gentle, as these things went—a corporal and two men at the workshop door, a verbal order read aloud from a paper: Nurse Kavanagh to remain within the hospital premises pending inquiry into irregularities of stores, all movement beyond the gate forbidden, all visitors barred except authorized medical personnel. Signed, countersigned, sealed. Ashworth had not even needed to see her. He had arranged her captivity in advance, the way he arranged everything, neatly, in another man's handwriting.

The soldiers searched her quarters while she stood in the middle of the room and let them. They took her spare ink, her pens, the stub of sealing wax. They did not take the ledger because the ledger was gone, and they did not take her because taking her would have required paperwork, and paperwork, for now, still cut both ways. A missing nurse was a question. A confined nurse was an answer.

When they were done, the room was exactly as it had been, minus the means of writing anything down. She sat on the cot and listened to the ice groan again, closer now, impatient, the river wanting open, and she thought: I have failed him. Not the ledger—the ledger was only paper, and paper was replaceable, and besides, the duplicate pages had been taken too, every copy, everything she had built for eleven nights, seized in one lazy bayonet prod. No. What she had failed was the boy. The boy who drew faces. The boy who said *good morning* like it was a miracle. The boy who had heard, at six years old, an order that killed his father, and who had carried it in his silence all winter because no adult in his life had proved safe enough to set it down—until her, until them, until a found family that had lasted one season and ended at a gate in front of a bored trooper with a bayonet.

She had told him to mind his elbows against the crate.

She laughed then, one dry crack of it, and pressed her sleeve against her mouth, and sat very still until the laugh either died or became something else. Outside the window, dusk was coming down blue and early over the frozen yard. Somewhere across the compound, in the colonel's quarters, Oscar was sitting in a fine warm room being questioned by a man with velvet manners, and Jude knew exactly how those questions would go—gentle at first, kind even, sweets perhaps, questions about drawings, about faces, about what the boy remembered—and she knew that Ashworth did not need the boy to talk. The boy talking was the catastrophe. The boy simply existing, in Ashworth's keeping, was already the solution: a witness could be made into a ward, a liability into a charity case, a forgotten son adopted by a forgiving officer. History rewritten not by burning but by kindness, which burned cleaner.

Or the boy would stop being convenient. Jude had seen what happened to inconvenient things in this army. They were sent forward, or they were lost crossing rivers, or they fell ill in the night, and there was always paperwork to explain it.

The door opened and Mags came in with a tray—bread, broth, the theater of care—and set it down and stood looking out the window with her back to Jude, waiting for the footsteps in the corridor to fade.

"Ferro's been summoned," Mags said quietly. "Tomorrow, ten o'clock, to account for the chest. Officially he's facing charges. Unofficially, Ashworth wants to know who helped build it, who carried it, who was meant to receive it downstream." She paused. "Brannigan's asking for you. Can't come to you—they won't let him cross the yard—but he sent a message through the laundry line. One sentence."

"What sentence?"

Mags turned. Her old face was gray in the fading light, and steady as a wall. "It says: *They didn't find everything.*"

Jude stared at her. "Everything was found. The chest, the pages, the—"

"The pages you hid in the bread loaves," Mags said. "Last week. The ones you'd already moved before the duplicates went in the chest. Brannigan's saying they never went near the chest. He's saying some are still where you put them." Her voice dropped further. "And he's saying Tilda has not yet given her signal. And that Ossler is still holding at the willow bend. And that the water will open soon."

Jude sat with the broth going cold in front of her and felt the despair shift its weight—not lifting, not yet, nothing so kind as lifting, but shifting, the way a wounded man shifts when he decides he is not finished. They had the ledger. They had the copies. But they did not have her memory, which held every name; they did not have the hidden pages; they did not have Ossler, waiting patient at the bend of the river with a barge and no idea yet that he waited for nothing.

And they did not have all of Oscar. Whatever room they had put him in, whatever story Ashworth told him, somewhere inside that mute, careful child was the testimony that had started all of this—an order heard through a door, a father sent to die—and Ashworth knew it, and that knowledge was the colonel's weakness, the one seam in all his neat paperwork. A man erasing evidence has to keep checking whether the erasure took. Every day Oscar lived was a day Ashworth had to decide, again, not to solve the problem permanently. Every day was a chance.

"They'll move him when the regiment moves," Jude said slowly. "Ashworth won't leave a loose thread in town. He'll bring the boy with the column—or he'll—" She stopped.

"Or he will," Mags agreed grimly.

Down in the yard, a guard coughed. The ice groaned, long and loud, and somewhere in the dark below the willow bend the river was working at its chains.

Jude looked at her empty desk, stripped of ink and pens, at the window too narrow for her hips, at the door with its boots outside it, and she thought of Brannigan's one sentence traveling the laundry line like contraband. *They didn't find everything.*

No, she thought. They hadn't. And tomorrow Ferro would stand in front of the colonel and give his account, and whatever Ferro did in that room—whatever magnificent, joinery-precise thing the carpenter had planned to say—would begin the next move. Jude pressed her palms flat against the cold sill and watched the first stars come out over the water, and made herself a promise with no ceremony at all: *Hold on, Oscar. Four minutes late. I will not be late again.*

The ice cracked once more, sharply, like a shot downriver.
