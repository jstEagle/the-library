## 19. The Night Before the Burning

### Mags

Mags had not slept a whole night since the winter of 1809, and she did not intend to start now. Sleep was for people whose hands didn't matter.

She sat in her corner of the workshop with a candle turned low and a mortar in her lap, grinding poppy heads that Ferro had bought off the bargemen at three times their worth. The smell was bitter and old-fashioned, like medicine from her mother's time. Around her the workshop breathed — the tick of the stove iron cooling, the creak of ice under the floor, the small wet sound of Oscar sleeping against Tilda's side on the pallet by the wall.

The boy had been taken back once already. He would not be taken twice. Mags had decided this the way she decided everything: without consulting anyone, and then telling everyone until they agreed.

Jude sat across from her, mending the same shirt she'd been mending for an hour, which meant she wasn't mending it at all. She was watching the door. She'd been watching the door since the soldiers took the chest at the gate, since they carried Oscar up to the colonel's quarters like a parcel of contraband, since Jude herself had been confined to the workshop with two sentries outside and a promise of "the colonel's pleasure" come morning.

"They'll burn us out tomorrow," Mags said, not looking up from her mortar. "Orders came down at dusk. I heard the corporal tell the cook."

"I know."

"Records first. Then the buildings. They'll want it done before the water opens, before there's anything left to float away." Mags ground harder. "And they'll want you standing on the bank to watch, Jude Kavanagh, so everyone knows what happens to women who write things down."

"Mags—"

"Don't shush me. I'm seventy-one years old and I've been dying since Bautzen. Let me say my piece while I still have the wind for it." She set the mortar down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was moving like water finding a crack. "There's laudanum enough in the surgery stores to put six men down like lambs. There's brandy in the officers' ration, and sentries are men, and men on cold gates drink what's offered them if a pretty story comes with it."

Jude's needle stopped. "You're proposing to drug His Majesty's sentries."

"I'm proposing to put them to sleep, which is kinder than what the colonel means to do to us." Mags looked at her straight. "I'm too old to run and too mean to weep. This is my part. Give me something better to do with my last night than grind poppies for other people's pain."

For a long moment Jude said nothing. Then she put down the shirt, crossed the room, and took Mags' hands in both of hers — the first time in four years of shared work that she had done such a thing — and said, "Tell me how much and where."

### Tilda

Tilda could lie. That was the thing nobody in the regiment had ever bothered to learn about washerwomen.

They thought because she bent over their filth all day and took their coins and called them *sir*, she was simple. They told her things in front of her — who owed whom money, who drank, which lieutenant had been sent forward with half a company and no support — because a woman with a washing paddle was furniture. Furniture didn't remember. Furniture didn't repeat.

But Tilda remembered everything. She remembered every name Jude had ever whispered over the ledger, every man whose shirt she'd boiled clean after he stopped needing shirts, and she remembered the day last autumn when Lieutenant Ashworth — no relation, God spare them both — had come to her in tears and asked her to carry a letter to the colonel's aide, and what the colonel had written back, because she'd read it upside down while folding. She'd been reading upside down since she was nine. It was the only education a scullery girl got, and she'd made it serve.

Now she stood at the wash-house door at midnight, looking across the frozen yard at the colonel's quarters, where a lamp burned in an upstairs window behind which a six-year-old boy was being kept like a document.

"You're certain," Ferro murmured beside her. The carpenter moved quietly for a big man. "You're certain they'll believe you."

"They believed me when I told the quartermaster the linens were short. They'll believe me now." She wrapped her shawl tighter. "I go to the gate at first light. I tell the sergeant of the guard that the nurse is dying — fever, quick kind, caught from the wounded. I tell him I've seen it before, in '09, and it kills in hours, and if the colonel wants his records burned by living hands instead of a dead workshop, he'd best let me tend her before she goes." She shrugged. "It's even true, near enough. We are all of us dying. I'm just choosing the hour."

"And if they search your basket?"

"Then they'll find feverfew and vinegar rags, which is exactly what a woman brings to a fever case." Tilda smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Ferro. Twenty-six years I've washed for these men. I have lied to majors. I can lie to a gate guard."

He nodded slowly. But he didn't leave, and after a moment he said, in a voice lower than the wind, "If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, Tilda. If they take you again." He couldn't finish it, and she understood why. Three days in the guardhouse had changed something in her that she hadn't explained and wouldn't, and everyone in the workshop knew it, and nobody spoke of it, and that silence was its own kind of bandage.

"What they did," she said at last, "they did to frighten me into being nothing. And here I am, standing in the dark, planning to lie to the entire British Army before breakfast." She patted his great forearm once, brisk as folding linen. "Frightened? Aye. Nothing? Never again. Now go cut your hinges, carpenter. Dawn comes whether we're ready or not."

### Ferro

Ferro worked best alone, and thank Christ for it, because what he was doing tonight would hang him if anyone saw.

The river gate was iron-banded oak, older than the regiment, hung on pintles that had rusted since the thaw before last. In an hour he could lift it off its hinges with two men and a lever. In ten minutes, alone, with a cold chisel and a mallet and forty years of knowing how wood gives way, he could drop the pins silently and swing the thing wide enough for a barge line — if the pins were cut right.

He knelt in the snow behind the tannery shed, chisel against the lower pintle, and thought about joinery.

His father had taught him that every joint fails along the line you don't see. The mortise looks strong; the failure lives in the grain. So you study the grain. You find where the wood wants to break, and you decide whether to argue with it or agree. Most of life, Ferro had found, was deciding where to agree.

He agreed with the grain. Two taps. The pin dropped into his gloved hand, soft as a tooth coming loose.

Above him, the upper pin waited. Below, the river ice groaned and shifted in the dark, talking in its sleep. Tomorrow it would wake properly, and the water would open, and the pilot Duroc would bring his barge up under cover of the breaking floes the way he'd promised — one barge among many fleeing downstream ahead of the burning, unremarkable, carrying a medicine chest with a false bottom and, God willing, a boy.

Ferro knew he would not be on it.

He had known since the night of the failed escape. Somebody had sold them — the informer at the gate, the soldiers waiting with the list already written — and somebody would sell them again unless someone stayed behind to be the obvious culprit. A foreign-born carpenter with no family and no papers worth the ink was the obvious culprit. He fit the shape of betrayal the way a tenon fits a mortise. Better him than Jude. Better him than the women. Better him, frankly, than anyone, because Ferro had buried a wife and two children on the road out of Galicia and had spent eleven years since building things for other people's futures. One more joint. Cut it true.

He set the chisel against the upper pin and paused, listening.

Footsteps. Light ones, deliberate, coming around the tannery wall. Ferro rose with the chisel palmed like a knife and nearly used it — and then the moon came off the snow onto a face he knew, and he let his breath out slow.

Brannigan. Wrapped in a blanket, gray as ash, walking on legs that had no business carrying him. The sergeant stopped an arm's length away and looked at the exposed hinge, the dropped pin, and then up at Ferro with eyes that held neither surprise nor judgment.

"Well now," Brannigan said. "That's fine work."

"You should be lying down."

"I've been lying down for a year. It's accomplished nothing." The sergeant eased himself down onto a crate with the care of a man handling glass, and Ferro saw how bad it truly was — the wound gone septic beneath the bandage, the skin around it shining. Hours, maybe. Not days. "I came to tell you the guards change at four. The new pair is drunk already; Mags' brandy is working faster than she hoped. And I came—" he stopped, and his voice went rough, "—I came to ask if there's a place on that barge for a dying man who'd rather see the water open than die in this yard."

Ferro stared at him. "You'd slow them. You'd be a burden—"

"Aye, likely. And I'd be a witness, which is worth more than speed." Brannigan laid his hand flat on his own chest, over the ledger pages Jude had sewn into his coat lining weeks ago — his testimony, his brother's name, the truth of the charge. "Everything I am is written down and riding in this coat. All that's left of me is the delivery. Put me on the boat, carpenter. Let me be delivered."

Ferro looked at the ruined hinge, the dark river, the old soldier sitting straight-backed on a crate like a man waiting for a coach.

"The lower step of the ladder is broken," he said finally. "Mind your feet."

### Brannigan

Brannigan had made peace with death a long time ago, in a surgeon's tent in '10, when the fever took him and gave him back. What he had never made peace with was uselessness, and tonight, for the first time in a year, the feeling lifted.

He sat in the workshop's back room while the others came and went — Mags wrapping laudanum doses in wax paper like sweets, Tilda packing her basket with theatrical thoroughness, Ferro oiling the gate pins — and Brannigan watched them and thought: *this is what an army is supposed to be.*

Not the colonel's parade ground. Not the dispatches with their lacunae, their elegant omissions, their dead lieutenants struck from the record between one paragraph and the next. This. A washerwoman rehearsing a lie under her breath. An old woman dosing sentries. A carpenter sacrificing himself like a man planing a board, without ceremony. And Jude at the center of it, pale and awake, holding the whole fragile machine together by refusing to call it anything grander than necessary work.

She came to him near three o'clock, when the others had scattered to their stations. She knelt by his chair and checked the coat lining with her fingers — the pages still there, dry, hidden against his heart — and then just stayed kneeling, close, her hand resting on his sleeve.

"You shouldn't be on that boat," she said quietly. "You should be abed."

"I've been abed through every important event of the past year, Mrs. Kavanagh. I decline to miss the finale." He managed a smile. "Besides. My brother died on a field no officer would name. The least I can do is die on a river with a name — yours, the boy's, mine — attached to something that floats."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her fight it, the tears, and win, mostly. "Dawn's in two hours," she said. "When Tilda raises the fever alarm, the guard shifts to the workshop door. Ferro takes the river gate. You and I carry Oscar down the ice path to the barge."

"And the colonel?"

"And the colonel," she said, "will be reading my handwriting." Something moved behind her eyes that Brannigan had seen once before, in a young lieutenant the morning of the charge — terror and resolve occupying the same body, agreeing to coexist. "I left a copy of the ledger where his clerk will find it within the hour. Every name. Every date. His signature forged beside the order that killed your brother's company."

Brannigan went very still. "Jude. That's not misdirection. That's a declaration of war."

"Yes," she said. "It is." She squeezed his sleeve once and stood. "We stopped hiding last month, Sergeant. Tonight we simply stop pretending otherwise."

She walked back toward the front room, where the candle burned low and the boy slept, and Brannigan sat alone in the dark with his heart hammering against the pages sewn inside his coat.

Outside, the river groaned — long, deep, unmistakable, the sound of a thing that had borne weight all winter deciding it would bear it no longer.

He found he was smiling. Four o'clock came. Somewhere across the yard, a sentry yawned, drank, and began, very gently, to fall asleep.
