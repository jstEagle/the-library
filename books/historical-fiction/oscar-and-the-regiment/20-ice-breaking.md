## 20. Ice Breaking

The fires were lit at first light.

Jude heard them before she saw them—the crackle of oil-soaked paper catching, the shouts of men ordered to feed the flames. From the workshop window, past the frost-ferned glass, she watched smoke rise from the quartermaster's yard in fat black columns. The record wagons had been drawn up in a half-circle, and soldiers stood around them like mourners who had been told to smile. Every ledger, every muster roll, every hospital register the regiment had carried through four winters of war—going into the fire because the river was about to open and the regiment was about to move, and dead paper was heavy paper.

They were burning her world's memory, and calling it tidiness.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. All night she had expected them to shake—Mags dosing the gate guards with poppy syrup in their supper stew, Tilda standing in the colonel's anteroom with her chin up, lying fluently about a chest of medicines that did not exist, Ferro down at the river gate with his crowbar and his carpenter's silence—and all night Jude had lain awake cataloguing everything that could go wrong. But now, with the smoke rising and the morning hard and blue as a blade, her hands were still. There is a kind of calm that comes when there is nothing left to lose that you have not already decided to spend.

The ledger was gone. The chest had gone out the gate at midnight, or it had been seized, or Oscar was in Ashworth's quarters right now learning what powerful men do to witnesses. She did not know which. The not-knowing should have broken her. Instead it had done something worse and better: it had emptied her of caution entirely.

She dressed in her good apron—the one without blood on it—and went downstairs into the cold.

* * *

The officers were assembled on the parade ground by eight o'clock. Ashworth had called them formally, she learned from Ferro's boy runner, for the ceremonial burning—a small theater to mark the closing of accounts before departure. The colonel liked theater. She had learned that much in their two encounters, the inspection and the bargain. He liked order, symmetry, the appearance of things resolving cleanly. A man who burned his records wanted an audience for the burning, wanted witnesses to the erasure, because erasure only worked if everyone watched it happen and understood that watching was consent.

So she would give him witnesses.

The parade ground lay between the workshop and the river, maybe three hundred paces of trodden snow. She crossed it alone, no cloak, ledger-less, carrying nothing but what was in her head. Behind her she heard Mags say something low and urgent, and Tilda answer, and then footsteps—her people falling in behind her, not because she asked but because that was simply what they did now, the way ice does not ask the river whether it may crack.

Ashworth stood on the low steps of the record wagon, reading aloud in a clear parade voice from a sheet of commendations. His aide held the torch. Beyond him the fire roared, and beyond the fire, gray and groaning, the river.

She stopped ten paces from the steps.

"Colonel Ashworth."

He looked up. For one heartbeat his face did what faces do before they decide—it flickered through surprise, calculation, annoyance—and then settled into courtesy so smooth it might have been poured. "Nurse Ishikawa. You should be packing your instruments. We march when the water does."

"I have no instruments to pack," Jude said. Her voice came out level, and louder than she intended, pitched to carry past him to the ring of officers. "Everything I needed to keep, I kept."

Something shifted along the line of officers. Small, involuntary—the adjustment of men sensing weather change.

"You are disturbing a ceremony."

"No, sir." She took another step forward. "I'm completing it. You're burning the names. I've come to read them."

The torch hissed in the wind off the river. Ashworth descended one step, unhurried, a man managing a difficult subordinate in front of guests. "This is grief talking," he said gently. "You've lost men this winter; we all have. Go inside. Major Hale will see you're given something warm."

"I lost nothing, Colonel. That's rather the point." And she turned—not to him but to the assembled officers, thirty faces, forty, staff and surgeons and majors with ribbons, men whose names she knew from her own nightly writing—and she began.

"Halloran, Private Denis, Third Company. Left hand taken by frostbite in December; died of fever in January. Not in any dispatch. His mother wrote twice asking if he'd been wounded at the charge, and the answers she got were written by clerks who never saw him."

A murmur moved down the line like wind through wheat.

"Weir, Corporal Samuel. Died of the belly flux in February. Buried under the willows with a plank for a marker because there was no time to cut stone. Your returns list him as transferred."

"Nkemdirim—" she stumbled once on the pronunciation and refused to be ashamed of it—"Private Joseph, a volunteer from the river towns, drowned when the ice gave under him fetching water for the hospital. No one reported it for six days because no officer thought to ask where the water-carrier had gone."

She kept going. This was the thing she had not known until this moment, standing in the cold with her breath steaming and every eye on her: she had never memorized the ledger. She had not needed to. She had written each name at night, by candle, in her careful hand, and the writing had written itself into her the way a path is worn into grass. Four hundred and eleven nights of ritual. The names rose in her throat in order, in the order of loss, in the order of neglect, and she gave them up one after another while Ashworth watched her with his face going slowly from porcelain to plaster.

"Brennan, Michael." Her voice caught here, and she let it catch; let them hear it. "Sergeant Brannigan's brother. Struck from the rolls after the December charge because the report of that charge named no cause and assigned no blame, because the order that sent two hundred men across open ice was given by an officer who could not be blamed, and so the men who died were made into arithmetic. Thirty-one names, Colonel. Thirty-one men who walked onto that ice and did not walk back, and your official account lists nine. Nine."

"That is enough." Ashworth's voice had changed. The velvet was gone; underneath was something flat and quick, a blade being drawn. "You are a nurse. You are speaking of matters above your station, based on papers you had no authority to hold."

"I had no authority," Jude agreed. "That's true. Authority is exactly what I haven't got. What I have is a ledger with four hundred and eleven entries, and a copy of it already downstream where you can't burn it, and something else besides." She paused, and made herself say it plainly, into the silence, so that no man present could later claim he hadn't heard. "I have a witness."

The word dropped among the officers like a stone into still water.

"A child," she said. "Six years old. Brought to my door in a blizzard in January by your aide, Colonel, with money and instructions to forget his face. He is Lieutenant Vasseur's son. He was in his father's quarters the night before the December charge. He heard the argument. He heard his father refuse to send men onto rotten ice. He heard the reply—that refusal would be treated as cowardice in wartime, and dealt with accordingly. And then his father led the charge he was ordered to lead, and the ice took him, and your inquiry closed in eleven days with testimony signed by men who weren't there."

Ashworth moved. It was fast for a big man—down the last two steps, into her space, his voice dropping to something meant for her alone while the officers strained to hear. "Where is the boy?"

"Gone," Jude said. "Or in your keeping. Either way, Colonel, it doesn't matter anymore, and that is the whole lesson of this winter. You taught it to me yourself, at my door, with your velvet voice and your talk of front-line reassignments. You taught me that a woman nobody sees can see everybody. So look around you." She raised her voice again, deliberately, giving the words away to the crowd. "Every officer here has lost soldiers this winter whose names appear nowhere. Ask yourselves why. Ask who benefits when the record burns clean. This man is not protecting you. He is protecting himself, and he is spending your dead to do it, the way he spent Lieutenant Vasseur, the way he tried to spend a child."

"Lieutenant Vasseur died honorably leading his men—"

"He died obeying a criminal order!" It tore out of her louder than she had ever spoken in her life, and somewhere behind her Tilda said, soft and fierce, "Tell it," and Mags said nothing at all but stood like a wall. "And the proof of it is a boy who draws faces because no one will let him speak them. Well—he has spoken now. And his words are written down, and witnessed, and beyond your reach."

"The chest was seized at the gate," Ashworth said, and there was triumph in it, ugly and sudden. "Your carpenter's clever box. My informer earned his pay. The boy is in my quarters as we stand here. Whatever testimony you imagine exists, madam, is sitting upstairs waiting for me, and the ledger you claim is downstream is in my hands."

The cold went through her like the river's own breath. *Seized.* She had not known—had spent the night not knowing, and now knowing landed worse than ignorance, because Oscar—

But she had been a nurse too long to show a wound while it was being probed. She looked at Ashworth, and she smiled, and she heard herself say, calmly, "Then you've seized a medicine chest full of bandages, Colonel. Ferro builds false bottoms. Where do you imagine I've been hiding laudanum from thieves for three years?"

It was a bluff. God forgive her, it was a bluff, and Ashworth's eyes narrowed because he could not tell, because she had learned from him how to make certainty out of posture. Behind the officers, at the edge of the parade ground, Ferro appeared—soot-faced, breathing hard, having run from the river gate—and his eyes found hers over the crowd, and he nodded once.

*Out,* the nod said. *On the water.*

* * *

And then the river answered.

It began as a sound none of them mistook for anything else—a deep, grinding boom beneath the ice, like a door slamming in the earth's cellar, followed by a long tearing shriek that ran the length of the valley. Every head turned. Downstream, near the bend, the white sheet of the river buckled visibly, a dark seam opening and widening, water leaping up through it black and glittering, floes rearing against each other with a sound like artillery. The thaw that had been whispering under the surface for a week chose that moment, that exact moment, to declare itself.

"The ice is going," someone said unnecessarily. "The ice is breaking up."

The ceremony dissolved. Officers turned toward the river, toward the moored barges, toward orders suddenly urgent. Ashworth stood a moment longer, facing Jude, and the noise of the cracking river filled the space between them like a held breath.

"You understand," he said quietly, "what happens to you now. There will be an accounting. On the march, perhaps. Accidents are common in spring."

"Yes," Jude said. "I know how your accidents work. I've been writing them down for a year."

She turned her back on him—actually turned her back, and heard the collective intake of breath from thirty officers at the insolence of it—and started toward the workshop. Halfway there she stopped, because the gates of the compound opened, and a barge came up the channel through the drifting floes with Ferro's boy at the tiller, and sitting in the bow, wrapped in Tilda's good shawl, alive, upright, watching the smoke and the crowd with those grave dark eyes—

Oscar raised his hand and waved.

Jude's knees nearly went. She gripped Tilda's arm and Tilda gripped hers and neither of them said anything, because there was no time and no need; the boy was safe on moving water, the duplicate ledger was in the false bottom Ferro had built into the boy's seat, and Ashworth was turning from the river to find his prize floating away from him in plain view of everyone he commanded.

"No," Ashworth said. Just that. Quietly. To himself. And then, louder, cracking: "Stop that boat! Halt that—"

"Sir." Major Hale, beside him, pointing urgently downstream. "Sir, the channel's closing—we have to get the baggage across today or we lose the crossing entirely—"

For one suspended instant the colonel stood between the drowning of his career and the escape of a child, and Jude watched him weigh them, and understood with perfect clarity which one he feared more. Then he strode toward the landing, shouting for oarsmen, for marines, for anything that floated—and the officers scattered after him like iron filings after a magnet—and the parade ground emptied of everyone but nurses and washerwomen and a dying sergeant propped against the workshop doorpost, laughing weakly at the sky.

Jude stood in the trampled snow amid the smoke and the thunder of the breaking river, and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked since a blizzard night in January.

Not finished. Nowhere near finished—he had oarsmen and she had a boy in an open boat, and the day would be decided on the water before noon. But the names had been said aloud. In front of them all. Unburnable now, carried away in forty memories that would scatter across the army with the spring.

Tilda's hand found hers. "Well," the old washerwoman said, eyes on the river, "now we run."

They ran.
