## 21. Downstream

The river broke at three in the morning, and the sound was like a cathedral falling.

Jude heard it from her cot in the workshop, through walls that had held back blizzards and soldiers and Colonel Ashworth's courtesy, and for one disoriented moment she thought the war had finally come to them. Then she understood: the ice. The ice that had held all winter like a lid on a pot, the ice they had walked across and cursed across and buried men beneath—groaning now, splitting now, shedding itself into a hundred thousand grinding plates.

The thaw had come early. Or late. She no longer knew which. It didn't matter.

What mattered was that the regiment's orders said *depart when the water opens*, and the water was opening, and tonight was the burning.

She dressed in the dark without lighting the lamp. Her hands found the duplicate ledger pages sewn into the lining of Mags' old shawl—the last of them, the ones she'd been keeping closest because Ferro's chest had gone down to the gatehouse at dusk with the barge pilot's cousin, and everything else was already hidden in bread loaves and bandage rolls and one hollowed hymnal that Tilda carried against her heart. What remained in Jude's keeping was the original. The true book. Every name in her own hand, four winters of small names, Brannigan's testimony still smelling faintly of lamp oil where she'd written it by his bedside.

She wrapped it in oilcloth and bound it under her coat, flat against her ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the workshop yard was chaos dressed as order. Wagons loading. Horses screaming at the river noise. Officers calling out in voices pitched carefully calm, the way men speak when they are pretending not to count what is being lost. Smoke already rose from the courtyard behind headquarters—not the workshop yet, but the first pyres of paper. Records first. That was always the rule of retreats and departures alike: burn the paper before the enemy or history can read it.

*They think the fire will finish what the winter started,* Jude thought. *They think forgetting can be scheduled.*

She went looking for Mags and found her at the surgery door, holding a mortar, wearing an expression Jude had learned to read over one long winter: fear, ground fine and mixed with resolve, taken in measured doses.

"The guards drank their tea," Mags said quietly. "Both of them. And the corporal who came to relieve them looked tired enough to sit."

"Will they sleep long?"

"Long enough." Mags' eyes were wet, but her voice never wavered. "Go get your boy."

*My boy.* Jude felt those words go through her like a splinter finding its way out. Oscar wasn't hers. He belonged to a dead lieutenant whose name Ashworth had spent a whole winter trying to bury, and he belonged to himself, and he belonged—to whatever degree children belong to anyone—to the circle of women and a carpenter and a dying sergeant who had decided, without ever voting on it, that this child would not be forgotten while any of them drew breath.

But when Mags said *your boy*, Jude did not correct her.

* * *

The colonel's quarters stood at the end of the lane, lamplit, guarded by one soldier who swayed on his feet with the particular looseness of Mags' poppy draught doing its slow work. Tilda was already there.

Jude saw her from the shadow of the woodshed: Tilda, broad as a doorway, standing in front of the guard and talking at him with tremendous animation about pay allotments and a discrepancy in her discharge papers, waving documents that were masterfully, beautifully wrong. The soldier blinked at her, swayed, tried to focus on the papers, and lost the fight with gravity by degrees, listing toward the fence post like a ship taking water.

Tilda caught his arm, steadied him with exaggerated concern, and looked directly across the yard—at Jude—and nodded once.

Inside the colonel's quarters, then. Inside, where they had taken Oscar after the betrayal at the gate, where Ashworth kept him like a document himself, filed away until departure made him someone else's problem.

Jude crossed the yard fast. The door was not locked—it was *held*, by a second guard slumped in a chair just inside, chin on chest, snoring gently. She stepped over his boots.

Oscar sat on a camp bed in the corner of a room full of maps and burned-paper smell, fully dressed, shoes on, a small pack beside him that was not his. Someone had packed for him. Ferro, she realized—Ferro had been allowed one supervised visit, and Ferro had packed the boy's bag the way he built cabinets: every joint considered, nothing wasted. In the pack she could see the corner of a wooden bird, the one Ferro had carved him in February, the one Oscar slept holding like a talisman.

"Oscar," she whispered.

He looked up. He had not spoken easily even after he began to speak—he rationed words the way starving men ration bread—but he said hers now, clear and certain, the first word he'd ever trusted to the world:

"Jude."

"We're going to the river. Can you walk fast and stay quiet?"

"Yes." He was already up, bag in hand, and then he stopped and tugged her sleeve and pointed at the desk.

On the desk lay papers in the colonel's neat hand. The closed inquiry. The forged testimony. And beneath them, half-covered, a list—a departure manifest, and on it, in the column marked *dependent persons*, a single line: *Boy, approx. 6 years, to be transferred to orphan asylum, K——, with escort.*

With escort. With a soldier paid to deliver him into a building where names were numbers and numbers were dust.

Jude took the page. Folded it. Put it inside her coat next to the ledger. Evidence, Mags would have called it. Ammunition, Brannigan would have said. She only knew that she was not leaving it behind for the fire to tidy.

"Come on," she whispered, and took his hand.

* * *

The lane down to the river gate was the longest walk of her life, though it took less than two hundred paces.

The regiment was everywhere and nowhere—wagons creaking toward the ford, columns forming in the market square, and above it all the smoke thickening as more records went onto the pyres. Nobody looked at a nurse walking a small boy toward the water. Nurses were furniture. That had been her whole life's lesson and tonight it was her armor.

At the gatehouse, Ferro stood in the doorway of his carpentry shop with the door barred behind him and a lantern lit, working—as far as any observer could tell—on a crate. He was, in fact, barring his own door from the inside, and the crate was real, and when Jude slipped past with Oscar he said only, in the voice he used for joinery:

"Chest went down at dusk. Pilot's waiting below the millrace. You have until the sky goes grey." A pause. "I'll be along the bank. If anyone asks, I'm salvaging my tools."

"You should come with us," Jude said. "There's room."

"There's suspicion," Ferro corrected mildly. "It falls on whoever's missing. I'll be missing from exactly nowhere." He knelt then, abruptly, to Oscar's height, and put both enormous hands on the boy's shoulders. "Mind the bird. Mind the lady. Learn your letters—Tilda will box your ears if you don't."

Oscar hugged him around the neck, fierce and brief, the way children hug when they understand that hugging is a kind of promise. Ferro stood very fast afterward and turned back to his lantern, and Jude pretended not to see him wipe his face with a sawdust-grey wrist.

Then the path along the bank, the ice plates grinding offshore like a fleet of wrecked ships, and there—tucked below the millrace where the current ran black and fast—the barge. Low in the water, lamps doused, and on her deck a medicine chest with a false bottom, and in the false bottom, Jude prayed, the original ledger's twin.

The pilot was a leathery woman named Corvane whom Ferro trusted because she owed him a coffin and hated the army for reasons she declined to specify. She took Oscar's bag, lifted the boy aboard like cargo, and jerked her thumb at Jude.

"Well? Water won't wait for sentiment."

Jude hesitated on the bank.

Because here was the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for weeks. Stay, and be safe—or rather, be *survivable*: a nurse reassigned, a witness dispersed, a nuisance dissolved into the machinery of the next garrison. Mags would shield her. Tilda had already paid three days of whatever had been done to her in that cell, and would pay again gladly. The safe road was the road where Jude Kavanagh stayed invisible, and invisibility had been her religion for forty years.

And downstream there was a free city, and a printing shop, and a roll of names that needed a press and paper and someone stubborn enough to stand over it.

"Get in the boat, Nurse," Corvane said. Not unkindly.

"I'm thinking."

"Don't. Thinking's how they catch you."

Downstream the sky was going from black to iron. Dawn was the deadline—dawn was when the escort for the orphan transfer would come for Oscar, dawn was when Ashworth would discover an empty room and a drugged guard, dawn was when everything would either be finished or finished with them.

Jude looked back up the slope at the workshop town. At the surgery window where Mags' candle burned. At the smoke rising off the record fires, all those names being eaten, and she thought of Brannigan, who had dictated his testimony with his breath failing between sentences, who had said: *Write it so close to Tam's name they can't cut one without cutting the other.*

Brannigan. She hadn't been able to reach him since the search; he'd been moved to the infirmary tent near headquarters, too weak to move, too stubborn to die on schedule.

"I have to see him first," she said. "One hour. Keep the boy warm."

Corvane swore comprehensively and creatively. "One hour. Then I leave with or without you, and I'll tell the lad you chose a book over him, and he'll believe me, because children always do."

"He won't believe you," Jude said. "He knows me."

And she turned and climbed back up the bank, faster than she'd come down, with the ledger beating against her ribs.

* * *

The infirmary tent smelled of carbolic and lamp oil, and Brannigan lay in the third cot with his eyes open, watching the canvas roof as if counting something on it.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Jude said.

"I'm supposed to be dead. We're both disappointed." His voice was a ruin, but the humor in it was intact, the last wall standing of a man who had spent thirty years making jokes about things that deserved weeping. He turned his head with difficulty. "Well? Is it done?"

"It's done. He's on the boat. Both ledgers. Everything."

Something went out of his face then—not hope exactly, but the tension that hope lives inside. He exhaled a long time, like a man setting down a load carried uphill.

"Tam," he said. "You wrote him?"

"Beside your testimony. They can't cut one without the other."

"Good lass." His hand found hers, grip feeble but deliberate. "Listen. The march will kill me anyway. Three days, maybe four, if I'm lucky, and I've never been lucky. So here's what you'll do—you'll not waste grief on me. Grief's for the unfinished. I'm finished. I got to see it loaded on a boat." A ghost of the grin. "Most men don't get to watch their own names escape."

"You could come," she said, knowing it was a lie, knowing he knew it was a lie, offering it because some lies are a form of respect.

"And slow a barge carrying the truth of the regiment?" He shook his head minutely against the pillow. "I'll ride with the column. Die in a proper ditch. Very traditional."

She bent and kissed his forehead, rough and grey-stubbled, and he gripped her wrist hard, suddenly, with the last of whatever fuel he had left.

"One thing. When they print it—all of it, the roll of names—" His breath caught, held, released. "Spell Tam right. Thomas. People always write Thomás, with the fancy mark, and he hated it. Said it looked like showing off."

"I'll spell him plain," Jude promised. "Plain as bread."

"That's my brother." His eyes closed. His grip loosened by degrees, not letting go so much as setting her down. "Go on, then. Go be visible. Scare the life out of the bastards."

* * *

She made the barge with minutes to spare and the sky gone from iron to pearl.

They pushed off into the current just as the first bugle sounded in the town behind them—departure calls, or alarm, impossible to say, and soon irrelevant. The river took them. It was running high with the melted winter, brown and muscular, carrying broken ice plates that rang off the hull like distant bells. Oscar stood at the rail with his wooden bird and watched the workshop shrink, and Jude stood behind him with her hands on his shoulders, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the river wasn't already saying.

Behind them, the town woke into confusion. She could imagine it precisely: Ashworth discovering the empty room, the manifest with its torn-out page, the guards who could not be roused. Could imagine the colonel's composure—that beautiful velvet thing—cracking down the middle like the ice. He would rage. He would investigate. But the boat was downstream and the evidence was in a false bottom and the witnesses were scattered like seed, and there was nothing power could do, in the end, to a thing already printed in two hands and moving away at the speed of water.

Mags would stay. Tilda would follow by the pilgrim road in a month. Ferro would bar his door, open it innocently, and know nothing about anything for the rest of his natural life. And Brannigan—

Brannigan died at dawn.

She learned it three days later at the first lock, when a coal barge overtook them and its pilot passed a bundle of newspapers and a verbal message from Ferro's network: *Sergeant died in the column, hour after departure, sitting upright against a wagon wheel. Left a parcel.* The parcel reached her a week on, forwarded through three pairs of hands, and inside was Brannigan's brother's ring, a worn prayer card, and a note in a stranger's careful hand: *He asked that these go to the woman with the ledger. He said, tell her the spelling matters.*

Jude read it on deck with the spring sun warm on her shoulders and cried the way she had not cried at her father's funeral or at any of the deaths in four winters of nursing: openly, hugely, without shame, while Oscar leaned against her side and patted her arm with the grave competence of a boy who had seen women weep before and understood it as weather.

"Grief's for the unfinished," she said aloud, testing it. It didn't quite hold her weight yet. Perhaps in a year.

"Who's unfinished?" Oscar asked.

"Not us," Jude said. "We're just getting started."

* * *

The free city announced itself by smell—woodsmoke, tar, frying onions, printing ink—before it showed itself at all. They came into harbor on a morning of high bright wind, and Corvane, who had spent nine days pretending not to be fond of either of her passengers, gruffly pointed out the street where the printers kept their shops.

"Ledger's dry?" she asked.

"Dry. Wrapped in waxcloth. Been dry since the second day."

"Then get it done before the ink in your memory fades. Memory's a liar." She spat expertly over the rail. "Paper remembers better."

They lodged above a chandler's, in a room with one window and a view of masts. Oscar unpacked his bird, his spare shirt, and the packet of letters Tilda had begun teaching him—he could manage his own name now, and most of the alphabet, and he practiced on the floor with a slate, tongue between his teeth.

On the third evening, Jude unwrapped the ledger at the table and read it through, start to finish, for the first time as a whole. Four winters of small names. The privates omitted from dispatches, the fever dead listed as *wastage*, the executed listed as *deserted*, the brothers and sons and neighbors reduced to numbers by men who counted only what they owned. And threaded through it now, in her freshest ink: Brannigan's testimony. Thomas Brannigan, spelled plain. Lieutenant Aldous Renard-Vey, murdered by an order, witnessed by his six-year-old son. The forged inquiry, transcribed from the stolen page. All of it. Everything the fire was meant to eat, sitting intact in lamplight, patient as seed.

Oscar climbed onto the chair beside her and studied the pages upside down, frowning with concentration.

"You're going to make a book," he said.

"A roll of names. A published one. Anyone can buy it. Anyone can read it."

"Even officers?"

"*Especially* officers." She touched the page where the lieutenant's name waited. "That's rather the point."

He was quiet a while, tracing the air above the letters with one finger, sounding them out the way Tilda had taught him. Then he looked up with the terrible directness of children and asked the question that had been waiting in him since the gate, maybe since the door, maybe since the night a man told him to forget a face:

"When they print it—will Papa be in it? Spelled right?"

"Spelled right," Jude said. "Every letter. I checked twice."

He nodded slowly, satisfied, and went back to his slate, and the scratch of his chalk was the only sound in the room for a long time.

Jude sat with the ledger open before her and understood that the work was nearly done. Tomorrow she would find the printer. She would argue about paper and price and type size, and she would win, because she had spent a lifetime learning to be overlooked and she intended to spend the rest of it being impossible to overlook. The roll would carry every name she'd gathered and every testimony she'd recorded, and it would go out into the world like the barge had gone out onto the river—small, laden, unstoppable.

But tonight, one page was still blank: the title page. It wanted a title, and a place, and a year.

She dipped her pen. And stopped.

Because a book like this needed a name on it—someone answerable, someone who claimed the words the way a mother claims a child, someone the powerful men could find if they came looking. Every instinct of forty years said *stay invisible, stay alive*. And every name in the ledger said otherwise. They had not survived on caution. They had survived because one woman wrote them down, and writing-down was a public act, and public acts required a signature.

Oscar glanced over, chalk paused. "Aren't you going to finish it?"

"Tomorrow," Jude said. "Tomorrow I'll sign it."

She blew out the lamp, and in the dark the river went on talking to itself below the window, carrying the winter away.
