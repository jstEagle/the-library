## 22. Her Own Name

The printing shop on the Rue des Trois Moulins smelled of ink and hot metal and the particular dust that paper makes when it is cut — a smell Jude had come to love more than any she had known, including bread, including spring.

It was not a grand establishment. The press was old, the owner a widower named Aubry with ink permanently blackening the creases of his knuckles, and the back room where they worked in the evenings had a window that looked out on nothing but a wall and a strip of sky. But it was in a free city, which meant no colonel could walk through the door, and no aide-de-camp could stand at her threshold with coins in his palm and an order to forget. A year ago she had not known such places still existed. She had begun to suspect they did not.

The type was set. Aubry had checked it twice, running his thumb along the rows of reversed letters like a man reading braille, and now the forme sat locked in its iron frame on the bed of the press, waiting for morning, waiting for paper, waiting for the world.

*Roll of the Fallen of the Winter Campaign, 1813–14.*

That was the title Aubry had insisted on — plain, he said, so plain no magistrate could call it sedition. Beneath it, in smaller type: *Being a true account of the common soldiers who died in the service of their regiment, whose names were omitted from all official record, together with testimony concerning the death of Lieutenant William Ashworth-Hale and the order that sent him to it.*

They had argued about that last part for three nights. Aubry feared the name. Tilda had refused to leave it off. In the end they had compromised by spelling the surname as the lieutenant himself had spelled it in his letters home — Hale, with the compound title folded away like a thing he had never liked wearing — and even so, Jude knew, somewhere a man in a fine coat would read the broadsheet and understand exactly what it said. Let him. That was rather the point.

She stood at the window now with the last proof sheet in her hands, reading it again though she knew every line by heart, because she had written every line twice: once in the ledger, once here, and once more in memory besides, where no fire could reach it.

Two hundred and eleven names.

She had counted them the way you count anything that matters — over and over, afraid the number will change when you are not looking. Two hundred and eleven men whose deaths existed nowhere but in her hand. Privates and drummers and farriers and one fourteen-year-old bugler whose age she had guessed and marked with a question mark she could never resolve. Sergeant Declan Brannigan's name stood near the top of the roll, beside his brother Padraig's, the two of them finally in the same line of text after thirty years of the army keeping them in different ledgers. Brannigan had died at dawn on the barge with the river running brown and broken around him, and his last words had been an instruction about commas — *put the comma there, miss, a man should have his rank read properly* — and she had put the comma there, and it was there now, in the printed forme, in iron, permanent as anything made by human hands can be.

Lieutenant William Hale's name stood further down than his rank would have earned him in any official list. Jude had ordered the roll by date of death, not by precedence, because the whole book was a refusal of precedence. He lay among the men who died the same week he did, shoulder to shoulder with them in death as he had been in the failed charge that killed them all.

And at the very bottom, after the last soldier, in slightly different type that Aubry had grumbled about setting:

*Also recorded herein: testimony of Sergeant D. Brannigan; testimony of J. Kavanagh, nurse; and the witness Oscar Hale, aged seven years, whose evidence was heard by this compiler and is appended in full.*

Aged seven. He had turned eight in March, in this city, with a cake Tilda had burned and Ferro had pretended not to notice was burned, and he had blown out the candles and then, because he was Oscar, asked whether dead people got cakes too, and whether anyone had ever baked one for his father. Mags had answered that one, because Mags could answer anything without her voice breaking. Jude had gone out into the yard and stood in the cold until her face was fit to be seen again.

* * *

"You're doing the face again."

Jude looked down. Oscar stood beside her at the window, a pencil behind his ear and a smudge of it on his chin, holding his slate against his chest like a little drum. Eight years old and already tall for it, dark-haired, watchful-eyed; he had his mother's face, everyone agreed except him. He had drawn his father obsessively for months, always the same face in the fine coat, until one evening in the workshop he had drawn it small instead, pocket-sized, and folded it into the lining of his cap, and after that he drew other things — horses, the river breaking up, the barge, the five of them standing in front of the burning workshop rendered with the calm accuracy of a court painter.

"What face?" she said.

"The thinking-about-the-book face." He tapped the proof sheet. "Is it done? Is it really done?"

"It's done. Tomorrow we print."

"Can I pull the lever?"

"You may ask Monsieur Aubry nicely, which is what pulling a press costs."

He grinned and then sobered, the way he did, weather changing fast across his face the way it changes across flat country. He had spoken his first words to her in a cellar smelling of carbolic, a single sentence that had taken a year off her life and given the ledger its heart: *My papa didn't fall, miss. He was sent.* Since then he had not stopped speaking, as if the words had been dammed up behind a year of silence and now came through everything, questions and corrections and small merciless observations, a boy making up for lost testimony.

"Mags is teaching me the long s," he said. "It looks like a snake pretending to be an f. I don't see why they need both."

"They don't. They just refuse to admit it."

"Tilda says the same about officers."

"Tilda," said Jude, "is a woman of profound scholarship."

He laughed, and then went quiet again, studying the proof sheet upside down in her hands. She watched him find the names, working along the columns with his finger in the air, sounding them out. He had learned his letters the hard way and loved them accordingly, the way you love water after thirst. Every name he reached he touched, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

Then his finger stopped.

He looked up at her. "Where's your name?"

"It's there," she said. "In the testimony part. *J. Kavanagh, nurse.*"

"That's not your name. That's your job with a letter stuck on it." He frowned with the particular severity he reserved for errors in typesetting, which he had inherited from no one and which Aubry declared made the boy a natural-born printer. "Brannigan's name is there. All of him, first and last. The lieutenant's there. Even the bad colonel's in it, sort of, hiding inside his nephew's name." He turned the problem over. "You wrote everybody in. Why aren't you in it?"

*Because I was the pen,* she almost said. *Because someone has to hold the book. Because I spent six years learning to be furniture, and furniture doesn't sign itself.*

She heard her own answers forming and found, one by one, that she did not believe them anymore.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

Oscar considered this with terrible patience. "I think you do," he said. "But if you don't, we can fix it. We have a pencil." He held it out to her, still warm from behind his ear.

* * *

She thought about it longer than a pencil deserves.

That night, after Aubry had gone home and the shop was lit by one lamp and the forme sat locked in its frame like a held breath, she took the composing stick and stood before the case of type, and did not touch it. Setting a name in metal felt like something that should be done by another hand. It felt like a thing you should be given, not take. She had been given so little in her life — a post, a wage, a regulation cot, orders — that receiving had come to feel like danger, and taking had come to feel impossible.

Tilda found her there. Tilda had a genius for finding people precisely when they wanted least to be found, and she came in shaking rain off her shoulders and set down a bag of apples and looked at the forme and at Jude and understood everything in about four seconds, because she always had.

"Well," she said. "About time."

"I haven't done anything."

"No," Tilda agreed. "That's what I said."

"The roll is theirs. It's for them. Their families. If I put myself in it, it becomes—" She gestured, losing the word.

"Becomes what? Yours?" Tilda took an apple, bit it, spoke through it. "Girl, I carried washwater through that camp for nine years and never once saw an officer write down a washerwoman's name. You think they left themselves out of books out of modesty? They left us out because leaving us out was how they stayed comfortable. You putting yourself in isn't pride. It's arithmetic." She pointed the apple core at the forme. "Every name in there was written by somebody. The book should say who. Otherwise some clever magistrate in fifty years says, *anonymous compilation, unverified, likely fabrication*, and burns it all over again. Put your name on it so they know who to come for." Her eyes were bright and hard and kind. "So they know it cost somebody."

"So they know who to arrest, you mean."

"I mean so they know you weren't afraid to be findable." Tilda tossed the core in the bin. "Mags says fear you keep private turns to rot. Fear you publish turns to fuel. Or possibly she said the reverse; she talks like a sermon with hiccups. Either way." She shrugged into her shawl. "Set the type or don't. But stop standing there letting the poor letters freeze."

* * *

Ferro came the next morning before dawn, because of course he did; the man had not slept past dawn since the workshop, and probably not since the war before that. He stood in the doorway stamping melting frost off his boots, and the new leg — his own leg, the one he had carved for himself after the old one went, walnut and steel, the finest piece of joinery in four provinces — knocked gently on the floorboards as he walked, a sound Jude had once hidden a book inside and would hear in her sleep until she died.

He looked at the press. He looked at the forme. He said, in his flat carpenter's way, as if reporting on a delivery of planks:

"Ashworth resigned his commission in February. There's talk of a parliamentary question. Something about the winter returns not matching the burial rolls." A pause. "Someone sent them the numbers."

"Did someone," Jude said.

"Anonymous packet. Very thorough handwriting." Ferro's mouth did the smallest possible movement that served him for smiling. "Whoever it was must have copied everything twice."

"She sounds reckless."

"He," said Ferro. "For safety. Anyway. The inquiry reopens next month, in London, in public. Brannigan's testimony will be read aloud, if we send it. Which we will." He laid a sealed packet on the workbench — certified copies, witnessed by a lawyer who owed Mags a favor from a war Mags declined to discuss. "Ought to be done under a real name, though. Testimony from *a nurse* carries less weight than testimony from a person. Lawyers are snobs about initials." He nodded at the forme. "Same logic everywhere. Names are load-bearing."

Everyone in her life had become, in the space of one day, a mason lecturing her on architecture.

Mags arrived mid-morning with Oscar trailing behind her carrying his slate, and did not lecture at all, which was somehow worse. She only looked at the empty line beneath the imprint where a compiler's name might go, and then looked at Jude, and raised one eyebrow with the precision of a surgeon lifting a flap, and said, "Whenever you're ready, dear. The ink's not getting younger."

* * *

It was Oscar who settled it, because it had always been going to be Oscar. That was the shape of the whole story, if she was honest: she had built her life out of the belief that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms with better floors — and then a mute child had been abandoned at her door in a blizzard, and history had followed him in, dripping snow, and refused to leave.

They pulled the first sheets in the afternoon. Aubry let Oscar work the lever with both hands, steadied by the old man's grip, and the press closed with its great sighing clack and opened, and there it was: black on white, two hundred and eleven names and five testimonies and one comma placed exactly where a dying sergeant wanted it.

Oscar wiped the ink from the fresh sheet with his sleeve, careful as a curate with a relic, then held it out to her.

"You do the last one," he said. "There's room."

"There isn't room. The page is full."

"There's room," he repeated, with the mild immovability he had inherited from absolutely everyone in his strange family. "We'll make it smaller. Aubry knows how."

Aubry, who had heard everything and pretended he hadn't, sighed the sigh of a craftsman asked to ruin good margins, and set three words in ten-point where twelve had been planned. He inked them, and rolled the forme, and left the bed open, and stepped back, and the shop went quiet enough that Jude could hear the rain starting on the roof and the river far off below the city, moving freely, unlocked, going wherever it pleased.

She picked up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. She noticed that with surprise — a year ago it would have; in the workshop, signing anything felt like signing a confession. Now it was just a hand, and a pen, and a line waiting.

She wrote: *Compiled and attested by Elodie Jude Kavanagh, late nurse to His Majesty's 44th Regiment of Foot, in the free city of — *, and gave the city, because Tilda had been right: let them know where to come for her. Let them know she could be found, and was not hiding, and would be here tomorrow, printing.

Below it, unprompted, in a round boyish hand that had clearly been practiced on scrap paper first, Oscar added: *and witnessed by Oscar Hale, who remembers everything.*

Nobody corrected the grammar. Some sentences you let stand.

They hung the first proof in the shop window as the light went, facing out toward the street, where anyone walking past could stop and read the names of men the army had decided never existed. Within an hour a lamplighter had stopped, hat off. Then two dockworkers. Then a woman with a basket who stood a long time at the column where her son's name presumably was, touching the glass, before she came inside without a word and pressed a coin into Aubry's hand and asked for a copy, and then another for her sister, and stood while Jude folded them, watching Jude's hands as if confirming they were real.

By dark they had sold forty copies. By dark, word was going out along the rivers and the roads, in saddlebags and bread loaves and bandage rolls and whatever else people carried, because that was how truth traveled — never by dispatch, always by hand.

Jude stood at the window with Oscar asleep against her side, his pencil still behind his ear, and watched the lamplighter work his way down the street, and beyond him the river running silver and fast toward whatever sea received it.

Somewhere downstream, in London, a committee would reopen a file. Somewhere upstream, a man without a commission read a broadsheet and learned that forgetting was no longer available to him. Somewhere a washerwoman's son would find his mother's name in a book for the first time in the history of books.

The presses would run again at dawn. There were more names coming — letters arrived weekly now, from widows, from old comrades, from regiments she'd never heard of, each envelope containing a scrap of paper with a name on it and sometimes a story, always a plea: *write him down. He was real. He was here.*

She had thought, a year ago, that she was keeping a ledger against the dark. She understood now that she had been keeping it for the light — that the book was not a wall but a door, and she had merely been the one standing at it, letting people through.

Oscar stirred. "Did you sign it?" he murmured, mostly asleep.

"I signed it."

"Good." He burrowed deeper. "Now it's true."

Outside, the rain eased, and the river ran on, carrying the spring down to the sea, and in the window the names caught the lamplight and held it, patient as iron, ready for morning.
