## 1. The Last Crossing

The tide gave us four hours that morning, and I meant to spend every one of them.

I woke in the dark to the sound of the sea pulling back from the rocks — that long, wet exhalation the island makes twice a day when it becomes, briefly, a place with roads. By the time the first grey light came through the kitchen window, I had already banked the fire, fed the gulls their crusts on the sill as my husband used to do before he was anything but a name on a brass plate below the lamp, and loaded the handcart with what Sergeant Brandt's men had ferried out on the last supply run: flour in paper sacks gone soft at the corners, a side of salt pork, lamp oil in tins I would carry up the hundred and twelve stairs myself because no one else on Paloma had the shoulders for it anymore. And paraffin for the stove. And a bundle of letters wrapped in oilcloth, though the letters were fewer this year than last. Fewer every year. The war had taught people not to write things down.

I dressed in layers — wool, then oilskin, then the long canvas apron with pockets deep enough to swallow my hands — and stood a moment at the door listening to the water withdraw down the channel, patient as a debt collector.

*Four hours,* the sea said. *Not a minute more.*

I took the cart down the ramp at half past seven, wheels rattling over stone worn smooth by two centuries of exactly this errand, and set out across the causeway with the whole of the tidal flat opening around me like a page being turned.

* * *

There is no describing the causeway properly to anyone who has not walked it. It is not a road so much as an agreement between the island and the mainland — a spine of granite and packed shingle, three-quarters of a mile of it, running just high enough above the seabed that twice a day the ocean forgives it and lets it exist. At its lowest edge the stones are furred with bladderwrack and barnacle, and in the winter they wear a skin of ice that makes each crossing a negotiation with God. The mainland gate stands at the far end: an iron barrier painted government green, chained now until spring, manned by Brandt and his two young soldiers in a tollhouse that smells of coal smoke and boiled tea. The gate exists, officially, to protect the light station from smugglers. Everyone knows what it actually protects, which is the magistrate's peace of mind.

I walked into the wind with my collar up and counted the stones the way I always do — not all of them, but certain ones. The seventh, where the mortar is crumbling and the footing goes soft. The thirty-first, where a storm in '13 tore out a whole yard of causeway and the masons filled it with whatever was at hand, so that it sounds hollow underfoot, a drumbeat in the middle of the road. The sixty-second, where the rock is pink instead of grey, some other mountain's bones carried here by ice ten thousand years ago. My husband called these the island's rosary. He said a keeper should know her ground the way a priest knows his prayers — not because she needs them, but because the day will come when she does.

He was right about that, if about little else worth saying aloud.

The tide was far out, and the flats on either side lay exposed and gleaming, pocked with the burrows of lugworms, stitched with the trails of birds. A heron stood in a pool near the halfway mark with the stillness of something that has made its peace with waiting. Beyond it, the water came back in a silver line so faint you could pretend it wasn't there. But I could feel it. You learn to feel the tide in your teeth after enough winters. Somewhere past the horizon bar of the sea, the great weight of it was already turning, deciding, beginning the long slow walk home.

Halfway across, I stopped and looked back at the island.

Paloma in November morning light is not beautiful; it is true. Grey rock, green turf cropped short by sheep that are mostly bone and opinion, the white tower rising from the summit with its gallery rail black against a sky the color of dishwater. Below the tower, huddled into the lee of the hill like children behind their mother's skirts, the village: nineteen roofs, counting mine and the boathouse. Smoke from a dozen chimneys standing straight up in the windless hour, as if the island were holding its breath. The kirk. The drying sheds with their dark nets hung out like the skins of enormous fish. The harbor wall, and beyond it the moorings, empty now except for Maren Kessler's little boat *Thora*, which she would not haul out because hauling her out would mean admitting there was no one left to sail her come spring.

Nineteen roofs. Last winter there had been twenty-three.

I did the arithmetic the way I do every year on this crossing, standing in the wind with the cart creaking at my elbow, and the arithmetic came out the same way it always comes out: four families gone since the drownings, and not one of them gone by choice alone. That is the sentence I have never once said plainly, in nine years of keeping this light, and I felt it sitting in my chest this morning like a swallowed stone.

* * *

By eight o'clock I had reached the mainland side, and the town was already awake in the way towns are awake — shutters banging open, a baker's boy running with a basket, the smell of woodsmoke lying over everything like a blanket someone had thrown on a fire too soon. Herringhaven in winter is a town of narrowed eyes and counted coins. The war ended a year ago and nobody here believes it, quite. The men who came back came back changed, and the men who didn't come back left gaps shaped like themselves in every doorway, and the magistrate, Herr Voss, walks the high street every morning at nine in his grey coat with his hands clasped behind him, nodding to everyone, known by everyone, loved by — I have searched nine years and cannot finish that clause honestly.

The ferry sergeant was waiting at the gate with his ledger open against the cold.

Brandt is fifty-eight now and built like the bollard at the harbor mouth — squat, iron-ringed, immovable by weather or sentiment, or so he would want you to believe. His face is a map of small cruelties done by the sea: a cheekbone reset crooked, a scar through one eyebrow, ears scarred white with old frostbite. He looked at me, then at the cart, then wrote something in the ledger without asking me anything at all.

"Flour, pork, oil, paraffin," I said, because the ritual requires speech even when the words are unnecessary. "And letters."

"Letters." He said it the way he says everything, as though the word had disappointed him personally. "Six?"

"Five." One less than October. "Mrs. Osla gets nothing again. Her son in the merchant fleet hasn't written since August."

Brandt's pen paused. Not long. Just long enough. Then it moved on, scratching.

"Last crossing tomorrow," he said. "Weather office says the low's coming down from the north early this year. They're talking about closing you off a week ahead of schedule." He glanced north along the coast, where the sky had begun assembling something greyer than itself. "You'll want your stores laid in proper, Mrs. Ishikawa."

"I always do."

"You always do," he agreed, and stamped the page, and waved the cart through the gate with the bored authority of a man who has inspected ten thousand carts and found contraband in none of them, or in all of them, depending on how you define the word.

It was while I was loading the last of the order — the chandler's boy bringing out rope and a new glass lantern-chimney packed in straw — that I saw them loading the boat.

* * *

They were taking people off the island today. That was the other freight, the cargo no ledger records.

Every autumn the same question moves through our nineteen houses like a draft under a door: *will you winter on Paloma, or will you go over?* Those with means go over — to the town, to cousins inland, to rooms above shops where the walls don't shake in a northeast gale and the doctor is forty minutes away instead of a prayer. Those without means stay, and keep the island breathing until the gates unchain in April. And then there is a third category, which no one names, which is those who are not asked.

I watched from the gate as the ferry crew — four men, hired by the council, paid by the council, answerable to the council, which is to say to Voss — helped aboard the season's leavers. Old Piet Almquist went first, waving his stick at the gulls, going to his daughter in the valley because his heart couldn't be trusted another winter on the rock, and good luck to him, and we would miss his accordion. Then the Sorensens, mother and the two girls, going to the aunt in Herringhaven for schooling — ordinary, willing, ordinary. The girls ran up the gangplank the way children run anywhere, delighted simply by motion, and their mother followed with the luggage and a face like a woman carrying water across ice.

Then the crew went to the third house. The one no one names.

Maren Kessler was not allowed to leave. I want to write that plainly because in nine years I have never once managed to say it plainly, not even to myself in the dark of the lamp room where no one hears. Maren had asked — I know she had asked, she told me so on the church steps in September, quietly, with her eyes on the harbor — asked the council for passage to her sister's family on the mainland, winter passage, medical passage, her chest being what it had become since the drownings took her husband and both brothers and left her alone with a boat and a salt field she no longer owned. And the council had considered, and deliberated, and answered through the harbormaster in that soft administrative voice they use for cruelty: that winter accommodation on the mainland was limited, that priority must go to persons with established residence, that Mrs. Kessler's circumstances were of course regretted.

Regretted. The word they use for everything they intend to keep doing.

She stood on the quay now in her black coat with her hands folded, watching the Sorensen girls board, watching the gangplank come in, watching the ferry cast off with everyone whose leaving had been permitted. She did not cry. Maren hasn't cried in public since the funerals, and I have privately wondered whether she cried at all, or whether grief in her simply calcified, went hard and mineral, became part of the island's geology. She caught me looking and lifted one hand — not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *You see it too,* the hand said. *We will not speak of it.*

I lifted my own hand the same way, and hated myself for the fluency of the gesture. Nine years of practice.

"Kessler woman again?" Brandt said beside me. He hadn't raised his voice. He never needed to; the gate was empty of everyone but us and the chandler's boy, who had developed a sudden fascination with the far end of the pier.

"She asked to cross."

"They all ask."

"And the council answers."

"The council answers," Brandt said, and closed the ledger, and looked out at the water with his ruined frostbitten ears going red in the wind, and for a moment — only a moment — his face did something underneath the iron that I might have mistaken, on a man capable of it, for shame. Then it was iron again. "Mind the seventh stone," he said. "Mortar's going. I've reported it three times."

"Three times," I said. "And the council answers."

Something passed between us then, brief as a bird's shadow over the flats — the thing that passes between people who both serve systems they do not respect. He almost smiled. Almost. Then he touched his cap and went back inside the tollhouse, and the kettle inside began its thin whistle, and I took the handles of the cart and turned back toward my island.

* * *

The tide had turned while I transacted my business. I could see it now, the silver line become an actual edge, a seam of moving water working its way up the channel with the unhurried certainty of everything the sea decides. I had perhaps three hours. Plenty, if the wind held from the west. Nothing, if it backed north — and the sky to the north had spent the morning building itself a second story, grey upon grey, with a bruised green tint at the base that old sailors call the widow's color and I call, having earned no better term, *trouble.*

I made the halfway mark as the first flakes came. Small ones, dry, experimental, melting on the pink sixty-second stone. Behind me Herringhaven was putting on its white shawl. Ahead, Paloma stood clear and dark under the last blue rent in the western sky, the tower catching what light remained so that it glowed faintly, like a candle seen through milk.

At the foot of the ramp, waiting for me with a barrel of fresh water rolled up from the spring, was the girl.

Ilsa. Maren's granddaughter — fourteen last midsummer, all elbows and stare, with her grandmother's dark brows and her grandfather's stubborn jaw and a way of looking at adults that suggested she was keeping notes for a later reckoning. She'd been sent down from the house to help, which meant she'd been sent down from the house because Maren wanted eyes on the causeway, wanting to be sure I crossed safely, wanting — I understood suddenly, watching the girl plant herself beside the cart with the barrel — wanting to send her love by proxy because sending it in person had become more than Maren's pride could finance.

"You're late," Ilsa said.

"The tide's late. I'm punctual."

"It's snowing."

"I can see it's snowing. Take the other handle."

She took the other handle, and we hauled together up the ramp, the cart complaining, the flakes thickening around us, and I found myself studying her sidelong the way I study weather. There was something in her this autumn that hadn't been there in summer. A hardness coming in behind the eyes, the first frost of adulthood. Children on Paloma grow up fast or not at all; the sea prunes the slow ones. Ilsa had lost her father and two uncles to the drownings when she was five, and had grown up the way grass grows through a crack in pavement — bent by the weight of it, but growing, always growing, toward whatever light the island allowed her.

"Grandmother says the bridge closes Friday," she said, as we gained the turf above the tideline.

"So it does."

"Not Thursday. Friday. Early."

"So I heard."

"And after that it's four months." She said it without drama, stating facts the way children state facts, but I heard underneath it the thing she was not saying, the same thing the whole island was not saying, the great unspoken fact of our winter: *four months sealed in with her.* With Maren, whose family had been erased from the land books as neatly as ink lifted with blotting paper. With the rest of us who had watched it happen and said nothing. Four months in a locked room with everything we refused to discuss.

"Yes," I said. "Four months."

"We'll manage," Ilsa said, in a tone so exactly her grandmother's that I stopped walking for half a step. Then, because she was fourteen and not yet finished being fourteen: "Will you teach me the lamp this winter? Properly? Grandmother says you'll say no."

"Your grandmother knows me well."

"But you'll say yes eventually. You always say yes eventually. Piet says you're soft, you just pretend to be stone."

"Piet," I said, "is on a boat to his daughter's house, where he will eat too much and complain about the wine, and his opinion of me may be safely disregarded."

But she had made me laugh, and she knew it, and wore her victory the whole way up the hill.

* * *

Evening came down early, as November evenings do, arriving like a creditor. I climbed the hundred and twelve stairs with the lamp oil on my back, two tins per trip, six trips, and trimmed the wicks, and polished the lens until it held the whole dark room inside its curved glass, and at sunset I lit the flame and set the clockwork turning and watched the beam go out across the water — that long white arm reaching, reaching, touching the mainland, sweeping on, touching the sea, sweeping on. Nine years I have lit it. My husband lit it eleven years before that. Before him, others. The lamp does not care about our silences. The lamp tells the truth every night to whoever is equipped to read it: *here is the rock; here is the danger; steer wide.*

If only everything could be said so plainly.

From the gallery rail I could see the whole island going dark below me, chimney by chimney, window by window. Nineteen roofs, minus Piet's, which would stand empty till April with its door battened and its garden untended. I could see the light in Maren's kitchen, steady and late, and the smaller light moving behind it that would be Ilsa doing lessons or chores or whatever fourteen-year-olds do when the world has been locked for the winter. I could see the harbor, and the black line of the causeway already vanishing under the returning tide, wave by wave, stone by stone, the sea reclaiming its property until Friday's storm arrived to seal the bargain with chains and ice.

And I could see, far off, at the very limit of the beam's reach, the north sky still building. Lower now. Greener. Organized. The weather office had said early, and the sea had begun agreeing with it, a long heavy swell already walking up from the northwest where no swell had any business walking yet.

Tomorrow was the last crossing. Tomorrow the cart would go over laden with everything we'd forgotten to buy, and come back laden with everything we couldn't do without, and by Friday the gates would chain, and the island would become what it becomes every winter: a sealed room, a held breath, a community kept safe from the world and from itself.

I stood in the wind a long time after the lamp needed nothing further from me, watching the swell build, feeling in my teeth the tide turn beneath the dark water, and thinking — the way I think every year on this night, and dismiss every year on this night — that this winter felt different. That the silences we kept were stacked higher than usual. That the sea, which gives back nothing without reason, seemed lately to be circling, choosing, preparing to return something.

Below me, Maren's kitchen light went out.

The swell walked on.
