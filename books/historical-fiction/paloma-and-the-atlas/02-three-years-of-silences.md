## 2. Three Years of Silences

The lamp wanted oil at half past four, and Ada Ishikawa did not keep the lamp waiting.

She climbed the spiral stair with the can balanced on her hip, counting the steps as she always counted them — ninety-one, ninety-two — because a keeper who stopped counting was a keeper whose mind had begun to wander, and a wandering mind on a winter sea was how lights went dark and ships went down. The stairwell smelled of brass polish and burning wick and, beneath it, the salt that got into everything on Paloma and never left, not from wool, not from wood, not from the creases of a woman's hands.

At the top, the great lens turned its slow, patient circles. She trimmed the wick, checked the oil reservoir, wiped a smudge of soot from the lower glass with the soft cloth she kept folded in her apron pocket. Outside, the afternoon light was failing in the long grey way it failed in November, the horizon dissolving into water without any clear line between them, as though the world had been left out in the rain and its colors had run.

*Three years,* she thought, and then refused to think it, and went back down the stairs to wind the clockwork.

That was the discipline of it. Grief came like weather; you could not stop it arriving, but you could decide what you would do while it passed through. Kenji had taught her that, or rather he had demonstrated it, standing at this same rail in storms that would have sent her running for the cellar, his face lit orange by the flame, saying nothing at all except once, very quietly: *The light doesn't care how you feel, Ada. That's why we trust it.*

She had hated him a little, that day. She understood now that it had been a kind of tenderness.

* * *

The lie she told most often was a small one, hardly worth the name. When Mrs. Callow from the mainland wrote asking after her health — Mrs. Callow who sold flour and gossip in equal measure — Ada answered that she was well, that the island suited her, that solitude agreed with her constitution. All of it nearly true, which was the trick. A lie built entirely of falsehood collapsed under its own weight; a lie built of true things could stand for years, load-bearing, invisible.

There were larger lies. There was the one about the boat.

In the spring of 1917, when the war's hunger had reached even their coast and the requisition officers came sniffing along the harbor, old Piet Voss had hidden two casks of herring under his floorboards rather than surrender them to the army stores. If the officers had found them, Piet — sixty-eight, bad lungs, a man who had already given one son to the sea and another to Flanders — would have been fined into ruin, perhaps jailed. They had come to the island on a Tuesday, boots ringing on the stones, and asked their questions, and Ada had stood in Piet's doorway with her arms folded and said, in her flat mainland-perfect accent, that she had seen no casks, that she had been at the lamp all week as everyone knew, that Piet Voss fished only what he ate and had eaten little enough that winter.

She had known about the casks. Everyone on the island had known about the casks. The officers had looked at her face, which gave away nothing because she had trained it to give away nothing, and they had believed her, or chosen to believe her, which amounted to the same thing.

Afterward Piet had tried to press a cask on her and she had refused it, and he had gripped her forearm with fingers like knotted rope and said, "You didn't have to do that," and she had said, "Yes, I did," and neither of them had spoken of it again. That was how debts worked here. Not spoken of. Carried.

Kenji would have done the same. Would have, if he had lived past the autumn of 1916.

* * *

The storm of that October had come up fast, the way autumn storms did — a green bruise on the southern horizon at noon, and by three o'clock the gulls gone silent and the air smelling of iron. Three boats had been out. Two had made it back. The *Maren Elisabeth*, Tomas Aldric's uncle skippering her — no relation to the widow Maren, though the island confused the names every season and had learned to laugh about it — had gone down off the northern shoals with five men aboard.

Ada had been in the lamp room already. She had done everything right. She had fed the flame, cleared the glass, set the fog signal going when the mist rolled in behind the rain, and burned the emergency flare twice over the shoal water, the red star climbing and bursting where the charts said deep channel lay. Any man who knew these waters could have read that light and lived.

Five men had drowned anyway. And afterward, when the inquiry came, sitting in the mainland customs house with its maps and its brass fittings and its men in uniforms too clean for any shore, someone had suggested — gently, in the way that inquiries suggest things — that the keeper might have neglected her duties, that the flare might have gone up late, that the light might have guttered.

They had produced a witness. A fisherman from the mainland town, a cousin of the magistrate's, who swore he had been watching from the point and seen the lamp dark for the better part of an hour.

It was a lie. Ada had known it was a lie the way she knew the weight of the wind by the sound in the wires. But the witness was the magistrate's cousin, and the magistrate signed the findings, and the finding was *inconclusive*, and inconclusive meant that Ada Ishikawa kept her post but carried the question forever after, like a stone sewn into her coat lining. Some people on the island still gave her looks across the market square. Old Osla had never given her such a look, not once, and had said only, "I saw your light that night. It never went dark." Osla had lost sons in other waters, older drownings, and knew what a light was worth and what a lie was worth, and which one weighed more.

But the cousin's testimony had stood, unchallenged, unchallengeable. Because who was she? A keeper. A widow. A foreigner's wife, though Kenji had been dead three years and buried under the rowan tree with full rites and half the island weeping. Paper decided things here, not truth. Paper and the men who wrote it.

She had not yet learned, that year, how much paper could be made to say anything at all.

* * *

Now she wound the clockwork, forty turns, feeling the mechanism gather itself beneath her hand like something alive and patient. Through the lantern glass the first stars were coming out over the water, hard and white. The causeway stretched toward the mainland, black stone vanishing into black tide, and beyond it she could see the single lamp of the ferry sergeant's gatehouse, a small yellow eye that watched the crossing night and day in season.

Sergeant Brandt. Another debt. Another silence.

Two winters ago Brandt had come out to the island on an inspection, half-frozen, complaining in his gruff way about the posting, and Ada had fed him fish stew and let him sleep in the spare bunk because the tide had turned early and the crossing was suicide. He had drunk her tea and looked at the photograph of Kenji on the mantel — Kenji in his keeper's uniform, young, squinting against sun — and had said nothing, and in the morning had inspected her logbook and written *exemplary* in the margin, and had left. Only later had she learned, from Piet, what Brandt had not said: that ten years before, working the mainland light as a young coastguard, Kenji had gone into November water after a capsized dinghy and pulled a half-drowned sergeant out of it, and had walked home soaked to the skin and said nothing about it either. Kenji collected no debts. He simply paid what he owed to whoever needed paying, and moved on.

*The light doesn't care how you feel.* No. But people remembered what the light did. People carried it.

She banked the fire in the small stove and sat in the keeper's chair with her mending, the needle going in and out of a sail-patch, her hands working while her mind did what minds do when hands are busy. Outside the wind had begun, a low moan out of the southwest, testing the shutters, finding the loose one above the store room the carpenter never came to fix.

Three years of silences. She counted them sometimes, the way she counted stairs. The lie to Mrs. Callow. The lie to the requisition officers. The silence about Piet's casks, about Brandt's inspection, about the money she quietly left in the collection plate for the widow Halm whose husband had been lost the year before the *Maren Elisabeth*. The silence she kept about her own nights — the ones when grief came up like flood tide and she stood at the lantern rail until dawn with her teeth clenched so hard her jaw ached, because a keeper alone on a tidal island could not afford to be heard crying, could not afford to be *seen*, could not afford to be the kind of woman the mainland called unstable and took lamps away from.

Every silence had a reason. Every silence had kept someone safe — mostly herself, though she dressed that up in nobler cloth. That was the honest accounting, done late at night, done honestly because no one was there to hear it.

And yet.

And yet there were silences on this coast that were not hers, that were heavier, that belonged to men in better coats. She had felt them for years without naming them — the way the magistrate's office handled the drowning estates, the way papers moved slowly or not at all when widows asked after land titles, the way Maren's face closed like a door whenever the salt fields were mentioned, fields her grandfather had plowed and which now, by some arrangement nobody explained, were leased to the magistrate's cousin at rents that were charity on paper and theft underneath it.

Ada had noticed. She had filed the noticing away with the rest of her disciplines. A keeper's job was the lamp, not the courts. The light doesn't care how you feel. Tend the flame. Count the stairs. Wind the clockwork. Let the big lies belong to the men who tell them.

The needle slipped. She pricked her thumb, and watched the bead of blood rise dark in the lamplight, and put the thumb to her mouth, tasting salt and copper.

Outside, the wind had strengthened. It came around the tower in long slow spirals, pressing at the glass, and far out on the water the swell was beginning to show white backs even in the starlight. The barometer in the passage below had fallen since noon — she had noted it in the log, 29.1 inches and dropping, the handwriting neat, the numbers honest, because whatever else she lied about, she did not lie to the log. The log was the one record on this island that told the truth, because she made it tell the truth, because someone had to.

She wrote the evening entry: *Wind SW freshening. Sea rising. Barometer falling steadily. Lamp satisfactory.*

Then she sat a moment longer, pen lifted, listening. The wind had a note in it she recognized, a note under the note, the sound the gale made when it was gathering its shoulders. The almanac said the closing storm was still a week out. The almanac had been wrong before.

On the mainland, the gatehouse lamp at the causeway's end burned steady and small, and she thought of Sergeant Brandt inside it, warming his tea, keeping his own accounts, carrying his own silences. She thought of the chained gates that would swing shut across the causeway when the storm came, and the island sealed off like a held breath, and the strange freedom and strange prison of that — a whole winter with nothing between her and the sea but her own discipline.

She finished the entry, blew out the desk lamp, and climbed once more to the lantern room, where the great light swept its circles over the darkening water, over the shoals, over the graves of five good men, over everything the sea had taken and everything the shore had buried and everything, somewhere out in that black immensity, that the sea had not yet finished giving back.

The barometer fell another tenth by midnight.

By morning, the gulls had gone silent, and the horizon wore the color of a bruise, and Ada Ishikawa stood at her rail watching the south and understanding, with the calm certainty of twenty years' keeping, that the storm had come early — and that the bridge would close tomorrow, whether the island was ready or not.
