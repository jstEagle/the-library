## 4. What the Sea Gave Back

The storm did not so much end as exhaust itself, and when Ada Ishikawa climbed the tower stairs at four o'clock to trim the wick, she could hear the difference in the sea the way a mother hears a change in a child's breathing across a dark room. The great voice of it — that grinding, bellowing thing that had spent two days trying to unmake her island stone by stone — had dropped to a mutter. Waves still came, heavy and sullen, but they came with their heads down now, like men leaving a fight they had lost.

She trimmed the wick anyway. She polished the lens housing anyway. Routine was the rope she had tied around herself three years ago, and she did not let go of ropes.

From the gallery, the world was rinsed and strange. The sky in the east had gone the color of pewter rubbed with ash, and along the horizon ran a thin seam of light like the first cut of a knife into bread. The causeway was invisible beneath the tide, as it would be for hours yet, and beyond it the mainland hills stood blue-black and dripping under ragged cloud. Gulls wheeled over the harbor rocks, screaming their grievances at the morning, and somewhere among the drowned-sounding houses of the village a cock crowed, absurdly confident, as though the last two days had been nothing at all.

Ada counted, as she always counted after a storm. One chimney down at the Kessler house — no, half a chimney, leaning like a drunk against the gable. A boat gone from its moorings, or dragged to splinters; she could see the pale ribs of something on the sand below the point, and she thought it might be the *Petrel*, which meant old Anders would be up on the headland by now, staring at the wreck of his livelihood with his hat in his hands, saying nothing, because that was what men here said about loss. They said nothing beautifully.

She went down. She put the kettle on, and while it heated she stood at the window of the keeper's cottage with her hands wrapped around her elbows, watching the light strengthen over the water, and she told herself what she always told herself: check the oil stores, check the log, walk the seaward rocks before the tide turns, because storms leave gifts and storms leave wounds, and both must be found early or they fester.

Her husband Kenji had taught her that. *The sea is not cruel, Ada. It is only honest. It gives back exactly what it takes in, eventually, rearranged.* He had said it laughing, once, holding up a Japanese fishing float the storm had carried three thousand miles to their beach, its glass green and perfect. He had said it differently another time, not laughing at all, when the sea gave back what it took from him.

The kettle sang. She made tea she did not drink and set it on the sill to go cold, because her feet were already moving, already pulling her boots on, already reaching for the oilskin coat and the long hooked staff she used to test the rocks. Some mornings the body knows before the mind consents. Her body had known since the sea changed its voice.

* * *

The rocks below the lighthouse point were black and streaming, hung with weed like torn banners. Ada walked them slowly, testing each footing with the staff, her breath smoking in air that still had winter's edge in it despite the season. Storm wrack lay everywhere in windrows: kelp in glossy brown coils, driftwood, a fish crate staved open, a gull's corpse with its wings spread as if it had died mid-argument. She catalogued these things absently, the way a housekeeper catalogues dust. Nothing human. Nothing —

Then she saw the boot.

It was wedged in a crevice between two boulders where the retreating waves had left it, toe-down, heel-up, and for one merciful second she thought it was only a boot, one of the dozens the sea keeps in its cellar and brings out to play with. But the boot had a leg in it. The leg had trousers, sodden and gray with salt, and above the trousers a body lay folded against the rock in the posture of a man who had been thrown there and had not decided whether to continue existing.

Ada did not run. Running on wet rock killed more people than drowning did — Kenji had drilled that into the whole island's children — but every step of the careful descent felt like wading through syrup, time gone thick and slow around her. Up close, the man was worse than the shape had promised. Young, perhaps thirty, though the sea had scrubbed ten years onto him overnight. His lips were the blue of slate. His skin was bloodless, pruned white at the hands, and his clothes were wrong for these waters — a city coat, good wool ruined past saving, and under it the ghost of finer tailoring than any fisherman's widow ever buried her men in. His right hand was locked around something at his chest, fingers frozen into a fist that no cold alone could have made. He had held on. Whatever it was, he had held on through all of it.

She knelt and put two fingers to his throat, expecting the answer the color of him had already given.

The pulse came back to her fingertips, faint and staggered, like a man climbing stairs in the dark. Alive. Barely, grudgingly, but alive.

"Hey." She chafed his wrist, hard, the way you worked feeling back into a frozen line. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyelids moved. Under them his eyes rolled, hunting, and then cracked open — and what was in them stopped Ada's breath. Not confusion. Drowned men looked confused, looked at her with the animal puzzlement of creatures hauled back from a door they'd already gone through. This man looked at her with recognition. Worse: with relief. His mouth worked, jaw shivering, salt and something darker crusted at the corner of it, and the sound that came out was barely a sound at all, a scraping like wind through a keyhole.

But she heard it. Three syllables, shaped with the last of everything he had.

"*Ishikawa.*"

The name went through her like a nail through pine. Nobody called her that anymore — nobody had called her that since Kenji died and the town, in its kindness, had folded her back into itself as Mrs. Dunmore-Ishikawa, then just the Keeper, then Ada. It was a name from ledgers and licenses, from the capital registry where her marriage certificate lived in a drawer beside ten thousand others. Strangers knew that name. Paper knew that name.

"How do you—" She stopped, swallowed, started again, louder, closer to his ear. "Who sent you? Who are you? Look at me."

He was looking at her. That was the terrible thing — even sinking back into whatever gray country waited behind his eyes, he kept his gaze fixed on her face as if she were the last fixed star in a spinning sky, as if he had crossed the entire black sea on the strength of this one appointment. His fist tightened around the object at his chest. Oilskin. She could see it now: a packet, flat, wrapped and tied, the kind of wrapping the government offices used for documents they didn't trust to ordinary paper.

"Must—" he said, and coughed, and the cough bent him double with a wet tearing sound she did not like at all. Blood showed bright at his lip. "Before— signing. Before they—"

"Stop talking." Command came easier than fear; she let it. Twenty years of keeping a lamp lit taught a woman to give orders to the dark and expect obedience. "You're on Paloma Island. You're alive. Nothing else matters until you're warm. Do you hear me? Nothing else."

Something in him wanted to argue — she could see the argument forming behind his eyes, urgent, enormous, a whole speech dying for breath — but his body had finished negotiating. His eyes rolled back. His grip on the oilskin packet did not loosen by a finger's width. And then he simply poured himself out against the rock, unconscious, breathing in shallow rattling pulls, alive the way an ember is alive.

Ada sat back on her heels on the streaming rock with the dawn coming up gray behind her and understood, distantly, that her ordered life had just broken along some seam she hadn't known was there, the way ice breaks — silently first, then all at once.

* * *

Getting him up the path was the hardest work she had done since the night she'd kept the lamp turning through the gale that took the *Marguerite*, and she did not let herself think about that night, or about why the comparison arrived so easily. She got her arms under his shoulders and walked backward, dragging him inch by inch up the goat track, pausing at each turn to listen to his breathing, which stayed — stubbornly, improbably — present. The oilskin packet rode against his chest between them, and twice she felt his hand twitch and tighten over it even in unconsciousness, guarding it the way a sleeping man guards a wound.

Halfway up she met the tide's reminder: a wave surged higher than the rest, sheeting across the low rocks and soaking them both to the waist, cold enough to make her gasp. The sea giving back, and taking its toll for the delivery. She laughed — a short, wild sound she didn't recognize as her own — and kept hauling.

By the time she got him through the cottage door, the sky had gone fully pale and the kettle, forgotten on the stove, had boiled itself dry. She built the fire up fresh, dragged the daybed close to it, stripped the stranger out of his ruined coat — working the rigid fist loose from the fabric with the patience of a woman who had mended nets in January — and wrapped him in every blanket she owned. His skin was cold as clay and would not warm. She heated bricks, she rubbed his limbs, she forced weak broth between his teeth and fought down the panic when he choked, and all the while the oilskin packet lay on the table where she'd finally pried it from him, tied with waxed cord, sealed with a stamp she did not recognize, looking for all the world like a piece of the storm that had blown in with the rest of the wrack.

She did not touch it.

That was discipline too. She had learned, in three years of small necessary silences, exactly how far her hands could reach before they pulled down things she couldn't put back up. The packet was his business until he woke and made it hers. She set it on the high shelf above the stove, where the heat would keep damp out of it, and she turned away from it deliberately, and she failed, and turned back, and looked at it again, and turned away again.

Outside, the island was waking. She heard doors bang along the village lane, heard Anders' raw silence carrying even from the headland, heard children being told to stay away from the rocks. By noon someone would come up the path — Maren, most likely, with bread and questions in equal measure, because Maren had made it her ministry to check that the Keeper ate. What would Ada tell her? A drowned man, alive, nameless, calling her by a name out of a ledger?

She looked at the bed. The stranger's breathing had steadied somewhat, deepened, taken on the slow rhythm of true sleep rather than the shallow fluttering of the nearly gone. Color had come back into his face — not health, but the memory of it. His fist, even freed from the coat, lay curled on the blanket as though still closed around the weight it had carried across the sea.

And under the bruising and the salt-scour, studying his face properly for the first time in daylight, Ada felt the second shock of the morning arrive quietly, like water rising in a hold. There was something familiar in the lines of him. Not his face — she had never seen his face. His manner. The way he lay even in sleep, guarded, precise, a clerk's economy in the very bones of him. A man from offices. A man from files and registries and stamped seals. A man from the same world that held her marriage certificate in its drawer.

Paper had washed up on her rocks wearing a man's body, and it had spoken her name.

The fire popped. Down in the village the bell at the mission began to ring the hour, eight slow strokes, and with the last stroke Ada heard footsteps on the gravel path outside — quick, light, unfamiliar, not Maren's deliberate tread. She straightened, smoothed her apron, arranged her face into the calm the island expected of its Keeper, and opened the door on a young woman she did not know, soaked to the knees, breathless, with eyes red-rimmed and desperate.

"Please," the girl said, before Ada could speak. "They said — the men on the point said — a body came ashore. Is it my father?"

Behind Ada, in the warm room, the stranger slept on, silent, holding nothing now but his own life. And Ada, standing in her doorway with the truth and the lie equally available to her tongue, reached — as she had reached so many times in three years — for the small silence that kept people safe.

"No," she said gently. "Come in out of the cold, child, and tell me your father's name."

But even as she spoke, her eyes went past the girl's shoulder, down toward the rocks, where the tide was turning — and where, far off along the cliff path from the mainland side of the bay, a figure was picking its way across the stones at low water, a man in a city coat walking fast against all sense, as though he had been waiting two days for the sea to open a door.
