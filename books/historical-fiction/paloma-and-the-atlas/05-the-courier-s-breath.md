## 5. The Courier's Breath

Tomas Dunmore had been on the island four days before he understood that he was not visiting it at all. He was being kept by it.

His father had arranged the journey with the same brisk efficiency he brought to sentencing — a letter to the ferry office, a purse of coins, a word of instruction delivered over breakfast without once meeting his son's eyes. *Go and see how your mother's people lived. It will do you good.* As if the island were a tonic, a bitter draught to be swallowed for the improvement of the constitution, rather than the place where his mother had been born and from which she had fled at nineteen with nothing but a name she never used again.

So Tomas had come, against his own will and everyone else's expectations, to a rock in a grey sea where the wind spoke a dialect he half-remembered from lullabies, and where he was known — he could feel it in every sideways glance at the inn, in the too-long pauses of the fishmonger's wife — as *the magistrate's boy*. Not a man of twenty-six with a clerkship in the capital and a future in the law. Only that: the magistrate's boy, come home like a debt.

He spent his mornings walking the causeway road until the tide turned him back, and his afternoons in the tavern pretending to read while he listened to the island talk around him. It talked in omissions. He had grown up among lawyers; he knew the architecture of silence when he heard it, the way a room is built around a thing no one will name. The islanders did not speak of the winter of 1916. They did not speak of the *Paloma*, or of her sister boats, or of why three family names appeared on no leases and no gravestones and no parish rolls, as though the sea had taken not only the men but the paper trail of their having ever existed.

He was thinking of exactly this — standing at the window of the tavern's back room on the fourth morning, watching gulls argue over the tideline — when the shouting started.

* * *

It came up from the rocks below the light station, thin and urgent, thinned further by wind. A woman's voice. He knew it by now; four days had been enough to learn the keeper's voice the way one learns a bell — its pitch, its carrying power, its habit of cutting through weather. Ada Ishikawa did not shout often. When she shouted, things were wrong.

Tomas took the stairs two at a time and went out into a morning so bright it felt accusatory. The storm had passed in the night, leaving the world scoured and glittering, the causeway stones dark with weed and running water, and below the seaward wall of the light station —

There was a man on the rocks.

He lay half in the reach of the surf, on his side, one arm flung out across the stone as if he had been reaching for something even as the sea gave him up. His clothes were wrong for a fisherman: dark wool, city-cut, soaked through and plastered to a body too thin for them. His boots were good boots. His hands —

Tomas came down the wet steps faster than sense allowed, and his foot went out from under him on a sheet of kelp, and he caught himself on the wall and kept going, because he had seen the hands. One of them still gripped a strap. An oilskin packet, tied flat against the man's chest under his coat, the way couriers carried things in the capital — the way Tomas had seen a hundred clerks carry a hundred packets down the archive corridors, documents too valuable to trust to the post.

The woman was already there. She had the man turned onto his back, her fingers pressed hard into the cold flesh of his throat, her ear to his lips, and when Tomas reached her she said, without looking up, "Help me lift him. On three."

They got him up between them, an awkward deadweight that was not quite dead — there was breath in him somewhere, a wet rattling sound like water finding its way through a broken pipe. They hauled him up the steps and into the station's lower room, where the stove still held heat from the night watch, and Ada stripped off his coat with quick, unsentimental hands while Tomas knelt and chafed at limbs gone grey-cold.

"Get the brandy," she said. "Second shelf, behind the lamp oil. Don't ask."

He didn't ask. When he came back she had the packet free — he saw her set it aside, deliberately, on the high shelf above the stove, out of the wet — and she was forcing brandy past the stranger's cracked lips in small, measured sips, her hand cradling the back of his skull with a tenderness that seemed, from her, almost startling.

And then the man opened his eyes.

They were brown, fever-bright, unfocused — swimming up from somewhere deep. His gaze drifted past Ada, past the low rafters, past everything in the room, and fixed on Tomas with the terrible precision of the drowning who have decided what they must say before they die.

"You're—" he rasped. Water and brandy. "You're Voss's son."

The room went very small.

Tomas felt it happen — felt the walls draw in, the firelight flatten, his own heartbeat become an audible thing. Because he knew the face. He knew it the way you know a face seen a hundred times in a corridor and never once thought of since: the pale, ink-stained men of the Records Annex, third sub-basement of the Ministry of Justice, where certified copies lived in their thousands behind iron doors and clerks moved among the shelves like patient moths.

"Reyes," Tomas said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Lucan Reyes."

The man's mouth shaped something that might have been relief or might have been terror. "You know me." A bubble of blood-tinted froth at the corner of his lips. "Good. Good — then you know what I am, and you know—"

"Hush." Ada's hand stayed steady on his forehead. But her eyes had come up, and they were fixed on Tomas now, and they were not warm. "You know this man?"

"I've seen him in the archives." Tomas heard himself answering in the flat tone of deposition. "Records Annex. Third sub-basement." He swallowed. "My father's ministry."

Something moved behind Ada Ishikawa's eyes — not surprise; calculation, swift and cold as a gull turning into wind. She looked from the man on her floor to the son of the magistrate, and back, and Tomas watched her arrive at some conclusion and lock it away behind her face.

"And what does a records clerk carry," she said quietly, "that brings him across forty miles of open sea in November?"

Reyes tried to laugh. It became a cough that bent him nearly double, and Ada held him through it, and when it passed he sagged against her arm, spent, whispering.

"The packet. You have the packet."

"On the shelf," she said.

"Don't—" His fingers clawed weakly at her sleeve. "Don't open it near a window. Don't let anyone see. If they find it on me — if they find me —" Another shudder went through him, and his teeth began to chatter, the deep mechanical chatter of a body surrendering its warmth. "Voss's son. Listen. Your father signed the orders. Three families. He erased them. I have the proof — certified, sealed, dated — I have all of it—"

"Stop." Tomas's voice cracked on the word. He was aware, distantly, of the absurd domestic details of the scene — the kettle ticking on the stove, the smell of paraffin and salt, a gull crying somewhere beyond the thick stone walls — while the floor of his life tilted beneath him. "You're fevered. You don't know what you're saying."

"I counted the pages." Reyes's eyes found his and held them with a lucidity more frightening than any delirium. "Fourteen pages. I sealed them myself. I carried them eleven days. Ask me anything. Ask me the file numbers."

And God help him, Tomas believed him. That was the horror of it — not doubt but belief, arriving whole and unwelcome, because he had spent his childhood learning to read his father the way other children learned to read weather, and he had always known — had always *known* and never once permitted himself to think — that the great calm machine of the magistrate's court produced its verdicts the way the sea produced drowned men: inevitably, and without malice, and with no record of what it consumed.

Ada rose. She crossed to the shelf, took down the oilskin packet, weighed it in her hand — and Tomas saw her thumb find the seal, find the wax stamp pressed into it, and go still.

He knew that stamp. Everyone in the capital knew it. The seal of the Special Commission on Coastal Inheritance, emblazoned with the double anchor of the admiralty courts.

"My husband," Ada said. Her voice was level as a spirit gauge. "Was one of the men your father erased?"

The question was aimed at Reyes, but her eyes never left Tomas.

"I don't know," Tomas said. And then, because lying to this woman felt suddenly more dangerous than any truth: "I don't know. But I believe this man knows."

Reyes made a sound — half sob, half laugh. "Ishikawa," he whispered. "Daniel Ishikawa. *Paloma*. Lost with all hands, January 1916. File 447-C. The claim was filed by his widow in March. It was marked —" his voice frayed, and he gathered himself, and finished in a thread of sound — "marked *withdrawn*. Her mark. A mark she never made."

The stove ticked. Outside, the tide was coming back over the causeway, filling the island's moat with cold grey water, sealing them all in together.

Ada set the packet down on the table between them, softly, as though it were something sleeping. Then she looked at Tomas Dunmore — the magistrate's son, stranded by the tide in a room with a dying witness and fourteen pages of his father's crimes — and said:

"Then we'd best decide, quickly, whose side of the water you're standing on."

* * *

They did not speak again for an hour. There was work enough to fill the silence: dry blankets, heated stones wrapped in flannel, broth spooned carefully into a mouth too weak to do more than swallow. Tomas performed his tasks like a man in a dream, moving where Ada pointed, fetching what she named, hyperaware at every moment of her watching him — not hostilely, he thought, but the way a surveyor watches new ground, measuring what it will bear.

Reyes sank in and out of consciousness. In the gaps between waking he muttered — file numbers, dates, the names of streets in the capital, once, unbearably, a snatch of some child's song. Twice he called out a name that was not a name Tomas recognized: *"Mirela"* — and each time his face wore an expression that Tomas recognized instantly, because he had worn it himself for years in front of his father: the look of a person apologizing to someone who cannot hear.

Near midday Ada sat down across the table from Tomas, poured two cups of tea without asking whether he wanted one, and pushed one toward him.

"Tell me about the archives," she said.

It was not a question. He drank, buying time, and then set the cup down and told her — because there seemed nothing left in him that wanted to withhold. The Records Annex. The iron doors. The registry system his father had helped design after the drownings, ostensibly to protect coastal claims from loss — *to protect them*, his father had said at the dinner table, in that reasonable voice, and the young Tomas had nodded, proud. How certified copies required a countersignature from the commission. How the commission answered, ultimately, to one man.

"And the special courts," Ada said. "The ones hearing claims now."

"Dissolve at the armistice signing." He heard the words leave his mouth and understood their weight only as he said them. "Eleven weeks. Maybe less. After ratification, jurisdiction reverts to the provincial courts, and the provincial courts—" He stopped.

"Were appointed by my father," he finished, quietly. "Yes."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Steam rose between them. Somewhere overhead, in the lamp room, the clockwork of the light turned through its slow revolutions, keeping time with the patience of machines.

"When Lucan can speak again," she said at last, "he'll tell us what has to be done. Until then, you'll stay here. Not because I trust you." She met his eyes without apology. "Because the tide doesn't care who I trust, and neither does the storm season. The bridge closes soon. We're all on this rock together now."

She stood, gathered the cups, paused at the door with her back to him.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning, "you didn't defend him just now. Most sons would have."

Then she was gone, up the spiral stair, and Tomas sat alone in the lower room with the fire ticking and the stranger breathing in shallow, laboring pulls on the cot beside him.

After a while he realized the breathing had changed — quicker, thinner, catching at the top of each pull like a saw biting into green wood. He leaned close. Reyes's eyes were open again, fixed on the ceiling, and his lips were moving.

"What is it?" Tomas asked. "What do you need?"

Reyes's hand crept out from under the blanket and closed on Tomas's wrist — grip astonishingly strong for a body so far spent, the grip of a man holding on to a spar.

"Eleven weeks," he whispered. "But not for us. For me — less." A flicker of terrible humor crossed the ruined face. "The sea started collecting early."

"Save your strength—"

"No. Listen." He pulled Tomas closer, and his breath was hot and smelled of brine and blood. "They know I took them. They'll have wired the mainland stations. Every gate, every ferry point, every checkpoint between here and the capital — they'll be looking for a records clerk." His eyes bored into Tomas's. "But no one is looking for the magistrate's son. No one would dare."

The words hung in the lamplit air between them, and Tomas felt them settle over his shoulders like a coat cut precisely to his size.

"Get them to the capital," Reyes breathed. "Before the signing. Before the courts close. Promise me — promise me you'll —"

The fever took him then, dragging him under mid-sentence, his hand sliding from Tomas's wrist to fall open on the blanket, palm up, empty.

Outside, the afternoon light was failing early, the way it did in November, and far out on the grey water the first white horses of the next blow were already showing. Tomas sat in the gathering dark and listened to the stranger breathe, each ragged pull counting itself down like a clock winding toward zero, and understood at last why his father had sent him here.

Not to see how his mother's people lived.

To be out of the capital — out of sight — while whatever was coming, came.
