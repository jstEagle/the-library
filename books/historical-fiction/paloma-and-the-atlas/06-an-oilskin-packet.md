## 6. An Oilskin Packet

*Ada*

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and cold iron, and beneath both — faint but unmistakable now that the door was shut against the wind — the smell of the sea's oldest cargo: a man who had been under and come back.

Lucan Reyes lay in my bed below, wrapped in every blanket the island owned, breathing in shallow pulls like a man counting coins he was afraid to spend. Tomas had not left his side since dawn. I had climbed to the lamp room because someone had to keep the light burning, and because I could not watch another person die slowly without doing something with my hands.

But it was not the dying man who occupied my thoughts. It was the packet.

It sat on the chart table where Tomas had placed it an hour ago, an oilskin roll tied with waxed cord, dark as a heart removed from a chest. Oilskin is honest material. It is made for one purpose only — to keep water from what must stay dry — and everything wrapped in it is by definition something precious, something meant to survive drowning. I had wrapped my husband's letters in oilskin when the damp came into the keeper's cottage walls. I had wrapped the lamp's spare wicks in it every winter. And now this stranger had crossed half a drowned sea carrying a roll of it, and the sea had taken everything else from him — his boat, his coat, most of his blood — and left him only this.

I did not touch it. Not at first. I stood at the window instead, watching the causeway disappear under the grey swell, watching the gates on the far shore stand chained and small as toys, and I thought: we are sealed in here with a dead man's errand.

The wind found some gap in the glazing and made a thin whistle of it, high and steady, like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

In the end it was practicality that moved me, the same practicality that has kept this lamp lit through nineteen winters. Paper rots. Paper molds. If the packet held what Tomas feared it held, then every hour it spent in a cold, damp tower was an hour it was dying. So I untied the cord — the knot swollen, needing my thumbnail and patience — and unrolled the oilskin on the chart table, weighting the corners with the brass weights I use for holding charts flat in wind.

Certified copies. That was the first thing I understood, before I read a single word. The stamps told me. Three different archives, three different registrar seals, one of them the capital's own great seal pressed deep enough to leave its mirror in the page beneath. These were not rumors. These were not a dying man's grievances. Someone had gone into the records offices with lawful authority and copied out the truth, page by page, and certified each copy so that no court in the country could call it forgery.

And the truth they certified was this: that after the drownings of '16 — the winter the *Paloma Maid*, the *Sister Ann*, and the *Kestrel* went down within nine days of each other, the winter that took eleven men off this coast and left this island full of widows — the inheritance claims of three families had been struck from the rolls.

Not lost. Struck. There is a difference, and the difference was written here in a clerk's careful hand: *entry voided by order of the magistrate's court, jurisdiction claimed under emergency statute.* Dated the March after the drownings. Signed.

I knew the names on those pages the way you know the names of the dead — which is to say completely and helplessly. Maren Voss. The Brandt fishing shares — no relation to the sergeant, though the coincidence sat strangely in my mouth. Old Osla's sons. Their fields, their boats, their shares in the salt works, all of it entered in the rolls for generations, and then all of it entered as *void*, as if the sea had taken not only the men but the very fact that their families had ever owned anything at all.

I have read death notices. I have read the casualty lists the war sends us, columns of names printed in ink too thin. But I had never read a document whose whole purpose was to make living people into nobodies. The paper was light. Three pages, four, five — I could have lifted them with one finger. They weighed nothing.

They weighed more than the lens above my head. More than the iron gallery rail. I stood there holding five sheets of paper and my arms ached as if I'd been hauling chain.

Below me, through the floorboards, I heard Tomas moving — the chair scrape, the wet cloth wrung out, the small sounds of tending. And beneath those sounds, once, his voice, low, saying something I couldn't catch. Speaking to Lucan, or perhaps to himself, or perhaps to a father two miles away across the water who had signed his name at the bottom of these pages in ink that was surely dry by now, dry as bone.

I rolled the oilskin closed again, retied the cord, and set the packet back where Tomas had left it. Then I went down the stairs to sit with the dying man and the magistrate's son, because whatever this paper was going to cost us, the paying had already begun.

* * *

*Tomas*

My father's signature I would know anywhere. He taught me penmanship himself, at this same table in another life — *the downstroke firm, Tomas, the upstroke light; a signature is a promise made visible* — and there it was, made visible, at the foot of the third certification: **Aldous Vane, Magistrate, District of Paloma Reach.**

I had read the pages twice before Ada came down. I read them a third time while she sat with Lucan and I pretended to change the fevered man's compress, because reading them felt like a duty I could not put down, the way you keep touching a broken tooth.

The documents were orderly. That was the worst of it. No drunken scrawl, no desperate cover-up done in a night. This was my father's work at its finest — methodical, patient, legally immaculate. Emergency statute invoked. Jurisdiction assumed over "maritime estates in probate limbo." The claims of the drowned transferred to custodial administration pending resolution, and then — six months later, quietly, in a supplementary order — resolved. Leased. Assigned. The Voss salt fields to a cousin. The boat shares absorbed into the district fund, which was administered by — I checked the name twice, hoping I was wrong — also a cousin.

Three families erased in perfect handwriting.

I had told myself, on the crossing out from the mainland, that I did not know what my father was. Estrangement is a soft word and it lets you keep your ignorance intact. We had not spoken properly in four years; I had built my life in the capital archives among other people's records precisely because I did not want to know what lived in ours. And now a half-drowned clerk had carried my ignorance across the sea in an oilskin packet and laid it on a chart table for me to unwrap.

"Is it what you thought?" Ada asked. She was wringing a cloth into the basin, her back to me, and her voice was level in the way I was learning was not calm at all but discipline — grief and anger poured into a mold and cooled until they took an edge.

"It's worse than I thought," I said. "I thought maybe pressure. Influence. A judge leaning on clerks." I smoothed the top page though it didn't need smoothing. "This is construction. This took months. There are cross-references."

Lucan stirred at the sound of our voices. His eyes opened partway, unfocused, finding the ceiling beams. "You've read it," he whispered. It wasn't quite a question.

"We've read it," Ada said.

"Then it's alive now." He tried to lift his head; Ada's hand went to his shoulder and pressed him gently back down, and he let her, which told me something about how much strength he had left. "Paper in a drawer is nothing. Paper in a drawer is just paper. But read—" a breath, ragged, "—read paper is testimony. You're witnesses now. Both of you. That's how it works. That's the only way it works."

"You should rest," I said.

"I should be dead," he said, almost pleasantly. "Rest is for people with time." His eyes moved from me to Ada and back, and even fevered there was a clerk's precision in them, an accounting look. "The magistrate's son and the lighthouse keeper. Do you know what the odds were? Of the packet reaching exactly the two people in this district with reason and means? I didn't plan it. I want that understood. I planned a boat and a tide and a courier's route. The rest—" his hand twitched toward the ceiling, toward the storm still worrying the tower, "—the rest was arranged by something with a sense of humor."

Ada wrung the cloth again. Water dripped into the basin, each drop distinct.

"There will be questions," she said. "When this gets out. People will ask why we waited. Why we didn't shout it from the causeway the day the storm chained us in."

"Because the causeway is chained," I said, "and shouting at the sea accomplishes nothing," and she nodded, but I saw that her answer and mine were different answers wearing the same clothes. Hers was about timing. Mine was about fear.

Lucan had closed his eyes again, but his voice kept coming, thin as the wind through the glazing upstairs. "Eleven days. Maybe twelve. When the armistice terms are ratified, the special courts dissolve. Every order signed under emergency statute gets reviewed — or buried, depending on who does the reviewing. If these pages reach the capital archive before the ratification, they enter the record of the old courts, and the old courts' record can't be amended afterward. Sealed forever. My father—" he coughed, corrected himself with a ghost of a smile, "—my *supervisor*, the chief registrar, used to say: paper is patient, but jurisdictions are not. Get it in before the door closes or it never goes in at all."

"And if it doesn't arrive?" Ada asked.

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd slipped into sleep. Then: "Then three families stay erased until everyone who remembers them is dead. And that's not a long wait anymore. Look around this island. Count the gray heads." His eyes opened, fixed on Ada with sudden terrible clarity. "You keep the light so ships don't die on these rocks. This is the same work. Different rocks."

Ada set down the cloth. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, in the voice she probably uses on sailors who argue with weather: "Then we'll need to know exactly where this archive stands, which doors open onto it, and who stands behind them. Because between here and that stamp there are three checkpoints, a frozen causeway, and every man my husband ever pulled out of the water — and every man he didn't."

She said it plainly, without drama, the way she says everything, and I understood that she had already decided. Perhaps she had decided the moment she cut the cord. Perhaps years ago, in some silence I knew nothing about.

I looked down at my father's signature, firm downstrokes, light upstrokes, a promise made visible.

I had not yet decided anything. But I noticed that my hand had moved, without instruction, to cover the name — the way you shield a flame from wind, or the way you hide a wound.

* * *

*Ada*

Evening came early, as it does after storms, the light failing sideways out of the west. I sent Tomas up to trim the wick and mind the mechanism — he was clumsy at it, but clumsiness up there is safe, the clockwork forgiving — and I stayed below with the packet open on my knees for the second time that day.

Reading it again changed nothing in it and everything in me.

The first time, I had read it as a keeper reads a weather report: facts, figures, the shape of the danger. Now, in the lamplight, with Lucan's breath rattling slow behind me, I read it as the widow I am, and the pages turned personal in a way I hadn't braced for. Because I remembered that winter of '16. I remember standing in this same lamp room burning oil past regulation, keeping the light up through the worst of it, and I remember the boats going out anyway, because the season was short and the rent was owed and hungry men do not respect forecasts. I remember Maren Voss coming up the causeway path three days after the *Sister Ann* failed to return, her coat still dripping, asking me — asking *me*, as if the sea reported to me — whether I had seen anything. Whether the light had been bright enough.

Whether it was her fault, for not making her husband stay ashore.

I told her the light had been bright. That was true. What I did not tell her — what none of us told any of them — was that the light could not have been brighter, that the sea that night was a wall of black water moving at the speed of a train, and that no lamp ever built would have brought those boats home. I let her carry the question because answering it honestly would have meant telling her that her husband's death was arithmetic, not sin. Some mercies are lies. I have told so many of them I could bind them into a book.

But this — these pages — this was the opposite kind of lie. This was a lie told not to soften a blow but to steal from the fallen. And the people who told it had signed their names.

I thought about Maren's face when she learns. Because she will learn; secrets of this size never stay wrapped. I thought about Osla, who has not spoken a full sentence to anyone since her sons went down. I thought about the men on the mainland side, the prosperous ones, the ones leasing the dead men's fields and mooring their own boats in the dead men's berths — comfortable men, fathers themselves, who will hear this news and feel the ground tilt under their kitchens.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, I thought about Sergeant Brandt at the ferry gate, and the debt he carries, and whether a debt twenty years old can still bear weight.

Tomas came down the stair, rubbing lamp soot from his fingers. He looked at the packet on my knees, then at my face, and whatever he saw there made him sit down slowly on the bench across from me.

"My father will offer money first," he said. "Then threats. Then, if he's clever — and he is — he'll offer something that looks like justice. A hearing. A review board. Anything to get the papers into a room he controls."

"He doesn't know yet."

"He will. Lucan didn't steal these alone — he thinks he covered himself, but requisition logs, archive keys, the capital is a sieve. Within a week, maybe less, there'll be a letter on a boat, or a warrant if it goes badly." He paused. "There's a copy of everything, isn't there? In the archive at the capital. The originals."

"The originals exist," Lucan murmured from the bed, without opening his eyes. "Under seal. Which is why copies matter — sealed things can be *un*-sealed, quietly, by the wrong hands. Certified copies in a public filing can't vanish. That's the whole design." A breath. "I designed it. Give the clerk credit. I designed it so that killing me wouldn't work."

The words settled over the room like frost.

Outside, the wind had backed around to the north, and the north wind in December carries ice in it the way a net carries fish. I rose, folded the oilskin around the pages, and knotted the cord — twice this time, the way my husband taught me for knots that must hold through weather.

"Then we keep you alive," I said, "and we keep this dry, and when the causeway opens — if it opens — we move. Until then, nobody speaks of this. Not to the gulls, not to God."

Tomas looked at me. "And when someone asks? This island eats rumors for breakfast."

"Then we feed it a better rumor first," I said, and heard my own voice take on a tone I recognized from my mother, from every woman on this coast who has ever managed a community the way you manage a fire — by knowing which way the wind sits. "Let them say the stranger is a revenue officer blown off course, checking lamp permits. Boring rumors starve. Let them chew on that."

Lucan laughed — a small, cracked sound that turned into coughing, and cost him visibly. But when it passed he was smiling at the ceiling.

"A lighthouse keeper," he said. "Smuggling truth inside a boring rumor. You'd have made a fine archivist."

"No," I said, banking the stove for the night, watching the coals settle into their red patience. "Archivists keep paper safe. Keepers keep *people* safe. It's a different trade."

I climbed the stairs to make the midnight winding, and through the glass the beam swept out over the black water, once every seven seconds, faithful as a pulse — over the drowned causeway, over the chained gates, over the dark miles between this rock and the capital where a registrar's stamp waited like a verdict not yet spoken.

Seven seconds. Seven seconds. Somewhere out in that darkness, eleven days were already bleeding away, and the paper in the oilskin downstairs weighed on the island like ballast, like keel, like the thing that would either steady us or drag us under.
