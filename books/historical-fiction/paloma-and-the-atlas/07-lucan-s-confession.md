## 7. Lucan's Confession

Fever does not come like a storm. It comes like a tide.

Lucan Reyes learned this in the grey hours of his second night on Paloma, lying on a straw pallet in the keeper's back room while the sea spoke its one long syllable against the rocks below. The cold had entered him first — that he remembered clearly, the cold of the water, the cold of the dark, the cold that had climbed into his bones somewhere between the mainland lights going out and the island's lamp finding him — and now the heat had come to answer it, and the two of them were arguing over the territory of his body like rival claimants to a drowned estate.

He drifted. He surfaced. He drifted again.

When he surfaced, there was always the same ceiling above him: whitewashed beams gone amber with lamp-oil smoke, and hanging from one of them a bundle of dried kelp that turned slowly, slowly, as though the room itself were afloat. There was always the smell — salt, tallow, camphor rubbed into his chest by hands that were not gentle but were not unkind. And there was always, at the edge of his hearing, the sound of a woman moving through the house with the particular economy of someone who has lived alone a long time and has made an art of not wasting a single motion.

The keeper. Ada Ishikawa. He had said her name on the rocks. He remembered that much, or believed he did — a name torn out of him by the last of his strength, because a dying man spends what he has left on the only thing that matters.

*Deliver it to the keeper. Deliver it before the signing.*

He had rehearsed those words for eleven days. On the train south he had mouthed them against the window glass. In the fishing boat whose skipper had taken half his savings and all his dignity to carry him past the first patrol line, he had whispered them to the bilge water. And when the storm took the boat apart off the island's northern teeth — when the mast came down and the sea stood up around him like a wall deciding to walk — he had held the oilskin packet above the water with both arms and shouted the words into the wind, though no one living could have heard him, because a man who has carried a thing that long cannot stop carrying it even when carrying it will kill him.

Now the packet was gone from his hands. That was the terror that woke him each time the fever let go. His hands kept searching the blanket for the stiff, waxy rectangle, the corner worn soft where his thumb had worried it across three hundred miles, and finding nothing they would begin to shake, and then a voice would come through the dark — low, unhurried, faintly accented in a way he could not place — saying: *It's here. It's safe. Sleep.*

The young man who had been sitting with him when he woke was another matter entirely.

Lucan knew his face. That was the second terror, the one that sat beside the first like a patient creditor. Fever made faces swim, but this one did not swim; it sharpened. A long jaw, a scholar's stoop already settling into shoulders too young for it, eyes the grey-green of old copper that looked at everything as if reading it for forgery. Lucan had seen those eyes across the reading tables of the capital archives four, five, six times over the years — a tall young man in a good coat copying land grants with the grim diligence of a man doing penance for his own name.

Dunmore. The magistrate's son. Tomas Dunmore, who had signed the visitors' ledger in a hand so controlled it might have been engraved, and who had once, without looking up from his papers, told a chattering clerk that *the dead do not need their files read aloud to them; they need them left intact.*

And now here was Tomas Dunmore in a fisherman's jersey, feeding a stove in a lighthouse keeper's kitchen, watching Lucan with an expression that was not quite recognition and not quite denial — the expression of a man standing on a bridge between two countries and unable to decide which side to fall on.

* * *

On the third day the fever broke its worst crest, and Lucan began to talk.

He did not decide to talk. It was more that the dam, which he had built plank by plank across eleven days and three hundred miles, simply gave way — and everything behind it came out in a flood that was part confession, part delirium, part the plain mechanical relief of a body that had been clenched too long.

Ada Ishikawa sat by the pallet with a basin of cooled water. Tomas stood in the doorway with his arms folded, as though he might still stopper the flood by force of posture alone. Outside, the wind had settled into the long steady moan of a storm that intended to stay the winter, and the lamp overhead turned and turned, sweeping its blade of light across the ceiling like a slow pendulum counting down something none of them could see.

"My name," Lucan said, and stopped, because his voice came out as gravel. Ada lifted his head and gave him water, and he drank like a man who had forgotten water existed, and began again.

"My name is Lucan Reyes. I am — I was — a junior clerk in the Office of Records and Registry, third division, lands and inheritances." He laughed, a dry cracked sound with no humor in it. "Third division. Do you know what the third division is? It is where paper goes to be forgotten. Every office has one. The capital has a whole wing of them. Rooms where the claims of people who cannot afford advocates go to sleep in boxes, and the dust settles on them like snow, and every few years a fire is conveniently discovered to have damaged certain folios beyond recovery."

Tomas shifted in the doorway. "I know the third division," he said quietly.

"I know you know it." Lucan's eyes found him, fever-bright and merciless. "I used to watch you copy grants, Master Dunmore. You copied them like a priest copies scripture. I thought: here is a man who believes the paper means something. I thought: if only he knew what his father's office does to the paper after dark."

"Tomas—" Ada began.

"No." Lucan pushed himself up on one elbow, and the effort cost him visibly; sweat sprang out along his hairline and his breath went thin. But he kept talking, faster now, a man racing a tide he could feel at his heels. "Let me say it while I can say it. The fever takes the edges off things. Tomorrow I may wake and not remember which box, which folio, which date. You need it now, while it's hot in me."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again he seemed, for a moment, entirely lucid — a small precise man with ink-stained fingers surfacing out of the burning water of himself.

"Three families," he said. "Voss. Maren — the widow Maren's family, the elder line, Halvard Maren. And Osla Bren's sons' holdings, though she won't know yet that hers were touched. Three families lost to the drownings of — what year was it — you'll know better than I, Keeper, it was your coast — the year the boats went out and only some came home. Eleven men. Three households with no man left to stand in a courtroom and say *this field was my grandfather's.*"

"The winter of 'sixteen," Ada said. Her voice had gone very flat and very quiet, the voice of a woman holding a door shut against weather.

"The winter of 'sixteen," Lucan agreed. "And in the spring of 'seventeen, a petition of inheritance was filed on behalf of those estates — properly filed, witnessed, sealed. I have seen the original. I carried a certified copy of it inside this—" his hand fluttered toward his own chest, toward the ghost of the oilskin packet— "inside this, wrapped in oilskin, sewn into a coat lining, for eleven days. The petition was sound. The surveyor's marks were sound. The witnesses were fishermen who could barely write their names but could swear to a boundary stone they'd leaned against since boyhood. It should have taken a month to confirm. It should have been the easiest justice the courts ever did."

"And?" Tomas asked. His voice had changed. Lucan heard it change — heard the son's voice drain out of it and leave something older and colder underneath.

"And it never reached confirmation," Lucan said. "Because between filing and hearing, the file was withdrawn from the third division under a writ of administrative review — signed by the magistrate of this district. Your father, Master Dunmore. Withdrawn, re-catalogued, and re-entered under amended titles showing the lands as *escheat to the district* — abandoned, unclaimed, reverted — and then leased within the year. One parcel to a cousin of the magistrate's household. Another to a shipping concern with interests the magistrate shares. The third—" he paused, and his eyes went to Ada— "the third was folded into the light dues assessment. The lighthouse district. Your district, Keeper. They taxed the dead men's fields to pay for the lamp that watches over their graves."

The stove ticked. The lamp swept its light across the ceiling, once, twice. Somewhere below, the sea hit the causeway gates and fell back, hit and fell back, patient as a clerk waiting for a signature.

"Amended titles require countersignature," Tomas said at last. Each word came out separately, like stones laid across deep water. "A clerk doesn't amend a title. A clerk *witnesses* an amendment. Whose hand was on the register?"

"Yours will be easier to ask than mine," Lucan said. "But I'll tell you what I know. The counter-signature block carries the seal of the district magistrate and the initials *T.D.* in the margin — attesting witness. I have stared at those initials until they swam. They are not your hand, Master Dunmore. Anyone who knows your hand knows that. They are a very good imitation of it, done by someone who has access to your correspondence." He let that sit. "Which narrows the world considerably."

Tomas did not move. But Lucan, who had spent nine years learning to read the small tremors of powerful households, saw the color leave the young man's face the way tide leaves a sandbar — gradually, and then all at once.

* * *

They gave him broth. He slept. He woke in the dark hours and talked again, because the clock in his chest would not let him rest.

"You don't understand the deadline," he said to the darkness, and then Ada's candle appeared, and her face above it, and he understood that she had been sleeping in the chair and had risen at the first change in his breathing. "You don't understand. Everyone thinks the armistice is an ending. It isn't. It's a *door closing.*"

"Slowly," Ada said. "You're bleeding energy into the air. Slowly."

"There is no slowly." He gripped the blanket. "Listen to me. Listen. The special courts — the wartime tribunals, the emergency registries, the whole apparatus they built when the fighting started — they exist under instruments of emergency authority. When the armistice terms are ratified — and they will be ratified, the negotiators have already initialed the framework — every instrument of emergency authority lapses. Automatically. By its own terms. No repeal needed. The old courts reopen under the restored civil code, and the restored civil code has a beautiful clause, a *lovely* clause, about finality of registration." His laugh cracked in the middle. "Titles confirmed under the old regime become unchallengeable after ratification except by original fraud proven within ninety days — and the emergency records themselves get sealed, transferred, microfilmed, and eventually, in ten years or fifteen, pulped. Pulped, Keeper. I have seen the destruction schedules. Box numbers. Dates. My own handwriting is on some of them."

"So the documents must be filed," Ada said slowly, working it through, "before the signing."

"*Registered* before the signing," Lucan corrected fiercely. "Filed is nothing. Filed is a box in a basement. Registered — stamped into the roll, given a folio number, entered in the day-book — registered makes it *juridically alive.* A registered challenge survives the transition. An unregistered one dies with the court that would have heard it." He struggled upright, and Ada put a hand on his shoulder and he shook it off, or tried to; his arm had no conviction in it. "Eleven days. Perhaps fewer — the delegations are ahead of schedule, the newspapers are printing peace as if wishing could make it true. Eleven days, and then the door shuts, and everything my — everything the three of us bleed for this winter becomes *historical interest.* A footnote. Dust."

"Why you?" Ada asked. Not unkindly. The question of a practical woman: why does this weight sit on *you*?

Lucan lay back. For a long moment the only sound was the lamp mechanism overhead, the steady metronome click of the rotating works that Ada Ishikawa must wind every few hours, that had been wound faithfully through storms and griefs and wars by hands that never missed.

"Because I catalogued the originals," he said finally. "In the autumn, doing routine consolidation, I pulled the 'seventeen petition to verify cross-references, and I found the withdrawal writ, and I found the amended entries, and I understood — in about four seconds, it takes about four seconds, that's the terrible thing, understanding takes four seconds and forgetting takes a career — I understood what had been done. And I certified copies. Certified, sealed, countersigned by a notary who owes me money and hates the magistrate anyway. And then I sat on them for a month, because I am a coward with a pension." His voice frayed. "Then the destruction schedule crossed my desk with my own initials on the authorization line, and I understood that if I did not carry these papers out of that building, no one ever would — not in my lifetime, not in the drowned men's children's lifetimes. So I stole them. From my own office. With my own keys. And I ran."

"To a lighthouse," Tomas said from the doorway. "Three hundred miles, past three checkpoints, in the first storm of the winter. To a lighthouse."

"To the one address in the district," Lucan said, turning his burning eyes toward Ada, "that the magistrate cannot search. Think about it. A tidal island. A sealed causeway. A royal appointment — the keeper answers to the Trinity authority, not the district bench. He can lean on ferrymen and tollkeepers and sergeants. He cannot set foot on this rock without a warrant he hasn't got and a boat that won't cross. If I could reach the keeper of Paloma Light, I would be past his reach, and the papers would be past his reach, and we would have eleven days to get them from here to the registry in the capital." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, feverish and triumphant and exhausted all at once. "So I came to you, Mrs. Ishikawa. I'm sorry about the boat. I'm sorry about the swimming. I'm sorry I said your name on the rocks like a madman. But you were the plan. You were always the plan."

* * *

Later — how much later he could not say; the fever made clocks into opinions — he woke to voices in the next room, low and urgent, and being too weak to rise and too restless to sleep, he listened.

"—eleven days," the woman was saying. "And a causeway that won't take a cart until the ice sets, if it sets. And three checkpoints between here and the capital, and every one of them flying his colors."

"And a dying courier," the son added, "who can't sit a horse, let alone survive questioning."

"He's not dying."

"He's dying, Mrs. Ishikawa. I've watched men die in the archives — the old registrars, coughing their lives out over the ledgers. I know the sound of it. He's further along than he lets you see."

Silence. Then Ada's voice again, quieter, and Lucan strained after it like a drowning man after a rope:

"Then we move sooner, and we move carefully. There's a sergeant at the mainland gate. Brandt. He—" a pause, the sound of a woman deciding how much truth a doorway can hold— "he knew my husband. There may be a debt there."

*There may be a debt there.* Lucan closed his eyes and let the words wash over him, and in his fevered arithmetic they were the sweetest numbers he had heard in eleven days: a gate, a name, a debt. Debts were the only currency that had never once failed him. Men forged seals and burned folios and falsified the holdings of the dead, but a debt — a true debt, owed in the old coin of a life for a life — that no magistrate could amend, no writ could withdraw, no armistice could void.

He slept, and dreamed of the archive basement, endless aisles of boxes receding into the dark, and somewhere in the dream a clock was striking, and it struck eleven, and then it began striking twelve, and he woke with his heart hammering and the word already in his mouth:

"How many days?"

From the doorway, Tomas Dunmore answered him, and his voice was the voice of a man who had just finished reading his own sentence:

"Ten. Ten days, Clerk. And the ice hasn't set."
