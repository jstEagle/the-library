## 9. Tomas Refuses

The ink was the first betrayal.

Tomas had chosen it deliberately — his father's own ink, the violet-black mixture the magistrate kept in a crystal well on his desk, the same ink that had signed away three families' names and then, with a flourish indistinguishable from any other flourish, signed Tomas's school prizes and his commission and his first appointment to the archives. He had carried a stopper of it in his coat for eleven years, a superstition he had never examined until now, when he uncorked it over Lucan Reyes's borrowed desk and understood at last why he'd kept it: so that whatever he wrote against his father would be written in his father's own hand's blood, as if to say *this comes from you, this is yours, you made me and I am making this.*

The pen scratched. The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind that had sealed the island was still working itself out of its fury against the tower, a great slow animal turning over in its sleep, and beneath that sound the house creaked in ways Tomas was only beginning to learn — the stair that spoke on the fourth step, the window in the north room that breathed in its frame, all the small voices of a house that was not his but had agreed, temporarily, to shelter him.

He had been writing for two hours. He had four drafts crumpled in the grate, and each one had failed for the same reason: he could not find a tone that was neither accusation nor appeal. Every sentence he began in anger curdled into something wheedling; every sentence he began in sorrow hardened into something his father would simply file. His father filed things. That was the whole horror of the man — not cruelty, which at least has temperature, but filing. Grief, guilt, the drownings of three boats and nine souls: all of it could be stamped, docketed, and set in a drawer where damp could get at it slowly enough that no one would notice the rot.

*Father.*

He wrote the word and looked at it a long time. Then:

*I have before me documents I wish you would deny. Deny them, and I will burn them tonight and speak of this never again. You taught me that the law is the last wall between the sea and the mob. Show me the wall is real. Show me the seals are forged by someone else's hand. I will accept almost any answer except silence, and you will understand that even now I am asking rather than telling, because I am your son, and asking is what sons do.*

*But if the seals are true — if your signature sits beneath those erased claims like a headstone — then know that I am carrying them to the capital, and that I would rather you hear it from me than from anyone else, though God knows there is no kind way to say it.*

*You once told me a judge must be able to look at any document and see it plainly, as if no name on it were his own. I am looking. Father, I am looking.*

*Tomas*

He read it three times. It was weak. It was the letter of a man still hoping to be told he had misread the world, and he knew — with the particular clarity that comes at the end of a long night's writing, when the body is cold and the mind has run through every kindness it owns — that his father would read the weakness instantly and answer it the way he answered everything: warmly, reasonably, and without moving an inch.

But it was honest. That was more than he'd managed in the other four drafts. He sanded it, folded it, sealed it with plain wax because he could not bring himself to use the family crest, and set it on the desk beside the oilskin packet, which sat there like a stone on a table, patient as geology.

Then he went out into the grey morning to find someone willing to carry a letter across water that had spent the night trying to unmake the world.

* * *

There was no crossing. Of course there was no crossing.

He learned it standing at the head of the causeway with the letter inside his coat, feeling it press against his ribs like a second heartbeat gone wrong. The bridge gates were chained — he could see the chain even from here, a black line drawn across the pale spine of stones — and beyond the gates the sea stood up in ridges, green-grey and muscular, hurling itself again and again at the causeway as if it meant to erase not just the road but the idea of a road. Spray came over the top in white sheets and fell hissing on the near stones. The air tasted of salt and iron.

A boy — one of the keeper's neighbors, wrapped in oilskins three sizes too large — watched Tomas watch the water.

"Post won't go till the storm's done," the boy said. "Nothing goes. Not till the ice, maybe. Da says we're shut in a bottle."

"When does it open?"

The boy shrugged with the magnificent indifference of the young toward questions of time. "When it opens."

Tomas turned back along the cliff path. The letter lay against his chest, and every step seemed to drive it deeper. He had imagined — he saw now how absurdly — that he could post his doubts like postage, send them across the water, receive absolution or damnation by return, and be finished with it either way. But the storm had closed the bridge, and the island had become what it perhaps had always been: a place where nothing could be sent away and nothing received, where every truth had to be lived with in the room where it happened.

Lucan was feverish. That was the other reason the letter had to go — or one of the reasons, though Tomas suspected the reasons were arranged like cards, the true one tucked somewhere in the middle where he wouldn't have to look at it. The clerk lay in the north room under two blankets and a quilt, his face the color of candle tallow, his breathing quick and shallow as a bird's. Between the fevers he talked — fragments about registries, about seal-wax, about a train timetable that existed only in his delirium — and once, terribly, clearly: *"Before the signing. It must be filed before the signing or it dies with the old courts."* Then his eyes had found Tomas and narrowed, lucid as a blade. "You're Dunmore's boy."

"I am."

"He'll write to you." Lucan's cracked lips had shaped something that was almost a smile. "He always writes to the ones who matter. It's cheaper than fighting them."

That had been yesterday. And now the letter Tomas himself had written sat undeliverable against his heart, and the island held them all in its wet fist, and somewhere beyond the ridges of water his father was going about his morning in the capital town — shaving, perhaps, taking his tea, winding the clock — entirely unaware that his son was composing treason against him in violet-black ink.

Tomas went back to the house and did the only thing left to do. He waited, and he tended the sick man, and he hated how much of the tending felt like devotion to a duty his father would recognize.

* * *

The reply came on the fifth day, which should have been impossible.

Tomas heard the knock at dawn — three knocks, unhurried, official — and found on the doorstep no boatman, no gull-winged messenger, but a fisherman named Halloran whom he had seen once at the ferry sergeant's elbow, a man with a face like weathered rope. In Halloran's fist, dry despite everything, wrapped in oilcloth inside a canvas satchel that smelled of tar: a letter bearing the magistrate's seal in full crimson, crisp as the day it was pressed.

"There's ways," Halloran said, when Tomas stared at him. "The sergeant keeps a skiff for matters that can't wait for the causeway. Matters that pay." He put the letter in Tomas's hands and did not hold out his own for coin, which frightened Tomas more than a demand would have. "I was paid already. Before you ask — no, I didn't read it. A man who reads what he carries starts answering questions about it, and I've got children." He touched his cap. "There'll be another boat when there's another letter. Or there won't."

He went down the path whistling, and Tomas stood in the doorway with his father's name staring up at him from the envelope, and behind him, down the hall, he could hear Lucan's fever-breath quickening like a rising sea.

The letter was heavy. Heavier than paper had any right to be. He carried it to the north room and set it on the desk and looked at it for a long time while the light came up thin and colorless through the salt-hazed glass.

His father's hand was on the address. He knew every stroke of it — the firm downstroke of the *D*, the little hook at the tail of the final *e*, the hand that had corrected his childhood copybooks in red and written *again, neatly* beneath each failure. He knew that hand better than he knew his own face. Somewhere under his sternum the old reflex stirred, the one that had governed thirty years of his life: *Father has written; Father wishes to be answered; open it, read it, do what it says, and the household of the world will stand.*

Lucan coughed in the next room. A long, tearing sound, followed by silence that lasted too long, followed by breathing again — thinner now, Tomas thought, or was that only his fear?

Tomas picked up the letter. It was warm from his hands. Through the paper he could feel the embossing of the seal, the raised crest he had licked and pressed a hundred times as a boy, allowed — honored — to help seal the household correspondence. *Again, neatly.*

If he opened it, he would read it. If he read it, some part of him — the part that still stood in his father's study at eight years old, desperate for the red corrections to turn to praise — would listen. His father did not shout and did not threaten; he reasoned, and his reasons arrived wrapped in warmth, and by the time you noticed they were chains the locks were already closed. Tomas had watched men leave his father's study lighter than they entered, comforted, agreeing to things they had sworn on their mothers' graves they'd never agree to. Reasonable men. His father collected reasonable men the way other collectors collected porcelain.

And there was this, too — the thing he could admit only in the privacy of his own skull: if he opened the letter and found his father innocent, then the packet on the desk was a libel and Lucan Reyes was a thief or worse, and the whole terrible machinery of what they intended — the smuggling, the checkpoints, the gamble with a dying man's evidence — collapsed into crime. Part of him wanted that. Part of him wanted it so badly his mouth watered. To be wrong. To fold the whole affair away and go home and sit at his father's table and be, once more, simply a son.

That wanting was exactly the size and shape of the trap. He could see its jaws. His father had built traps his whole life out of precisely this material — love, duty, the longing of the loyal to be told they had misunderstood.

Tomas carried the letter to the hearth.

The fire had been laid but not lit; the room was cold enough to see his breath. He knelt, struck a match, and fed the flame into the kindling, and while it caught he held the letter over the growing heat, and his hand — his own hand, his mother's steadiness in it, wherever she had gone — did not shake.

*Deny it,* he said silently to the sealed envelope, to the man it came from, to the whole architecture of authority the crimson seal represented. *One word. One line in your own hand saying these papers lie, and I will carry the ashes to the capital myself and apologize on my knees.*

The seal caught first. Crimson wax goes soft before paper burns; the crest swelled and lost its shape, the eagle or lion or whatever device it bore melting into a blind red eye, and then the flame walked up over his father's name and took the downstroke of the *D*, the hook of the final *e*, and the handwriting that had ruled his childhood curled black and lifted into the chimney in flakes of ash that glowed like dying stars.

He fed the whole of it in, page by page as it separated, unread. The fire ate warmly, without comment. Ash settled on the hearthstones like grey snow.

It was done in less than a minute. Thirty years, less than a minute. He stayed kneeling afterward, watching the flames, feeling something in his chest torn loose — not grief exactly, grief's cousin, the sensation of a structure coming down inside him that had held up the roof of his life, and the strange cold light now falling through where the walls had been.

"You didn't read it."

Her voice came from the doorway. Ada Ishikawa stood there with a pail of water in one hand and a shawl thrown over her shoulders, her hair still marked from sleep, watching him with eyes that missed nothing and pardoned less. She set the pail down without hurry.

"No," Tomas said. His voice came out scraped.

"Why?"

Because if I read it I might obey it. Because obedience is the one language I'm fluent in. Because I wanted — He looked at the fire instead of at her. "Because I already know what it says. Whatever words are on it, it says *wait*. It says *trust me*. It says *your father loves you, and love is a reason.*"

"And burning it answers?"

"Burning it means I don't have to hear love used as a lock." He got to his feet. Ash dusted his knees; he brushed at it and only ground it deeper into the wool. "You'd have read it," he said. "Anyone sensible would have read it."

"I'd have read it," Ada agreed. She came into the room and stood at the hearth's edge, warming her hands above a fire that was, he realized, hers in fuel and coal and kindling, in the house that was hers, on the island that was hers, and he a guest burning letters in her grate like a child. "And then I'd have known exactly what I was refusing." Her eyes went to the desk, to the oilskin packet sitting there in the grey light. "You're refusing blind. That's not courage, Mr. Dunmore. That's just the other side of the same coin he spends."

The words went into him like a splinter driven home with a thumb. He opened his mouth — to argue, to defend — and found nothing there but the truth of what she'd said, ringing.

"He'll send another," Tomas said at last. "Halloran said there'd be another boat when there's another letter."

"Mm." Ada looked into the flames. "And when it comes?"

"I'll burn that too."

"And the one after?" She turned to face him fully, and the light moved on her face, and he saw in it something he hadn't expected: not judgment, but recognition — the look of a woman who had burned her own share of letters, literal or otherwise, in her own long apprenticeship to silence. "Listen to me, because I've had years of practice at this and you've had one morning. Burning a letter isn't refusing your father. It's refusing to know. Those aren't the same act, and only one of them costs anything." She nodded toward the packet. "That paper cost a man his life to carry. Maren Ostrander's family cost nine graves. If you're going to break with him — truly break — do it with your eyes open. Read the next one. Let him say his warmest, most reasonable worst. And then burn it knowing."

She picked up her pail and paused at the door.

"The fever's worse," she said, quieter. "Come quickly."

Tomas followed her down the hall, ash still on his knees, and behind him in the cold hearth the last flakes of his father's seal glowed and went dark.
