## 10. The Island Takes Sides

By the third morning, the island knew.

It did not know the whole of it — no one on Paloma ever knew the whole of anything — but it knew enough. It knew that a stranger lay fevered in the keeper's house below the lamp, that Tomas Dunmore had not gone back across with the last ferry, and that something had come out of the sea wrapped in oilskin that made the magistrate's son walk the cliffs at night like a man being followed by his own name. The storm had chained them all together behind the gates, forty souls sealed in salt and stone, and in such a place a secret is a lamp left burning in a window: everyone sees it whether they mean to or not.

The rumor traveled the way weather does — first along the low road where the fishing families lived, then up through the terraces, arriving at the chapel last, as rumors always do, slightly changed and wearing better clothes. By the time it reached old Father Ellery's ears it was no longer *a clerk washed ashore with papers* but *a courier sent from the capital itself*, carrying *warrants*, or perhaps *pardons*, depending on who told it. In the widow Osla's kitchen it was said he was dying, which was true, and that he carried proof of what had been done to the drowned families' land, which was also true, though only three people on the island could have confirmed it and none of them were talking. In the tavern it was said he was a spy. In the bakery it was said he was already dead. In the schoolhouse, Ilsa Maren — sixteen, sharp-eyed, with her grandmother's jaw and her grandmother's stubbornness set into her like a keel — told her classmates nothing at all, which is how they knew she knew everything.

Ada felt the rumor arrive the way she felt changes in pressure: an ache behind the eyes, a wrongness in the gulls' voices. She had spent three years learning to keep the small silences that kept this island whole — the silence about which husbands drank, which wives had gone hungry so their children wouldn't, which boats were rotten under fresh paint. Those silences were mortar. She had believed them to be load-bearing. Now there was a man coughing blood in her bed, and the mortar was showing cracks.

* * *

Maren came up the path at dusk, when the light went the color of brass fittings and the wind had dropped to that held-breath stillness that precedes bad news. Ada saw her from the gallery and knew before she reached the door that this would not be a visit for tea. Maren walked like a woman carrying water: carefully, both hands full, spilling nothing.

She was seventy-one and had been a widow since the drownings — not of a husband alone, though she had lost him too, but of a family entire. Her grandfather's salt fields lay on the mainland side of the strait, leased now to Cousin Aldric, and every autumn she watched the harvest wagons cross the causeway carrying away what should have been her grandchildren's inheritance. She had never once spoken of it aloud. That was part of the arrangement the island had made with its grief: you could look at the fields; you could not claim them.

Ada met her at the door and took her cloak without being asked. The two women sat by the stove, and for a long while neither spoke, because between old friends the silence is a room you both know how to live in.

"He's worse," Ada said finally. "The fever's in his chest now."

"Then there isn't much time." Maren folded her hands. Her knuckles were swollen, the joints of a woman who had wrung out nets for fifty years. "Ada. I'm going to ask you for something I've never asked anyone for. And I need you to let me finish before you answer."

Ada waited. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it filling the channel, that long slow breath the sea takes against the rocks.

"I want you to use those papers."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish." But her voice cracked on the second word, and for a moment the careful woman wavered, and Ada saw beneath her the girl who had stood on this same shore sixty years ago watching her father's boat come apart on the bar. "Three families, Ada. Three. They said my husband's boat was uninsured. They said our claims weren't properly filed. They said it so smoothly, with such kind faces, that we believed them — we *thanked* them — because grief makes you stupid and they knew it. And then Aldric leased our fields at half their worth from a magistrate whose signature made us landless on paper we never got to read." She looked down at her ruined hands. "I have watched another family's cattle drink from my grandfather's well. Every spring. For six years. I have smiled at that man in the chapel."

"Maren, if I take those papers to the mainland—"

"You'll bring soldiers. Yes." Maren raised her head, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "I've heard them saying it down the lane. That testimony will bring the garrison back. That the last time soldiers wintered here, folk starved quiet and nobody wrote it down. I know what they're afraid of. I'm afraid of it too." She leaned forward. "But Ada — I am seventy-one years old. I will not die owing my grandchildren an explanation. Whatever it costs. Whatever it brings down on us. Use the papers. Say my name when you say it. Let me stand wherever they make people stand and say it myself."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, in the keeper's room, Lucan Reyes coughed — a deep wet sound, like an anchor coming up through weed.

"I can't promise you anything," Ada said at last. "Not safety. Not success. There are checkpoints between here and the capital, and eleven days until the signing closes the old courts forever, and one man is dying on my floor who may not live to be carried anywhere."

Maren nodded slowly, as if Ada had recited a psalm. "Then don't promise. Just decide." She rose, took her cloak from the peg, and paused at the door without turning around. "You lit the lamp for my husband's boat the night it went down. I watched it from this window. You couldn't save him, but you didn't stop burning, and I have thought about that every day since." Now she turned, and her voice gentled into something almost like apology. "Don't stop burning, Ada."

The door closed. Ada sat a long time in the dark, listening to the tide.

* * *

But the island was not only Maren. The island was forty souls, and by morning every one of them had taken a side, quietly, the way islands do — not in the open square with fists and banners, but in doorways, in lowered voices over fish racks, in the particular angle of a hat brim when paths crossed on the cliff stair.

Osla came at first light, before the fog lifted, and she did not beg. Olsa had buried two sons in the drownings and had run out, decades ago, of any talent for asking. She stood in Ada's kitchen with her arms crossed and said only: "If you go, you go knowing some of us won't thank you. And if you stay, you stay knowing I'll remember that instead." Then she left, because Osla considered that a complete position, and it was.

Old Piet the cooper took the other road entirely. He cornered Ada by the drying sheds, his voice pitched low and urgent, his big hands opening and closing like things trying to hold water. "They'll send the garrison, woman. You were here in 'eleven — you remember what a winter with soldiers in it looks like. Requisitions. Curfews. My daughter's boy sick all that season because they took the coal." He stepped closer, and there was genuine fear in his face, the honest fear of a man who has been hungry. "One dead clerk and three dead families versus forty living ones. Do the arithmetic. The sea already did it once. Don't do it again."

"The sea didn't do arithmetic, Piet. Men did. Men with stamps."

"And men with rifles will come if you wave those papers in their faces!" He caught himself, glanced down the empty lane, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Bury the packet. Bury it deep and bury it whole and let God sort the ledgers. That's all I'm saying. That's all most of us are saying."

*Most of us.* Ada heard the phrase settle over the island like fog. By noon she could map it: the low road mostly with Piet, the older widows with Osla's cold neutrality, the young ones — Ilsa's crowd — with a bright, dangerous eagerness that frightened her more than either faction. The children had begun playing a game called *courier*, running messages between houses, dodging imaginary checkpoints behind the smokehouse. Their parents hushed them. The children only hid better.

Even Father Ellery was drawn in, though he fought it like a man refusing a fight. He came to the lighthouse on the fourth evening ostensibly to see whether Lucan wanted confession, and stayed to stand at Ada's window looking down at the channel, where the frozen causeway gleamed faintly under the rising moon like a spine laid bare.

"There are two sins," he said, without preamble, in the way he had of beginning conversations mid-sermon. "The sin of doing evil, and the sin of letting it be done so your own hands stay clean. I have preached against the first my whole life and been very comfortable about the second." His shoulders sagged. "I signed nothing, Ada. I protested nothing. When the claims came to the parish for verification, I verified them. I looked at the blanks where names should have been and I initialed the margins anyway." He turned to her, and she saw that he had brought this to her door the way penitents bring things to priests — because someone had to carry it and he could no longer lift it alone. "Whatever you decide, I will not be the reason it fails. But I wanted you to know that the church's silence on this island has a signature, and it's mine."

He left before she could answer. She suspected he had planned it that way.

* * *

Tomas found her at midnight in the lamp room, trimming the wick, and stood in the doorway a long moment before speaking. He had grown thinner in the days since the packet was opened; the capital polish was wearing off him like paint off a harbor buoy, and underneath there was something rawer, and younger, than the man who had arrived against his will.

"They're saying my father's men will come the moment the ice holds," he said. "That even if the papers get through, the reprisal starts here. With the people who helped."

"People say many things when they're afraid."

"My father counts on it." He came to the window beside her. Below, the island lay dark except for the few lamps of the low road, each one a household deciding. "When I was a boy, he told me that order isn't cruelty — that a judge who keeps the peace does more good than a hundred honest men who start fires. I believed him. I built my whole life on believing him." He was quiet a moment. "And now half this island wants to protect him, and the other half wants to bury him, and both sides think I'm either a traitor or a weapon, and the terrible thing, Ada, is that they're both right."

"What do you want?"

He laughed, short and without humor. "Eleven days ago? A quiet posting and my father's approval. Tonight?" He looked down at the black channel, at the white teeth of the frozen causeway. "Tonight I want to know if a man can owe his blood a debt and pay it in something besides obedience."

From below, faint through the floorboards, Lucan coughed again — longer this time, and weaker, a sound like a tide going out further than it should.

Ada trimmed the wick. The great lens gathered the flame and threw it out across the water, five miles, ten, steady and indifferent, touching ships that would never know her name and rocks that would never forgive her. She thought of Maren's hands. Of Piet's fear, which was love wearing fear's face. Of Osla's terrible clean arithmetic. Of Father Ellery's initials in the margins of a lie.

The island had taken its sides. Forty souls, forty weights on the scale, and the beam trembling.

"We rest tonight," she said. "Tomorrow we weigh the crossing."

Below them, unseen, the fever climbed.
