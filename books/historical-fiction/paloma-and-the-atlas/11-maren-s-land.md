## 11. Maren's Land

The frost had come in the night without asking permission, the way everything came now, and Maren Voss woke before the gull-cries with her hands aching in the old way, the way they always ached when the weather turned honest. She lay a while beneath the quilts her mother had stitched from sailcloth and listened to the island breathe. Wind through the gap in the headland. The sea working at the shingle below the cottage, patient as a creditor. Somewhere a shutter loose on an empty house, knocking its small wooden knuckle against the frame.

Three empty houses between hers and the shore now. She counted them every morning the way other people counted their blessings.

She rose, dressed in layers, wound the grey wool about her throat until only her eyes were bare to the cold, and took down her grandfather's stick from behind the door. It was ash wood, worn smooth at the grip by three generations of palms before hers, and it was not for leaning on — though she leaned on it, she was seventy-one and the ground was hard — but for walking the line. For marking where the land began. Her grandfather had cut it himself the year the surveyor came and told him the fields ran to the old stone wall and no farther, and Halvard Voss had laughed, a sound like a keel grinding gravel, and said the sea could have what the sea wanted, but no man living would tell him where his own dirt stopped.

The magistrate's cousin had never heard that story. Or he had heard it and smiled the way men smile at children describing heaven.

Maren let herself out into the blue hour. The island lay under a skin of frost so fine it looked like breath held too long. Every fence wire was strung with beads of ice; every gorse bush wore a ruff of white. Her boots crunched on the path past the well, past the chapel with its locked door, past the house where the Brennans had lived until the water took four of them in one afternoon and the fifth within the year, of nothing anyone could name, just the going-out of a man like a lamp left in wind. The door stood open to the weather because there was no one left to close it. Inside, she knew, frost furred the windowpanes in feathers, beautiful and terrible, and the table still bore the ring-marks of cups no one would ever wash away.

She did not look long. She had learned, these nine years, the discipline of the half-glance. Grief you fed grew fat; grief you rationed kept you alive. That was the arithmetic her mother had taught her in a harder winter than this one, and Maren had done the sums daily ever since and never once come out even.

* * *

The salt fields began where the grazing ended, marked by a change in the grass itself — coarse, grey-green, salt-bitten, good for nothing but sheep and stubbornness — and by the low dry-stone walls that her grandfather's hands had raised and his father's before that, stone on stone without mortar, fitted so cunningly that the winter gales could find no purchase. She walked the boundary first, as always, the stick tapping the capstones in greeting. *Good morning, good morning,* said the tap, *still here, still here.* Some mornings she fancied the stones answered. Old women were permitted their fancies; it was the young who were required to be sensible, and look how sensibly the world had gone to ruin.

From the eastern wall she could see the whole of the Voss holding spread out below her like a map drawn in earth and salt. Sixteen acres, once. The flattest, whitest ground on the island, where the sea came in over the sluices at the spring tides and was let stand, and was let go again, leaving its white sweat behind to be raked and stacked and sold — salt for curing, salt for the fish-houses on the mainland, salt that had bought the land itself two generations back, when Halvard's father carried the first pan on his own back up from the landing beach because the horse died on the road and he refused to wait for another. Maren had been told that story so many times, in so many voices, most of them her grandfather's, that she no longer knew whether she remembered it or had merely inherited it, which amounted to the same thing. Memory was inheritance. That was the whole of what she had come to believe in her long life: papers burned, coins spent, bodies buried, but a story told true enough times became a thing with weight, with title-deeds of its own.

And then the drownings came, and the magistrate came after them, and she learned that paper could be made to outweigh memory after all.

She stood at the wall a long time, letting the east wind find the gaps in her wool, looking down at what had been done with her grandfather's sixteen acres.

The sluice-gates hung open and rusted. No one had worked the pans since the year of the water. The tide-sheets that should have been tended were silted flat, and coarse grass had come in across the lower field in slow green tongues, reclaiming inch by inch what the family had taken from the sea, the sea taking it back without malice, the way it took everything. But the upper field — the best field, the one Halvard had plowed last of all, at seventy years old, saying a man should end where he began — the upper field had been plowed. Recently. Its furrows ran straight and dark under the frost, and along its far edge stood the new fence, post-and-wire, machine-strained, with the paint hardly faded on the warning boards: **LEASED — ALDRIC VOSS-MARSH — NO TRESPASS.**

Aldric Marsh. He had added the Voss to his name the year he took the lease, she'd been told, some clerk's flourish to make the tenancy look like kinship. As if ink could make a stranger blood. As if borrowing a dead family's name were the same as carrying it.

He ran sheep on it now. Fat lowland sheep, wrong breed for this coast, their fleeces full of salt-grass seed every spring, and everyone said they'd do poorly here, and everyone was right, and Aldric Marsh lost money on them every year and kept them anyway. Because it was never about the sheep. Maren understood that the way she understood tides — not from study but from being raised inside it. The lease cost the magistrate's cousin almost nothing; the rent went into the estate's accounts and thence, by routes no one traced aloud, back into the estate. The point of the lease was not profit. The point was occupation. A field left empty remembers its owners. A field full of someone else's sheep forgets them faster.

But it did not forget. That was the thing Aldric Marsh had never reckoned on, being a mainland man with mainland ideas about land as paper. The field remembered her. Every morning she walked it, and the field rose in her like water in a well-shaft.

* * *

She went down through the gate — the old gate, hers by every right that mattered and none that didn't, its hinges long since seized with rust so that she squeezed through the gap beside the post as she had done for nine years — and walked the furrows of the upper field with the stick tapping ahead of her. The frost was giving up by degrees; where the low sun struck the slope, the dark earth showed through the white in patches, breathing again. She crouched, joints cracking like kindling, and put her bare hand flat on a furrow-crown. Cold, yes, but a living cold, a cold with worms and roots under it, not the dead cold of the flagstones in the chapel yard.

Her grandfather had taught her to read soil this way when she was six, setting her small hand on the warm side of a heap and telling her, *Dirt is not dead, girl. Dirt is the only honest man in any parish. It takes what it's given and tells no lies about it.*

Well. Dirt had kept her grandfather's secrets and everyone else's too. There were things under these fields that told no lies because they told nothing at all. In the spring of the drownings, when the boats came back without their men, the families had buried what the sea returned — and the sea had returned very little, and late, and some of it not fit for consecrated ground, so the burials happened where they happened. Two of Halvard's grandsons lay under the middle field, she was nearly sure, near the old drain, though no marker stood and none had been permitted. The priest had wanted to help. The magistrate's office had explained, gently, in writing, that remains recovered under such circumstances must be recorded and interred according to regulation, and that regulation required documentation, and documentation required identification, and identification required — and here the letter had become very careful — confirmation of details the families were, given their distress, perhaps unable to provide. And so the ground had received them quietly, without names, without numbers, and the field had closed over them like a hand.

Nine years, and she still could not walk that stretch without slowing. The sheep grazed over them now, Aldric Marsh's fat wrong-bred sheep, cropping the grass above her boys' faces, and did not know, and could not know, and their ignorance was somehow the worst injury of all — worse than the lease, worse than the fences. To be forgotten is grief. To be grazed over is desecration wearing the face of ordinary life.

She straightened, slowly. Breathed. Told herself the old catechism: *They are in the ground, and the ground is mine, whatever the paper says.* The paper said otherwise. The paper said the holding had lapsed — death duties unpaid, heirs unrecorded, title escheating — and the paper was signed and stamped and filed in the district registry in a hand she had seen once, at the hearing, and never forgotten: neat, unhurried, entirely reasonable. Magistrate Corvus wrote the way ice forms. Nothing wasted. Nothing felt.

They had shown her the document at the settlement, nine years ago, laid on a table in the customs house while a clerk with kind eyes explained the word *escheat* to her twice. She had not wept. She had noticed, even then, that she was not weeping, and had thought distantly: *this is what it looks like when a person breaks quietly.* Then she had signed where the clerk indicated, because refusing to sign changed nothing and signing at least got her home before dark, and she had walked back across the causeway with the paper's words lodged in her chest like fishhooks: *heirs unrecorded.* As if her sons had been cargo. As if the sea had shipped them and lost them and the loss was a matter for freight ledgers.

Her sons had names. Torren, twenty-six, who sang badly and often. Emeric, nineteen, who could whistle gulls down off the wind and was saving, coin by coin, for a boat of his own. She said the names to the field every morning, out loud, because the field was the last place they were spoken. *Torren. Emeric.* The wind took them east, toward the water. That was proper. They had been water-men. Let the water hear them.

* * *

She finished the boundary walk as the sun cleared the headland and set the frost-fields burning white-gold, and she came at last to the corner where the old wall met the new fence, and stood a while regarding the junction the way one regards a scar.

Here was the whole argument in timber and stone. Her grandfather's wall: fitted, patient, built by hands that expected to be dead long before the wall failed, and had been right. Aldric's fence: galvanized wire and creosoted posts, efficient and temporary, already leaning where the frost-heave had found the shallow footings. Give it five winters, she thought, and the wall would still be standing when the fence was kindling. Everything the magistrate's family built had that quality — quick, bright, shallow-rooted. They mistook possession for permanence because they had never had to keep anything through a real winter.

Except that they had kept this. Nine winters, and counting. The wall kept the sheep in or out according to its nature, but the paper kept her out altogether, and paper did not heave or rot or lean. Paper, properly stamped, outlasted everything except fire and truth, and both of those were in short supply on this island lately.

Or were they?

She had heard things. An island hears everything eventually; sound carries over water the way guilt carries over years. The storm had chained the bridge and sealed them in like pickles in a jar, and in the two days since, the talk in the smoke-room of the Anchor and at the fish-drying racks had gone strange and low and quick. A man washed up on Ada Ishikawa's rocks. Alive, barely. Not one of theirs. A mainland face, soft-handed, archive-pale. And Ada — quiet Ada, widow Ada, keeper Ada, who had not crossed to the mainland social side of the street in three years — had taken him in, and Tomas Corvus had gone up to the light that same night and had not come down again before dark the next day.

Tomas Corvus, son of the magistrate, walking the cliff path to the lighthouse like a man going to his own trial. Maren had watched him from her window and felt something turn over in her chest, slow as a mooring buoy in a swell. She was too old to call it hope. Hope was a young woman's luxury, and she had paid for her luxuries long ago, in full, in sons. But she recognized the sensation the way you recognize a tune heard once in childhood: this, this is the feeling that comes before things break open.

And yesterday, Ilsa — Maren's own granddaughter, sixteen and fearless as her great-grandfather had been stubborn — had come in flushed from the harbor and said, too casually, that Ada Ishikawa was asking after her grandmother. Asking whether Maren still had her grandfather's papers. The old leases, the receipts, the pan-records, anything in Halvard Voss's hand.

Maren had sat very still, with her mending in her lap, and looked at the girl until the girl stopped pretending to examine the fire.

"What does the keeper want with Grandfather's papers?"

"I don't know," Ilsa said, which was a lie, and a poor one, and proof of how frightened she was, because Ilsa lied beautifully when she was merely avoiding trouble. "Ada didn't say."

"Then you'll carry my answer back." Maren threaded her needle, unhurried. "Tell her Maren Voss keeps her grandfather's papers in a tin under her bed, every receipt and every lease and the original grant with the surveyor's seal, and has kept them dry through nine winters for a reason she couldn't name until now. And tell her—" her voice did something she hadn't authorized, cracked like thin ice, and she mastered it, and went on flatter, harder, "—tell her I'll bring them myself. Tomorrow. At first light. Before my courage goes out with the tide."

Ilsa stared at her. Outside, the wind shifted and came round to the north, and somewhere down the coast the sea changed its voice.

"Maren—" the girl began, using the grown-up name, which she only did when something mattered.

"Go on," Maren said. "Run, before I think better of it. Thinking better is how the guilty stay safe."

The girl ran. Maren sat alone with her mending and her tin and the sound of the shutters knocking on the Brennan house, and she understood that she had just stepped off a ledge she had been circling for nine years, and that there was no climbing back, and that the fall might kill her — might kill more than her, might bring the soldiers back, might burn what little the island had left — and that none of it, not one hour of it, weighed against the middle field and what slept beneath the frost.

That night she did not sleep. She took down the tin and opened it and read her grandfather's grant by candlelight, the surveyor's seal brown as an old apple, and she touched the inked line that said *Voss, Halvard — sixteen acres saltern, bounded east by the sea* — bounded east by the sea, as if the sea were a neighbor one might borrow sugar from — and she wept at last, quietly, the way ground takes water, and dried her eyes on her apron, and packed the tin in a oilskin bag with a strap, and set it by the door beside her grandfather's ash-wood stick.

Before dawn she was awake anyway. She dressed in her layers, wound the grey wool high, and let herself out into the blue hour with the bag on her shoulder and the stick in her hand, and turned not east toward the fields but west, toward the causeway and the tall black shape of the tower, where a single light still burned against the coming day — burning in daylight, which no keeper does without a reason — and she set her face toward it and walked, an old woman crossing her own island in the dark, carrying sixteen acres in a biscuit tin.

Behind her, at the top of the lane, a lantern swung. Footsteps. Someone else was abroad before the gulls, moving fast, and the light caught for one instant on a uniform button, brass and bright as a coin.

Maren did not run. She was too old to run and too angry to hide. But her heart went down into the frozen ground where her sons lay, and stayed there, listening, as the footsteps came on.
