## 12. Preparing the Smuggle

The storm had spent itself by morning, but the wind kept a low grumbling voice in the chimney, like an old man muttering in his sleep. Ada climbed the tower stairs with the lamp key on a ribbon around her neck and paused at the second landing to look down into the lamp room's iron heart — the great lens, the wicks, the reservoir of oil she would fill at dusk as she had filled it every dusk for eleven years. The glass was salted at the edges. She wiped it with a rag out of habit rather than need.

Below her, muffled through two floors of stone, Lucan Reyes was breathing.

She had listened to that breathing all night without meaning to. It came up through the floorboards in a thin, uneven thread, sometimes quick, sometimes catching on something invisible, and each time it caught she found herself standing still with the oil can in her hands, counting. Tomas sat with him now, in the keeper's quarters where the stove burned peat and the windows rattled in their frames. Tomas had not slept either. She could tell by the way he'd come up yesterday evening — too carefully, holding the banister like a man on a ship.

Three days since the sea gave him back to them, half-drowned, blue-lipped, whispering her name against the rocks. Three days since the packet. And today, she decided, they would stop being people who held a secret and become people who moved one.

* * *

She laid everything out on the kitchen table while Tomas watched from the doorway, Lucan asleep behind him on the cot that had been her husband's.

The table was scrubbed pine, scarred by forty years of knives and kettles, and on it she arranged the evidence the way she arranged anything that mattered: with a place for every piece and no piece touching another. The certified copies first — six sheets, heavy stock, stamped in violet ink by an archive clerk whose name was E. Voss and whose signature slanted hard to the left, as if written in a wind. Then the covering letter, unsigned, explaining what the magistrate Halden Dunmore — no relation, thank God; Tomas flinched anyway when she read the name aloud, and she resolved never to say it again in his hearing — had done after the drownings of '09. How three families' claims to their own land had been struck from the rolls. How the strokes were neat, official, deniable. How the armistice courts, once signed and seated in the capital, would dissolve the old registry's jurisdiction forever, taking the truth with them like water taking a footprint off sand.

"Say it plainly," Tomas said. He had not sat down. "If I have to hear this said to me one more time, let it be plain."

"Eleven days," Ada said. Maybe twelve; the papers disagreed and Lucan's fever-talk disagreed with both. "After the signing, these are paper. Before it, they're proof."

He nodded slowly, the way men nod when they've understood something for days and are only now letting their body admit it.

Then the originals. These Lucan had hidden differently — not in the oilskin packet but sewn into the lining of his satchel, wrapped in waxed cloth: the true registers, the drowned families' original filings, the magistrate's annotated drafts with his corrections in his own small, precise hand. The handwriting was the worst part. Ada had expected fury or forgery in it, some visible violence. Instead it was tidy. Accountant's letters. A man balancing books.

"You're sure about splitting them?" Tomas asked.

"I'm sure about nothing," Ada said. "That's why we split them."

She took the pencil stub from behind her ear and drew, on the back of last month's oil ledger, a line across the page. "Two crossings. Two chances. If one is taken from us, the other survives." She tapped the left half. "Originals stay here. In the tower. No soldier tears apart a working lighthouse during wartime — the light is the light, and everyone from here to the capital knows my name is on the permit. They can search my kitchen, my boat, my person." Her mouth went dry saying *my person*, but she made her voice flat as slate. "They will not take apart the lens assembly. Not in winter. Not with the shipping season closing."

"And if they do?"

"Then they'll find them, and I'll be a thief of state records, and it won't matter anymore because there'll be nothing left to protect." She looked up at him. "But they won't."

Tomas pulled out the chair opposite her and finally sat. Up close she could see how young he was under the tiredness — younger than her thirty-four, though his face carried its own weather. His father's jaw. Nothing else, she thought. Whatever else the man had passed down, it wasn't in the bone.

"The copies go with Lucan," Tomas said. "Sewn into his coat."

"He can't walk to the gate."

"He can be walked. A sick man going home to his mother in the mainland town. I'm his cousin. We hire Brandt's ferry when the sergeant opens the causeway, or we cross on foot if the ice holds." Tomas turned his cup on the table without drinking from it. "Brandt owed your husband. You told me."

Ada said nothing. The debt was a coin she did not like to look at directly; looking at coins wore off their faces. Elias had gone into the surf eleven years ago for a stranger's boat, and had not come out of the water himself, and Sergeant Brandt had stood at the funeral with his hat in his hands and never afterward met her eye on the causeway. Some debts you spend. Some debts spend themselves quietly, over decades, in small unstamped mercies. She suspected Brandt's would be the second kind, and she prayed she would never have to find out which.

"Tomas," she said. "When we get past the first gate — if we get past it — there's a tollhouse at the mill road. Aldric runs it. Your father's cousin. The man who leases Maren's fields."

"I know who Aldric is."

"Do you know what he is?"

Tomas was quiet a moment. "A man who counts everything twice and keeps the better count for himself."

"Good. Then you know he doesn't love us and doesn't hate us, and either of those might be safer than what he actually feels, which is arithmetic." She folded the ledger page with the line drawn down it and put it in her apron pocket, where the lamp key already hung. "We plan for him anyway."

* * *

The needle work took the afternoon.

Ada had learned sewing the way lighthouse keepers learn everything — alone, badly, then adequately, then without thinking. Mending sails taught you a different stitch than mending skin, and mending skin taught you a different patience than both. She unpicked the lining seam of Lucan's coat along the left side, where a man's hand naturally falls when a guard pats him down, and shook her head.

"No. Too obvious. Everyone checks the left inside." She turned the coat over, spread it flat, ran her palm across the shoulders. "Here. Under the collar canvas, right shoulder. A pat-down goes high on the left and low on the right, because guards are mostly right-handed and lazy in the same direction."

"You sound like a smuggler."

"I sound like a woman who has moved brandy past excise men for eleven years because the island drinks and the island pays and nobody asks the keeper what's in her crates." She didn't smile saying it. It was true, and truer than she liked: the neutrality of the light had always been a kind of license. Boats came to her with cargo no manifest described, and she logged them as ballast, and the mainland logged nothing at all, because the alternative was admitting how much of the coast's life floated just outside the law's clean lines. She had told herself those silences were kindnesses. Small lies to keep neighbors safe. Now the same skill pointed the other way, and she felt no cleaner for it — only more certain.

She cut the six copied pages into four panels each, folding them flat as communion wafers, and wrapped them in a scrap of oiled silk from an old signal flag. Twenty-four pieces of paper, thin enough together to pass for padding. She basted them into the collar canvas with waxed thread, stitches small and even, then closed the lining seam so cleanly that when she held the coat up to the window even she couldn't see where it had been opened.

Tomas watched her work with an expression she couldn't name until she could: it was the look of a man watching someone do easily a thing he'd believed impossible.

"My father," he said suddenly, "used to say paper is the cheapest weapon ever invented. That's why he respected it. A sword you have to swing. Paper swings itself."

"Then we've armed it," Ada said, "and now we have to carry it."

* * *

At dusk she went up to light the lamp, and Tomas followed her unasked, carrying the originals wrapped in waxed cloth against his chest like something newborn.

The lens room smelled of brass polish and burning oil and, underneath, the cold mineral breath of the sea two hundred feet below. She lit the mantle, watched the flame stand up, and set the clockwork turning; the great lens began its slow rotation, sweeping its white blade across the darkening water, once every seven seconds. Seven seconds. She had lived her whole grief in multiples of seven seconds.

"Now," she said.

The hiding place had been in her mind for three days, assembling itself the way plans do when the mind refuses to sleep. Not under the floor — every searcher starts at floors. Not in the wall — walls breathe, walls show seams. She knelt at the base of the lens pedestal, where the iron column rose out of its housing, and showed Tomas the access plate: four bolts, painted over so many times they looked like part of the casting.

"Every spring I service the clockwork. Every autumn I strip and grease the rotation gear. I am the only person on this coast with a documented reason to open this column, and the maintenance log proves it, year after year, in my handwriting, countersigned by the district office." She fetched her wrench from the tool locker and broke the bolts loose one by one, each giving with a small scream of rust. "Paper goes inside, in oilcloth, packed dry with charcoal to take the damp. If anyone ever opens this, they find a mechanism mid-service and a keeper who says she'll finish after the storm. Boredom is the best lock ever forged."

Tomas handed her the bundle. Before it went in, she unwrapped one corner and looked one last time at the top sheet — the magistrate's draft, his corrections marching down the margin in that tidy accountant's hand, striking out a fisherman's name, striking out a widow's claim, striking out, in effect, three families from the memory of the world. Maren Ostrand. The Voss brothers. Old Piet and his sons. Names she had said at gravesides. Names the ground remembered even when the registry didn't.

She wrapped it away from the air, oiled the cloth, packed the column, drew the plate home, and drove the bolts tight. Then she painted over the fresh scratches on the bolt heads with lens-black, and when it dried there was nothing to see at all.

Tomas exhaled, long and slow, fogging in the cold of the glass.

"That's it," he said. "That's everything."

"That's half of everything. The other half is sleeping downstairs with a fever." She stood, knees cracking, and looked out through the lantern glass. Below, the island lay black and small, one lit window — her kitchen — the rest given over to wind. Beyond the causeway the sea ran gray-green under the last light, and far out, where the channel markers should be, nothing moved. The bridge gates were chained. The world was sealed. Somewhere beyond that sealed world, clerks in the capital sharpened their pens toward a signing that would bury the old courts, and somewhere nearer, a sergeant named Brandt slept in the gatehouse with a debt on his conscience older than the war.

"Ada." Tomas was looking at her sideways. "Lucan asked me something tonight, before the fever took him under again. He asked if you'd decided."

"Decided what?"

"If you're coming with us. Past the gates. All the way." Tomas's voice was careful, the way voices get when they're carrying more than words. "Because he says the copies aren't enough. Copies can be called forgeries. Copies can be dismissed. He says the originals have to reach the registrar too — or at least a witness who saw them, who can swear to them under oath. He says—" Tomas stopped, and rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped. "He says the courier has to be someone the court will believe. And there's exactly one person on this island whose word carries weight at the hearing, and she's standing next to me refusing to look at the ocean."

Ada looked at the ocean.

Seven seconds of white blade swinging over black water. Seven seconds more. She thought of Maren's face in the churchyard, and the widow's hands, and the word *please*. She thought of the maintenance log, and the permit with her name on it, and the little lawful life she had built so carefully out of other people's silence — and how, if she stepped past that first gate, she would never fit back inside it, whatever happened at the capital.

"Tell him," she said at last, "that the keeper doesn't leave her light unmanned."

"And?"

She took the logbook down from its shelf, opened it to the current week, and wrote, in the steady hand that district offices trusted and sergeants recognized and magistrates feared precisely because it never trembled:

*Dec 3rd. Wind NW moderating. Lamp lit 16:40. Keeper's note: expect extended absence from post beginning within the fortnight. Relief arrangement pending.*

Tomas read it twice. "You're writing your own departure into the official record."

"I'm writing it where it can't be unwritten," Ada said. "If I vanish, if we're stopped, if your father's men come asking — the record says the keeper left for legitimate business, countersigned by the district, and any man who claims otherwise is claiming the district office lies." She closed the book. "Paper swings itself. You told me."

"That was my father's line."

"Then let's put it to honest use for once in its life."

Downstairs, the fever-breath caught again, and this time it did not resume evenly — it thinned, and quickened, and became something with an edge in it, a sound like a man arguing with someone only he could see. Ada was moving before Tomas finished turning toward the door, her hand finding the banister in the dark by pure habit, eleven years of stairs mapped into her feet.

By the time they reached the cot, Lucan Reyes's eyes were open and burning, and he was trying to sit up, and the sweat on his forehead shone like the sea at dawn.

"The coat," he rasped. "Is the coat ready? Because I don't think—" He coughed, wet and deep. "I don't think I'm going to make the crossing standing up."
