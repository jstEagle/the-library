## 13. Lucan Worsens

The fever came in the night, as fevers do — not like a storm you can see building on the horizon, but like damp working into stone, silent and total, so that by the time you notice it the wall is already soft.

I woke in the lighthouse's lower room to a sound I could not at first place: a wet, rattling breath, rhythmic as surf but wrong somehow, too shallow, catching on something invisible on its way out of Lucan Reyes's chest. For a moment I lay in my cot listening to it and to the wind above, which had settled from the gale into that long, low moan that seems to come not from the sky but from under the island itself, as though the rock were an instrument someone had left sounding. Then I understood what I was hearing and I was out of bed before my feet knew they were awake.

The stove had burned down to embers. In the grey light leaking through the shuttered window I could see Lucan lying where we had laid him, wrapped in every blanket Ada owned except one, his face turned toward the ceiling with an expression of such perfect attention that I thought for one absurd moment he was awake and reading something written up there among the beams. His lips moved. No words came. The oilskin packet — or rather the empty oilskin wrapping, for we had long since divided its contents — lay folded beneath his pillow like a child keeps a treasure, and his hand had found it in sleep and gripped the edge of it.

"Lucan." I knelt by the cot. "Reyes. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened partway. What looked back at me was not the dry, precise clerk I had known across the archive tables in the capital — the man who could put his finger on any folio in a room of forty thousand without consulting the index. It was a boy's face, almost, smoothed of everything but effort, the effort of staying in his own body another minute, another hour.

"Tomas." The name came out frayed. "Is it signed yet?"

"Not yet. Eleven days."

"Eleven days." He said it the way a man repeats a sentence handed down against him. "Then there's still—" A cough took him, bending him nearly double, and I held his shoulders while he shook, and when he subsided he was breathing that wet rattle again, faster now, and his skin when I touched his forehead was furnace-hot and dry as paper.

I have stood in courthouses and watched men learn they will die; I have seen that particular stillness settle over them. But watching a man burn alive from the inside, slowly, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and salt and old paint, was different. It required nothing of me but presence, and presence felt like the most useless thing I had ever been asked to give.

* * *

Ada was already in the kitchen when I came down, standing at the window that faced the causeway, and I knew before she spoke what she had been doing, because I had been doing it too, in the hours I couldn't sleep: looking at that black ribbon of stones running out toward the mainland gate and measuring it, over and over, against the tide table pinned beside her, against the weather, against the eleven days.

"He's worse," I said.

"I know. I heard him at two." She did not turn. The lamp above us had been extinguished at dawn — no ships would risk these waters in winter unless desperation drove them — and the house felt strangely naked without its turning heartbeat overhead. "There's ice on the first hundred yards of the causeway. I checked it myself last night, after the wind dropped. After that the water's over the stones until the far shelf."

"The doctor, then. Dr. Halloran — he's on the mainland side, isn't he? At Ferry Row?"

She turned then, and her face in the unlit kitchen was all planes and patience. "He is. And the causeway gate is chained, and even if Brandt lifted the chain for a doctor, no physician alive walks a frozen causeway in the dark for a stranger with a fever. Not in January. Not for a man who isn't from here."

"For a man who is dying to save this island's families—"

"Tomás." My mother's name for me, the Spanish syllables I had carried like a splinter since childhood, since the years before my father remade himself into whatever a magistrate must be. She used it rarely and always deliberately, and it stopped me as she meant it to. "I am not refusing. I am telling you what is true. If you go to Halloran and he says no, you will have spent hours and learned nothing. So think first about what we ask, and how."

We stood in the cold kitchen while the wind worried the eaves, and I thought about the shape of the problem the way I had once been taught to think — my father's training reaching into me even now, even against him, like a voice from a burned letter. There were three facts. One: Lucan could not walk the causeway in his present state; he could barely stand. Two: the documents had to reach the capital archive before the armistice terms ratified the new courts and dissolved the old ones, and the copies sewn into his coat were useless without a living courier to carry them past scrutiny — or without someone else willing to carry them, which raised questions neither Ada nor I had yet said aloud. Three: every hour the fever climbed made the first fact more absolute and the second less possible.

"Or," I said slowly, "we don't wait for the doctor to come to him."

Ada's eyes narrowed. "Say it plainly."

"We move him. Tonight, if the tide allows. Carry him across — Ilsa's family has the flat cart, the one they use for kelp. Blankets, hot stones, brandy if there's any left. Get him to Halloran's door on the mainland side before the fever decides for us."

"And if the fever decides on the causeway?" Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible discipline of the woman; grief had burned everything soft out of her years ago and left only load-bearing walls. "A seizure on those stones, Tomas. Wet ice, black water on either side, and two of us to hold a grown man. You'd be gambling his life to save it."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what I'm proposing. And I'd like to pretend I haven't already decided, but I'd be lying, and you'd know it, and I've had enough of lying in this family to last several lifetimes."

Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth exactly, but the recognition of warmth at a distance, like seeing a lit window across dark water. "Wait until the tide turns," she said. "And wait for Maren. If we're moving him, we'll need more hands than ours, and Maren owes Lucan nothing, which means whatever help she gives will be given freely. That matters, on this island, right now."

* * *

The day passed in the particular agony of preparation, which is to say in waiting made physical. I sat by Lucan through the middle hours and sponged his arms and chest with cold water while he talked — not to me, mostly, but to the ceiling, to the archives, to some tribunal convening in his fever. He recited file numbers. He apologized, over and over, in a thin cracked thread of voice, to someone named Ferro, to someone named the Widow Oyelaran, to three names I did not know and understood, with a sickening clarity, were the drowned: the heads of the three fishing families whose claims my father's signature had erased like chalk from slate. He apologized to them as though they were colleagues whose desks he had failed to keep in order.

"They trusted the registry," he murmured once, focusing on me with sudden terrible precision. "Do you understand? People sign their lives into those books believing the ink means something. A birth. A marriage. A boat. A field. They believe the state is keeping faith with them." His hand came off the blanket and caught my wrist, and though the grip had no strength in it, I did not pull away. "Your father kept faith with no one. And I kept the ledgers tidy while he did it. Ten years, Tomás. Ten years of tidy shelves."

"You stole the evidence," I said quietly. "You crossed the water half-dead already. That wasn't keeping shelves."

"It was arithmetic." His mouth bent toward something that might have been a smile. "Late arithmetic. But mine."

Near midday he slept — truly slept, the rattle easing — and I went up into the lamp room because I could not bear the smallness of the sickroom. From the gallery the whole world of our confinement arranged itself below me: the island's grey fields stitched with stone walls, the clustered roofs smoking thinly, the causeway running out like a sentence that trailed off into water, and beyond it, faint with distance, the mainland shore where the town sat with its warm windows and its convenient forgetting. Somewhere in that town was Dr. Halloran. Somewhere was my father's house, where tonight a fire would be burning and a decanter standing open, and a man who falsified the deaths of fishermen would be reviewing petitions in a warm study, entirely unaware that his tidy world had begun, very quietly, to come apart.

I thought of my brother — no. I had no brother. I thought of the letters. The first one, which I had burned unread, and the strange liberty of that burning, the way ash had seemed lighter than any answer could have been. I wondered what the second letter would say, and whether it had already been written, already traveling, already sitting in some sorting room waiting for a boat that could not cross. Some part of me — the part trained in law, the part that believed in procedure the way other men believed in God — wanted that letter desperately, wanted to know my enemy's next move. The rest of me wanted never to touch my father's handwriting again as long as I lived.

Below, Ada came out onto the causeway's first stones with a lantern, though the sun was still up, and walked perhaps thirty yards, testing each slab with her boot before trusting it, then turned and came back. Watching her, I understood that she was rehearsing the crossing in her body, learning the ice the way a sailor learns a sea, and that whatever decision was finally made tonight would be made by the woman who had walked the stones, not the lawyer who had counted the risks. I found I was glad of that. I had spent my life inside decisions made by men who never touched the ground they legislated over.

* * *

Maren arrived at dusk with Ilsa's uncle Breck and a handcart that had clearly been built for kelp and repurposed, in its long career, for everything else an island asks a cart to carry. She came into the sickroom without ceremony, looked at Lucan for a long moment — this stranger, this clerk from the capital, this paper-man who had carried her family's stolen history across the sea on his own broken body — and then rolled up her sleeves.

"Well," she said, in the tone of a woman addressing weather. "He's lighter than my husband was, God rest him. That's something."

We worked while the last light died. Hot stones from the oven wrapped in sacking and packed around the cart's bed. Every blanket in the house, layered. Ada's storm lantern hung from a hook Breck screwed into the cart's frame. Brandy — half a bottle, Ada's own, saved against some emergency she had evidently decided had arrived. Lucan, half-conscious, protested in a whisper that we ignored completely, and I will confess that ignoring a man's stated wishes while strapping him to a cart gave me my first real understanding of what my father must feel every working day, and I resolved to feel appropriately ashamed later, when there was time.

At the door, Ada paused with her lantern. The tide table said the water had fallen off the far shelf an hour before; the ice on the near stretch would be at its hardest for perhaps ninety minutes, no more. Ninety minutes to move a dying man four hundred yards of frozen stones in the dark.

"Maren," Ada said. "You've crossed at night?"

"Twice a year, my whole life, carrying things I shouldn't name in front of the boy." She nodded at me, entirely without apology, and I found myself almost smiling.

"Then you lead. Tomas and I take the sides of the cart. Breck takes the rear rope." Ada looked at each of us in turn, and the lamplight made her face look carved, ancient, certain. "If the ice fails anywhere in the first third, we go back. No argument. If it fails past the halfway mark—" she paused, and I saw her do the arithmetic and hate it, "—we push forward, because turning a loaded cart on wet stone kills faster than walking on."

Nobody argued. On this island, I was learning, nobody argued with the keeper about the water.

We started out at full dark, four figures and a cart and a dying man between us, into a wind that came off the sea in long steady pushes rather than gusts, as though it had decided to be thorough rather than cruel. The first hundred yards were as Ada had described: black ice glazing the stones, treacherous as glass, and we moved at a shuffle, Maren ahead with her boots finding purchase by some knowledge older than language, the cart runners hissing behind her. Lucan had gone quiet — too quiet — and twice I stopped and pressed my fingers under his jaw to find the pulse still ticking there, fast and light, like a bird's.

Halfway. The ice gave way to wet stone where the tide had only recently let go, and the going improved even as the cold deepened, spray freezing on the rope in Breck's fists, on Maren's shawl, on the cart's iron fittings, until we creaked when we moved. Once, out in the black water to our right, something large turned over with a sound like a door closing — a seal, or the sea itself shrugging — and nobody spoke of it.

It was on the final third, with the mainland gate's lantern visible ahead, a smudge of yellow through the sea-fret, that Lucan began to speak again. Fever-talk, rising, urgent. He said my father's name. He said it clearly, twice, in a voice that carried strangely over the flat water, and then he said: "The registry is a promise, gentlemen, and you have broken it," which was, I suppose, as close to a battle cry as a records clerk ever comes.

And then the lantern at the gate moved. Detached itself from the gatehouse. Started toward us along the causeway's far end, swinging with the deliberate pace of a man who has seen something and is coming to decide what it is.

Sergeant Brandt. Of course it was Brandt — who else kept the gate on a night like this — and in the space of ten steps my mind ran the whole board: the sergeant who owed my family nothing and my father his post, or owed Ada's dead husband everything and his own life besides; the man who would find a magistrate's son and a lighthouse keeper smuggling a fevered thief of state records across a sealed causeway, and who held, in his one ring of keys, the power to end everything we had bled for before it properly began.

Maren stopped the cart. Ada set her lantern down on the stones, unhurried, and stepped forward alone to meet the approaching light, and I stayed with Lucan's burning hands and listened to the wind and the sea and my own heart, and waited to learn which debt the night intended to collect.
