## 14. First Ice

The cold came in the night like a debt collector, and by morning it had taken the sea's voice.

Ada noticed it first as an absence. For three years she had woken to the causeway speaking its one long syllable — water over stone, water over stone — and now there was only a hush so complete she could hear her own blood moving in her ears. She lay in the narrow bed beneath the lamp room for a full minute before she understood what the silence meant, and when she did, she was out from under the blankets before the thought had finished forming.

From the gallery rail she looked down at the bridge.

It had gone white.

Not snow-white — ice-white, a skin of frost thrown over the black basalt ribs of the causeway where the last storm-surge had soaked them and not drained away. The tide pools along its length had frozen into lenses, each one holding a small distorted sky. And at the far end, where the mainland gate rose out of the mist like the jaw of something patient, she could see the shape of Sergeant Brandt's watch-hut, its chimney smoking, smoke rising perfectly straight in the windless cold.

The causeway had frozen enough to walk.

That was the thought that frightened her. Not enough to trust — never that, not on the first ice, which is the ice that kills, the ice that gives you four steps of confidence and takes you at the fifth — but enough. Enough that a man could cross if he were careful. Enough that a man could cross if he were desperate.

Lucan Reyes was both.

She found him in the warm kitchen where they had kept him these nine days, propped in the chair by the stove with Tomas's coat over his knees and Ilsa's old school blanket around his shoulders, his fever burned down to a low ember but his eyes still lit with that terrible purpose that made Ada think of a lamp burning oil it could not spare. He was trying to write. She watched from the doorway as his hand moved across the page in slow, drowning strokes, the pen dipping and dragging, and she saw that he was copying out the order of the documents again — the certified copies, the seals, the dates — committing them to paper in case his body failed before his errand did.

"You'll ruin your eyes," she said.

He looked up and smiled, and the smile cost him more than the writing did. "I've ruined worse things with better eyes."

"Has the fever broken?"

"It's lying low. Like me." He set down the pen with the exaggerated care of a drunk man or a dying one. "Mrs. Ishikawa. The ice."

"I know."

"The signing is in twenty-two days. If the courts close—"

"I said I know." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she softened it by crossing the room and laying the back of her hand against his forehead. Cool. Damp. The ember banked but not out. "You are not walking anywhere today. Or tomorrow. The first ice is a liar, Lucan. It looks like a road and it is a mouth."

"And when it stops being a liar?"

Ada went to the window. The light coming through it was the particular light of hard cold — thin, silvered, without mercy in it, the kind of light that shows you everything exactly as it is and makes no apology for the showing. Out past the harbor wall the fishing boats sat locked in their moorings, masts bare, hulls furred white at the waterline. Nobody worked the water now. Nobody crossed. The island held its breath, and the town on the far shore held its, and between them lay two hundred yards of stone wearing a mask.

"When it stops being a liar," she said, "I will know. Because I will have asked every stone myself."

* * *

She began that night.

Not because the plan required it — Tomas had drawn up the plan with a clerk's precision, checkpoints and timings and contingencies listed in his small severe hand — but because Ada Dunmore-Ishikawa had kept this lighthouse for three years through storms that peeled slate off the roof like orange peel, and she had learned the one law that governed everything here: *trust nothing you have not tested with your own hands.* The lamp. The winch. The shutters. The rope. A keeper who trusted was a keeper whose light went dark on the night some family needed it, and Ada had buried enough people to know what darkness cost.

So: the stones.

The causeway ran two hundred and ten paces from the island stair to the mainland gate — she had counted them years ago, in another life, walking beside a man who counted with her. It was built of great basalt blocks laid by the old engineers, each block broader than a coffin lid, fitted so tightly that in summer you could walk it barefoot. But the sea got into everything. Between the blocks, in the joints, the water pooled and lifted and gnawed, and after forty years of gnawing some of the stones had begun to tip — a finger's width here, a knuckle's width there — invisible in dry weather, treacherous under frost, because water in a tilted joint freezes and expands and lifts the lip of the stone into a ridge that will take an ankle like a snare takes a hare.

At eight o'clock, when the sky had gone the color of iron filings and the cold had sharpened until breathing felt like swallowing glass, Ada put on her husband's old coat, wound wool around her hands, and went down the stair with a lantern in one fist and a broom handle in the other.

Tomas met her at the door. He had been sleeping badly — she heard him turning in the room next to Lucan's, hour after hour, the springs telling the story of a conscience — and he stood now in the doorway with a shawl over his shoulders and his father's jaw set at the angle that meant argument.

"You can't go out in this."

"I've been out in worse."

"That's not an answer, Ada."

"No," she agreed. "It's a fact." She held up the broom handle. "I'm going to test the stones. Every one. If we're going to move a sick man across this bridge, I intend to know exactly which steps will hold him and which ones want to kill us. You're welcome to come. Bring your own stick."

For a moment he looked as though he might argue further — she could see the magistrate's son in him, the boy raised on the principle that the world ought to be argued into agreement — and then something else surfaced in his face, something wearier and younger, and he simply nodded and went back inside for boots.

They started at the island end, at pace one.

The method was simple and slow. Ada walked ahead, lantern held low, and swept the beam across each block in turn, looking for the telltales: the gray bloom of ice in a joint, the dull wet gleam where spray had frozen into glaze, the slight wrongness of a stone sitting proud of its neighbors. Where she found ice she struck it with the broom handle — a sharp crack, sometimes a satisfying shatter, sometimes nothing, the blow swallowed whole. Where she found a tilt she crouched and pressed her palm flat against the stone and rocked it, gently, the way you'd rock a cradle, feeling for movement, listening for the grinding click of a loose block shifting on its bed.

Pace seven was glazed. Pace eleven was sound. Pace twelve had a lifted lip at its seaward corner that would catch a boot toe in poor light; she marked it with a knot of red yarn pulled from her pocket, tying it to a bolt-head rusted flush with the stone.

"What are those?" Tomas asked, catching up, his breath smoking.

"Marks. For Lucan's feet. We'll walk him along the line of them, and where there's no mark, we step where I step."

"You've done this before. Marking paths."

"My husband used to say a keeper has two jobs: keeping the light, and knowing the ground between the light and everyone else's doors." She moved on to the next stone, knelt, pressed, rocked. "He tested the cliff path every autumn after the gales. All four miles of it. The fishermen laughed at him — said the path knew itself better than any man did. Then the year the storm took out the middle section, nobody laughed. Eleven men came home along that path in the dark with the wind behind them, and every one of them lived, because every one of them stepped where Emeric's marks told them to step." She paused. "Nine of them drowned the following spring anyway, in their own boats, in sight of their own houses. But not on his path. Not on his watch."

Tomas said nothing for a while. The lantern threw their shadows long and doubled across the frozen stones, two figures stitched together at the edges, walking nowhere slowly.

"Your husband," he said at last. "The sergeant — Brandt — the one at the gate. He was there that day, wasn't he? The wreck."

"He was nineteen. Clinging to a spar for six hours." Ada found a joint brimming with ice, cracked it, cracked it again, heard the water underneath shift and settle. "Emeric went out for him when the harbor boats wouldn't. Pulled him in half-dead. The boy never spoke of it afterward — shame, probably, being saved by a lighthouse keeper instead of saving himself — but he's never once turned us away from the gate, not in three winters. Not once has he searched our cart properly, though it's his duty to." She straightened, knees cracking louder than the ice. "That is what a debt looks like, Tomas. It doesn't look like money. It looks like a man looking away, winter after winter, and calling it weather."

* * *

By midnight they had reached pace ninety, the exact middle of the causeway, where the tide channel ran deepest and the stones were oldest and the spray in a storm came over the top like a living thing. Here the ice lay thickest, a smooth gray plate welded across three blocks, and here Ada stopped and did the thing she had come out to do.

She stamped.

Once, softly — the plate rang, a high thin note like a wineglass touched. Twice, harder — the note changed, dropped, went dull. On the third stamp she put her whole weight behind it, and the ice answered with a sound she knew too well: a groan, deep and structural, the sound of something bearing weight it had no intention of forgiving.

"Tide channel," she said. "The water moves under here even at slack. The ice forms and breaks and forms again. It's the strongest-looking stretch on the bridge and the worst. In the morning it'll look solid as the church floor." She tied a double knot of red yarn at the near edge, then a second at the far. "We cross here at dawn tomorrow, when the tide's lowest and the channel's running shallowest, and we cross in single file, and we do not stop, and we do not look down. Understood?"

"You're going to carry him yourself, aren't you," Tomas said. It wasn't quite a question.

"If it comes to it."

"He weighs nothing. He's mostly fever and paper."

"He weighs a grown man's worth of fear, which is heavier than anything." Ada lifted the lantern and looked back the way they'd come — ninety paces of pale stone strung with her little red knots, receding toward the island like beads on a black string — and ahead, the remaining hundred and twenty, untested, unknown, waiting. "Come. We finish tonight. Tomorrow there won't be time."

* * *

They finished at half past two.

The last hundred and twenty paces took them nearly two hours, because the frost thickened as they neared the mainland shore — the land's own cold bleeding out into the bridge — and because Ada refused, absolutely refused, to pass a single stone she had not pressed with her palm. Tomas stopped arguing somewhere around pace one-fifty. Somewhere around pace one-eighty he stopped watching the way ahead and started watching her instead: the deliberate swing of her arms, the economy of her movements, the way she crouched and rocked and listened as though each stone were a patient confessing. Once, when she slipped on a glazed patch and caught herself with a hiss, he shot out a hand and steadied her elbow, and she let him, and neither of them remarked on it.

At pace two-ten — the last stone before the gate's shadow — she stood upright, blew on her fingers, and looked at the watch-hut fifty yards beyond the gate. Dark windows. Banked chimney. Brandt asleep, or pretending to be.

"Every stone," she said quietly. "One hundred and eighty-three good. Twenty-seven marked bad. Two that will need carrying over." She turned to Tomas. "Now we know the ground. Now we only have to survive the men standing on it."

They walked back together in silence, following her own red knots home, and the island received them at the stair with its single lamp burning steady above, and Ada felt — not comfort, exactly, but something adjacent to it: the specific satisfaction of work done thoroughly in the dark, known to no one, needed by everyone.

* * *

Lucan was awake when they came in. Of course he was. His kind always was.

He had dragged himself to the window of the kitchen and sat wrapped in the blanket with his forehead nearly touching the frosted glass, and when Ada entered he didn't turn around — just spoke to the window, to the reflection of the lamp burning in it.

"I saw your light out there. Moving along the bridge." His breath fogged the pane. "I thought — someone's testing it. Someone's out there in the dark asking every stone if it will hold me." He turned then, and his eyes were bright with fever and something fiercer than fever. "Mrs. Ishikawa. In the capital they told me no one would help. They told me the coast was dead, that everyone had been paid or frightened, that the packet would die with me in the water. I believed them. That's why I swam for your rocks instead of the harbor — I thought, better to die where it's quiet."

Ada hung the lantern on its hook. Her hands, she noticed, had not yet stopped trembling from the cold — or from something older and colder than the night.

"Well," she said. "You were wrong about the coast."

"And about the quiet?"

She looked at him a long moment. Through the wall she could hear Tomas feeding the stove, coal clattering into iron, the fire taking hold. Above them the lamp turned its slow wheel of light over a sea gone silent and white-rimmed, guarding nothing, guarding everything.

"There's no quiet left on this island," Ada said. "There hasn't been since you washed ashore. Rest. Tomorrow at dawn, the ice decides whether it lies — and then we find out what the sergeant decides."

Outside, unseen, the first flakes of new snow had begun to fall, softening every red knot she had tied, and by morning the causeway would be a blank white page again, all her night's work erased, and she would simply have to remember.
