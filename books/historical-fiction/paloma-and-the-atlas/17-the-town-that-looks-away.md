## 17. The Town That Looks Away

The town was called Vesterhavn, though the name had worn away like paint on a harbor rail, and what remained of it was prosperity. That was the first thing Tomas noticed as their cart rattled down the cobbled hill into the lower streets: not poverty, not ruin, but comfort — the thick, upholstered comfort of a place that had never been asked to account for itself.

He had expected something else. He had expected, without admitting it to himself, a town that would look like its own conscience: shuttered windows, salt-eaten houses, the gray residue of three drowned families still clinging to the gutters like old ice. Instead there were geraniums in painted window boxes. There was a butcher's shop with a brass bell and a boy sweeping sawdust from the step. There was a new clock in the church tower, white-faced and confident, striking the hour over rooftops that had been re-slated within the last decade. He could tell because the slates were too uniform, too dark, unweathered by any honest winter.

"They've rebuilt," Ada said beside him. She held the reins, since Lucan was past holding anything and Tomas was past pretending he knew horses. Her voice carried no accusation. That was somehow worse. "Look at the quay. New pilings. New cranes."

"Whose money?"

She didn't answer at once. A gull hung above them on the wind off the water, screaming once, twice, then folding itself away toward the breakwater. "Three boats went down in 'sixteen," she said finally. "The *Solveig*, the *Katrine*, and the little one — Halvard's boat, I never learned her proper name. Nine men. Three families. The boats were insured, the houses insured, and the land—" She stopped herself, the way she stopped most sentences that mattered, and clicked her tongue at the horse. "The land stayed behind like a coat left on a chair."

Tomas understood then that she was speaking for his benefit, translating the island's arithmetic into terms a magistrate's son could follow. He hated how grateful he was for it.

* * *

They took rooms at the Anchor & Rope, because it was the only inn large enough to have rooms, and because Ada judged — rightly, he suspected — that a woman known as a lighthouse keeper traveling with a sick man and his doctor's papers would draw less attention than two strangers with no story. The innkeeper, a broad man named Foss with forearms like mooring posts, took their coin and asked no questions beyond whether the feverish gentleman wanted broth. He did not ask where they'd come from. Tomas noted this the way he now noted everything: with the professional attention of a man raised on testimony, cataloguing what people chose not to say.

It was only later, coming down the stairs at dusk while Ada sat with Lucan, that he heard it — through the taproom door, half-open, the low music of conversation that stops and starts again when a floorboard creaks.

"—the keeper woman, from Paloma—"

"—heard she ferried him across herself, before the storm—"

"—no, after. After the gates chained. Which is its own question, isn't it—"

And then a laugh, easy and untroubled, and someone saying, "Well. The sea gives and the sea takes," which was, Tomas thought, the oldest sentence in the language and the most useful. It explained everything and required nothing. It turned nine drowned men into weather.

He stood in the dark stairwell longer than he meant to, listening to the town talk around the truth the way water talks around a stone — wearing it smooth, year by year, until nothing sharp remained.

* * *

In the morning he went looking for the families' neighbors.

He told himself it was reconnaissance — that if they were to carry the packet through this town, past its checkpoints and into its archive, they needed to know who might remember, who might be moved, who might be bought. But there was another reason, one he did not examine closely: he wanted to see the faces of people who had profited from his father's pen. He wanted, God forgive him, to find guilt somewhere outside his own house.

The first house belonged to the Vestergaards. He knew the name from the documents — he had read them so many times by lamplight that the names had worn grooves in him. Erik Vestergaard, neighbor and witness to the original deed of the Halvard fields, whose mark appeared on the 1916 re-survey that transferred those same fields to the magistrate's cousin at a tenth of their value. Witness. The word had always sounded clean to Tomas. Now he imagined the ink drying under this very roof.

The house was handsome. Two stories, fresh whitewash, a greenhouse glinting at the side where tomatoes ripened out of season against the glass. A dog slept on the step with the boneless entitlement of a well-fed animal. When Tomas knocked, Erik Vestergaard answered the door himself — sixty or so, silver-haired, hands clean, wearing the soft cardigan of a man whose working days were done.

"Yes? Can I help you?"

"I'm researching land records," Tomas said, and heard how easily the lie came, smooth as his father's voice. "From the capital. The Halvard parcels, near the salt fields. I understand your family has farmed adjacent land for generations."

Something crossed the old man's face. Not guilt. Tomas watched for it, watched the way a bird-watcher waits for a rare species, and it did not come. What came instead was a kind of polite fog, a settling of the features into mild incomprehension, like frost forming on a window between him and the world.

"Halvard," Vestergaard repeated. "There was a family of that name. Fishers." He said *fishers* the way other men said *strangers*. "Long time ago now. They're gone."

"Gone where?"

The old man's smile was genuinely pleasant. "The sea keeps what it takes, young man. You'll know that, being from the capital — you people write it in your books." And then, before Tomas could shape another question: "Is there anything else? My wife's expecting the fish delivery."

The door closed with the softness of good joinery.

Tomas stood on the step a long moment, looking at the greenhouse, at the tomatoes fattening behind glass in February, at the dog dreaming its fat dreams. Somewhere under this ground, or beside it, or just beyond the wall, were men who should have inherited this. Men who had gone out in a storm because the season demanded it and the price of herring did, and who had come back as wreckage and grief, and whose absence had been converted — efficiently, legally, within eighteen months — into whitewash and glass and a dog asleep in the sun.

He walked on.

* * *

The second house was worse, because the woman who answered was kind.

Her name was Fru Dahl, and she was Vestergaard's sister-in-law, and she invited Tomas in for coffee because the morning was cold and he looked pale, she said, and no one should walk these streets pale and unfed. Her parlor was small and immaculate, crowded with photographs in silver frames — children, grandchildren, a confirmation group squinting into the sun. On the mantel stood a model fishing boat, hand-rigged, its sails set, riding an invisible swell on the polished wood.

"That was my husband's," she said, following his eyes. "He built her the winter after—" She paused. Refilled his cup without asking. "After the bad year. He said every man ought to keep a boat he can't lose."

"The drownings," Tomas said quietly. "Of 1916. Were you here?"

"Oh yes. We were all here." She settled into her chair with the sigh of a woman preparing to be agreeable about something disagreeable. "Terrible time. Terrible. The whole coast mourned. My husband rowed in the search boats three days running; he never got warm again properly, poor soul. Died in 'eighteen, his chest." She looked at the model boat, and for a moment — a single, flickering moment — something true surfaced in her face, some cold water beneath all that warmth. Then it sank again, deliberately, like a diver choosing the deep over the air. "But you mustn't dwell on such things. The war's ended. We're to look forward now. That's what the papers say, and the papers aren't wrong about everything."

"Their families," Tomas pressed gently. "The Halvards, the Solveigsens, the Katrines' people. Their widows. Did anyone help them?"

Fru Dahl stirred her coffee long after there was anything to stir. "People were given what people were owed," she said at last, in the tone of a recitation, and Tomas recognized it — the flat cadence of a sentence that has been handed down, approved, rehearsed. A sentence with a stamp on it. "There was compensation. Proper channels. The magistrate saw to everything personally, very fairly, everyone said so."

Everyone said so. Of course everyone said so. His father had made sure of it, and the town had made sure of wanting to be sure, because certainty was cheaper than doubt and doubt cost more than anyone here seemed willing to pay.

He thanked her for the coffee. At the door she caught his sleeve, and her grip was surprisingly strong, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Young man. Whatever you're writing — write it kindly. This town buried enough in that year. Don't make us dig."

Then she shut her door, and the latch clicked, and Tomas stood in the street understanding that he had just received both a warning and a confession, wrapped in the same thin paper, tied with the same kind string.

* * *

At midday he found the church, and the churchyard, and the truth the town kept best.

The graves of the drowned were there — he found them by the dates, three clusters of stones along the north wall where the light rarely reached. But the stones themselves troubled him. They were small, smaller than the others in the yard, granite gone soft-edged with lichen, and several bore only initials where names should have been. One read simply: *For those the sea returned.* No names. No dates beyond the year. No *beloved father*, no *rest in peace*, none of the furniture of mourning.

Beside them, separated by a gravel path no wider than a boot, rose the newer stones — the town's own dead, tall marble slabs with gilt lettering and carved anchors and weeping willows cut deep enough to hold rainwater. Even the dead of Vesterhavn had been sorted. Even the dead had been assigned their places: the drowned to the north wall, in shorthand, and the town to the open ground, in full script, facing the morning.

Tomas knelt at the smallest stone and brushed the lichen from the initials with his thumb. *H.H.* Halvard Halvardsen, perhaps, or Halvard's son. A man reduced by erosion and omission to two letters, while forty paces away a merchant's wife lay under four lines of marble praising her charity.

He thought of Maren, walking her grandfather's salt fields on the island, reading inheritance out of the ground itself. He thought of the oilskin packet sleeping under the floorboard back at the inn, nine pages of certified truth that weighed less than this pebble of lichen under his fingernail. Paper against stone. Names against initials. It seemed impossible that paper could win. It had seemed impossible to him all his life that anything could stand against his father, whose signature moved property the way God's finger had once moved seas.

And yet the courier had crossed the water to deliver it. And yet Ada had sewn copies into a dying man's coat. Small things, moving against great ones. A lamp against a storm.

"Tomas Dunmore."

The voice came from behind him, and it was not a stranger's.

* * *

He turned too fast, blood drumming. Standing at the churchyard gate in a good wool coat and a fur hat was a man he hadn't seen in eleven years — Aldric's clerk, no, more than a clerk now, Aldric's right hand in the tollhouse and the tithe office and whatever else the cousin's fingers had learned to work. Soren Vask. He had been at the house in the capital often enough in Tomas's boyhood, carrying papers, drinking his father's brandy, laughing at his father's jokes with the precise volume of a man measuring his own advancement.

"Soren." He kept his voice level. Behind him, gravely, the north wall kept its silence. "You're far from the tollhouse."

"The tollhouse comes to me, these days. I audit." Vask smiled. It was a fine smile, well maintained, like the greenhouse glass. "And imagine — auditing the churchyard rolls and finding a Dunmore among the stones. Though not, I think, among the buried." His eyes went past Tomas to the small grave, to the initials, and lingered there one heartbeat too long. "Paying respects?"

"Paying attention," Tomas said. "I'm told the capital values accurate records."

"Accuracy." Vask tasted the word. "A dangerous appetite. Hereabouts we prefer memory. Memory is kinder — it forgets what it's told to." He stepped closer, boots crunching the frozen gravel, and lowered his voice to the register men used for transactions. "Your father wrote to me about you. He worries. He says you've fallen in with island people, with grievance-keepers. He asks that if you hear from certain parties — clerks, couriers, that sort — you'll send word home. As a son should."

The cold went through Tomas like a spike of ice through plank seams. So the letters had already flown ahead of them. So his father's network reached even here, even into a churchyard, even into the mouth of a smiling man standing on the bones of the erased.

"And if I don't send word?"

Vask shrugged pleasantly, adjusting his gloves. "Then I suppose we'll both continue our audits, and the sea will keep what it takes." He touched his hat brim. "Give my regards to the keeper woman. She's braver than the town remembers. That's her misfortune — and yours."

He walked away up the path, unhurried, a prosperous man in a prosperous town, and Tomas stood alone among the initials and understood three things at once, each arriving like a wave behind the wave behind the wave.

That Vask suspected. That Vask would sell what he suspected to whichever buyer paid most. And that somewhere in this town of geraniums and kind women and tidy lies, the clock his father had wound years ago was still running, ticking toward them through every comfortable street.

Behind him, the north wall held its two-lettered silence. Eleven days, the newspapers said, until the signing. Eleven days for paper to outrun stone.

He turned toward the inn, walking fast now, and the town looked away from him on every side — the curtains, the shopkeepers, the bright blank windows — and he understood at last that this was not indifference. It was discipline. The whole street, practicing its forgetting in unison, like a choir.

And somewhere behind one of those curtained windows, he was nearly certain, someone was already composing the letter that would name them.
