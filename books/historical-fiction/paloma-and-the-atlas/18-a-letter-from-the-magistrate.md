## 18. A Letter from the Magistrate

The letter came on a morning of iron-colored light, when the harbor water lay so still it looked poured rather than fallen.

Tomas saw the courier first from the window of the inn's back room — a boy on a wet gray horse, riding up from the ferry road with the particular urgency of someone paid by the mile rather than by the hour. The horse's breath steamed. The boy's satchel was government leather, brass-buckled, the kind Tomas had grown up watching cross his father's study threshold twice a day for twenty years. He knew that leather the way other men knew their mother's voice.

He did not go down to meet it. He stood at the window and let the knock come, and let the innkeeper's wife climb the stairs with her careful, apologetic tread, and let her hold out the envelope with both hands as though it might be hot.

"From the capital," she said. "Marked urgent."

"Thank you, Frau Behrens."

She lingered. She was a broad, kind woman with flour to her elbows and a widow's calm, and she looked at him the way half the town had begun looking at him since he'd arrived with a sick clerk and a lighthouse keeper and no satisfactory explanation for any of it. "There's bread down when you want it," she said, and left.

Tomas turned the envelope over. The wax seal was unbroken — his father's seal, the scales of justice pressed into crimson, slightly off-center because the old man never could be bothered to center anything he considered beneath him. The handwriting on the front was not his father's. It was the study hand, the secretary's, the neat copperplate that had copied out Tomas's school reports and, later, the verdicts.

*To T. Vosstraat, Esq., c/o the Harbor Inn, Merelstad.*

His father did not know where he was staying. Someone had told him. That was its own small cold fact, worth setting aside and examining later: the magistrate's reach extended even here, even now, even to the address of a son who had left home under a cloud two years ago and written exactly one letter since.

Tomas sat on the bed's edge. The springs complained. Downstairs, Frau Behrens was arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of cod, and the ordinary noise of it steadied him the way a handrail steadies a man on stairs he has climbed drunk.

He broke the seal.

---

The letter was three pages. His father's own hand this time, once past the salutation — which meant the old man had dismissed the secretary and written it himself, which meant it mattered, which meant it was dangerous. His father's handwriting was smaller than men expected, cramped and precise, slanting forward like a runner leaning into wind. Tomas had seen it last across the top of a verdict sentencing a smuggler to four years' labor, and before that across the inside of a birthday card that said nothing but *Your mother would be proud of your marks.* The same hand. It had never learned the difference between those documents, or had never cared to.

*My dear Tomas,*

*I will not insult you with surprise at your silence, nor with reproach, which I am told fathers are supposed to employ and which I have never found useful between intelligent men.*

So it began. *Intelligent men.* The old flattery, the old framing: whatever followed would be presented not as plea or threat but as a proposition between equals, though they both knew the scales were weighted and always had been.

*Word reaches me — as word reaches me of most things, eventually, imperfectly, through channels I neither control nor entirely trust — that you have been seen on the coast road in company with one Lucan Reyes, formerly of the Records Office, and that Reyes is gravely ill, and that you have taken rooms in Merelstad under circumstances the gossips find interesting.*

*I confess I do not know what Reyes carries or whether he carries anything at all. A records clerk who flees toward the provinces in winter is more likely fleeing a debt or a woman than carrying state secrets. But I know you, my son. I know that if there were something to carry, you would be the man standing beside the carrier, because you have always stood beside lost causes the way other men stand beside hearths — for warmth, out of habit, without much thought as to what the burning costs.*

Tomas read that sentence twice. It was very good. It was the kind of sentence his father wrote in closing arguments, the kind that made young lawyers in the gallery lean forward and older judges nod slowly — an insult so precisely shaped it felt like insight. *You have always stood beside lost causes.* As though the drownings were a cause. As though Maren Ostergaard's grandfather's fields were an argument. As though three families erased from the rolls after their boats went down were a philosophy Tomas had adopted out of sentimental weakness rather than the simple arithmetic of what had been done.

*Let me speak plainly, then, as one intelligent man to another.*

Here it came. He could feel it coming the way sailors on the island coast — Ada's coast — claimed to feel weather in old breaks and healed wounds.

*The special courts close within the fortnight. When the armistice terms are ratified, every commission constituted under wartime authority dissolves, and the records those commissions touched pass into general archive, where they become paper among paper — consulted by doctoral candidates, ignored by everyone else. Whatever Reyes believes he is accomplishing, the clock is against him, and I confess I do not fully understand why he is running at all. Perhaps fever has addled him. Perhaps you have encouraged him. You were always susceptible to moral drama, Tomas; you get it from your mother.*

There. Her name, deployed like a card played from the bottom of the deck. His mother, dead nine years, who had indeed been prone to moral drama — who had wept at verdicts, who had once refused to attend a dinner because the guest of honor owned mills built on seized land. His father invoked her the way other men invoked saints: selectively, and only when useful.

*Now. To the matter of your future.*

Tomas set the letter on his knee. Through the wall he could hear Lucan coughing — that deep, wet, ruinous cough that had worsened by degrees all week until Dr. Halloran, crossing briefly on the morning ice before it turned, had shaken his head and said only, "Keep him warm, keep him sitting up, and send for his people." There were no people to send for. There was only Tomas, and Ada, and a girl named Ilsa who had appeared out of nowhere with her grandmother's jaw and her grandmother's fearlessness, and eleven days — fewer now — until the courts closed forever.

He read on.

*I have spent thirty years building what I have built. A judiciary does not forgive weakness, but it does reward patience, and I have been patient with you — with your resignation from the bench track, with your wandering, with your silence. I have kept your name clean. Do you understand what that means? In the capital, names are currency, and yours has sat untouched in a strongbox while lesser men debased theirs. I have done this not from sentiment. I have done it because a father's work should pass to his son, and because I believe — despite everything, despite the sulking years and the wrong-headed friendships — that you have the mind for it.*

*The Coastal Circuit opens in spring. A judgeship, real and permanent, with jurisdiction from the river mouth to the border lights. I can place you in it. Not recommend — place. There are men who owe me the way tides owe the moon, and one of them chairs the appointments board.*

*What I ask in return is nothing you have not already, in your heart, decided to do.*

Tomas laughed. It came out of him alone in the room, short and ugly, startling a gull off the sill outside. *Nothing you have not already decided.* The old magic trick: make the concession sound like the reader's own idea, so that surrender arrives wearing the costume of agreement. How many defendants had signed confessions shaped just so? How many witnesses had recanted in words his father had drafted for them, gently, reasonably, over tea?

*Reyes's papers — whatever they are — belong in a fire. Not because they contain falsehoods; falsehoods I could survive. Because they contain a version of events that serves no living person. The drowned are drowned, Tomas. No document raises them. The families gone under in 'sixteen went down to weather and war and the plain indifference of the sea, and grief has made some of the survivors hungry for a villain, and I will not pretend to you that I have never given them one. But a version of events that serves no living person serves only the dead, and the dead do not vote, do not pay taxes, do not need the coast kept orderly through the hard winter coming. Order is not cruelty. Order is the only charity a state can practice at scale, and I have practiced it for thirty years, and I am asking my son to help me practice it a little longer.*

*A fire, Tomas. Then come to the capital. Then take the circuit. And in ten years, when they write the history of this province's reconstruction, your name will be in the first paragraph and mine in the second, and neither of us will ever again have to say the word Reyes.*

*Your father,
Aldous Vosstraat*

---

Tomas read the whole thing a second time, standing now, because sitting had become unbearable.

It was masterful. That was the horror of it — not that it lied, but how little it needed to. Every sentence was technically defensible. *Order is not cruelty.* Who could prove otherwise? *The drowned are drowned.* God, the sheer load-bearing honesty of it — the truth used as a keystone for the lie built on top. His father had constructed a cathedral of reasonableness around a single missing beam, and the missing beam was three families' names scraped from the inheritance rolls in the weeks after the Paloma boats went down, while the widows were still sewing mourning bands and the sea was still returning what it took.

And the judgeship. The judgeship was real. That was the worst part, the part that lodged under his ribs like a fishbone. His father did not offer hollow things; hollow things wasted leverage. The Coastal Circuit was real, the appointment was real, the future laid out in the first paragraph of some provincial history was — if not real — then plausible enough to live inside comfortably for forty years. A warm room. A respected chair. A name in a strongbox, kept clean.

All it cost was a match and a packet of oilskin and paper.

Tomas carried the letter to the small stove in the corner of the room. Lucan slept — or drifted in the shallow, rattling sleep that was not rest — propped against two pillows, his cheeks burned red above a gray beard of three days' growth. On the table between them lay the copies: the certified duplicates sewn into the lining of a dead man's coat, and beside them, wrapped in a linen cloth, the working notes Ada had made by lamplight, her small fierce handwriting listing dates and registry numbers with the exactness of a woman who had spent years keeping a log against fog.

He thought of Ada reading her husband's death back to herself every night in that logbook, and telling the neighbors he'd died quick, when the truth had taken six hours. He thought of Maren Ostergaard walking her grandfather's salt fields leased to the magistrate's cousin, stooping to pick up stones from soil she owned on no map anymore. He thought of Sergeant Brandt at the mainland gate, stamping them through without looking up, buying mercy with complicity and asking only to be forgotten for it.

*Order is not cruelty.*

"No," Tomas said aloud, to the empty room, to the sleeping man, to his father across two hundred miles of frozen road. "But it isn't innocence either."

Lucan stirred. His eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding the ceiling instead of the world. "Is it lit?" he asked — the same question, every waking now, the same question he'd asked Ada at dawn. Whether the lamp was lit. Whether somewhere the light was burning that was supposed to burn.

"It's lit," Tomas said. "Go back to sleep."

Lucan slept.

Tomas held the letter over the stove. The paper was good stock, heavy, capital-made, and it resisted a moment — the way good things resist — before the corner caught and the flame walked up the page. He watched *My dear Tomas* curl and blacken. He watched *intelligent men* go. He watched the judgeship burn last, the ink lifting off the words *Coastal Circuit* in a brief ghost of silver before the flame took that too, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that he was not burning an offer. He was burning the last version of himself anyone had ever offered to love.

The ash settled in the grate, fine and gray, three pages collapsed into a spoonful.

Behind him, on the table, the linen-wrapped packet waited with the patience of paper — patient as evidence, patient as the dead, patient as the truth that serves no living person except, his father had never quite grasped, the ones still to come.

Downstairs, the inn door banged open and Frau Behrens called up the stairwell, her voice pitched wrong, too fast:

"Herr Vosstraat! Herr Vosstraat — there's a soldier at the door asking after you by name."
