## 19. Lucan Dies

The fever broke in the small hours the way a wave breaks — not all at once, but with that terrible gathering pause before it comes down. I had sat with dying men before. I had sat with one husband in particular, in this same room, in this same chair by the same narrow window, and I had learned then what no one tells you: that the last night is not a single thing but many nights folded together, each hour its own small country with its own weather, and you cross them all without a map.

Lucan Reyes lay in my bed under three blankets and the good wool coat Tomas had brought up from the mainland two winters ago and never reclaimed. His breathing had changed around midnight. It had gone from the wet, laboring pull of the past four days to something thinner, higher, like wind through a keyhole, and I knew what that meant even before I put my hand on his forehead and felt the heat there had gone strange — not less, exactly, but scattered, as if his body had stopped bothering to hold itself together and was letting the warmth leak out wherever it pleased.

Tomas slept in the chair by the stove, or pretended to. He had not truly slept since the third day, when the doctor's letter came back from the mainland unopened because no boat could cross and no doctor would risk the ice on the causeway for a stranger with no name anyone recognized. *No boat could cross* — I had written those words myself a hundred times in the keeper's log over the years, and they had always meant safety. Now they meant a death warrant written in my own hand.

I got up quietly and went to the lamp room.

It is a habit of thirty years, that climb. Forty-one steps, worn hollow at the ninth and the twenty-third where my feet have fallen ten thousand times. The tower held the cold of the whole winter in its stones, and my breath went up ahead of me like a small ghost showing the way. At the top I did not light the lamp. There was nothing to light it for. The shipping lanes had been empty for weeks; the fishing fleet was laid up under canvas; the war had taken most of the boats and the armistice talks in the capital were taking the rest of the reason to sail anywhere at all. The Admiralty notice said the light would be extinguished for the season, and I had obeyed it, as I obeyed everything, and the great lens sat dark in its cage like a sleeping animal.

But I stood there anyway, in the black glass room, and looked out at the sea.

Dawn was still an hour off. The water below the rocks moved with that slow, muscular heave it gets in deep cold, black and silver at once, and far out — so far I might have imagined it — there was a smudge of paler gray that was either the coming morning or a cloud deciding to become one. I thought: *somewhere out there is the channel he crossed.* Somewhere out there, four nights ago, a boat had gone down or been sunk or simply given up, and this man had wrapped his papers in oilskin and tied them to his own body and jumped into water cold enough to stop a heart in ten minutes, because the papers mattered more than the heart. Because there was a clock running in some ministry in the capital, and paper has a longer life than flesh only if someone carries it fast enough.

He had carried it fast enough. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. He had done everything right, and the sea had taken its toll anyway, and now the papers lay wrapped in sacking under the floorboards of my kitchen while the man who stole them died twelve feet above them in my bed.

I heard Tomas on the stairs behind me. He climbed slowly; he had learned the tower's rhythm quickly, the way mainlanders do, as if apologizing to it.

"He's asking for you," Tomas said. His voice was rough from disuse. "Not me. You."

"What does he say?"

"He says—" Tomas stopped, and I heard him choose between the words. "He says he wants to know if the lamp is lit."

I turned and looked at him. In the dark of the lantern room his face was mostly shadow, but I could see the question in it, the same question I felt: why that, of all things. A man at the end of his life, a man who had carried state secrets through a frozen sea, and what he wanted to know was whether a light was burning on a rock island where no ship needed one.

"Go down," I said. "I'll come."

* * *

He was smaller. That is the first thing I understood when I knelt beside the bed. Four days ago they had pulled a full-grown man off my rocks, blue-lipped and heavy with seawater, and in four days the fever had burned him down to something a strong wind could have lifted. His face had gone fine-boned, almost young. The clerk's ink stains were still on his fingers — I noticed that, absurdly, the way you notice a hat left on a grave. Whatever else Lucan Reyes had been, he had been a man who worked with his hands in ink until the end, and the end had not bothered to wash them.

His eyes found me before I finished kneeling. They were clear. That was the worst part. Fevered men see things that are not there, call for mothers dead thirty years, argue with generals in the corners of the room. Lucan saw me. Only me. And he knew exactly where he was, which meant he knew exactly what was happening to him, and he looked at me with the calm of a man who has already done his sums.

"Ishikawa," he said. My name. He had whispered it the first morning on the rocks, half-drowned, delirious, and we had never understood it — how a records clerk from the capital archives knew the lighthouse keeper of Paloma Island by name. Tomas had pressed him about it twice, gently, and both times Lucan had smiled the small private smile of a man holding one card too valuable to play. Now, with dawn coming and the card worthless, I waited for him to spend it.

"The lamp," he said instead. "Is it lit?"

"No," I said. Because I do not lie to dying men. I had lied enough in my life — small lies, merciful lies, the silences I had built into walls around my neighbors' griefs — but I had made myself one promise at my husband's graveside, and it was this: the truth, at the end, for everyone who asked for it straight. "The season's closed. No ships. The Admiralty order says—"

"Light it."

I looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at the floor. Outside, the first true gray of dawn was seeping into the window glass, and somewhere below us the sea was working at the rocks with its patient hands.

"It's a long climb," I said. "And it means nothing. There's nothing out there to see it."

"There's me," Lucan said.

I stayed very still. The stove ticked. Down in the kitchen the oilskin packet lay under its floorboard, forty pages of certified lies undone, heavier than iron, and this man above them had maybe an hour in him, maybe two, and he wanted a light.

"When I was in the archives," he said, and his voice was thinning now, fraying at the edges like old rope, "there was a file. Shipping casualties, wartime. Your husband's boat. The *Paloma Star*, lost with all hands, spring of 'sixteen." His breath caught, steadied. "There was a report attached. A rescue report. A sergeant — Brandt, coastal patrol — pulled from the water. Said a man named Dunmore—" his eyes flicked to Tomas, and something passed over his face, recognition rearranging itself one last time, "—no. Not Dunmore. Said a man had gone back into the wreck twice. Twice. For strangers. The second time he didn't come out."

Tomas made a sound low in his throat.

"They flagged the file," Lucan went on. "Special commendation, posthumous. And your name was in it. Widow, resident keeper, Paloma Light. I read it six years ago, Ishikawa. I was twenty-three and I thought it was just a file." He coughed, deep and wet, and when it passed he was quieter. "Then I found the inheritance erasures. Three families. The drownings. Same waters, same magistrate signing away their land while the boats were still being dragged ashore. And I remembered the file. I remembered that the man who went back into the wreck had a wife keeping a light on the island nearest to where it happened. And I thought — if I can get the papers to her, she'll know what to do. Because she's the kind of person who goes back in."

The room was very quiet. I could hear Tomas breathing, unevenly, the way men breathe when they are trying not to make a sound.

"So light the lamp," Lucan said. "Not for ships. For the record. Let there be one honest light burning over this coast tonight, even if nobody logs it. Even if it's only for me." His hand moved on the blanket, an inch, asking. I took it. His fingers were cool now, not hot, and ink-stained, and lighter than a bird's.

"All right," I said.

* * *

I lit it properly. That matters to me, writing it here, that it be known I lit it properly, not as a gesture but as the work itself, the whole rite of it, because a lamp lit halfway is worse than a dark one — it promises and fails, and the sea punishes promises that fail.

Forty-one steps down, and Tomas came with me this time, and we worked in the old choreography though neither of us spoke it aloud. I primed the mantle while he checked the fuel line, and his hands were clumsy with feeling and mine were steady, and between us the lamp woke the way it always wakes: the small sulfur bloom of the match, the soft gasp of the vapor catching, then the slow swelling of light inside the lens until the whole room filled with it and every prism threw its piece outward and the beam went leaping across the water, white, absolute, swinging its long arm over the black sea as it had swung for sixty years.

We stood in the gallery and watched it turn. The wind was coming up lightly from the northeast, carrying the smell of ice, and the beam swept out and swept back, out and back, touching nothing, saving no one, illuminating miles of empty water and the first pale seam of dawn along the east, and it was the most useless light I had ever lit and the truest.

"He can't see it from the bedroom," Tomas said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it.

"He doesn't need to see it," I said. "He needs to know."

We went down. I stood in the doorway of the bedroom so the lamplight fell across the bed in its slow rotation — bright, dimming, bright again, the rhythm of it, the pulse — and Lucan watched the ceiling take the light and give it back, and he smiled.

"Yes," he said. "That's it. That's—" and then he said something in another language, low, maybe a prayer, maybe just a word for *good*, and his hand loosened in mine not suddenly but gradually, the way a knot eases, the way tide goes out, and between one sweep of the beam and the next he was not using it anymore.

The lamp kept turning. Of course it kept turning. That is the whole of what a lighthouse knows: the light does not grieve, the light does not stop, the light burns because burning is its work and someone must tend it, and I stood there in the rotating brightness holding the cooling hand of a man who had crossed a frozen sea for the sake of three drowned families he had never met, and I made the vow the way you make vows at funerals — silently, specifically, knowing exactly what it will cost.

*I will carry them,* I vowed. To him, to the sea, to my husband's memory, to whatever keeps accounts on this coast. *The papers will reach the capital before the courts close. I don't care what it costs, and I name the cost now so there can be no bargaining later.*

Outside, the sun came up over the water, ordinary and enormous, and the beam faded into daylight as beams do, still turning, still true, simply no longer needed to be seen.

* * *

Tomas washed the body. I want that recorded too, because it was not required of him — a magistrate's son, raised with servants, a man who had spent four days flinching from this sickroom — and he rolled his sleeves and heated water and did it thoroughly and gently, and when he came to the ink-stained fingers he washed them last and longest, carefully, as if the ink were something Lucan had earned.

Maren came up from the village before noon, having heard — news travels on a sealed island faster than any ferry could carry it, borne on some current of its own — and she stood in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat and looked at the bed and said, "One of ours, then. However he came to us, he died doing our work." And she crossed herself in the old way, the pre-war way, the way the church had frowned on for a generation, and left bread and salt on the sill, which is what this coast does for its dead.

It was Maren who said it, standing at the door with the wind pulling at her shawl: "Consecrated ground won't take him. No papers, no parish, no priest will cross the ice. They'll bury him at the crossroads like a suicide, or not at all."

"Then we bury him ourselves," I said.

She looked at me a long moment. "Where?"

And I found I already knew, the way you know a thing you've been walking toward all week. "Above the high-tide line, on the point. Where the light reaches first at dusk." Where the beam would pass over him every night of the winter, whether the world approved or not. Whether any record acknowledged him or not. Some ground you consecrate yourself, with work and light and the refusal to look away.

Maren nodded slowly, and then she said the thing that turned the day from grief toward trouble, because trouble is patient and waits its turn behind mourning:

"There's a girl looking for you, Ada. Came over the ice this morning before it softened — walked the causeway alone, sixteen years old and mad as March. Maren's blood, my son's daughter." She paused, and I saw fear move under her sternness like a fish under ice. "Ilsa. She says she found the loose floorboard in your kitchen. She says she knows what's under it. And she says—" Maren's voice dropped, though there was no one on the path but wind, "—she says she wants to talk about who's going to carry them to the capital. Because she's counted the days till the signing, and she says we've got eleven, and she doesn't think we should waste a single one."
