## 20. Burial Without Records

**Ada**

We buried Lucan Reyes on the third morning after his death, in the ground above the tide line where the consecration of any church ends and the salt begins.

There is a strip of such land on every coast — the churchyards keep their hedges tidy and their souls sorted, and beyond them lies the other country: the wrack line, the marram grass, the sand that takes what the sea refuses to give back. Suicides went there once. Pirates. The unbaptized. On our island we called it the grey acre, though no one had needed to use it in living memory, because no one had died on the island in winter since the bridge was built. The dead waited for the thaw like everything else did. They lay in the icehouse or the cold room, decently sheeted, until the ferry came.

Lucan could not wait eleven days. Fever does not respect calendars, and neither, it turned out, does decay, and so on the third morning Tomas and I carried him out ourselves.

He was lighter than I expected. The sea had already begun its accounting, taking back what it could from him even through wool and canvas. We wrapped him in his own oilskin coat — the coat Maren's granddaughter would later search, though none of us knew her name yet — and beneath the oilskin, sewn flat against his chest where a man might carry a letter he meant to answer, were the copies. I had unpicked them the night before he died, while Tomas held the lamp steady and Lucan drifted in and out of a fever-dream about filing cabinets. Forty-one pages. I read every one by lamplight before I sewed them back, because I wanted the weight of them in my hands, and because if I was going to bury a man with contraband against his heart I wanted to know what his heart had been carrying.

The ground was hard as iron. Tomas hacked at it with a mattock while I stood with my hat off and the wind pulling at my hair, and between strokes he said nothing, and I said nothing, and the silence between us had become by then a kind of language — the language of two people who have stopped pretending the thing they are doing is ordinary.

We had no priest. There is no priest on the island; the mainland curate crossed twice a year at most, and the gates were chained, and even if they hadn't been, even if the causeway ran dry and golden under a kind sun, no curate in this district would have spoken words over a man who died carrying stolen state papers in his coat. So I spoke them instead. I am not a religious woman. My husband was, in his quiet way — he crossed himself at wrecks and thanked God for calms — but grief had burned most of that out of me and left only the habit of speaking to the sea, which is nearly the same thing and costs less.

I said: "Lucan Reyes, clerk of records, born wherever he was born, who chose the truth over his safety and lost." And then, because the wind took the first words almost whole, I said them again, louder, and Tomas set down the mattock and bowed his head, and for a moment we looked like mourners anywhere, except for the shovels and the oilskin and the smell of the tide coming in over the flats.

"He asked me last night," I told Tomas afterward, "whether the lamp was lit."

"I know. I was there."

"It wasn't lit. There are no ships. There hasn't been a ship worth lighting for since the war emptied these waters." I looked up at the tower, white against the low grey sky. "I lit it anyway. He heard the mechanism turn. That was enough for him. He smiled like a man whose ledger finally balances."

Tomas wiped his face with his sleeve — sweat, or something else, I didn't ask — and said, "My father will have him declared a thief. When this is over. A clerk who absconded with state documents, found drowned, buried without rites by persons unknown. There will be an official account. It will say nothing true."

"Then let's make sure the official account isn't the only one," I said. And that was when we heard the footsteps on the frozen path.

* * *

**Tomas**

She came down the causeway road like something out of one of the ballads my nurse used to sing and I used to pretend to be too old for: small, hooded, walking with the deliberate flat-footed stride of someone who has learned on ice that confidence is more dangerous than caution. Behind her the causeway stretched back toward the island, glazed and treacherous, the tide pools sealed under plates of grey glass. She had crossed it alone. At dawn. At sixteen.

Maren reached her first. I watched the old woman take the girl's arm — half welcome, half arrest — and speak to her quickly, low, the way you speak to a horse that has spooked. The girl shook her off. Not rudely. Firmly. Then she saw us standing over the raw earth of the grave, and she stopped, and I saw her understand in one long breath what the turned soil meant.

"Who died?" she called across the distance.

"A friend," Ada said.

The girl came on. Up close she was all Maren — the same sharp hinge of the jaw, the same eyes the colour of shallow water over slate — but younger, unweathered, the stubbornness not yet worn smooth by decades. She looked at the grave. She looked at the oilskin showing through where we hadn't finished covering it. She looked at me longest, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being inventoried.

"You're the magistrate's son," she said.

"Tomas."

"Ilsa." She didn't offer a hand. Her fingers were blue at the tips; she'd crossed four miles of ice without gloves, or had given them to something else. "I know what's buried with your friend."

Ada went very still beside me. It is a particular stillness, lighthouse-keeper stillness, the stillness of a woman who has spent years learning that sudden movement in a storm makes everything worse. "Ilsa," Maren started, "you shouldn't—"

"I found the floorboard." The girl's voice didn't rise. That frightened me more than shouting would have. "You were all so careful, and nobody thought about the kitchen. I go to the house Tuesdays to bring milk, and Tuesday the board was loose, and I'm not stupid, and I'm not a child, whatever everyone keeps deciding without asking me." She crouched suddenly at the grave's edge and put her bare hand flat on the frozen spoil, as if taking an oath on it. "I read all forty-one pages. Twice. I know about my grandfather's lease. I know about the Olsens' field and the Brandviks' mooring rights. I know the magistrate signed three erasures in one sitting and dated them six months apart to look like clerical drift." She stood. "And I've counted the days to the armistice signing. Eleven left. After that the special courts close, and those papers become history instead of law, and history doesn't get anyone their fields back."

The wind moved through the marram grass with a sound like paper turning. Nobody spoke. Somewhere below the ice the tide was working, patient, indifferent.

"You buried the copies with him," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question. "That's why the coat. That's the safest place in the world, isn't it — inside a dead man no one wants to dig up." Something flickered across her face that I couldn't read, and then she said, quietly, almost gently: "But dead men can't walk to the capital. And I can."

* * *

**Ada**

I want to record, here, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because the hearing will ask me eventually and I refuse to lie even in my own head.

I felt terror. Not the clean terror of storms — I know that one, I keep it fed and manageable like a work animal — but the other kind, the human kind, which is the sight of something precious standing on ice that you know better than she does. Because that is what I saw. Not a courier. Not an ally. A child with Maren's jaw and her grandfather's field in her eyes, standing on ice I had tested stone by stone two nights running and still did not trust.

And underneath the terror, worse: recognition. Because I remembered being sixteen, and certain, and ready to carry anything.

"Ilsa," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "come away from the grave. Come to the house. We'll talk where there's tea, and where the whole coastline can't hear you planning treason."

"It's not treason," she said, falling into step beside me anyway. "It's filing. That's what Lucan would have said. He'd have liked that word." She glanced back once at the half-covered mound. "Did he suffer?"

"Yes."

"Was he brave?"

"At the end? Yes. At the beginning too, probably. Bravery usually starts earlier than anyone notices."

She nodded slowly, filing that away as well, and walked with us up the path, and behind us Tomas finished filling the grave alone, the sound of the mattock following us like slow applause.

* * *

**Tomas**

In the kitchen, while Ada heated water and Maren sat by the stove radiating disapproval like a second fire, Ilsa laid it all out. She had prepared. That was the thing that kept striking me — she had *prepared*. She'd made lists. She produced one from inside her coat, written in a schoolgirl's copperplate, each item numbered:

1. Copies (forty-one pp., sewn location known).
2. Route (causeway — tollhouse — town — capital road — archive).
3. Checkpoints (three; names supplied by Tomas Dunmore, per conversation overheard 14 December).
4. Deadline (signing, eleven days minus travel).

She'd overheard my conversation with Ada. Of course she had. Walls on this island are paper and everyone under twenty moves through them like weather.

"You listed *me*," I said, "as a supply."

"You know the checkpoints. You know how officials think. You're the only person here who grew up inside that machine." She said it without malice, the way you'd note that someone knew horses. "Also, your father will be looking for you eventually. That's either a liability or a decoy. I haven't decided which yet."

Maren made a sound like a kettle taken off the heat. "Girl, you'll not speak to him that—"

"It's all right," I said, and found, surprisingly, that it was. There was a strange relief in being seen plainly, itemized, useful or useless. My father had spent my whole childhood telling me I was destined for greatness and treating me as furniture. This girl wanted me for my inventory. It was nearly restful.

"The copies stay with the originals," Ada said. She set the tea down — four cups, though Maren had made five trips' worth of noise about not staying — and sat, and folded her hands, and became the version of herself I had come to think of as the Keeper, the one who had held this island together through three winters of silences. "Nothing leaves this house split again. We tried splitting it. Splitting means two chances to lose everything and no chance to protect both."

"Then the originals travel too," Ilsa said immediately.

"No."

"Then—"

"*No.*" And then Ada did something I had never seen her do. She softened. Deliberately, visibly, like a lamp turned down. "Ilsa. Maren's granddaughter. Sit properly and listen to me, because I'm going to tell you the part nobody tells children." She waited until the girl sat. "Three families drowned in 'eleven. Not at sea — in the flood, in the night, because the seawall money went into a cousin's pocket, and the records that would have proved it went into the magistrate's drawer. Everyone on that island knows. Everyone has known for eight years. And everyone stayed silent, because silence was safe, and silence was cheap, and silence was what you did to keep the soldiers away and the leases intact and your children fed. I have told a hundred small lies to keep my neighbors safe. Some of them I'd tell again tomorrow. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"That you're a coward," Ilsa said.

"That silence is a *debt*," Ada said, "and debts compound. Every year we didn't speak, the interest grew. Eight years of interest is sitting in that floorboard. And now you — sixteen years old, no debt yet, nothing to lose that you can see — you want to walk into a system that ate three families and a records clerk, wearing a school satchel and your grandfather's temper." She leaned forward. "I won't stop you. I've decided that already, and Maren will hate me for it, and I'll live. But you don't go alone, and you don't go untrained, and you don't go until I say the ice and the route and the story are right. Eleven days is enough. Barely. If we start today."

The stove ticked. Outside, the light was already failing — December hoards its daylight like a miser — and somewhere far off the sea boomed against the causeway pilings, testing the gates.

Ilsa looked at the list in her lap. Then at Ada. Then, briefly, at me, with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the arithmetic of a person recalculating everything they'd planned around one new variable.

"You'll come with me?" she asked Ada.

"I'll come as far as the checkpoints allow. Further than that, maybe. I've spent three years keeping this island's silences." Ada rose, and took the loose floorboard's edge, and lifted it, and the lamplight fell into the dark gap where the packet lay wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping thing. "I find I'm done being careful with other people's secrets. Only mine own."

* * *

**Ada**

We worked past midnight, the three of us — Maren sewing, Ilsa memorizing, Tomas drafting the false papers that would dress a sixteen-year-old as a courier's assistant traveling under her uncle's authority, which was even true, in the way that mattered. The originals we rewrapped. But Ilsa argued one change through, and it was hers, and it was right: she would carry a single page — the first erasure, her grandfather's name — sewn into her satchel strap, separate from the main body. Proof of concept. Insurance. If the rest was seized, one page would survive, and one page in the registrar's stamp was enough to reopen everything.

"The secret's growing," Tomas said quietly to me near dawn, watching Ilsa copy the checkpoint roster out in her copperplate for the fourth time. "First us. Now her. Tomorrow someone else. Secrets multiply, Ada. That's how they kill you — not by being kept, but by needing more and more people to keep them."

"Or that's how they survive," I said. "A secret one person holds dies with the person. A secret a community carries gets where it's going."

Below us the tide was turning, and the ice on the causeway groaned once, long and low, like something enormous shifting in its sleep — or waking.
