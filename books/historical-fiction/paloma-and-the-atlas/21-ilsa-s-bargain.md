## 21. Ilsa's Bargain

I had been awake since before the gulls, which on the island in winter meant I had been awake since the dark itself, listening to the wind work at the shutters like a patient carpenter who would never finish his job but would never stop trying. Grandmother slept through it the way she slept through everything now — lightly, with one hand curled near her ear as though even in dreams she expected someone to come to the door with bad news and wanted to be ready.

I dressed in the cold. My fingers knew the buttons without my eyes. Down the ladder, past the room where Mother used to sew before her hands went stiff, into the kitchen where the last of yesterday's peat still breathed its slow orange heat in the grate. I put the kettle on not because I wanted tea but because the sound of water beginning to move is a kind of courage you can pour.

Lucan Reyes was dying in our back room. That was what everyone on the island said now, in the lowered voices people use for weather that has turned against them. *The courier is dying. The courier is dying and the papers are still here.* Eleven days, Tomas Dunmore had said, standing in our doorway two nights ago with frost melting off his coat like the man himself was thawing into something new. Eleven days until the armistice terms were signed and the old courts closed their ledgers forever, and every claim written in them — including ours, including Grandfather's fields, including my mother's name that was never entered anywhere because there was no mother left alive to enter it — would be sealed under ice for a hundred years.

Eleven days. And the causeway still too soft with ice to trust, and Lucan too far gone to walk ten steps, and Ada Ishikawa testing stones in the dark like a woman counting beads on a rosary made of granite.

I was sixteen years old, and I had decided something, and the deciding had happened so quietly inside me that I only noticed it this morning, watching steam climb the window glass: I was going to carry the papers myself.

* * *

Ada came down from the light just after seven, stamping snow off her boots in the doorway, bringing the sea in with her — salt and kelp and the iron smell of the lamp room. She looked older than she had in autumn. Not weaker. Older the way a cliff is older than the beach beneath it: something had been taken away and what remained stood harder.

"You're up," she said.

"I'm always up."

"You're always up because you're young. Being young is mostly a failure of imagination about how tired you're allowed to be." She hung her coat on the peg, and I watched her hands do it, those hands I had known my whole life — hands that had pulled me out of the water three years ago when the punt went over by the north rocks, hands that had held my head while I coughed half the harbor out of myself, hands that had kept a light burning through storms that unroofed churches on the mainland. I owed her my breath. Everyone said so. She never once mentioned it.

"Where are they?" I asked. "The copies."

Her eyes came to me slowly, the way a lighthouse beam comes around — not fast, not avoidable.

"They're where they need to be," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer you get." She filled her own cup from my kettle, uninvited, comfortable, the way family is comfortable. Then she sat across from me at the table and looked at me straight on, and I understood that she already knew why I'd asked. Maybe she'd known since the day Ilsa came ashore with the satchel hidden under my coat and my heart going like a gull in a net. Maybe grandmothers' daughters can smell a decision on a girl the way they smell rain coming.

"I've been thinking," I began.

"Don't."

"—that Lucan can't make the crossing."

"Ilsa."

"And Tomas can't go alone, because if he's stopped, they take him and the papers and nobody on this island knows where either of them ended up—"

"*Ilsa.*"

"—and Sergeant Brandt owes you, and Aldric is hedging, and maybe checkpoint two holds, but checkpoint three—" I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop, made myself breathe the way she'd taught me that day in the water, when I was drowning and panicking and she put her mouth against my ear and said, *breathe when I tell you, not when you want to.* "Checkpoint three is a stranger," I said, quieter. "A stranger who owes nobody anything. You said so yourself. So the plan has a hole in it shaped exactly like everything we can't control, and I am asking you to let me fill part of it."

Ada set her cup down. The ceramic made almost no sound against the wood, and somehow that was worse than if she'd slammed it.

"You're sixteen," she said.

"I'm sixteen and I found the hiding place you thought was secret, and I read every page of what's in it, and I can recite the parcel numbers of the drowned families' claims in order. Can Tomas? Can you?" I leaned forward. "I'm not asking to be brave. I'm asking to be useful. There's a difference and I know it."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the wind changed its mind and came around from the northeast, and somewhere below the cliffs the sea worked at the causeway stones with its cold gray thumbs.

"The copies are sewn into Lucan's coat lining," she said at last. "That's the plan. He goes because a dying man draws less suspicion than a live girl carrying documents, and because if they open the coat they find a fever case and a frightened clerk, which is a story soldiers believe."

"Lucan weighs nothing," I said. "Tomas told me. He couldn't stand at the window yesterday without holding the frame. What happens at checkpoint one when they ask the dying man to step down from the cart? What happens on the frozen causeway if the cart slips and Tomas has to choose between the man and the papers?" I shook my head. "You taught me to test every stone yourself. So test this one."

Something moved behind her eyes then — not anger. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing her own younger face wearing somebody else's stubbornness, and not enjoying the view.

"And if they search you," Ada said. "At any gate, any tollhouse, any muddy stretch of road where a bored soldier wants entertainment. A girl alone travels lighter than a cart, yes, but she also gets searched more thoroughly, because a girl alone is interesting. They will put their hands in your satchel. They will turn out your pockets. They may do worse than that, and I will say the word plainly because you demanded plain words: they may do worse."

"Then I won't carry them in the satchel."

"Then where?"

"In me," I said. "In the place no soldier thinks to look because it's the one place they're trained not to respect and therefore don't examine. In the schoolbooks. Nobody searches a girl's schoolwork. Nobody reads a sixteen-year-old's composition exercises all the way through. The pages are thin. Certified copies on archive paper — I've held them, they're thin as onion skin. Sewn flat between the leaves of my arithmetic book and my geography atlas, along the spines, and stitched with thread the same color as the binding. I've been practicing the stitching for four days on paper I ruined on purpose." I turned my palms up on the table. "Check my hands. Needle calluses. Go on."

She didn't take my hands. But she looked at them, and I saw her see it.

* * *

She didn't say yes that morning. I hadn't expected her to. People like Ada Ishikawa don't say yes to anything before they've walked all the way around it in the dark and counted what it costs.

So I spent the middle of the day being useful in the ordinary ways, which is another thing she taught me without ever saying it aloud: make yourself indispensable in small things and people stop noticing the large thing you're becoming. I carried peat. I fed the hens whose combs were frostbitten at the edges. I sat with Lucan for an hour in the back room while he drifted, his breathing doing that terrible double rhythm — a long silence, then two quick gasps, as if his body kept forgetting and remembering the bargain it had made.

His eyes opened while I was wringing the cloth for his forehead.

"The lamp," he whispered. It was the first full sentence he'd managed in a day. His voice sounded like paper being unfolded.

"It's lit," I said. "It's lit every night. She never misses."

He stared at me, or through me, at some ceiling far above our ceiling. "Eleven days," he said. Or maybe he said *eleven pages*. Fever makes its own arithmetic. "They have to arrive before... before the seal..." His hand came up off the blanket, trembling, and closed on my wrist with a strength that astonished me, that frightened me a little, the way a drowning man's grip frightens. "Promise me. Promise me it doesn't matter who carries them. Only that they arrive. Swear it."

I looked at his hand on my wrist. Thin as a bird's foot. And underneath the fever-glaze his eyes were clear, terribly clear, the way the sky is clear right before the coldest night of the year.

"I swear it," I said.

He smiled — a small, cracked thing, a door opening onto an empty house — and his hand slid away, and he slept, and I sat there with my pulse hammering and thought: *he just handed it to me.* Whatever the grown ones decided among themselves, the man whose life these papers had cost had just given them to whoever would carry them. To me, in this room, in this hour. Promises made to the dying are the strongest kind. Even the church says so, and the church is wrong about most things.

* * *

I cornered Tomas at dusk, down by the boat shed where he'd gone to be alone, because Tomas Dunmore was a man who went to be alone the way other men went to drink. He was looking at the causeway, at the pale spine of it lying exposed between tides, glazed here and there where spray had frozen in sheets. From the mainland side, faint as a memory, the first lamp of the town had come on.

"Tomas."

He didn't startle. He'd gotten used to my footsteps, which I considered a small victory. "Ilsa."

"Your father wrote you again."

His jaw tightened. "How did you—"

"The postman talks to the fishwives, the fishwives talk to my grandmother, my grandmother talks to me. Nothing on this island stays secret longer than a tide. That's rather the point I've come to make." I stepped beside him, close enough that our breath mingled white in the freezing air. "Everyone keeps deciding things for everyone else. Ada decides what the sergeant needs to hear. You decide what your father deserves. Lucan decides — well. Lucan's decided, and it cost him everything. Meanwhile I sit in kitchens being *protected*, and protection, Tomas, is just another word for being left out."

"You don't know what these men are like."

"I know what men are like. I've buried enough of them." My grandfather. My uncles. The names carved on the sea-wall that the magistrate's ink had tried to make into nobodies. "And I know what soldiers are like, better than you think. Last spring, when the revenue cutter tied up at the quay, I sold three of them hot chestnuts and talked my way past their questions about the boats so old Rurik could run his unstamped brandy up from the cove. They laughed. They bought more chestnuts. I was fourteen." I turned to face him fully. "I'm not brave the way Ada is brave — she's brave like stone, she endures. I'm brave like fire. I burn fast and I don't mind being spent. This job needs both kinds. Let her be the keeper. Let me be the flame."

Tomas looked at me for a long time. He had his father's bones, everyone said so, but he had none of his father's ease, and tonight his face was all sharp angles and doubt, a man being argued out of a fortress he'd built to keep others safe because building it was easier than admitting he couldn't.

"If they catch you," he said quietly, "they won't treat you as a courier. They'll treat you as leverage. Against Ada. Against the testimony. A magistrate facing ruin does not behave like a reasonable man, Ilsa. I have dinner with him. I *know*."

"Then don't get caught," I said. "Neither of us."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan anyone's offered that fits in a satchel."

And then — I'll remember this as long as I live, the exact quality of the fading light on the ice-glazed stones — Tomas Dunmore laughed. Just once, short and startled out of him, fogging the air. "God," he said. "You sound like her. You sound exactly like her at your age, Maren says. She told me. Ada at sixteen argued the harbormaster into reopening the winter moorings during a gale, and she won."

"She tells it differently."

"She would." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them something had shifted, some load redistributed. "I can't decide this. Neither can I, honestly. But I'll speak for you. That much I'll do — I'll stand in whatever kitchen or lamp room they argue in and say the girl should go, and say it's my judgment, so it lands on me if it goes wrong."

"It won't go wrong."

"Everything goes wrong," Tomas said. "The art is in choosing which wrongs you can live inside afterward." He glanced back toward the lighthouse, where the great lamp was waking for the evening, the first pale rotation of its beam sweeping the darkening water like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. "Come on. Before she sends a search party. And Ilsa — bring your arithmetic book to supper."

"Why?"

"Because if I'm going to argue for you," he said, walking, "I intend to examine the evidence first."

* * *

The argument happened that night in the lamp room, of all places, because Ada would not leave the light unattended during the changeover hour and none of us were fool enough to ask her to. So we climbed the ninety-one stairs — I counted them years ago out of boredom and never stopped — and conducted the future of three drowned families in the humming amber circle of the lens, with the whole black sea turning slowly beneath us and the wind pressing at the glass like a crowd at a door.

Maren was there, wrapped in two shawls, silent, her hands folded. Tomas laid out the routes and the timings. I unwrapped my arithmetic book and opened it flat on the chart table and peeled back a page to show the thin ghost of document-paper stitched along the inner spine, invisible unless you knew, unreadable unless you destroyed the book.

Ada examined it the way she examined everything: thoroughly, silently, with those keeper's hands that had diagnosed a hundred broken things by touch alone. She turned pages. She tilted the book to the lamplight. She ran her thumbnail along the stitch line, once, twice.

"Who taught you this stitching?" she asked finally.

"A sailmaker's daughter teaches herself from watching sailmakers."

"Hm." She closed the book and set it down, and looked at me, and the beam wheeled overhead and for one heartbeat her face went dark and then amber again, and I understood I was looking at a woman paying out rope — slowly, hand over hand, because the person on the other end of it was worth saving and the depth below was real.

"Conditions," she said. "One: you travel with Tomas until checkpoint three, and at checkpoint three you do exactly what he says, even if what he says is to run. Two: if anyone is taken, the books burn — you memorized the parcel numbers, so burn them rather than surrender them, do you hear me, the *paper* means nothing next to the *people*, and I will not have you hanged for onionskin. Three—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do, dropped down into a register I'd only heard from the sea itself, "—you come home. Whatever happens at that archive, whatever the registrar stamps or refuses, you walk back through those gates and you cross that causeway and you come home. That's not a condition, Ilsa. That's the price. Say it back to me."

"You come home," I said. My throat had gone tight. "All three. I swear."

Maren reached out and took my hand then, her old fingers cold as the rail of a wrecked boat, and pressed something into my palm. A button. Brass, worn smooth, stamped with an anchor. "Your great-grandfather's," she whispered. "Off his coat, the coat he wore the day the sea took him. He'd have wanted it to make the trip he never got to make." Her eyes were dry. Old grief doesn't cry; it just stands at windows. "Make them enter his name properly this time. Spelled right. He spelled it wrong on purpose, at the registry, to spite them, and the clerk wrote the spite down forever."

I closed my fist around the anchor.

Below us, far down the black water, the mainland lights had multiplied — the town, the tollhouse, the distant capital glowing on its hill like a coal that wouldn't die. Eleven days. Somewhere in that glow sat a registrar's desk and a stamp and a deadline, and between here and there lay three checkpoints and a frozen causeway and every kind of weather the sky had left in its pockets.

Ada turned back to the lens, adjusted a screw that needed no adjusting, and spoke without looking at us.

"First ice thins Thursday," she said. "We cross at dawn, before the sun touches the stones. Rest now, all of you." A pause. The beam swept round. "Ilsa — leave the arithmetic book with me tonight. If I'm sending my nerve out into the world sewn between pages of sums, I want one last night to check my stitching."

I put the book in her hands. Our fingers touched on the cover, hers steady, mine not quite, and I saw her notice, and I saw her decide — the way she decides everything, completely, once, forever — that steadiness could be taught later, on the road, in the hours that remained.

Thursday, dawn, the ice. The anchor button warm in my fist like a small swallowed sun.
