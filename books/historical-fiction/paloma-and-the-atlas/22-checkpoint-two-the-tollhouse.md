## 22. Checkpoint Two: The Tollhouse

The road to the tollhouse ran between two walls of frozen gorse, and by the time we reached it the light had gone thin and grey as fish skin. I had driven a cart along this road perhaps two hundred times in my years as keeper — supplies out, oil and wicks and flour — but never once had the stones seemed so loud beneath the wheels, never once had I been so aware that every mile of it was watched ground.

Ilsa rode in the back with the satchel flat against her stomach, her hands folded over it as if she were merely cold. She was good at looking merely cold. She had been good at everything since the net-loft, since Osla's sworn pages went into her coat lining beside Lucan's copies, and I told myself that her steadiness was courage and not the terrible calm of someone too young to know what fear costs. Sixteen-year-olds do not price things properly. That was her gift and my terror both.

Tomas sat beside me on the bench, his father's letters burned to nothing three nights past, his hands resting on his knees like a man in a pew who has stopped believing in the sermon but remembers how to sit still. He had not spoken for an hour.

"You'll do the talking," he said finally.

"I always intended to."

"He knows your face."

"He knows everyone's face. It's a small coast." I kept my eyes on the gorse. "He also knows my handwriting, my seals, and my ledger habits. That's worth more than a face."

"And if he's already had word from my father?"

"Then we're finished before we stop, and there's no sense spending the last mile rehearsing it." The words came out harder than I meant them, and I felt him flinch beside me, and I was sorry for it, and did not say so. Grief does that — it makes you spend your tenderness on the dead and ration what's left for the living.

* * *

The tollhouse stood where the coast road narrowed to a single lane between the cliff wall and the sea wall, a low stone building with a red lantern burning over its door even in daylight, because the magistrate's cousin had never trusted anything he could not illuminate. Aldric Vane. Even the name sounded like a man who counted other men's coins. For eleven years he had held the lease on Maren Kestrel's grandfather's fields — the salt meadows below the eastern ridge, the ones the old woman walked each autumn though she no longer owned a blade of grass upon them — and for eleven years he had paid the magistrate a rent so modest it was practically a family allowance, because that was what cousins were for.

I had met him twice before. Once at a harvest auction, where he wore a grey coat too fine for the weather and shook my hand as though measuring the bones of it. Once at the ferry office, the winter after my husband drowned, when he offered condolences in a voice like warmed butter and asked, in the same breath, whether the lighthouse accounts might be made available to "interested parties." I had told him the accounts belonged to the Crown and to God, in that order, and he had laughed as if I'd said something charming instead of true.

Now his lantern swung above the door and his figure filled it, broad in a brown greatcoat, a ledger open under one arm and a clerk shivering behind him with a rubber stamp worn smooth from use.

"Keeper Ishikawa." He came down the step unhurried, boots ringing on the frozen mud. "And Master Vane. The magistrate's son himself, hauling freight through my gate like a common carrier." His smile was wide and showed all his teeth, which is not the same as warmth. "Your father will be pleased to hear you've taken up honest work."

"My father," Tomas said evenly, "is aware of my travels."

"I'm sure he is. I'm sure he's aware of a great many things." Aldric turned to me, and the smile adjusted itself, degree by degree, into something more careful. "You've come from the island, then. Late in the season for a crossing."

"The causeway froze. We walked it."

"In this wind?" He glanced toward the sea, white-capped and furious beyond the sea wall, then back, and I understood that the glance had not been about weather at all. It had been about plausibility. He was weighing our story against the wind and finding us slightly underweight. "You must have ice in your veins, Keeper."

"Salt water freezes slower than fresh. Everyone on the island knows it."

"Mm. And yet here you are, thawed enough to drive." He let that sit a moment, then gestured with the ledger. "Well. Papers, please. Manifest, cargo declaration, names of all persons crossing, purpose of travel. New forms since the armistice negotiations began — everyone wants to know who's moving and why, right down to the last sack of turnips. You'd think the fate of Europe depended on it." He laughed at his own joke, and the clerk behind him laughed too, a fraction too quickly.

I handed over the folder I had spent half of First Ice preparing, page by page, by lamplight, while the stones crackled outside and Ilsa slept — or pretended to sleep — in the loft. Lighthouse manifests. Supply requisitions stamped by the Trinity office. A letter of passage signed by Sergeant Brandt at the first checkpoint, his stamp fat and official beside words that asked nothing and promised less. And beneath the legitimate papers, sewn into the folder's spine where only a man who knew ledgers would think to look, the thing that would buy us our seconds: a discrepancy.

Every man who keeps books believes, somewhere below reason, that another man's books contain errors, and that finding them is a kind of victory. I had given Aldric Vane an error. A transposed figure in the oil requisition — forty-two gallons entered as twenty-four — small enough to be real, large enough to matter to a man who leased stolen fields and counted other men's coins.

He opened the folder and began to read, and I watched him find it. I saw the moment arrive in his face: the slight narrowing, the finger tapping the line, the little animal pleasure of a bookkeeper catching a bookkeeper out.

"This figure's wrong," he said.

"Is it?" I let my voice go flat with fatigue, the voice of a woman who has driven four hours on frozen stones. "Trinity's clerks copy everything twice by hand. Half their figures come through scrambled. I've sent back corrections three times this quarter alone."

"Hm." He tapped the line again. "Forty-two gallons. That's a season's burning for a lamp your size. Or nearly."

"It's exactly a season's burning, which is why I'd like the correction processed before the spring order, or I'll be lighting tallow candles in the lantern room like my grandmother did." I heard Ilsa shift in the cart bed and forced myself not to look. Do not look at what you must not seem to protect. "Can you process it here? Your clerk has a stamp."

Aldric looked at me for a long moment, and behind his eyes something moved — not suspicion exactly, or not only. Calculation. He was a man forever running two columns, the public one and the private one, and I could almost hear the abacus click as he weighed which column this moment belonged in. The magistrate's cousin, doing the magistrate's work at the magistrate's tollhouse, on a day when the magistrate's son sat in a cart wearing the expression of a man attending his own audit.

"The correction goes to the district office," he said at last. "But I'll note it. Expedite it." He turned to the clerk. "Manifest's in order?"

The clerk had barely looked at it. "In order, sir."

"Cargo inspection's still required. New regulations." He said it lightly, watching me as he said it, and my heart performed one slow, deliberate contraction. "Standard procedure since the capital started making noises. Nothing personal, Keeper."

"Of course," I said. "Inspect away. Though I warn you the cargo's mostly lamp oil and dried cod, and the cod's been offended by the journey."

He smiled again — that measured smile — and lifted the canvas at the back of the cart, and the wind came through the gap smelling of salt and kelp and the far-off ice, and Ilsa sat very still with her hands in her lap and her eyes lowered like a girl bored past endurance, and the satchel lay beneath the fold of her skirt where Osla's needle had built it a second home among the coat seams, flat as a psalm, invisible as sin.

* * *

While Aldric worked along the cart's edge — lifting sacks, prodding the oil tins, holding up a side of cod with an expression suggesting it had personally disappointed him — I occupied myself the way any tired traveler would: badly. I got down from the bench, stamped my feet, complained about the cold to the clerk. I asked him whether the district office had a new form for corrections or whether the old one would serve, and he didn't know, and I said wasn't that always the way, and produced from my bag a blank requisition sheet and asked could he lend me a pen, because I may as well draft the correction now and save the post a day.

It was theater, all of it, and I played it badly and it worked anyway, because the truth about men like Aldric's clerk is that they want nothing more than to be left out of the story. He lent me the pen. He pointed at the correct form number without getting up. He looked anywhere but at the cart, at the girl, at the seam-lined coat folded under her arm — because looking is the beginning of responsibility, and he had a stamp to mind and a supper waiting and no wish whatsoever to appear in anyone's testimony.

Tomas, meanwhile, did the hardest work of all, which was nothing. He stayed on the bench. He kept his face arranged in the mild, faintly resentful boredom of a gentleman dragged along on family errands, and only I knew that his pulse was going like a bird in a closed room, because his knee pressed against mine and it trembled, very slightly, the whole time. Once, when Aldric straightened up and looked directly at him across the cart, Tomas said, "Do you intend to weigh the cod, cousin, or simply admire it?" and the word *cousin* landed between them like a coin tossed on a bar — an insult dressed as kinship — and Aldric's smile thinned by a single degree and recovered.

"Weighing comes later, Master Vane. At the assizes, usually."

"Ah," said Tomas. "Then you've practice."

For a heartbeat the air between them went sharp enough to cut, and I understood that these two had history I had only glimpsed — the son who despised the machinery and the cousin who loved oiling it — and that Aldric was deciding, in that clicking abacus behind his eyes, how much of today's discomfort to report upward and how much to keep as currency. A man who hedges bets must keep some stakes hidden even from the table.

Aldric dropped the canvas. "Cargo's in order," he announced, and wrote something in his ledger — I would have given a year of my life to read it — and nodded to the clerk.

The stamp came down. Once on the manifest, once on the passage letter, the sound small and final as a knuckle on a coffin lid.

* * *

"Before you go." Aldric held up one finger, and the world stopped turning for the length of a breath. "The fields."

"The fields?" I said.

"Kestrel's fields. Below the eastern ridge." He said the name casually, folding his ledger shut, but I saw the care in the casualness, the way a man sets a trap and then stands well back from it. "Your island folk pass this gate more often than most. Word reaches me, sometimes, of talk. Talk about leases. About old claims." He smiled his full smile again. "I trust there's no trouble brewing out there. Bad for trade, trouble. Bad for everyone."

"No trouble," I said, and my voice did not shake, because I had learned long ago that grief and lying are taught by the same schoolmaster. "Maren Kestrel walks her grandfather's rows every autumn because walking is free and remembering costs nothing. If that troubles your ledgers, cousin, I'd take it up with whoever sold her the memory."

Something flickered across his face — I could not name it then and cannot fully name it now; something almost like being struck in a place he'd thought armored. He looked at me a long moment, and the wind pulled at the hem of his fine coat, and behind him the red lantern swayed on its bracket, painting his cheeks briefly the color of warning.

"A colorful answer," he said quietly. "From a quiet woman." He stepped back from the cart. "Go on, then. Mind the ice past Harrow Bend — it doesn't hold weight the way it looks like it does. And Keeper—" he raised the ledger in something halfway between a salute and a threat— "give my regards to the widow Kestrel. Tell her the fields are well tended. Better than they ever were under her family, if we're honest."

"They're not hers anymore," I agreed. "That's the only part you tend honestly."

I climbed onto the bench. Tomas took up the reins. The cart rolled forward through the gate, past the clerk and his stamp and the red lantern swinging its slow alarm, and I did not let my shoulders drop until the tollhouse was a smudge of stone behind us and the road bent north toward the flats.

Only then did Ilsa exhale — a long, shaking breath she had held so long it came out like steam from a kettle — and say, "He knew. Didn't he? He knew something."

"Perhaps," I said.

"He looked at the satchel. When he lifted the canvas, he looked right at it."

"He looked at a schoolgirl's satchel and saw a schoolgirl's satchel, because that is what he wanted to see. Men like Aldric Vane believe everyone can be bought, so they never look closely at what can't." I heard how certain I sounded, and knew how uncertain I was, and hated the gap between them.

Tomas spoke without turning his head. "He wrote something in the ledger before he waved us through. Something he didn't read aloud."

"Yes."

"If it reaches my father tonight—"

"Then we ride through the night," I said, "and pray the registrar's stamp falls before the magistrate's does."

Behind us, small in the grey distance, the red lantern burned on over the tollhouse door, and I thought of Maren walking her grandfather's rows in the wind, and of the man who called those rows *tended*, and I understood that we had passed the second gate but not escaped it — that Aldric Vane had let us go the way a fisherman lets line run off the reel, not because the fish is free, but because the hook is set deep and the reel can always be wound back.

The road ahead ran straight toward the capital, eleven days short of the signing, and somewhere behind us a pen was already moving across a page, writing down the color of our cart and the hour of our passing and the name Ishikawa, Ishikawa, Ishikawa.
