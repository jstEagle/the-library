## 23. The Search

The tollhouse at Merrow's Cross sat where the coast road narrowed between two granite walls, and Tomas understood at once why his father's cousin had chosen it. A man could stand in that gap and touch both sides of the road with outstretched arms. Anything passing through had to pass *through him*.

Aldric was not there yet — his gig stood empty by the tollhouse door, its horse dozing under a blanket — but three soldiers were, and they had already begun turning over the crates of a fishmonger's cart ahead of them. One of the soldiers was young, barely shaving, and handled a crate of smoked herring as though it might detonate. The other two worked with the flat efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more before the ice melted.

"Keep your hands visible," Ada said quietly. She did not look at him. She looked at the road, at the sky, at the smoke rising from the tollhouse chimney, and her face was the face she wore when trimming the lamp wick: attentive to everything, alarmed by nothing. "We are what we appear to be. Say it until you believe it."

"We are what we appear to be," Tomas said.

"A lighthouse keeper delivering quarterly paperwork to the harbormaster's office. A cousin assisting her. A girl going home from school." Ada's eyes flicked to the back of the cart, where Ilsa sat on top of the oilcloth tarpaulin with a book open on her knees — an arithmetic primer, upside down. Ilsa turned it right way up without looking down, which somehow made Tomas more frightened, not less. "The satchel stays on her lap. Whatever happens. Whatever they say. It stays."

"I know what's at stake," Tomas said.

"No." Ada turned to him then, and for one moment he saw beneath the discipline, down into the place where three years of silences had been stacked like stones in a wall. "You know what it cost to get here. That is different from knowing what's at stake. What's at stake is Maren's grandfather plowing those fields with a wooden plow because iron was too precious. What's at stake is Osla's boys. What's at stake—" she paused, and the wind off the water pulled at her gray hair— "is whether this country signs its name on paper or keeps signing other people's names instead. Now breathe. We're next."

* * *

Tomas had spent his life around officialdom. He had sat in his father's court as a boy, swinging his legs beneath a bench too tall for him, watching the machinery of law grind through its motions: the bailiff's cough, the clerk's pen scratching, the magistrate leaning forward with that expression of patient sorrow he wore like judicial robes. He knew how searches went. He knew the choreography of power — the demand, the compliance, the small humiliations performed briskly so no one had time to feel them.

None of that prepared him for watching a soldier's hands go through Ada's things.

It was not violence. That was the terrible part. The soldier — a sergeant with a mustache the color of wet straw — lifted each item from the cart bed with a kind of bored courtesy. The crate of lamp glass, wrapped in straw: unwrapped, counted, rewrapped. The sealed envelope of lighthouse forms: held to the light as though the sun might reveal something beyond the harbor office's printed header. The tin of biscuits: opened, sniffed, offered half-jokingly to the young soldier, who shook his head.

"You keep a tidy cart," the sergeant said.

"My husband kept a tidy cart," Ada said. "I only inherited the habit."

Tomas heard the sentence land and understood its double weight. The sergeant did not; he grunted and moved on to the spare axle grease, poking a finger into it and wiping it on the wheel of the cart with an air of having confirmed nothing and suspected less.

But the third soldier — the young one, whose name tape read VEY in letters too new to be faded — had stopped moving. He was standing at the rear of the cart, beside the tarpaulin, and he was looking at Ilsa.

She looked back. Sixteen years old, chapped lips, a schoolgirl's coat two sizes large because it had belonged to a dead brother, and on her lap, gripped between both hands like a hymnal, the leather satchel. Inside it: thirty-one pages of certified copies sewn into the false bottom, beneath a stratum of genuinely completed homework, a slate pencil, a dried apple, and a copy of a novel called *Paloma and the Atlas* that Ilsa had insisted on bringing because, she said, if you are going to lie, lie inside a bigger story.

Tomas's mouth had gone dry. He made himself count the tollhouse roof slates. Forty-one. Forty-two. If Vey asked to see the satchel, there was nothing any of them could do. Ada's papers were genuine. His own travel permit was genuine, signed by his father's own clerk, which was either exquisite irony or exquisite protection depending on how the hour went. But the satchel —

"What's in the bag?" Vey asked.

* * *

Later — much later, years later, in another life — Tomas would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find that memory had rearranged them entirely. In memory, everything happened slowly. In fact it happened fast, the way drowning happens fast.

Ilsa said, "My schoolwork."

Vey said, "Open it, please."

And Ada said, "Sergeant. Is my niece's homework a matter of state?"

She came forward along the side of the cart with the envelope of harbor forms in her hand, and her voice was pitched exactly between offense and cooperation, the register every woman on the island used with officials, and it worked, because the straw-mustached sergeant turned toward her with the relief of a man handed a familiar problem.

"Lighthouse keeper, is that right? Paloma?" He took the envelope, thumbed the seal. "Quarterly returns. You've come all this way in winter for quarterly returns?"

"The harbormaster's office closes its books at month's end. And the causeway—" Ada gestured back down the coast road, toward the gray line of the bridge and the chained gates just visible beyond the headland— "the causeway will be closed behind us soon enough. I'd rather not carry the paperwork home again."

"Efficient," the sergeant said, in a tone suggesting the word cost him something.

While he flipped through the forms — genuine forms, Tomas reminded himself, genuine ink, genuine stamps, the boring armor of bureaucratic truth — Vey still waited at the cart's tail. He had not raised his voice. He had not repeated the request. He simply stood there, and his patience was worse than shouting, because it could not be redirected or flattered or worn down.

Ilsa opened the satchel.

She did it slowly, and Tomas watched her do the thing that Ada must have drilled into her on the long drive, though he had never seen the drill: she let the fear show. Her chin trembled. Her hands fumbled the buckle once, twice. Any soldier who had ever searched a smuggler knew that smugglers were smooth, and this child was not smooth; she was a girl whose brother had drowned, whose family had been erased from the land rolls, whose hands were shaking because she was afraid of authority, as girls her age were supposed to be.

Inside, on top: the arithmetic primer. A page of sums, checked with red ticks. The slate pencil. The apple, shriveled.

"Schoolwork," Vey said. Not a question. His eyes hadn't gone to the sums at all. They had gone to the shape of the bag — the way the leather bulged at the base, the way it sat too heavily for its contents — and Tomas saw the moment the young soldier noticed, saw it arrive in his face like weather.

Then the tollhouse door banged open, and Aldric came out into the cold pulling on his gloves.

* * *

Cousin Aldric was not a large man, but he filled doorways the way certain men fill doorways: by right of appetite. He was sixty-odd, silver-haired, dressed better than any tollkeeper ought to be able to afford, and his eyes went from the cart to Ada to the sergeant to the satchel with the speed of a man doing accounts in his head.

"Well," Aldric said. "The Paloma light comes down from its rock. The island honors us."

"Just the quarter's paperwork, Aldric," Ada said. "And taking the girl home to her aunt in town. School term ended early — the storm."

"So I hear." Aldric smiled. It was a good smile, well maintained, like the gig and the gloves. "Terrible business, the storm. Terrible business, most things lately." He walked the length of the cart with his hands clasped behind him, a landlord inspecting fences, and stopped at the tail where Vey stood. "Trouble, Corporal?"

"No trouble, sir. Just finishing the inspection."

"Then finish it quickly. This lady keeps the light over my family's shipping lanes. I'll thank you not to keep her freezing at my gate while you inventory her biscuits." He glanced into the satchel without appearing to, a glance so brief and oblique it might have been accidental. "Sums and apples. My God, we've come to searching children's schoolbags. Is there a war on that nobody told me about?"

The joke landed the way such jokes land among soldiers: nowhere. But it gave the sergeant permission to close the envelope of forms, stamp it against his knee, and hand it back.

"Papers in order," the sergeant said. "You can pass."

Vey stepped aside. He did it a half-second too late, and everyone saw the lateness, and no one said anything. As the cart rolled through the gap in the walls, past the sleeping horse and the tollbox and Aldric's smiling scrutiny, Tomas felt the young corporal's gaze on the satchel like a hand resting lightly between Ilsa's shoulder blades — not pushing, not yet. Remembering.

Ada paid the toll. Aldric waved the coins away and then accepted them anyway, because principle was for poorer men. As the cart cleared the walls and the coast road opened ahead — long, gray, running toward town and the capital beyond — Aldric called after them in a voice pitched to carry:

"Give my regards to the magistrate, boy. Family should stand together these days. You never know when the ground shifts under a family."

Tomas did not answer. Beside him, Ilsa had finally closed the satchel, and her knuckles were white around the strap, and she did not speak for four miles.

* * *

It was Ada who spoke first, when the tollhouse was long out of sight and the road bent inland behind a screen of black pines.

"He knew."

Tomas kept his eyes on the horse's ears. "Aldric? Yes. He suspects everything, always. It's how he's survived three administrations. Suspecting everything and proving nothing."

"Not Aldric." Ada's voice was very level. "The corporal. The young one. He saw the weight of that bag. He asked twice. And then he stepped aside."

"His sergeant told him to step aside."

"Yes." Ada was quiet for a while. The wheels crunched frost. Somewhere behind them, faint as a bell under water, the tollhouse bell rang for the next traveler. "Which means the sergeant chose. Men like that don't disobey orders by accident, Tomas. They disobey them once, deliberately, and then spend twenty years being careful. He decided in front of my face to let us through, and he decided it fast, and I want to know why."

"Does it matter why?"

"It matters enormously why." She turned in her seat to look back at Ilsa, who had fallen asleep sitting upright, the satchel still locked in her arms even in sleep, fingers curled around the strap like a swimmer around a spar. When she turned forward again, her expression had changed — softened into something Tomas had rarely seen on her and found more frightening than the discipline. Tenderness, on this woman, meant danger. "Because a favor is a debt, and a debt gets called. Sooner or later someone asks that sergeant what passed through his gate in the winter of 'nineteen, and everything depends on what kind of man answers."

"And if he's the wrong kind?"

"Then we'd better be in the archive before the asking." Ada gathered the reins. Ahead, through the thinning pines, the roofs of the mainland town showed under low cloud, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, and beyond them — invisible, forty miles east, eleven days away — the capital, the courts, the clock running. "Hold tight, Ilsa," she said softly, to the sleeping girl who could not hear her. "The easy gate is behind us now."

Tomas looked back once. Far down the white road, a single figure still stood in the gap between the granite walls: the young corporal, watching them go, motionless as the stone around him, one hand raised to shield his eyes against the low sun.

He was still watching when the bend in the road took him away.
