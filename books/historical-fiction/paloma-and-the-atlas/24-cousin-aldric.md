## 24. Cousin Aldric

The tollhouse at Merrow's Bend had been in Aldric Vane's family for three generations, and in all that time it had taught him one lesson above all others: the road does not care who you are. It cares only what you know.

He had learned it young, watching his father weigh wagons with a ledger open on the counter and a second, unwritten arithmetic running behind his eyes. The written numbers went to the magistrate's office in the capital district; the unwritten ones stayed in the house, folded into the family's quiet fortune. A farmer who could not pay his grain tax might pay instead in a field deed. A fisherman short on tolls might sign over a mooring right. Nothing was ever stolen, his father liked to say. Things were only *redirected*. The law was a river, and the Vane family had simply built their mill where the current ran.

Aldric had inherited the mill, the river, and the saying. He repeated it to himself now, standing at the tollhouse window on a morning so cold the glass had grown feathers of frost along its lower panes, and he watched the cart come down from the coast road through the bare birches, and he felt — before he knew anything, before he could name a single reason — that the river had changed course.

* * *

It was the horse he noticed first. Or rather, the absence of one. The cart was drawn by two shaggy island ponies, the kind that swam cargo across the tidal flats when the causeway was under water, and no mainland farmer used island ponies in winter because they were small and stubborn and their hooves threw ice badly. Which meant the cart had come from the island. Which meant the bridge gates had been opened, or climbed, or bribed — and Aldric kept the only key east of the sergeant's post.

He set down his tea. The cup left a dark ring on the toll ledger, on the page recording yesterday's takings, which were correct, and the day before's, which were not, and he found himself staring at that false line of figures as though it had suddenly become evidence in someone else's case.

Then there was the driver. Tomas Vane. His cousin — though the blood between them was thin enough that they had always been more rivals than kin — sitting up on the bench of a supply cart in a coat too fine for hauling flour, with the particular stillness of a man carrying something breakable. And beside him, wrapped in wool and windburn, Ada Ishikawa herself, the lighthouse keeper, whose face Aldric had seen perhaps six times in fifteen years and never once forgotten, because she had the kind of face that looked *through* things rather than at them.

And behind them, wedged among crates in the cart bed, a girl in a school satchel's worth of winter clothing, sixteen or thereabouts, with her chin up and her eyes doing exactly what Ada Ishikawa's eyes did: looking through him already, cataloguing him, filing him away.

Three travelers. One cart. Eleven days, by Aldric's own reckoning, until the armistice terms were signed and the special courts closed their books forever — after which every record, every claim, every crime committed under the old jurisdiction would be either ratified into permanence or drowned in amnesty, depending entirely on what paper reached the capital archive before the seals fell.

His father-in-law had dined with the magistrate three nights ago. Aldric knew this because he paid for the dinners, indirectly, out of the same redirected river that paid for everything. He knew the magistrate was nervous. He knew the magistrate had doubled the watch on the coastal road and sent word down every checkpoint between here and the capital: *a packet of stolen documents, certified copies, inheritance claims of the drowned families — stop anything moving toward the archive.*

He also knew — and here was the mill-wheel turning, the cold water rising through the gears — that the magistrate had made one fatal error in all his careful years. He had told Aldric too much. Not deliberately; never deliberately. But over cards last autumn, drunk enough to be expansive, he had explained precisely how the drownings had been handled. How the claims of three fishing families had been quietly erased while their men lay unburied, and how the salt fields had passed, at a fraction of worth, to a cousin who leased them back to the widows at rates designed to keep them grateful.

The cousin was Aldric.

Aldric had signed those leases himself, in his neat clerk's hand, feeling nothing in particular. That was the trouble with redirected rivers: you never tasted the water. It was only now, watching the cart come down through the frost-killed birches with its silent driver and its keeper with her seeing-through eyes, that he understood he had been drinking from it all his life, and the taste had finally arrived, late, like grief.

* * *

"Morning, Aldric." Tomas reined in the ponies and did not quite meet his eye. "Toll, is it? We're bound for the capital. Lighthouse stores, mostly." He gestured vaguely at the crates. "Lamp oil, wicks. The usual."

"The usual," Aldric said.

It was a poor lie, and they both knew it. Lamp oil came up from the south depot by government barge twice a year; everyone at Merrow's Bend knew the schedule the way they knew the tides. Aldric let the silence sit just long enough to be felt, then came around the counter, unhurried, stamping his boots against the cold, and walked the length of the cart with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring livestock.

Up close, the story unraveled further. The crates were genuine — he could see the depot stencils — but they were packed wrong, oil tins nested loosely, straw stuffed without care, the whole arrangement shouting that no one aboard cared whether the cargo survived the journey. People protected what mattered to them. Nobody protects lamp oil like that unless the real cargo is elsewhere.

Elsewhere meant the girl. She sat very straight among the crates with a school satchel flat against her stomach, both arms wrapped around it the way children hold a cat they have been told to put down. She was watching him with an expression he recognized from his own mirror at twenty: the absolute conviction that she was smarter than every adult present, combined with the dawning terror that this time it might not be true.

"And who's this?" Aldric asked pleasantly. "Roads are no place for schoolgirls."

"Ilsa," said the girl. "I'm going to my aunt's."

"In the capital?"

"For schooling," she said, and lifted her chin another degree.

Ada Ishikawa spoke then, for the first time, and her voice did what her voice apparently always did: it rearranged the air in the tollhouse. "Her grandmother is Maren Ostergaard, Aldric. I expect you know the name. You collect her rent."

Something moved through him — not guilt, guilt required an organ he'd had removed long ago for practical reasons, but something adjacent. A draft under a door. "I administer the leases," he corrected mildly. "The land belongs to—"

"To whom?" said Ada Ishikawa. "That's the question, isn't it. That's the entire question."

She held his gaze, and Aldric Vane, who had stared down smugglers and soldiers and once a customs inspector with a warrant and two armed escorts, discovered that his hands wanted to fidget. He turned it into adjusting his scarf.

"You'll want the search," he said briskly, retreating to procedure the way other men retreated to prayer. "Standard for capital-bound traffic. New orders." He said it lightly, watching their faces. Tomas's jaw tightened. The girl went white, then set her mouth. Ada Ishikawa didn't move at all, which told him more than any flinch would have.

He searched anyway, because refusing to search would have been louder than searching. He went through the crates properly, lifting the oil tins, running his thumb along the crate bottoms, taking his time. Then he opened the cart bed boards, one by one, letting each fall with a bang. The girl's knuckles whitened on the satchel strap. He noted it. He noted everything. That was the trade he was in.

He did not touch the satchel.

This was the moment, and Aldric had known it was coming since the ponies first appeared through the birches, and he had spent the whole walk around the cart deciding, with the cool bookkeeping part of his mind that had never once failed him, exactly what he was going to do.

Here was the arithmetic. If he seized the documents, he delivered his cousin's father a victory, saved the salt-field leases, and tied himself permanently to a man whose position was cracking like spring ice — because the magistrate would not have doubled every checkpoint if he did not believe, deep down, that the paper existed and that it would sink him. Men who feel safe do not mobilize. Fear was expensive, and the magistrate was spending it freely.

If he let the cart pass and the magistrate fell — and Aldric gave it better than even odds, given that someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to get these documents moving — then the new courts, hungry to establish themselves, would need cooperators. Witnesses. Men who could explain, with ledgers, exactly how the old system worked. The armistice would not look kindly on the magistrate, but it would look very kindly indeed on the cousin who had administered his dirty leases and could document every signature, every redirected payment, every dinner.

Corruption, properly practiced, was not loyalty. It was portfolio management.

And there was one more entry in the ledger, smaller, in a different hand. Ada Ishikawa had named Maren Ostergaard to his face, and the girl with the satchel was the widow's granddaughter, and somewhere behind Aldric's eyes his own grandfather's ledger floated up unbidden — the page where the Ostergaard salt fields had entered the family's holdings, purchased at eleven percent of value from an estate whose owners had drowned with their claims still pending. He had never read that page closely. He had never needed to. Now it seemed to rise off the paper and hang in the frost-feathered window light between himself and the cart.

He closed the cart bed board with a final bang and dusted his hands.

"You're clear," he said.

Tomas exhaled through his nose, carefully, like a man setting down something heavy without letting it clatter.

Aldric returned to the tollhouse, took up his stamp, inked it, and pressed it to their passage chit with a firmness that surprised even him. "Give my regards to your father," he added, mild as milk. "Tell him the roads are quiet. Tell him—" he paused, and let the smallest blade of a smile show, "—tell him I've been thinking about the future."

* * *

They rolled on. He stood in the doorway of the tollhouse and watched the cart shrink down the capital road, the girl's face turned back toward him for a long moment, reading him the way her grandmother presumably read weather. When the birches swallowed it, Aldric went inside, poured the cold tea into the frost flowers on the step, and unlocked the bottom drawer of his father's old desk.

Inside lay the lease agreements. Forty-one of them, going back nineteen years, every signature his own, every figure annotated in his father's hand before that. He had always assumed he would burn them someday, in some crisis, on principle. Instead he sat down with fresh paper and began, in his neat clerk's hand, to make copies — dates, amounts, names — building a second ledger, the honest one, the one that had waited all these years in the shadow of the first.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north, and it carried the smell of snow. Somewhere down the capital road, a cart with a hidden satchel was rattling toward a city full of soldiers, and eleven days remained on the clock, and Aldric Vane — hedging, always hedging, the river-man to the last — addressed the first envelope to the office of the incoming armistice commission and wondered, with something almost like appetite, whether his cousin's father had yet noticed how thoroughly he had just been sold.
