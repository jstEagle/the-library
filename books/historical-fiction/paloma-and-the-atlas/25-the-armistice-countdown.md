## 25. The Armistice Countdown

*From the* Paloma Gazette *and Advertiser, morning edition:*

**TERMS EXPECTED WITHIN FORTNIGHT. SPECIAL COURTS TO BE DISSOLVED UPON RATIFICATION.**

Readers will recall that the provisional tribunals convened during the emergency years — including the Special Court of Records sitting in the capital — are to be wound down upon final signature of the armistice instruments. All matters remaining before those courts must be concluded or transferred to civil jurisdiction by that date. After ratification, no claim arising from the emergency period may be heard under the old commissions.

The Gazette reminds its readers that the government regards this as a return to normalcy, not an erasure. *A nation may forgive; it need not forget.*

---

Ada read the notice twice, standing in the doorway of the inn's back kitchen where the morning light came gray through salt-fogged glass, and then she folded the paper into quarters, and then into eighths, until it was a small hard square she could close her fist around.

Eleven days.

She had known it, of course — Lucan had told them often enough, fevered and clear-eyed by turns, his voice rising out of delirium like a swimmer surfacing: *before the signing, before they close the doors forever.* But knowing a thing and reading it in cold print were different animals. The newspaper made it official. The newspaper made it a wall with a door in it, and the door was already swinging shut.

She put the folded square in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone.

Outside, the town of Greyharbor was going about its business with the particular diligence of people pretending not to listen to one another. The fish carts rattled past. Smoke stood up straight from the chimneys because there was no wind, only that flat, listening stillness that precedes weather on this coast. Ada had learned to read such stillness on the island the way farmers read clouds; here in town she read it differently. The whole town felt like that hour before a lamp is lit — everything visible, nothing yet illuminated.

Ilsa was at the table when Ada came back through, hunched over the school satchel with its false bottom opened, counting the copies again. She counted them every morning now. Eleven leaves of thin paper, each stamped and certified in Lucan's careful clerk's hand, each one a small hammer aimed at a wall.

"Same number as yesterday," Ada said.

"I know." Ilsa didn't look up. "I count them anyway."

"That's not madness," Ada said. "That's seamanship. You check the lamps whether they need checking or not."

But the girl's hands were quick and tight, and Ada saw that it wasn't ritual driving her. It was fear, worn like youth wears everything — badly, brightly, at full volume. Sixteen years old and carrying three families' worth of drowned history in a satchel designed for arithmetic books.

"Eleven days," Ilsa said, echoing the thought before Ada could stop herself from thinking it aloud. "It says eleven days."

"I know what it says."

"And the archive closes at the same time as the court?"

"The registrar's office keeps ordinary hours," Ada said. "But the special commission — the authority that can act on these papers without a year of procedure first — that dies with the signing. After that, Maren's grandchildren will be filing petitions until their hair goes white."

Ilsa closed the satchel. Her jaw was set in a way that Ada recognized from the mirror of her own younger face, though hers had been tempered by thirty years of keeping a light burning for ships that mostly never came. The girl's anger was still raw metal. It would hold an edge. It might also snap.

"Then we go soon," Ilsa said. "Not in eleven days. Soon."

"Soon," Ada agreed, and hated how much the word cost.

Because the truth — the truth she kept behind her teeth the way she'd once kept small merciful lies behind them — was that every road from Greyharbor to the capital now passed through eyes and hands, and somewhere in the last week the quality of the watching had changed. She had felt it first at the market: the butcher's boy looking too long at their cart. Then the man outside the posting house who wore his coat like a man wearing a coat over a uniform. And yesterday, the photograph.

No. Not the photograph. Not yet. That was ahead of them, though none of them knew it. What Ada knew was only this: the air had changed pressure, and on this coast, changing pressure meant storm.

*Fragment found later among Sergeant Brandt's effects, undated, in his own hand:*

They posted the new notices at the gate this morning. ELEVEN DAYS TO RATIFICATION. ALL EMERGENCY COMMISSIONS EXPIRE. Below it, smaller: *Persons holding documents pertaining to emergency-era proceedings are reminded of their statutory obligations.*

Statutory obligations. In thirty years on this coast I have learned what such phrases mean. They mean: bring us what you find, or be found holding it.

The sergeant-major asked me at muster if I'd seen anything unusual come across from the island side since the freeze. I said the ice had been bad and traffic lighter than usual, which was true. He looked at me a moment longer than he needed to. I looked back exactly as long as he did.

Debts don't expire the way commissions do. That's the difference between the law and the sea.

---

Tomas read the same notice at the same hour, but he read it standing in the lobby of the posting house, where the paper was pinned above the timetable board, and where anyone might see him reading it — which was, he admitted to himself, half the reason he was reading it there. Let them see. Let it travel back up the road to the house on the hill that his father's son stood in a public lobby in a shabby traveling coat, studying the dissolution of the courts his father had served, his face giving away nothing.

His father would hear of it anyway. His father heard everything. That was the whole architecture of the man — not cruelty, not even ambition, really, but *hearing*. A life spent positioned at the point where information changed value.

Eleven days. Tomas did the arithmetic the way he'd once done it over case files in the archives, back when he'd believed the numbers meant something stable. Eleven days minus the two days' travel to the capital, minus whatever delay the checkpoints chose to impose, minus — his stomach tightened — minus the possibility that they were already watched, already measured, already weighed in some office he couldn't see. Subtract all that and what remained? Five days of margin. Perhaps six.

Lucan Reyes had died for a margin of days. That was the sentence Tomas kept returning to, walking back through the gray streets toward the inn. A man had carried certified copies through open water in winter, had drowned within sight — perhaps within sight — of the light Ada kept, and all so that a window would not close. And the window was closing anyway, day by day, with the patient mechanical certainty of a tide table.

At the inn, Ada was waiting in the corridor with that stillness she had, the stillness of lighthouses, which is not peace but readiness.

"The tollhouse," she said quietly. "We should talk about the tollhouse."

"Aldric."

"Aldric." She let the name sit between them like a coin on a table. "He leases Maren's fields. He knows our faces. He knew your face before you ever came to the island, I'd wager."

"He's my cousin," Tomas said. Saying it aloud still had the flavor of confession. "Second cousin. He sat across from me at dinners for fifteen years and talked about drainage and seed prices while his tenants starved politely around him."

"And he hedges," Ada said. "That's what I keep coming back to. Men like Aldric don't stand anywhere. They stand wherever the ground is about to be highest. If he thinks the magistrate might fall—"

"Then he'll let us pass and sell us both afterward, whichever way the wind finishes blowing."

"Yes." Ada's eyes held his. "So we don't give him time to finish calculating. We go through fast, or not at all."

That night Tomas wrote nothing. He had burned his father's letters weeks ago — the reasonable ones, the warm ones, the one offering a judgeship wrapped in reconciliation like poison wrapped in honeycomb — and the burning had felt like a door closing, and doors closing were supposed to feel like freedom, and instead he had dreamed of the fire each night since, and in the dream his father stood on the far side of it, unhurt, patient, *listening.*

Eleven days. Ten, now, when midnight came. He lay awake doing the subtraction again, and again, as though the numbers might behave differently if he checked them often enough.

---

*Overheard in the taproom of the Anchor & Chain, Greyharbor, recorded in the constable's watch-book, source unattributed:*

"—telling you, the wireless at the hotel picked it up clear as church. Signing on the eleventh. The minister himself said the emergency courts were 'a scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down when the building stands.'"

"Scaffolding." A laugh. "Whose building, then? Mine never got built."

"Your lot should've filed proper papers back then, shouldn't you."

"Filed papers. With *him*?" — lower, here, the words nearly lost under the taproom noise — "My grandfather plowed fields that man's cousin grazes sheep on now, and the papers saying so went into the harbor with him, didn't they. Papers don't float."

Another laugh, but shorter this time. And then a third voice, older, a woman's: "Papers float if somebody carries them."

Silence after that. The constable noted: *conversation became general.*

---

Maren heard the news from the fishwife who brought her herring, and she received it the way she received all news now — standing very straight, hands folded, her face arranged into the mildness that sixty years of being unheard teaches a woman to wear like a shawl.

Eleven days. She walked out afterward to the edge of the salt fields, because she always walked there when something moved inside her, the way other people went to church. The land lay flat and silver-gray under the low sky, fenced now, posted with Aldric's signs, grazed down to stubble by sheep that had never tasted anything her grandfather had planted. But beneath the grass she could still find them if she knelt — the old drainage channels, the lines of stones laid by hands three generations gone, running true as scripture under everything men had written on top of them.

*After the signing,* the notice had said, *no claim may be heard under the old commissions.*

Her grandfather's claim was an old claim. It lived in the old world entirely — drownings, erased registers, a magistrate's pen moving in the dark. When the armistice was signed, that whole world was scheduled for demolition, and everything inside it that hadn't been carried out in time would go down with the walls.

She thought of Ada Ishikawa, and of the girl with the fierce jaw, and of the magistrate's son who burned letters. She thought of Lucan Reyes, whom she had never met, dead in a borrowed grave outside consecrated ground, and she thought: *he carried them out in time. Or he tried.*

She knelt in the wet grass and pressed her palm flat against the soil, an old gesture, older than grief, older than law.

"Hold on," she told the field, or the dead in it, or herself. "Hold on. Eleven days."

Then she stood, and straightened her shawl, and walked back toward town with a decision settled in her body the way the stones were settled under the grass — and that evening, for the first time, she sent word to the inn asking to speak with Ada privately, and what she brought with her, wrapped in oilcloth, was a page of her own: her grandfather's planting ledger, half water-ruined, with names and plot-lines in a hand like rows of seedlings.

"It isn't certified," she said, when Ada unwrapped it. "It isn't anything a court would call evidence. But it says who worked this land, and it says it in ink older than his lies, and I want it in the packet. Whatever happens. Even if it changes nothing. I want my family *in* the record."

Ada looked at the ledger a long moment. Then she looked at Maren, and something passed between them that needed no stamp, no seal, no commission — the oldest kind of certification there is, witness to witness, hand to hand.

"It'll be in the record," Ada said.

---

Aldric read the notice at breakfast in his warm dining room, with his warm coffee, and did not touch it for a long moment after finishing.

Eleven days.

He was a careful man, which everyone mistook for a slow one. Careful men win. He turned his cup slowly on its saucer and considered the position of every piece on the board.

If the magistrate survived, then any cart leaving Greyharbor bound for the capital with islanders aboard was a cart worth having searched — and Aldric's tollhouse was where such searches happened, which made Aldric either an instrument or an obstacle, depending on how he played it. If the magistrate fell — and the wind off the capital had been smelling strange lately, wireless rumors like weather coming in off the sea, ministers resigning, commissions dissolving — then whoever had *helped* the fall would want the helping remembered, and whoever had *hindered* it would be remembered longer.

He thought of his cousin's face at the last dinner they'd shared, so certain, so well-fed, so entirely unaware that certainty was a crop that failed.

He thought of the Ishikawa woman's face at his tollgate, calm as a chart.

Eleven days. Aldric finished his coffee and rang for his clerk, and instructed him — mildly, reasonably, in the tone of a man discussing drainage — to prepare a second set of ledgers for the tollhouse. The real ones, and the others. A careful man kept accounts of everything, including his own choices, in two hands, so that whichever hand history finally shook, he had shaken it first.

---

And in the capital, in a barracks room that smelled of boot polish and stove iron, a young officer named Vey read the wire dispatch pinned to the noticeboard — **RATIFICATION ADVANCED. SIGNING CONFIRMED FOR THE ELEVENTH DAY FROM DATE. ALL SPECIAL COMMISSIONS EXPIRE AT RATIFICATION** — and copied the date into his duty log with a pencil worn to a stub.

He was twenty-three. He believed, in the uncomplicated way of twenty-three, in the uniform he kept brushed. He had no idea yet that within the fortnight a satchel would pass under his hands and make the uniform a question instead of an answer.

---

On the ninth night before the signing, they gathered in the inn's back room to make the final arrangements: Ada, Tomas, Ilsa with her satchel, and Osla the net-mender, who came up from the harbor with the smell of tar and hemp on her and a rolled paper under her arm — her sworn account, witnessed by the priest who owed no one anything, of the night her sons went down and whose signatures were missing from which register.

Osla spread her paper on the table beside Ilsa's counted copies and Maren's ruined ledger, and the four documents lay together in the lamplight like pieces of one wreck washed ashore separately, now assembled.

"Eleven days," Ilsa said. "Ten, now."

"Then we cross tomorrow," Ada said. "Through the tollhouse, before Aldric finishes deciding what he is. Straight through, no lingering."

Tomas nodded. Osla nodded. Outside, the wind had begun at last to rise, coming off the water in long slow pulls, and the inn sign creaked on its bracket like a ship at anchor.

What none of them knew — what the lamplight hid along with everything else — was that the friendly clerk at the posting-house desk had already noticed the girl with the school satchel, and had already noticed that she carried it everywhere, even to meals, even to prayers, and had already begun to wonder what a thing so carefully guarded might be worth, and to whom.

The eleventh day was coming for all of them. But for one small greedy heart in a lamplit lobby, it had already come early.
