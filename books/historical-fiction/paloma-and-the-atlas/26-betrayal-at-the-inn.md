## 26. Betrayal at the Inn

The inn at Harrow's Bend had a sign painted with a whale that had never lived in any sea Ilsa knew — a fat, smiling whale, blue as a child's drawing, its tail curled like a question mark. She had stared at it for a long while from the cart, because staring at it was easier than thinking about what was sewn into the lining of her school satchel, wedged between her arithmetic primer and a change of stockings.

*Eleven days,* Ada had said on the cliff path, when the wind had finally dropped and they could speak without shouting. *Eleven days until the signing. After that, the special courts close and the paper in your bag becomes nothing but old ink.*

Eleven days. And now the whale smiled down at her, and the inn's windows glowed amber against the early dark, and inside there would be soup, and a fire, and a bed that did not smell of salt and fear. Ilsa found she wanted those things so badly it frightened her. Wanting things, Maren had warned her once, was how people got caught. You reached for the warm thing and the warm thing turned out to be a trap with your name already carved on the lid.

But her ankle still throbbed where she'd twisted it on the cliff stones, and Lucan was four days dead in unconsecrated ground, and Tomas had not slept properly since the warrant went out with his father's seal on it, and Ada — Ada stood at the inn door with her lighthouse paperwork under her arm like a shield, and said, "We're travelers bound for the capital on keeper's business," in a voice so flat and official that even Ilsa half believed her.

So they went in.

* * *

The clerk's name was Pellam Ostrow, and he introduced himself before anyone asked, which should have been the first warning. He was a small man with soft hands and spectacles that caught the firelight in two round coins of gold, and he sat at the corner table nearest the hearth with a ledger open before him and a pot of tea going cold at his elbow. A traveling clerk for the provincial registry, he said. Auditing toll receipts all along the coast road. Terribly dull work. He said it with a smile that invited them to agree how dull the world was, how harmless, how full of nothing but receipts and road dust.

Ilsa knew about clerks. Her grandfather had been one, before the drownings, before the magistrate's pen moved across three families' names like a tide erasing footprints in sand. Clerks were the ones who made forgetting possible. A soldier only took what was in front of him; a clerk reached back through years and unmade it.

But Pellam Ostrow was kind to her. That was the second warning, and she missed it too, because kindness had become a currency so rare on this journey that she spent it the moment it touched her palm.

"You'll be the girl from Paloma island," he said, when Ada had gone up to arrange the rooms and Tomas had taken the cart to the stable. "The keeper's ward. They talk of you at the tollhouse — well." He adjusted his spectacles. "They talk of everyone who passes through Cousin Aldric's gate. It's a wonder anything gets done on this coast, so much talking."

"We're just passing through," Ilsa said.

"Of course you are." He poured her a cup of tea without asking, and the steam rose between them like something alive. "And how is the light? Still burning? My grandmother used to say you could set a clock by the Paloma lamp. Two flashes, rest, two flashes. She swore by it."

"Two flashes, rest, two flashes," Ilsa agreed, and hated herself a little for the warmth in her voice. But it was true — she had grown up falling asleep to that rhythm through the shutter slats, and some traitor part of her heart leapt whenever anyone spoke of it as if it mattered, as if it were a real thing in the world and not just a tower at the edge of everything.

"A good keeper, then, your Mrs. Ishikawa."

"The best."

"Mm." He sipped his tea. "They say she keeps wonderful records. Logs, charts, weather tables. The registry loves a thorough keeper. Half my audit is chasing after harbormasters who can't spell *January*." He laughed at his own joke, a small dry sound, and Ilsa laughed too, because the fire was warm and the tea was hot and her ankle ached and laughing was easier than counting warnings.

That was the third warning, and she missed it most completely of all.

* * *

Upstairs, in the room with the sloping ceiling and the window that whistled, Ada laid out the evening's discipline the way she laid out everything: plainly, without decoration.

"Tomorrow we're on the capital road by dawn. The satchel doesn't leave your body. Not for washing, not for sleeping, not for anything. You sleep with the strap around your wrist."

"I know."

"And if anyone asks—"

"I'm a schoolgirl going to sit an examination in the capital. My aunt is escorting me. The examination is in surveying and arithmetic." Ilsa recited it wearily. "Ada, I've said it forty times. I could say it in my sleep."

"You may have to." Ada sat on the edge of the narrow bed, and for a moment the lamplight found all the lines in her face, and Ilsa saw how much the last weeks had cost her — the courier dying in her keeping, the sergeant's stamped mercy, the long frozen miles, Lucan buried in the shingle with no prayer and no record. "Ilsa. Look at me."

She looked.

"I have spent three years learning which silences keep people safe and which ones bury them. I'm still not always sure I've learned it right. But I know this: the moment you start believing that a place is safe — an inn, a room, a face — that is the moment it stops being safe. Fear isn't the enemy. Comfort is."

"Yes, Ada."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it." And she did know; that was the terrible thing. She knew it the way she knew the tide tables, perfectly and uselessly, knowledge sitting in her head while her heart went on wanting soup and fire and someone kind to pour her tea.

Ada blew out the lamp. In the dark, Ilsa lay with the satchel strap looped twice around her wrist, and told herself she would not sleep, and slept.

* * *

Pellam Ostrow was at breakfast before any of them, established at his corner table with fresh tea and toast and the air of a man who had been awake for hours accomplishing nothing in particular. When Ilsa came down with the satchel — she had carried it to the privy at dawn, she had carried it to breakfast, she had not let it out of arm's reach except to sleep, and even then it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat — he waved her over with genuine delight.

"Miss Ishikawa's ward! Sit, sit. Your aunt is settling the bill, I expect. These keeper's accounts take forever — all those columns." He pushed the toast rack toward her. "You'll want to eat. The capital road is brutal before the frost lifts. Tell me — are you really sitting the surveying examination? My own niece failed it twice. The triangulation questions, you know. They eat girls alive."

"They're not so bad," Ilsa said, mouth full of toast. "If you remember the base line is always the thing you trust least."

Ostrow laughed, delighted again. "Ha! Listen to her. You'll pass, certainly." Then, casually, folding his napkin: "May I see the satchel?"

The words landed so softly she almost didn't feel them.

"It's just — I collect them. Satchels. School bags. A foolish hobby, but there it is. Provincial leatherwork, yours has that double-stitching they do on the islands, don't they? For the salt air?" His eyes behind the gold-ringed spectacles were mild and interested and utterly empty, two windows into a room where no one was home. "Just to look. I won't bite."

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road that she would walk back down ten thousand times in the years afterward, in dreams and in waking, turning left instead of right, saying *no thank you, it's nothing,* pulling the strap tight and walking out into the frost. Every betrayal has a hinge, and hers was a rack of toast and a lonely little man with a foolish hobby, and she was sixteen and tired and flattered to be treated as a person rather than a parcel.

"It's nothing special," she said, and lifted it onto the table, and opened the flap.

Inside: the arithmetic primer. The stockings. The slate. The examination papers, forged in Tomas's careful hand two nights ago, bearing the seal of the capital school for girls. She showed him these things the way you show a neighbor your garden — a little proud, a little bored. He turned the primer over. He admired the stitching. He asked where the bag was made, and she said the mainland tannery at Saltmarsh, and he nodded as though filing that away among ten thousand other useless facts.

He never touched the lining. He didn't need to. While she held the flap open, tilting the bag toward the window light to show him the maker's stamp, his hand rested on the table beside his teacup — and beneath the table, below the sightline of the serving girl and the stable boy and the whole honest world, a small black box sat in his lap with its brass lens pointed upward, and it had been pointed there since she first sat down, and it made no sound at all when it worked.

Photographs. The new process, the one from the capital, the one that needed no flash and no fuss and no more than a quarter second of stillness. He had photographed the satchel's contents as she displayed them — the primer, the papers, the slate — and earlier, when she'd gone up to bed, he had done something cleverer. The chambermaid, a dull-eyed girl named Petra whom Ostrow had tipped generously the night before, had been instructed that if the young guest's bag was ever set down unattended — even for a minute, even in a corridor, even propped against a chair while its owner wrestled with a bootlace — she was to bring it to the gentleman's table for "safekeeping." And at half past six that morning, while Ilsa had stood in the yard arguing with Tomas about the horse's lame shoe, Petra had fetched the satchel in from the bench where it leaned, and Ostrow had opened the lining with a penknife's patience, and photographed every page of Lucan Reyes's certified copies laid out on the deal table in the grey early light: the inheritance rolls, the drowned families' names struck through in the magistrate's violet ink, the countersigned affidavits, the dates. Eleven exposures. He was thorough. Thoroughness, after all, was his profession.

Then he had sewn the lining shut again with thread from his own kit, slightly finer than the original, a difference no tired girl would ever notice, and returned it to the bench, and tipped Petra another coin, and ordered more tea.

All of this Ilsa learned later. At the time she knew only that the clerk was kind, the toast was good, and when Ada came down with the settled bill and said, "Ilsa. Cart," she closed the flap, thanked Mr. Ostrow for the tea, and walked out into the white morning feeling lighter than she had in days — lighter, she thought, because they had made it through the night, because the worst checkpoints were behind them, because eleven days was enough, was plenty, was time itself stretching out generous as summer.

Behind her, in the corner by the fire, Pellam Ostrow finished his tea, paid with exact change, packed his ledger and his little black box into a leather case, and hired the fastest horse at the livery.

* * *

They made nine miles before the weather turned, sleet coming sideways off the hills, and stopped at noon in a drover's shelter where Tomas built a fire with hands that shook from cold. Ada dozed against the wall. Ilsa fed the horse and came back and sat close to the flames and said, without meaning to say it aloud:

"That clerk. At the inn. Do you think he was all right?"

Tomas looked up from the fire. He had his father's eyes — she had noticed it before, the same grey, the same way of going still when he was thinking — and just now they were very still indeed.

"Why?"

"He was friendly. He knew about the lamp. He asked to see my satchel and I showed him and—" She heard herself, and stopped, and the fire crackled into the silence.

"You showed him." Tomas's voice was level, which was worse than shouting. "You opened it. On the table. In front of him."

"Just the top. The primer, the exam papers, the—"

"The papers with the school seal on them. Which match the story. Which prove nothing." He rubbed his face. "It's fine. It's fine, Ilsa. It was probably fine. A lonely man with a hobby."

"Probably," she echoed, and the word tasted wrong, like water from a bad well.

But it was Ada who woke then — Ada, who slept like a gull, one eye always on the weather — and Ada who said quietly, without opening her eyes all the way: "Describe his hands."

"What?"

"The clerk. His hands."

Isla frowned, reaching back. "Soft. Clean nails. He had ink on the side of his right — no. Both sides. Both hands stained to the wrist."

"Ink to the wrist," Ada repeated. "A toll-receipt auditor carries a stamp and a pencil. An archivist carries ink." She opened her eyes fully now, and the look in them made the fire seem suddenly far away. "Lucan Reyes was an archivist. The men who want what he carried employ others of the same trade. Tomas — how long does a photograph take, the new capital process?"

Tomas had gone very pale. "Seconds. Less. There's a box camera now, no flash, no—"

"How many hours ahead of us is the fast livery horse from Harrow's Bend?"

No one answered. Outside, the sleet ticked against the shelter's tin roof like a clock counting down, and somewhere on the capital road ahead of them, moving fast through the same grey weather, a small man with soft hands was carrying eleven photographs toward the magistrate's men — and behind him, nine miles back, a girl of sixteen sat with her arms wrapped around a satchel whose stitches had been replaced by kinder, finer thread, understanding at last that the trap had never been the whale-shaped sign or the warm room or the fire.

It had been her own wanting. And it had her name carved on the lid.
