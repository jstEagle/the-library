## 28. Flight Along the Cliffs

The wind came up out of the west an hour before dark, the way it always did in that country, as if it had been waiting all day for permission. Ada felt it first in the lamp room glass — a low hum in the panes, a shiver in the brass fittings — and she knew what it meant before she knew anything else: the soldiers would move tonight, and so must they.

She had been a keeper's wife for eleven years and a keeper herself for three, and there was very little weather she had not learned to read the way other people read letters from home. This was storm-weather, or near enough. Not the great closing storm of winter that chained the bridge gates, but something meaner and more particular — a wind with intent behind it, a wind that wanted things moved along.

"They'll come at dusk," Tomas said. He stood at the foot of the tower stairs with his coat already buttoned wrong, and Ada did not correct him. There was no time for the small kindnesses of tidiness. "A warrant like that doesn't wait for morning. They'll want us found in bed, found stupid, found grateful to be arrested gently."

Ilsa came down the stairs two at a time, the satchel strapped across her chest the way Ada had shown her — high, tight, under the coat where a searching hand might mistake it for nothing. Sixteen years old and she moved like someone who had been preparing for catastrophe her whole short life, which perhaps was true of every child raised on an island that fed its men to the sea.

"The copies are sewn in," she said. "All of them. I checked the stitches twice."

"You checked them twice because I asked you to check them twice," Ada said. "Check them again."

Ilsa grinned at her — that grin, quick and unrepentant, Maren's blood showing through like a vein of ore — and went back up the stairs rather than argue. Good girl. A girl who argued spent time arguing. A girl who obeyed spent time running.

Tomas watched her go, then turned to Ada. In the lamplight his face had the gray, planed look of a man who has burned his father's letters and not yet decided whether he is free or ruined. "The cliff path," he said. "You're certain."

"I've walked it in worse than this."

"You've walked it alone. You've walked it knowing every stone. She hasn't." He nodded upward, toward the girl on the stairs. "And neither have I."

"No," Ada agreed. "Neither have you." She took down the storm lantern, the heavy one with the shielded flame, and lit it with hands that did not shake, because she had trained them long ago not to shake, because a keeper whose hands shook let ships die. "Then we'll go slow, and I'll tell you where to put your feet, and you'll hate me by midnight. That's how it's done."

* * *

They left by the seaward door, the one visitors never used, and dropped down onto the black rock below the tower where the spray came up cold as a hand slapped over the mouth. The tide was halfway through its turning, and the causeway was drowned and would be for hours — which meant no one could cross after them either. The island had closed its fist around their leaving, and Ada felt the strange gratitude of it. For three winters the sealed bridge had been a prison. Tonight it was a wall built just for them.

The cliff path began at the old winch house, a track worn into the turf by generations of feet hauling nets and coffins and once, according to Osla's mother, a piano. It ran westward above the sea for four miles before it bent inland toward the coast road and the town that looked away, and for those four miles there was no gate, no checkpoint, no lantern but theirs. Only the drop on one side and the rising moor on the other, and the wind coming in off the water like a congregation singing too loud.

Ada went first. She had said she would and so she did, though every instinct of command told her to put the man ahead, since a man's fall broke fewer hearts than a girl's. But Tomas was a clerk's son and a magistrate's son and a lawyer who had never in his life trusted his body further than a staircase, and Ilsa — Ilsa was island-born, and island-born children learned balance in the cradle. Still, it was Ilsa who frightened her, because Ilsa was sixteen, and sixteen believed itself immortal.

"Hand on the rock, not the grass," Ada called back, when the path narrowed above a gully where the waves were detonating white. "Grass lies. Grass says it will hold and then it comes away in your hand like a confession. Rock keeps its promises even when it's wet."

"Did your husband teach you that?" Ilsa shouted against the wind.

"My husband taught me navigation. The cliff taught me the rest."

Behind them both, Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by fear. Good, Ada thought. Let him laugh. Fear laughed at was fear halved.

The light went fully then, the last gray drained off the sky, and the world contracted to the circle of the storm lantern and the memory of the path beneath her boots. Ada had walked this track perhaps a thousand times, and in daylight she could have done it blindfolded; but night changed stone the way grief changed faces — same features, different meaning. She counted her steps without deciding to. Forty-two to the split boulder. Thirty to the stile with the broken top rail. Nineteen to the place where the turf gave way to scree and you crossed sideways, heel to toe, like a dancer on a wire, with the sea breathing below you in the dark.

At the scree she stopped and turned and held the lantern low, and the two shapes behind her resolved out of the blackness — Tomas crouched, both palms flat to the rock, moving with the exaggerated care of a man crossing a frozen river; Ilsa upright, almost casual, her chin lifted into the wind as if she were tasting it.

"Sideways," Ada said. "Heel to toe. Tomas first, hold my hand. Then the girl, and she holds nothing, because if she falls I want her arms free."

"That's not fair," Ilsa said.

"It's not fair that any of us are here. Go."

Tomas crossed it badly and honestly, gasping once when a stone shifted under his boot and went clattering down into the dark, a long time falling. Ada held his wrist hard enough to bruise and thought, distantly, that she was holding the magistrate's son over a four-hundred-foot drop and trusting him with her whole weight, and that a year ago the sentence would have been nonsense in any language she spoke.

Then Ilsa came across lightly, almost dancing, and Ada's heart unclenched by half a degree.

It was two miles later, past the ruined chapel and the field where the sheep stood like monuments, that the wind turned from enemy to assassin. It had been blowing from the sea all evening, steady as tide. Now it swung south, and came down off the moor in gusts that arrived like decisions — sudden, total, gone. Between gusts the air lay flat and listening. In the gusts it shoved.

Ada had known this wind her whole life. It was the wind that took chimneys and boats and, twice in her memory, people — a thatcher off the roof at Harrow Point, a boy off the harbor wall who had been dared. It was the wind you did not argue with on high ground.

"We should cut down to the fisherman's stair," she shouted, "while we're level with it—"

And the gust came.

Later she would assemble it from fragments, the way one assembled any disaster: the flat lull, the roar arriving from the south, Ilsa turning to look back at Tomas — why had she turned, what had she heard or imagined, some small stumble behind her, some cry — and the wind taking her at the exact moment her weight left the uphill foot. Ada saw it happen with the terrible clarity that disasters grant: the girl's arms pinwheeling, her boot skidding on turf slicked by the earlier rain, the satchel swinging out from under her coat like a pendulum, the dark beyond her shoulder not empty but full — full of slope and scree and the long silver smear of foam far below.

Ada lunged. Her hand closed on wool — coat sleeve, then forearm, then the fine bones of a wrist — and the weight of the girl came down the slope into her like a boat taking water, and the two of them went to their knees together on the path with the wind screaming over their backs and Tomas's voice somewhere above, high and cracked, saying her name, saying the girl's name, saying both names over and over as if repetition were a rope.

"My ankle—" Ilsa's face was pressed against Ada's shoulder. "My ankle, I heard it—"

"Don't stand on it. Don't stand." Ada got her arm around the girl's waist and held on and breathed, three breaths, four, until the gust passed and the ordinary wind resumed, and the world was only the world again: dark, cold, dangerous, survivable.

Tomas reached them and knelt, and by the shielded lantern they looked at the ankle together. It was already swelling inside the boot, a hot shape under the leather, and Ilsa sat with her jaw set and her eyes bright and furious — at herself, Ada guessed, more than the wind.

"Can you walk on it?" Tomas asked.

"She can walk on it," Ada said, "badly, leaning on me, for maybe a mile. Maybe less. And then we are three people standing on a cliff path in a rising wind with a warrant behind us and nowhere open ahead." She looked down the track, toward the faint smudge of the coast road, toward the town with its lit windows and its convenient forgetting, and she measured the distance and hated the arithmetic. "Or we find the net-loft."

"What net-loft?" Tomas said.

"Osla Vane's family drying-loft. It's off the path above Kell Cove — half a mile down. Old woman lives alone in the cottage beside it. Her sons—" Ada stopped. Everyone on the island knew about Osla's sons; it was one of the silences the community kept together, the way a family kept a grave weeded without speaking of the person in it. "Her sons were lost. In the drownings. Before the war."

Tomas understood at once, because he was not stupid, only late. "The families whose records were erased."

"Whose records his father erased, yes." Ada said it plainly. She had promised herself, burning alongside Tomas at the stove, that she would stop softening the truth for him. Softening was how the rot had gotten in everywhere, in ledgers and in love alike. "So we are going to ask a woman who lost everything to your father's pen whether she will hide his son in her loft. And you are going to let me do the asking."

* * *

The half mile took an hour. They made a machine of themselves, the three of them: Ada bearing most of Ilsa's weight with the girl's arm across her shoulders, Tomas ahead with the lantern calling out the ground — "stone, step, loose turf, step" — in a flat reciting voice that Ada suspected he did not know he'd adopted, the voice of a man reading evidence aloud. The wind rose steadily as they went, packing itself into the cove below and booming up out of it like surf inside a drum. Once, far behind, a light flickered along the cliff path where they had come from — small, yellow, deliberate. A lantern being carried by someone who knew the way.

Nobody spoke about it. Speaking would have cost breath they needed for walking.

Kell Cove announced itself by smell before sight — weed and tar and old fish, the honest stink of working water — and then by the pale seam of sand far below, and then, when they had taken the steep drover's track down in a series of controlled slides that cost Tomas his dignity and Ilsa several bitten-back cries, by the cottage itself: low, stone, one window lit amber against the dark, and beside it the long black bulk of the drying-loft on its posts, netting hanging from its beams like the sails of a wrecked ship.

Ada hammered on the door with the heel of her fist and waited, and in the pause between knocks she heard the wind carrying something down the cliff behind them — voices now, or the imagination of voices — and understood that whatever happened next would happen quickly or not at all.

Bolt. Chain. Door.

Osla Vane stood in the gap with a gutting knife in one hand and a rushlight in the other, seventy years old and straight as a mast, her face seamed like driftwood and absolutely unreadable.

"Keeper Ishikawa," she said.

"Osla." Ada did not explain. Explanation was for people who had time. "We need the loft. Tonight. Maybe tomorrow night. The girl's hurt and there are men on the cliff path with a warrant, and what they want is in this bag." She touched Ilsa's shoulder. "It's the papers. Your sons' papers. What was stolen from the drowned families. We're carrying it to the capital, and we're nearly there, and I am asking you for the last mile of it."

The wind gusted between them and guttered the rushlight, and in the darkness Osla said nothing for the length of three waves breaking. Ada stood in it and did not fill the silence, because she had learned that from the island too: some questions you had to leave out like bait and walk away from, trusting the sea.

Then Osla stepped aside, and the knife-hand gestured them in, and her voice, when it came, was dry as shingle.

"Bring her to the fire. And you—" the knife-point indicated Tomas, and the old woman's eyes, pale as winter water, fixed on him with a steadiness that made him flinch, "you'll tell me your name before you cross my floor. I'll know who I'm sheltering, or the loft stays shut."

"Tomas," he said. His voice failed and he gathered it again. "Tomas Dunmore-Vale. My father is the magistrate. My father erased the record of your sons, and I burned his letters tonight, and I am going to see it set right if it costs me everything I have." He swallowed. "That's my name. All of it."

Osla looked at him a long moment. Somewhere up on the cliff, faint but unmistakable, a whistle sounded — two notes, answered by one.

"In," she said. "Quickly, all of you. And keeper—" she was already barring the door behind them, driving the bolt home like a period at the end of a sentence, "—whatever you're carrying in that bag, you'll show it to me before dawn. An old woman doesn't hide strangers on faith anymore. Faith drowned with my boys."

She took the rushlight and led them through the cottage, past the fire, out the back door and up the plank ramp to the loft, where the hanging nets stirred in the wind like a congregation rising — and it was there, among the nets, with Ilsa's ankle packed in snow and the satchel open between them, that Osla Vane sat down across from Ada, folded her seamed hands, and said:

"Now. Before I decide anything else — show me what my sons died for. And then I'll tell you something about that night that isn't in any record, erased or otherwise. Something you'll need, come the capital. Something I've been keeping twenty years, waiting for someone to carry it."
