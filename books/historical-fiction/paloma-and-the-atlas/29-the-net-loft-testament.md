## 29. The Net-Loft Testament

The wind came off the water the way grief comes off a grave — steady, without malice, and impossible to argue with.

Osla Venn felt it through the walls of her net-loft before she heard anything else: the long low shove of a northwesterly against the boards, the creak of the ridge-pole like an old man rising from a chair, and beneath it all the sea working at the rocks below, patient as a woman unpicking a seam. She had lived sixty-one years on this coast, and she had learned that the sea never hurried. It only persisted. Everything else — men, magistrates, governments, even the ice on the causeway — wore itself out eventually. The sea just kept coming back to the same stones.

She was mending a herring net when the knock came. Three soft raps, spaced wrong for a neighbor. Neighbors knocked loud and called out your name before their knuckles touched wood, because out here privacy was a courtesy you offered by pretending not to have any. These knocks were careful. Ashamed, almost.

Osla set down her needle. She looked at the lamp burning in its glass niche above the door — a whale-oil relic her husband had installed before he drowned too, though no one ever said so plainly, because on this coast drowning was not an event but a weather — and then she went and lifted the latch.

Ada Ishikawa stood on the landing with the wind pulling at her coat like a dog on a lead. Behind her, half-hidden in the dark of the cliff path, were two figures Osla knew only by reputation: a tall young man holding up another young man who seemed made mostly of wet wool, and a girl of perhaps sixteen with her ankle bound in what looked suspiciously like one of Ada's good table linens, leaning on a driftwood stave.

"Osla," Ada said. Not asking. That was the thing about Ada Ishikawa; she had learned, somewhere in those three silent years since her husband's boat came back without him, how to say a person's name as if the answer had already been given and found sufficient.

Osla looked past her, down the path, listening with the particular attention of someone who has spent a lifetime listening for engines that should not be there. The night was empty but for wind. Still, she stepped aside and pulled them all in fast, the way you pull a child back from a wave that has already begun its withdrawal.

"In," she said. "And don't stand where the window shows you."

* * *

The net-loft sat above the high-tide line on the shoulder of Gannet Head, half boatshed, half chapel, all of it hers since her husband's insurance money had gone further than his wages ever had. Nets hung from the beams in pale curtains — some whole, many torn, none thrown away, because Osla could not bring herself to discard a net whose knots her own hands had tied in the years when her hands still belonged to a woman with three sons.

Three sons. The number sat inside her the way a fishhook sits in a finger: worked deep, healed over, always there when the weather changed.

Joren, twenty-two, lost with the *Merry Alice* in the spring drownings. Piet, nineteen, lost the same week, different boat, same storm, same water. And Tam, seventeen, who had not been lost at all but taken — marched off by soldiers the following winter for asking questions about why his brothers' boats had been licensed to men who had never set foot in them, and who had come back two years later in a box marked only with a number, no letter permitted, no inquiry answered, buried outside consecrated ground because desertion was written on his papers and desertion was what they called it when a boy refused to serve the men who had stolen his family blind.

Osla had stopped going to church after that. Not out of anger. Out of arithmetic. A God who counted her son among deserters while the magistrate counted her land among his cousin's leases was a God whose ledgers she declined to audit further.

Now she fed these strangers salt fish and barley broth and watched them eat like people who had forgotten food was a thing bodies wanted. The fevered one — Lucan, Tomas called him, and the name meant nothing to her except as a word spoken over a body — lay on her cot under every blanket she owned, breathing in shallow pulls, his lips moving around syllables that never finished. The girl, Ilsa, slept sitting upright against a stack of creel baskets, her bound ankle propped on an overturned dory, her chin dropping and jerking up, dropping and jerking, a girl fighting sleep the way you fight sleep only when you have learned recently that sleep is when things are taken from you.

Tomas stood at the small seaward window with his back to the room. He had introduced himself with his full name — Tomas Verrin, son of Magistrate Aldous Verrin — and Osla had looked at him for a long moment before answering, long enough that Ada had shifted her weight as if to step between them.

"You have his hands," Osla said finally. "Your father's. I've seen them sign the death notices."

Tomas had flinched. Good, she'd thought. A man who flinches can still be reached.

"He doesn't know we're here," Tomas had said. "He'll know soon enough."

"Then we'd best be done with knowing soon," Osla had replied, and ladled him broth.

* * *

It was near midnight when Ada came to sit beside her at the mending bench, and the others were asleep or pretending, and the wind had settled into its long monotonous argument with the roof.

"I'm sorry to bring this to your door," Ada said.

"Whose door would you rather have brought it to?" Osla asked. "Brandt's? He owes you a stamp and a silence. Aldric's? He owes nobody nothing." She threaded her needle by touch, an old trick, eyes on the firelight. "You brought it to my door because my door is where this belongs. My sons' names are in that packet. Don't think I haven't guessed."

Ada didn't deny it. She reached into her coat and brought out the oilskin packet — the original now, the copies sewn into Lucan's coat having already crossed the causeway weeks ago, or so Osla understood from the fragments she'd pieced together — and laid it on the bench between them like something rescued from a wreck. Which, Osla supposed, it was.

"The clerk stole these from the capital archive," Ada said. "They're certified copies. Inheritance records. After the drownings, the special court declared three families' claims void — unregistered, lapsed, abandoned. Your sons' boats. Your husband's berth rights. Maren's grandfather's salt fields. All of it erased on paper signed by—" 

"I know who signed it." Osla's voice came out flat and even, which surprised her; she had expected it to crack. "I've known since the year the tax rolls came round and my name wasn't on them anymore. You don't forget the shape of your own erasure. You just learn to stop pointing at it, because pointing gets your last boy conscripted."

The words hung between them. Outside, the wind found a loose shingle and worried at it, tick, tick, tick, like a clock that had given up counting hours and was counting something smaller.

"They're taking this to the capital," Ada said. "To file before the armistice terms ratify and the old courts close forever. Eleven days. But there's a hearing afterward — a restitution hearing, once the documents are registered. They'll need witnesses. Sworn accounts. Testimony from the families themselves, or the paper is just paper." She paused. "Ilsa told me about your sons. About Tam."

"Did she." Osla looked toward the girl, asleep against the creels, her mouth finally slack, her fists still closed even in sleep around the strap of that wretched school satchel. Sixteen and fearless, Ada had said, and Osla saw it was true, and saw too that fearless was just another word for somebody who hadn't yet learned what fear cost.

"She wants me to write an account," Osla said. "A sworn statement. To put my boys into a government ledger."

"Yes."

"And you came to ask me yourself because you thought an old woman might refuse a girl but not a keeper." Osla almost smiled. "Or because you couldn't ask it without looking at me while I decided."

"Both," Ada said simply. "I've spent three years learning that silences keep people safe. And I've spent the last month learning what they cost instead. I won't choose for you. Nobody gets to choose that for you again."

Osla was quiet a long time. She turned the packet over in her hands. Oilskin, wax-sealed, heavier than it looked. Paper. She had mended nets her whole life, and she knew the physics of small things: a knot the size of a fingernail holds a ton of fish; a signature the size of a thumbprint had held three families underwater for six years. Small things carried great weights, if you tied them right.

"My Joren had a laugh you could hear across the harbor," she said at last, not looking at Ada, looking at the fire. "My Piet was shy. Sang only when he thought no one listened, and had a voice like — I don't know what. Like something the church wouldn't let in and the sea wouldn't give back." Her thumb moved along the oilskin's edge. "Tam wrote letters. Forty-one of them. The army returned eleven after he died, censored to ribbons. I have them in a tin under my bed. The rest they kept. Evidence, they said. His own words, evidence against him."

She stood, joints cracking like the ridge-pole, and went to the corner where the tin box sat beneath her cot. She brought it back and opened it: letters, a lock of hair gone dull, a wooden bird Tam had carved at twelve, and beneath everything, folded twice and yellowed, a single sheet of official stationery stamped with the seal of the special court.

"What's that?" Ada asked.

"The notice. When they declared the claims void." Osla smoothed it flat on the bench with both hands, the way you smooth a shroud. "I kept it because Maren said burn it and I said no. You don't burn evidence. Even when it's evidence of your own ruin. Especially then." She looked up. "Six years I've kept a dead man's paperwork dry in a fishing loft waiting for someone to come ask for it. Tell your girl the account will be ready by dawn."

* * *

She wrote it by lamplight while the others slept, and she wrote it the way she mended nets: slowly, knot by knot, testing each one.

*Osla Venn, widow, of Gannet Head, being of sound mind and full memory, do swear as follows.*

Her pen scratched. The wind kept its time.

*I had three sons. Joren Venn, born such-and-such a year, master of the Merry Alice, license number 4471, which license I hold in my possession to this day though the court record says no such license was ever issued. Piet Venn, born such-and-such, deckhand aboard the Sea Lark, whose share-book I also hold, entered in his own hand, dated the week before the storm. The court declares these vessels unlicensed and these men unrecorded. I swear before God and whatever is left of the law that this is false, and that it was made false by hand.*

Then the harder part. She dipped the pen and let it hover, and the lamplight moved on the wall, and she thought of Ada saying *nobody gets to choose that for you again,* and she wrote:

*My third son, Tam Venn, aged seventeen, was taken by gendarmes in the winter following the drownings, for asking publicly why his brothers' licenses had passed to men who never sailed. He was charged with sedition, then desertion when he attempted escape. He died in custody at Halden Depot. His body returned in a numbered box. I was forbidden to hold a public mourning. I am forbidding myself nothing now. His letters are appended. Read them and tell me what a seditious boy sounds like. He writes about the weather. He writes about whether I am eating. He asks after the nets.*

She read that last line back and had to put the pen down and go stand at the seaward window until her breath steadied, watching black water work at black rock, thinking: *he asks after the nets.* Of all the sentences in forty-one letters, that was the one that broke her open, because it meant he had understood, even at seventeen, even in that place, that the nets were not rope and twine but the family itself, its whole woven memory, and that as long as someone was mending them the family existed.

When she came back to the bench, Tomas was awake. He had risen without a sound and stood a few feet off, holding his father's second letter — she had seen it earlier, warm and reasonable, offering him a judgeship — and he was feeding it to the stove a corner at a time.

They watched each other over the flame.

"My father signed the order that took your son," he said. "I found the countersignature in Lucan's papers tonight. My own name isn't on it. But it would have been, in time. He was training me to sign things."

"Was he," Osla said. "And what did you train yourself for?"

"To know the difference between a document and a lie with a seal on it." The last corner curled and went. "I'd like to witness your statement, Mrs. Venn. If a sworn account needs witnesses. I know what my name is worth right now — not much, maybe less than nothing. But it's mine, and it's honest, and I'd spend it on this."

Osla studied him a long moment. Then she nodded toward the bench. "Sit. Take the pen after me. Write beneath my signature: *Witnessed, Tomas Verrin, who acknowledges the foregoing as true and names his father's office as the source of the falsification.* Can you write that?"

"Yes," Tomas said, and there was something in his voice she recognized — the sound of a man setting down a weight he had mistaken for part of his own body.

* * *

Dawn came gray and hard, the color of unwashed wool, and with it came the sound Osla had been listening for in her bones all night: an engine, far off, throttling low along the cliff road.

Ada heard it too. She was on her feet before the second pulse of it, waking Ilsa, waking herself fully in the space of a breath, and Osla marveled at that — a woman who could move from sleep to decision faster than most people moved from sleep to sitting.

"How did they find us?" Ilsa whispered, hauling her bad ankle under her, one hand already locked on the satchel.

"The inn clerk," Tomas said grimly. "Or the photographs he sold. Or plain luck. It doesn't matter. What matters is the road below us and how much of a head start we have."

"It matters to me," Osla said, and her voice stopped them all. She rose, stiff and slow on purpose now, an old woman making the world wait for her, and she took the oilskin packet — her notice folded inside it now, her statement inside that, Tomas's witnessed line inside that, Tam's letters copied fair and the originals staying in the tin where they belonged — and she pressed it into Ada's hands.

"Then hear what matters," she said. "That road curves at the old signal post and doesn't come back into sight of this loft for four hundred paces. There's a goat track behind the peat stack that drops to the shingle, and from the shingle a fisherman's walk runs north to the estuary flats, where no wheeled vehicle has ever gone and none ever will. My husband cut that walk. My sons walked it. It's yours now." She looked at Ada, then at the girl, then at the son of the magistrate, and she found — to her own surprise, after six years of practicing neutrality toward all living men — that her eyes had gone hot. "Take my testament to the capital. Put my boys' names in the ledger where the law can't reach in and scratch them out. And when they ask who helped you—"

"We'll tell them," Ada said. "All of it. Every name."

"Aye." Osla swallowed. "Every name. That's the only kind of record worth keeping."

Below, the engine climbed. Somewhere down the cliff road, boots would be hitting gravel, a warrant would be unfolding, a sergeant or a lieutenant or worse would be reading out the words *thief of state records* in Ada Ishikawa's direction. Osla pulled her door wide and stood in it, an old woman in the doorway of a net-loft full of ghosts, arms folded, blocking the view up the path with nothing but her own stubborn silhouette.

"Go," she said. "The tide's with you, and so am I. Now run, before I start crying and shame us all."

And behind her, as they slipped out the back into the wind, the lamp in its glass niche guttered once and held — and Osla Venn stood watch over her empty loft, guarding nothing now but the truth already walking north, and listened to the soldiers come up her hill.
