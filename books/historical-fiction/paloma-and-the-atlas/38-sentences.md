## 38. Sentences

### I.

*Tomas*

They gave him a chair with a cushion, which was the strangest part of it all.

Tomas had expected the dock — the hard rail, the warder at his shoulder, the gallery breathing behind him like weather. What he got instead was a side room off the assize court, a solicitor who smelled of ink and camphor, and a judge who kept looking at him as though trying to place him at some dinner party years ago. The cushion was green velvet. Tomas sat on it and felt like an impostor in his own trial.

The charge had been read that morning: theft of state records, conspiracy to remove certified documents from the custody of the special courts, obstruction of the armistice administration. The clerk's voice went through the list the way a man reads names off a monument he has walked past every day for twenty years — without hearing any of them.

Tomas pleaded guilty to all three counts.

He had considered pleading otherwise. There were arguments available; there always were, for men like him. He could have claimed coercion, claimed filial duress, claimed that a son cannot be expected to prosecute his own father's house. He could have hidden behind Lucan Reyes's ghost and said the clerk acted alone. Every argument would have worked, or worked well enough, because the courts were already embarrassed by the whole affair and wanted it over quietly, the way one wants a funeral conducted before the flowers wilt.

But he had stood in front of young Officer Vey at the capital gate and traded himself for a moment of hesitation, and a trade is a trade. A man who breaks his word at a checkpoint will break it everywhere. His father had taught him that much, though not in those words, and not meaning it kindly.

So: guilty, guilty, guilty. The clerk wrote it down. Somewhere in the building, he supposed, a record was being amended, and the irony of it — Tomas Dunmore, erasing himself into the official paper — did not escape him. He almost smiled. The solicitor mistook it for nerves and patted his arm with a dry hand.

The sentencing took eleven minutes.

Suspended sentence, two years, conditioned on good behavior. Struck from the roll of eligible magistrates for life. Exile from the bench — the phrase was actually spoken aloud, *exile from the bench*, as though the courtroom were a country and Tomas a citizen renounced. He was fined an amount that stung but did not ruin him, and the judge, delivering it all in a voice like a man reading a laundry list, paused only once. At the end. When the gallery had emptied and the clerks were stacking papers.

"You understand," the old judge said, not unkindly, "that this is not mercy."

"I know what it is," Tomas said.

"Do you? Because half this court thinks you're a traitor and the other half thinks you're a hero, and both halves are wrong. You are a man who chose late." The judge gathered his spectacles. "Late choices cost less than early ones. That is not justice. It is arithmetic. Go home."

*Go home.* Tomas walked out into the grey afternoon with the sentence folded inside his coat like a letter he'd rather not reread. Home. His father's house was sealed now, its windows whitewashed against the curious. The bench was closed to him forever. What remained was a rented room above a chandler's shop, and beyond that —

Beyond that, the causeway. And Ada. And the island, whose gates were even now being unchained; he'd heard it in the town, heard it the way you hear spring coming under winter — not yet visible, but audible, if you listened.

He found he was not bitter. That surprised him most of all. He had expected bitterness to arrive like luggage after the verdict, heavy and addressed to him, and instead there was only a lightness that felt almost indecent, given everything. Uneven justice, he thought. His father might hang — no, no, nothing so dramatic; his father would serve years, quiet ones, in a prison where the food was bad and no one called him *Your Honor*. Aldric would walk away richer than he'd entered. Brandt would retire on full pension. And Tomas, who had burned letters and chosen sides aloud, would carry a suspended sentence around his neck like a medal struck in lead.

It wasn't fair. He weighed it in his hands, walking down the courthouse steps, and found the weight acceptable. Some scales you don't correct. You just learn to read them honestly.

### II.

*Aldric*

Cousin Aldric watched the assize proceedings from the public gallery, wearing his second-best coat, because a man turning crown's evidence should look prosperous but not gaudy.

He had made his calculations months ago, back at the tollhouse, when the cart came through with the schoolgirl and the lighthouse paperwork and the lie so thin you could have read a newspaper through it. He had known then — known with the particular clarity that comes to men whose fortunes are built on reading which way a wind will turn — that the magistrate was finished. The packet existed. The packet had witnesses. The armistice courts were closing, and when they closed, every falsified record became either ammunition or ash, depending entirely upon who held it first.

Aldric held it first. Or rather, he had let it pass, and then, when the soldiers came asking questions with warrants attached, he had remembered — vividly, movingly, with details he barely had to invent — every conversation he'd ever had with his cousin about the drowned families' land. He had turned crown's evidence the way other men turn mattresses: thoroughly, and for comfort.

It had worked. It had worked beautifully. His lease on Maren's fields was voided, yes — a loss, but a survivable one, written off against the far greater asset of his own liberty. The prosecutor had called him *a cooperative witness of considerable courage*, and Aldric had nearly laughed aloud in the witness box. Courage. He'd have accepted the word if it paid better.

What he hadn't anticipated — and this annoyed him, genuinely annoyed him, the way a miscalculated tide annoys a man who owns boats — was how little satisfaction there was in it. He had expected to feel clever. Instead, walking out of the courthouse past Tomas Dunmore's hollow-eyed exit, he felt something adjacent to shame, which was a sensation he had not entertained since childhood and found as uncomfortable as a wet boot.

The trouble was Maren. He couldn't stop thinking about Maren.

Not remorse — he want to be clear about that, he told himself, striding down toward the harbor where his accounts waited. Not the soft-hearted nonsense the newspapers liked. But he had walked those salt fields himself, twice a year, collecting rents, and he had seen her standing at the boundary stone some mornings like a woman visiting a grave. And he had taken her grandfather's plowland and leased it back to her at rates calculated to the penny, and she had paid, every season, without a word, and there had been something in her silence that his ledgers had no column for.

Now the fields were hers again. The surveyors had been through in the thaw, driving their white stakes into the black earth, and the papers restoring title bore the registrar's stamp — the same stamp, someone told him, that Ada Ishikawa had raced the armistice clock to reach. The land was going home. And Aldric had helped send it there, for reasons that would look noble in the retelling and had been anything but at the time.

He bought a drink at the harbor tavern and drank it standing, like a man between errands.

"Turned evidence, then," said the barman, wiping the counter with the aggression of a man who has opinions. "That true?"

"It's true."

"And your cousin in the dock."

"My cousin is where his paperwork put him." Aldric set down the coin. "We all go where our paperwork puts us, friend. Some of us just get to choose which documents."

The barman looked at him the way islanders look at mainlanders — a long, flat, unhurried look that assessed and dismissed in the same breath. Aldric recognized it. It was the look Maren gave him across the boundary stone. He had a sudden, unwelcome premonition that he would be seeing that look for the rest of his life, in every mirror in every room he ever owned.

He finished the drink and left a second coin, which was unlike him, and which he refused to examine.

### III.

*Brandt*

Sergeant Brandt retired on a Tuesday, in his uniform, with his boots polished, and nobody said a word against him.

There had been talk, of course. In the weeks after the magistrate's fall, when the hearings ran like spring water and every name in the packet got read out over breakfast tables from here to the capital, there had been talk. *Brandt waved them through. Brandt saw the satchel and stamped it cleared. Brandt owes the Ishikawa woman.* Men talked the way gulls talk — loudly, and without consequence.

But the inquiry, when it came — and Brandt had expected it, had polished his boots for it — was brief. An officer with a notebook asked whether he had inspected a party crossing from the Paloma causeway in the first week of ice. Yes. Asked whether he had searched them. Inspected them, yes; searched, no — the distinction was noted. Asked why.

Brandt had rehearsed several answers on the walk to the inquiry room. Clever answers. Evasive answers. He discarded them all somewhere between the gate and the chair.

"Because I owed a debt," he said, "and I paid it."

The officer's pen stopped. "To whom?"

"To a dead man. Who isn't dead. You'll find him in the wreck records of the *Merrow*, nineteen years back — pulled off the north rocks in a February sea by a lighthouse keeper named Dunmore, who broke two fingers doing it and never once mentioned it afterward. I was the man on the rocks." Brandt folded his hands. "Ada Ishikawa is his widow. She asked me for one stamp. I gave it. Write down whatever you need to write."

The officer wrote for a while. Then, without looking up: "You knew what she carried?"

"I knew it was heavier than her coat."

The inquiry concluded that Sergeant Brandt had exercised *regrettable discretion within the scope of his inspection authority*. No charges. No suspension. A letter for his file that no one would ever read.

His pension came through the same week as his retirement papers, and he signed both with the same pen. On his last morning at the mainland gate, he stood a final time on the stones where he had watched the little party come up out of the freezing fog — the keeper woman straight-backed, the magistrate's son gray as ash, the girl with the satchel clutched like scripture — and he found, taking inventory of himself as a man does at such thresholds, that he was untroubled.

Not innocent. He didn't claim that. A stamp is a small thing, but small things tip scales, and his had tipped along with everyone else's. The justice being handed out that season was uneven as a cobbled street — Tomas carrying his exile, Aldric walking free with his accounts balanced, the magistrate doing hard years while lesser men did none. Brandt had listened to enough of it in the taverns to know the ledger wouldn't balance. It never had. Justice was a lighthouse beam, not a sun; it showed you the rocks nearest the light and left the rest dark, and you steered by what you could see.

But he had looked at Ada Ishikawa in the fog, and he had thought: *her husband pulled me off those rocks with two broken fingers, and he did it because the man on the rocks was the man on the rocks, no other reason, no ledger at all.* And beside that debt, a career spent stamping papers for men who deserved less scrutiny than she did seemed a poor thing to defend.

He hung his cap on its last peg, nodded to the young corporal taking the shift, and walked out through the gate he had manned for thirty-one years.

The causeway was open now. He could see the island across the water, the tower white in the spring light, and he stood a while looking at it, hatless, letting the wind do what it liked with his hair.

Then he went home to his garden, and was untroubled there too.

### IV.

*Tomas*

The letters began arriving in April.

Not from his father — those had stopped, or perhaps were being intercepted; Tomas neither knew nor asked. These were other letters. From clerks in the capital archives who had read the Reyes documents and wept over their desks. From sons and daughters of the drowned families, writing to thank the magistrate's son, of all people, in careful handwriting on cheap paper. One from a law student who asked, with the breathtaking directness of youth, whether Tomas intended to practice again someday, because *the courts need men who have learned what silence costs.*

He answered that one. He answered most of them. It was slow work, answering by hand in the rented room above the chandler's, with the smell of tar and hemp coming up through the floorboards, but it was work that pointed somewhere, and he had learned the value of that.

On a bright cold morning near the end of April, he took the coast road north, walking, because he had no horse and no reason to hurry. The land was coming alive the way it did here — grudgingly, then all at once. Gorse going yellow on the cliffs. Lambs in the salt meadows that used to be Maren's, that were Maren's again, their voices carrying thin across the flats.

At the head of the causeway he stopped, because the gates were open.

Not merely unlocked — *open*, flung wide, the chains gone from the posts, the barrier timbers swung back against the abutment like arms uncrossed. The tide was low and the causeway stones stood bare and drying, a long spine of granite running out to the island through shallow glittering water. And coming across it, in twos and threes, in carts and on foot, with bundles and children and dogs — the people. The ones who had crossed before the closure and the ones who had stayed, meeting in the middle of the stones, shouting greetings that the wind shredded and delivered anyway. Someone was laughing. Someone was crying. From this distance it was impossible to tell which, and from the sound of it, unnecessary.

And at the far end of the causeway, small against the tower, a figure stood watching the crossing. White hair whipping loose from its knot. Standing very straight, the way she stood at the lamp.

Tomas lifted his hand — a foolish gesture at that distance, invisible surely, meaningless certainly — and lifted it anyway.

She raised hers.

He started walking down onto the stones, out into the sound of the water and the returning voices, toward the tower and the woman in it, and toward whatever the island meant to ask of him next.

* * *

Behind him, up the coast road, a rider was coming — fast, raising dust, carrying something that would reach the causeway before he reached its middle.
