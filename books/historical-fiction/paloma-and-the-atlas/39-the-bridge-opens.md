## 39. The Bridge Opens

The chains came off on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the gulls woke, and Ada heard the sound of it from her kitchen — a long iron complaint, link by link, as though the bridge itself were being unbound from sleep.

She had not expected to hear it so clearly. All winter the wind had carried the noise of the island to her: the slap of frozen rope against the mast, the groan of the lamp room's shutters, the sea working at the causeway stones like an old woman worrying a loose tooth. But this morning the air was still, and the sound of Sergeant Brandt's men striking the padlock free came across the water with terrible clarity, each blow landing somewhere beneath her ribs.

Ada set down her cup. The tea had gone cold hours ago; she had made it at midnight and forgotten it, because she had spent those hours standing at the window watching the ice on the causeway soften, then weep, then run in bright threads into the black water. For three days the thaw had been eating the winter away, and now the stones stood bare and dripping, dark as whaleback, running in a straight line from her island to the mainland gate.

The gates were open. She could see them from here — two timber leaves swung wide like arms, their hinges still rusted red from the chaining, and beyond them the road, and beyond the road the town, and beyond the town everything else.

She had lived four months inside a sealed world. Now the world was coming back through the gap, and she found herself afraid of it in a way she had never been afraid of the storm.

* * *

They came first as sounds.

A cart wheel on stone — that unmistakable wooden knock, rhythmic and patient, that Ada had not heard since autumn. Then voices, more than one, overlapping, and the particular pitch of children arguing about something of no consequence whatsoever. A dog barking itself hoarse with joy at being allowed to run ahead. The creak of harness leather. Someone whistling — badly, three notes repeated, a fisherman's habit she knew without knowing whose it was.

Ada stood in her doorway with her hands folded at her waist and watched the first crossing since the storm.

It was Maren who came first, of course. Maren, who had waited out the winter on the mainland side only because the surveyors' summons had come and she would not miss it; Maren, who walked now with her granddaughter Ilsa's arm through hers, though Maren needed no support and both of them knew it was Ilsa who leaned. They came up the causeway path slow as a funeral procession and bright as a wedding, and when they reached Ada's gate Maren stopped and looked at her for a long moment.

"Well," Maren said.

"Well," said Ada.

Neither of them moved. Four months of sealed silence stood between them, and all the letters neither had written, and the hearing that still hung over them all like weather. Then Ilsa let go of her grandmother's arm, crossed the space between them, and hugged Ada hard enough to make her stagger, and the spell broke, and Maren laughed — a rusty, cracked-bell laugh — and embraced her too, smelling of woodsmoke and wool and the mainland town she had been forced to inhabit.

"You look thin," Maren said, holding her at arm's length.

"You look old."

"I am old." Maren's eyes went past her, up the slope, to the lighthouse standing white against the pale sky. "Is it true? What they're saying in town?"

"That depends on what they're saying."

"That you lit the lamp every night this winter. Every night, even after—" Maren stopped. Even after Lucan. Even after there were no ships left to guide, no boats willing to cross, nothing on that water but ice and the memory of drowned men. "Every night," she finished softly.

"The lamp doesn't know there's no one to see it," Ada said. "That seemed no reason to lie to it."

Maren looked at her strangely then, and Ada understood that she had said something truer than she intended, something that belonged to the woman she was becoming rather than the woman she had been. She turned toward the house. "Come in. There's tea. There's very little else, but there is tea."

"There's forty people behind us and a ferry due at noon," Maren said. "Tea later. Today the island wakes up, and I intend to be awake for it."

* * *

By midmorning the causeway was a river flowing the wrong way.

Ada climbed to the lamp room to watch it, because from up there she could see the whole shape of the returning — the carts and the handcarts and the men carrying children on their shoulders, the women with bundles tied in checked cloth, the surveyor's wagon with its brass instruments glinting. The chain-gates stood open and would stand open now until the next great storm, months away, and everyone who had been kept out by winter and law and fear was pouring back onto the island as though the intervening season had been a held breath finally released.

She counted boats in the harbor. Eleven where there had been two. New paint on hulls, new caulking, nets mended over the winter by hands that had nothing else to mend. Men she recognized calling up to her — "Keeper! Still burning?" — and her answering wave, and the strange looseness in her chest each time.

Osla came up the cliff path in the afternoon, moving slowly, one hand on the rock. She had aged a decade since autumn; the net-loft testimony had cost her something no court could weigh. But her eyes were clear, and when she reached the lamp room door she did not ask permission but came in and stood beside Ada at the rail, looking down at the harbor filling with masts.

"My boys used to race from that point to the buoy," Osla said. "Every spring, first day the ice went. The loser gutted the catch alone for a week." Her voice did not waver. "I'd forgotten the sound of this harbor full. Isn't that a wicked thing? To forget your own harbor."

"No," Ada said. "Forgetting is how we survived some winters. Remembering is how we survive others."

Osla snorted. "You've been talking to clerks."

"I've been talking to everyone. That's the difference." And it was — she felt it even as she said it, the strangeness of speech unguarded. All winter she had measured every sentence before releasing it, weighed every name, calculated who might hear and what it would cost them. Now the gates were open and the words came out plain, and the sky did not fall.

* * *

Tomas arrived near dusk, walking alone, which was how he had done everything since the hearing began.

He looked worse than the last time she'd seen him — thinner, greyer, the suspended sentence hanging on him like wet wool. Exile from the bench. His father's name struck from his own mouth. He came up the path slowly, and Ada went down to meet him halfway, because some distances should not be walked alone.

"They released me this morning," he said, without greeting. "Formally. The suspension stands, of course. No bench will have me." He looked out at the water. "I keep waiting to feel ruined."

"And do you?"

He considered. "No. I feel — light. Which frightens me more, if anything does. Ruin I was prepared for. Lightness I don't know what to do with."

"Neither did I," Ada said. "For years. You learn."

They walked up together, and at her gate he stopped and looked back at the causeway, at the last cart of the day crossing in the long gold light, its driver raising a hand to them without knowing who they were or what they had carried through three checkpoints to make this ordinary evening possible.

"Do you remember," Tomas said, "the night we opened the packet? On your table, with the lamp hissing above us? I thought we were carrying paper. Just paper." He shook his head. "It was the bridge. We were carrying the bridge. Everything that crosses it now crosses because of what was in that oilskin."

"The papers crossed," Ada corrected. "We just walked beside them."

"You always did that. Diminish it." He almost smiled. "Lucan didn't die for copies, Ada. He died so that this—" he gestured at the harbor, the boats, the smoke rising from chimneys that had stood cold all winter— "could happen without anyone asking permission."

Ada said nothing. Below them, someone had lit a fire on the shingle — a welcome-fire, the old custom, for boats returned from far water — and the smoke went up straight in the still air. She thought of Lucan asking whether the lamp was lit, and answered him silently, as she did every dusk: yes. It is lit. It has always been lit.

"Tomas," she said. "Stay tonight. The guest room's cold but the bed's honest."

He looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — gratitude and grief and something older than either. "I don't know how to be here," he admitted. "On this island. Among people my father robbed."

"Then learn," Ada said. "We'll teach you. We're patient. We've had all winter to practice surviving things we didn't know how to survive."

* * *

That night she lit the lamp, and for the first time in four months there were ships to need it — two fishing boats working late beyond the point, their lanterns bobbing like low stars. She trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork, watched the beam sweep out over the darkening water, and understood that this was the last season of her keeping. Not because anyone had asked her to go. Because Ilsa was seventeen now and had already memorized the winding mechanism, and because the keeper's log in the drawer below held a year of entries in a hand steadier than Ada's own, and because a light must pass to living hands or it becomes a monument, and monuments don't save anyone.

She wrote the evening's entry while the beam turned above her:

*Lamp lit 6:40. Wind SW, light. Two vessels sighted off the point, both local. Bridge open. Island repopulating. All quiet.*

She paused, pen lifted, and then added a line she had never written in thirty years of logs:

*Nothing to hide.*

The ink dried. Somewhere below, Tomas was building up the kitchen fire, and Ilsa's laughter carried up the stairwell — she had come for supper, of course she had come, she came every evening now, fearless as spring. Ada closed the log and sat for a moment in the small round room with the light turning above her, listening to the island breathe.

Voices. Music, faintly, from the harbor — someone had brought a fiddle across on the ferry. The smell of woodsmoke and salt and, impossibly, bread baking at this hour, because Maren's oven was lit and Maren baked when she was happy, and Maren was happy, and the whole island smelled of it.

Ada put out the lamp room's little work-lamp and went down the stairs into the warmth and the noise, into the returning world, and found — walking down into it, step by step, hand trailing along the cold stone wall — that she was not bracing herself. Not measuring. Not silent.

She was simply going home, and the door was open, and tomorrow the surveyors would come to Maren's fields, and the spring would come after them, and there was nothing left between her and any of it.

The fiddle struck up a tune she hadn't heard since she was a girl.
