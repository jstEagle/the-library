## 40. One Last Lighting

The spring came the way it always came to Paloma — not announced but noticed, one morning when the gorse on the headland had gone gold overnight and the wind had lost the iron edge that had held it all winter. Ada stood in the gallery with her hands on the rail and let it move over her face, this new air, smelling of salt and thawed earth and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the town across the water where the chimneys were lit again for cooking rather than survival.

Below her, the causeway lay open. The gates had been unchained a month now, and still she caught herself looking at them each morning, half expecting to see the padlock glinting, the sergeant's men standing at the mainland end with their ledgers and their questions. But the gates stood wide, and people crossed freely — fisherfolk with their creels, children running the stones at low tide with their trousers rolled, Maren twice a week with her basket, going over to sit with Osla and argue about net-mending as though the winter had been an ordinary one.

Nothing about it had been ordinary. Nothing about it would ever be ordinary again. That was the strange gift of it.

She went down into the lamp room to begin the day's work, and found Ilsa already there.

The girl was sixteen and had been keeper's-apprentice for exactly eleven days, and she had already reorganized Ada's polishing cloths by size, which Ada had decided to find amusing rather than infuriating. She stood at the lens now with a chamois in her hand, working the glass in slow circles the way Ada had shown her, her dark hair tied back with a scrap of red ribbon, her whole face tilted up toward the light coming through the bull's-eye panels in long green and amber blades.

"You've missed a spot," Ada said.

Ilsa didn't turn around. "There is no spot. You say that every morning to see if I'll agree with you."

"And every morning you don't." Ada picked up her own cloth and joined her at the lens, working the panel beside the girl's. "One of these days you'll doubt yourself just to be polite, and then we'll know the apprenticeship has failed."

"Not this one." Ilsa said it lightly, but Ada heard what sat underneath it, the way she heard everything the girl said now — the winter beneath the spring. Ilsa had carried the satchel through three checkpoints and a betrayal and a night flight along the cliffs; she had sat in the hearing room for six hours with her sprained ankle propped on a stool, listening to Ada speak the whole truth with names attached, hers among them. A girl did not come out of that winter unchanged. But she had come out of it upright, which was more than many grown men could claim.

They worked in silence for a while. The lens turned above them on its bath of mercury, slow as a thought, gathering itself and letting itself go.

"Show me the clockwork again," Ilsa said finally. "The escapement. Tomas explained it once but he explains things like a judge."

"He would," Ada said. "All right."

* * *

She had trained keepers before — two, in the years after Kenji died, both young men from the mainland who'd lasted a season apiece before deciding the island was too quiet or too loud or too much like being married to something that never spoke. Neither of them had lasted long enough for her to teach them the parts that mattered. The winding was easy. The cleaning was easy. What mattered was learning to hear the mechanism the way you'd listen to a child breathing in the next room — knowing its healthy sound so well that sickness announced itself before any gauge could show it.

"The weight comes down," she said, laying Ilsa's hand flat against the descending drum, "and the escapement lets it down slowly, tick by tick, so the lens turns at exactly the speed the chart says. Too fast, and the light's character changes — sailors read the flashes like handwriting, and if your handwriting changes, they think they're somewhere else. Somewhere with rocks."

"Somewhere else is bad," Ilsa said, in the tone of a girl reciting a catechism.

"Somewhere else is death," Ada said, without softening it, and saw the girl absorb that, file it away, respect it. Good. The sea did not soften things either.

They wound the clockwork together, taking the crank in turns, and Ada watched the girl's arms — thin still, but steady — and thought about Kenji teaching her this same motion twenty-two years ago, his hands over hers on the handle, laughing because she'd tried to hurry it. *The light doesn't reward hurrying,* he'd said. *It rewards showing up.* Every night. In every weather. Especially the nights you told yourself it didn't matter, no ships out there tonight, the sea's empty, let it go dark once. Those were the nights it mattered most. Not because a ship would come, but because a keeper who skipped nights was a keeper whose judgment you could no longer trust on the nights ships did come.

She had understood him completely by the end. She wished she'd understood him sooner. Grief was mostly that, she had found — not missing the person, exactly, but missing the version of yourself that existed only in their company. The laughing version. The unhurried one.

"I'm going to write it in the log tonight," Ilsa said, coiling the crank rope. "My first full solo entry. What do I put?"

"Wind, weather, state of the mechanism, hours burned, anything unusual." Ada paused. "And then whatever's true. The log isn't just for the Board. It's the island's memory. One hundred and forty years of hands have written in it — storms, wrecks, births, the war, the closure. Write so that a stranger reading it in fifty years will know we were people, not just a roster of shifts."

Ilsa looked at her sidelong. "Like the entry about the man on the rocks?"

Ada's hand stilled on the brass rail. Of course the girl had read it. She'd read the whole log, cover to cover, the first week — Ada would have done the same at sixteen.

"Yes," Ada said. "Like that."

"What did he look like? Lucan. When you found him."

The question hung in the salt-smelling air. Below them, through the lantern's glass floor, the tower's spiral stairs descended into darkness, and beyond that the island lay green and gold in the morning, and beyond that the sea did what the sea does.

"Tall," Ada said. "Younger than I expected. His lips were blue and his hands wouldn't open, even unconscious — he held that packet like it was part of him." She wiped a smudge from the panel that wasn't there. "He asked me, at the end, whether the lamp was lit. And it wasn't yet — dusk hadn't come — but I said yes. And then I lit it early, four hours early, in full daylight, so that if he opened his eyes he'd see it burning."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then: "That's going in my entry tonight. Not the log book. My own notebook. The true record." She tapped her chest, over the pocket where she kept it. "Maren says everyone should keep two records. The one the world requires and the one the soul requires."

"Maren," Ada said, "has become a philosopher in her old age."

"She says she learned it from you."

"She says a great many things she learned from me that I don't remember saying." But Ada smiled, and Ilsa smiled, and the lens turned above them, patient as forgiveness.

* * *

At noon the surveyors' boat came around the point — the third visit that week — and Ada watched through the telescope as the men in their city coats paced Maren's salt fields with their chains and stakes, measuring out what had been measured out of families forty years ago with nothing but a magistrate's pen. Maren walked with them. She had insisted on walking with them every day, pointing out boundaries from memory: *my grandfather plowed to that stone; the low field flooded every spring tide and that was good for it; the well is under the blackthorn, dig carefully.* The surveyors had learned to listen. Word had come down from the capital — from the new courts, from the armistice terms, from the fall that had ended a magistrate's career at his own dinner table — that the land was to be restored, and restored properly, and that the names on the restored deeds would be spelled correctly this time.

Tomas had written to her about it, from wherever he was living now — the inland town, the small law office doing small honest work, suspended from the bench forever and apparently relieved of a weight he'd been carrying since boyhood. *My father's lawyers are appealing,* he wrote, *but appeals require someone willing to swear the records were genuine, and it turns out no one will. Even Aldric has testified. He cried, they say. I find I can't make myself care whether the tears were real.* At the bottom of the letter, smaller: *Tell Ilsa the escapement explanation was perfectly clear and she's ungrateful.*

She would write back tonight. There was time for letters now. There was time for everything now — that was the strangest inheritance of the whole year. The silences that had structured her days like load-bearing walls had come out of her one by one during the hearing, each named name a beam removed, until she'd stood in the empty space wondering whether the building would hold. It held. It turned out silence had never been structural. It had only been heavy.

* * *

In the afternoon she took the log book down from its shelf and sat with it at the little desk below the lamp, reading the last entries of the winter the way you'd reread a letter from someone who'd survived something.

*December 4th. Bridge chained. Island sealed. Wind NNE, force 9.*

*January 17th. Found a man on the north rocks at dawn. Alive. Barely. He knew my name.*

*February 2nd. Buried L.R. outside the wall. No priest came and we did not need one. Tomas spoke. Maren sang. The sea took his body's labor and gave back his purpose and I intend to see it delivered.*

*March 30th. Hearing concluded. Truth spoken entire. Land ordered returned. I named every soul who helped us and not one has reproached me, and Sergeant Brandt sent word that he retires content, and I understand now that mercy given freely does not ask to remain secret — it asks only to be worth what it cost.*

And the last line of the last page, written in her own hand the night the bridge opened:

*April 12th. Gates unchained. The town looks back. Lamp lit at usual hour. All well.*

All well. Two words that keepers had written at the end of entries for a hundred and forty years, and that she had written hundreds of times herself as ritual, as hope, sometimes as lie. Tonight it would be true in a way it had perhaps never been before, in the whole history of the phrase.

Because tonight was her last lighting.

The Board's letter had come a week ago, courteous and faintly embarrassed, offering her a pension and a posting at the mainland depot — *in recognition of extraordinary service.* She had written back accepting the pension and declining the posting. The island needed a keeper, and she was no longer sure the keeper needed to be her. This was not surrender. She had learned the difference between surrender and completion the way she'd learned the sound of the escapement: by listening for years. Her work here was done — not finished, done, the way a coat is done when the last seam is closed. Ilsa was ready. Not perfect — no keeper is ever perfect; perfection is the sea's department — but ready, with steady hands and a true record and a spine that three checkpoints had tested and found sound.

Besides — and this was the part she had not said aloud to anyone, not even to Maren, especially not to Maren, who would have wept and made it into an occasion — Ada wanted to walk into the town. She wanted to do it in daylight, at her own pace, carrying nothing hidden. For three years she had crossed that causeway wrapped in silences, and before that, in grief, and before that, in the particular loneliness of being the only woman on a tidal rock who talked to a machine. Now there was a fiddle tune she hadn't heard since girlhood playing in the harbor tavern, and neighbors who met her eyes, and a debt ledger that had been balanced not by concealment but by truth, and she wanted, simply, to be among them. To be looked at. To look back.

* * *

At half past seven, with the sun going copper behind the mainland hills, Ada climbed to the lamp room one last time as keeper.

Ilsa was waiting, having laid everything out in perfect order — wicks trimmed, mantles checked, the clockwork freshly wound, the paraffin reservoirs full. The girl had prepared the lighting entirely herself, and stood now with her hands clasped behind her back, vibrating slightly with the effort of appearing calm.

"Your show," Ada said. "I'm only watching."

Ilsa lit the mantle. The flame caught, steadied, bloomed; she adjusted the feed with small precise turns of the valve, watching the color climb from yellow to that hard white core that meant it was right, and Ada felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Then the girl threw the switch that raised the shade, and the great lens gathered the light and began to send it out — one flash every seven and a half seconds, white, the character of Paloma, the handwriting unchanged.

They stood together in the gallery while the dusk came up out of the east and the light went out over the water, touching the swell, the headland, the distant windows of the town beginning to kindle in answer.

"Well?" Ilsa said.

"Perfect," Ada said. "Now log it."

Ilsa went down to the desk and wrote, tongue between her teeth: *April 28th. Wind SW light. Sea moderate. Lamp lit 19:42 by Ilsa Reyes-Voss, keeper, first independent lighting.* She hesitated over the page, then added a line, and showed it to Ada before closing the book — asking permission, which Ada waved away, because the log belonged to the keeper now, and the keeper was Ilsa.

What she had written: *Previous keeper retired this evening after 22 years. She taught me that the light doesn't reward hurrying. She also taught me other things not in the manual. The island remembers her. So will I.*

Ada read it twice. Then she went down the stairs quickly, before her face could do anything undignified, and stood a moment in the cold clean air of the rock with her hand pressed flat over her mouth, and laughed at herself, and wiped her eyes with her sleeve like a girl, and was not ashamed.

* * *

She left at dusk, as the tide fell and the causeway stones rose gleaming from the retreating water, worn smooth by a century of feet. Her bag was light — clothes, letters, Kenji's photograph, the original documents, which she was carrying personally to the capital archive to deposit beside Lucan's copies, because some deliveries a courier should finish herself.

Halfway across, she stopped and looked back.

The lighthouse stood black and tall against the last band of sunset, and high in it the lamp turned, steady, seven and a half seconds, faithful as a heartbeat. Ilsa's silhouette moved in the gallery — checking the rail, checking the horizon, doing the rounds exactly as taught — and Ada watched her for a long moment, this girl who had carried the truth in a school satchel and would carry the light for thirty years, and felt the specific peace of a thing handed on whole.

Then she turned and walked on toward the town, where the lamps were lighting one by one along the harbor street, and the fiddle music had started again, and doors were opening for the evening, and the people coming out of them — Maren with her shawl, Osla leaning on her stick, Brandt's widow waving from a doorway, faces she had known for decades and was only now truly seeing — looked toward her across the shining stones.

They saw her coming. None of them looked away.

Ada lifted her hand, and walked on into the light of the town, and the tide came in behind her, gentle, filling the causeway's tracks with silver, erasing nothing that mattered.

**THE END**
