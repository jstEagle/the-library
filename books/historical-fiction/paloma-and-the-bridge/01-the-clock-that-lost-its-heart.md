## 1. The Clock That Lost Its Heart

The summons came on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day nothing happened in the clockmakers' quarter. Tuesdays belonged to oil and brass wire and the slow, patient tuning of pendulums; they belonged to men like me, who had made a quiet art of being overlooked.

I was cleaning the teeth of a carriage clock when Master Horvath's boy appeared in the doorway of Yarrow & Sons — a shop name that flattered my mother's memory and lied about my family entirely, since it had only ever been her and me. The boy held out an envelope sealed with the observatory's stamp: a circle of stars over a bridge.

"From the Director," he said. "He wants the apprentice. Not the master."

Horvath looked up from his bench, one eyebrow climbing. He was seventy-one that winter and read summonses the way other men read weather. "The observatory," he said slowly, "wants *you*."

"So it seems, Master."

"Hm." He handed back the envelope without opening it further. "The master clock there is older than I am. Its escapement has been failing since autumn — they've written twice already and we've sent parts. Now they want a person. Which means the parts weren't the problem." He wiped his hands on his apron, though they were already clean, which was what he did instead of sighing. "Go. Take your good screwdrivers and your worst opinions of other people's maintenance. And Jonah—" he caught my sleeve as I passed, and his grip was still iron under all that paper skin, "—observatories keep state time. Be dull. Dullness is armor."

I said I would be the dullest thing to cross the Vessen since the last census. He almost smiled.

* * *

The observatory stood on Kestrel Hill like a stone onion skewered by its dome, white in the grey morning, above the city where the river ran brown and patient under its skin of ice. I climbed the switchback road with my toolbag knocking against my knee and the wind pulling at my collar, and I confess I felt something close to contentment. This was the whole shape of my ambition: to be summoned, to repair, to be forgotten by nightfall. In Vessengard that year, being forgotten was a trade like any other, and I was very good at it.

You have to understand what the city was like in February of 1911, if you were someone like me. History happened elsewhere, to louder people. There were posters on the telegraph poles — I passed them without reading them, the way you stop seeing advertisements for soap — and men argued politics in the coffeehouses in voices pitched to carry into the street, and once a week the *Vessengard Courier* printed names in boldface: agitators, pamphleteers, a man called Anselm Voss whose face stared out from the front page like a man daring the engraver. I knew the name the way everyone knew it, the way you know the name of a storm. It did not concern me. My concerns were escapements and the price of whale oil and whether Horvath's knees would survive another winter of cellar work.

A guard at the observatory gate checked my papers against a list, glanced at my face, and found nothing worth remembering. Good. That was the idea.

"The Director's expecting you?" he asked.

"He'll be expecting a clockmaker. Whether it's me is his affair."

Inside, the corridors smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, and the sound reached me before anything else did. A great clock does not tick; it *breathes*, a slow tidal thud you feel in your sternum before your ears sort it out. But this one breathed wrong. I'd heard failing escapements my whole life, and this was not the weary stumble of worn pallets. It was a hitch — a catch, a half-second where the mechanism seemed to reconsider itself — regular as sin.

The clock room occupied the tower's heart, a round chamber walled in instruments, star charts curling off their rollers, and at the center of it the master clock itself, nine feet of black walnut case and polished steel, the regulator against which every clock in Vessengard set its face. The station clocks. The customs house. The hospital, where nurses timed pulses by it. The prison, though I didn't think of that yet.

The Director was a small man named Pell, round as his own dome, with ink-stained fingers and the hunted look of someone who answered to ministries. He shook my hand too quickly.

"Yarrow. The apprentice? Horvath's boy?"

"His apprentice. Yes, sir."

"You came yourself. Good. Good." He lowered his voice as if the room might inform on him. "It started three weeks ago. Losing four seconds a day, then six. We've had the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs writing because the telegraph office syncs to us, and the railway writes, and yesterday—" he rubbed his eyes, "—yesterday a ministry man came in person. They don't like it. A city whose time disagrees with itself looks like—" he waved a hand, "—it looks like *something*. You understand. It looks like something."

"I'll have a look, sir."

He left me alone with it, which surprised me a little; directors usually hover. But Pell fled up his stairs with visible relief, and I set down my bag, rolled my sleeves, opened the case, and fell into the oldest silence I know — the silence of looking closely at a thing that has stopped behaving.

The movement was beautiful. Older than Horvath, older than the century, a gravity escapement with a double three-legged gravity arm, the kind of mechanism built by men who expected their grandchildren to maintain it. Every wheel was clean. Every pivot oiled. Someone had loved this machine.

And someone had broken it.

I found it within the hour, and finding it changed the temperature of the room for me. The lifting pin on the escape wheel — a piece of steel no bigger than a grain of rice — had been filed. Not worn. *Filed.* Worn metal fails along its grain, gently, with rounded shoulders and a story you can read. This had been cut across the grain with a fine edge, deliberate strokes, then burnished afterward to fake age. I sat back on my stool and looked at it for a long time while the clock hitched and caught, hitched and caught, losing the city's seconds one by one like a pickpocket working a crowd.

Sabotage. In the observatory. On state time.

I thought: I will fix it, and I will say nothing, because saying things is how people like me get noticed, and noticed people end up printed in boldface.

I replaced the pin from my stock — I'd brought spares, because Horvath taught me that optimism is carrying the right part — trued the pallets, re-oiled the bearings with the thin watch oil from my smallest bottle, and let the beat settle. The breathing evened out. Thud... thud... thud. The clock took up its old authority, and I could feel through the floor how the whole tower relaxed around it.

That was when I saw the note.

It was tucked behind the rating nut at the base of the pendulum rod, folded small, wedged where only a hand reaching inside the case would find it — where only a clockmaker's hand would reach. Paper of good quality. No seal.

I should have given it to the Director. I want that understood, because everything after follows from that hinge: any sensible person hands strange paper to authority and washes their hands. I unfolded it instead, standing inside the open case of the most important clock in Vessengard, with the mechanism breathing steady behind me, and read five words in a careful, upright hand:

*Fix it, and wait.*

No signature. No date. The handwriting was disciplined — every letter formed like it had been drilled, like someone who had learned penmanship under supervision and never unlearned it.

I looked at the sabotaged pin again, lying in the tray beside my screwdrivers. Filed by a fine edge. Burnished to deceive. Done by someone with tools, patience, access, and a reason to want the city's clocks disagreeing with each other — or a reason to want a clockmaker summoned here, alone, to a locked tower, at a chosen hour.

*Fix it, and wait.* Wait for whom? For what?

My hands were not quite steady, so I busied them, because that is also a lesson from the bench: when fear arrives, give it work. I closed the case. I squared my toolbag. I wrote out a proper invoice — new escape-wheel pin, labor, adjustment — because paperwork is a kind of armor too, and I wanted a paper trail that ended at a broken pin and nothing stranger.

Pell came down when he heard the case shut. He put his ear near the walnut like a doctor listening to a chest, and his shoulders dropped an inch.

"It's true again?"

"She'll hold true now, sir. Someone had filed the lifting pin. Deliberately." I watched his face do several things quickly and land on alarm. "You may want to ask your staff who has access."

"Yes. Yes, of course. The Ministry will want—" He caught himself, glanced at the stairs, lowered his voice. "They'll send someone. There's a man, an inspector, Brandt. If he asks you questions, answer them plainly and be gone. Don't be interesting, Yarrow. Believe me, you don't want to be interesting this year."

"No, sir," I said, meaning it completely. "Being interesting has never been among my talents."

He paid me from a cash box and walked me to the door himself, hurrying, as if the building embarrassed him. Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter, and the wind came up the hill smelling of river ice — that faint green mineral breath the Vessen gives off when the thaw is thinking about beginning. Far below, the city lay under the first lamps, ten thousand clocks and watches and kitchen timers all quietly agreeing with the mechanism I'd just healed.

I went down the switchback road with my bag knocking against my knee, telling myself the note meant nothing. A prank by a bored assistant. A test. Some ministry game I wanted no part of.

But I am a clockmaker's apprentice, and I know how mechanisms fail, and I know that a thing placed deliberately inside a machine is not a malfunction. It is a *setting*. Someone had wound a spring somewhere in my city, and fitted an escapement to it, and the first tooth of that escapement had clicked past me today in a tower full of stars.

*Fix it, and wait.*

So I fixed it.

And I told myself I would wait for nothing — that tomorrow I'd be at my bench by seven, invisible as ever, sanding a case for a jeweler's wife, history happening elsewhere to louder people.

I was wrong. Before dawn, there would be a child at the observatory door.
