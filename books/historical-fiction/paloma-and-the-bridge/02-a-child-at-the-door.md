## 2. A Child at the Door

Jonah did not sleep in the observatory. That had been the arrangement, stated plainly by the night porter with the flat authority of a man reciting rules he did not make: repairs were to be made by day, tools removed by dusk, and no apprentice was to remain after the last bell. The master clock was a matter of state now, and matters of state did not tolerate loitering.

So Jonah went home through the blue hour, past the shuttered chandlers' stalls and the lamp-lighters working their way down Kessler Street with their long brass poles, and lay awake in the narrow room above the shop of Master Ferencz the lens-grinder, listening to the river.

The ice spoke all night. It had begun two days ago—a low groaning from the Vessa, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and the whole city pretended not to hear it. In Vessengard you learned early not to comment on the river. The river commented on you. But Jonah lay beneath the eaves and listened to the ice complain, and thought about escapements.

Someone had wanted that clock to fail. Not broken—*persuaded*. The pallet stones had been filed, patiently, over weeks perhaps, so that the wear would look like honest age. And then the note: *Fix it, and wait.* Written in a careful hand, ink still sharp when Jonah had found it folded into the case beside the great wheel. Whoever left it had known the observatory would send for a clockmaker. Whoever left it had known which clockmaker.

Or perhaps it meant nothing. Perhaps it was a porter's joke, or a fragment of some older correspondence lost behind the works for years. Jonah turned this over and over the way one turns a suspicious coin, looking for the false ring, and found none, and slept anyway, because exhaustion is more persuasive than fear.

The knocking came before dawn.

At first Jonah took it for part of a dream—the dream involved the great wheel coming loose and rolling down the observatory hill through the fish market—but the knocking persisted, small and irregular, more like a bird striking glass than a fist on wood. Jonah sat up. The room was grey. The stove had gone out hours ago. Down in the street, Ferencz's shutters were still closed, and no carts yet, and the lamps along the quay burning yellow against the last of the dark.

The knocking came again. From below. From the street door.

Jonah dressed without lighting a lamp—some instinct older than thought said *do not show light to whatever is asking*—and went down the stairs on the outsides of their feet, the way Mother had taught them when Father's debts brought strangers calling. The stairwell smelled of cold ash and linseed oil. At the bottom, through the frosted pane set into the door, there was a shape.

Small. A shape the height of the door handle.

Jonah stood with a hand on the latch for three full breaths. Then opened it.

A girl stood on the step, eight years old perhaps, maybe nine and underfed. She wore a coat too big for her, a man's coat, the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs past her wrists, and a scarf wound up to her chin so that only her eyes showed—dark eyes, red-rimmed, dry. Behind her the street ran empty to the corner where the lamplighter's pole leaned abandoned against a wall; even the lamplighters had gone home. Her breath came out in little clouds. In one mittened hand she clutched a canvas satchel by its strap, and she held it the way children hold things they have been told, absolutely, not to put down.

She looked up at Jonah and did not say anything.

"Child," Jonah said. "It's not yet light. Where is whoever brought you?"

The girl shook her head once. Then she held up her other hand, the empty one, and uncurled the mitten fingers with the deliberate care of someone performing an instruction learned by heart. In her palm lay three coins and a scrap of paper, both damp with her grip.

Jonah did not take them. "Who gave you these?"

The girl looked at the coins, then back up. When she spoke, her voice was small and scraped hollow, the voice of a child who has been crying somewhere that sound does not carry. "He said to give them to the clock person. He said knock until the clock person comes." A pause. Her jaw tightened. "He said I'm not supposed to cry anymore."

"Who said? Who brought you here?"

"I'm not supposed to say his name either."

The word *either* landed like a dropped tool on a stone floor. Jonah crouched slowly, bringing their face level with hers, and saw what the scarf had hidden: a fine-boned face, chapped at the cheeks, a smudge of soot along one jaw as if she had been carried pressed against a coat that carried coal dust or powder smoke. Her hair was cropped short and unevenly—cut in haste, cut to disguise.

"May I read the note?" Jonah asked gently.

She considered this with a gravity that belonged to someone much older, then nodded and held out the paper between finger and thumb, as though it might bite.

Jonah unfolded it. The handwriting was hurried but educated, the letters leaning forward as though the writer had been moving while he wrote:

*Her name is Liesl. Do not write it down. Do not say it aloud inside walls. Forget this face when she is gone—you never saw it. You are the only door left. Forgive me. —A.V.*

The street tilted very slightly. Jonah's knees told them they were still crouching; the rest of them seemed to be standing very far away, on another morning entirely, in another doorway, seven years ago.

*A.V.* Anselm Voss. Printed in the gazettes now in letters tall enough to read across a room: *TRAITOR OF THE REPUBLIC EXECUTED AT DAWN, WEDNESDAY.* Jonah had read it standing in the queue at Brenner's bakery, had watched the man ahead fold the paper away and refuse to meet anyone's eye, had bought bread with hands that felt borrowed. Everyone in the clockmakers' quarter had done the same arithmetic in silence: *he helped us, we helped him, let us hope the ledger balances and closes.* Voss had been a name on a wall poster for a week, a cautionary tale told in low voices over beer. Dead men's promises expire.

Except the dead do not leave daughters on doorsteps.

Liesl. The name went into Jonah like a splinter. *Do not write it down. Do not say it aloud.* The child's own father—or whoever wrote in his hand, and Jonah's stomach clenched at the alternative, that this was bait, a trap dressed in a child's coat—had trusted a stranger's door because it was the only one left. Because somewhere in the wreckage of his life, Anselm Voss had remembered a promise made by a boy of fifteen whose mother's mantel clock had stopped the week before winter.

*You never saw this face,* the note said. And Jonah, looking at the girl—looking properly, letting the sight settle somewhere permanent—understood already that this was the single instruction they would fail.

Inside the satchel, when Jonah finally lifted it—it was heavier than a child's bag should be, and Liesl surrendered it only after a visible struggle with herself—there was bread wrapped in cloth, a tin cup, a child's dress folded flat, and beneath the lining of the flap, sewn in with crude stitches that had been made by a man's impatient hands: a bundle of papers Jonah did not dare open on a public doorstep. And a wooden toy, a little carved boat with a crooked mast, worn smooth by love.

"You're cold," Jonah said. It came out steadier than they felt. "Come inside. Just inside. We'll decide everything else after."

Liesl looked past Jonah into the dark stairwell, and Jonah saw her weigh it—not the way a child weighs a strange house, but the way a soldier weighs ground. Eight years old, and already measuring exits. Whatever weeks had made this child, they had not been kind ones.

"He said the clock person keeps time," she said. "He said clocks are the one thing the government can't lie about."

Something in Jonah's chest cracked along a fault line that had been forming since yesterday's sabotaged escapement.

*Fix it, and wait.*

They were inside before Jonah fully decided to move—one hand on Liesl's shoulder blade, gentle as touching a bird, steering her over the threshold, closing the door on the grey street and the lamplighter's abandoned pole. The lock sounded very loud. In the stairwell dark, the girl stood perfectly still, satchel-less now, hands hanging at her sides like a child awaiting inspection, and asked in that scraped little voice:

"Are you going to forget my face?"

And Jonah—who had spent twenty-three years perfecting invisibility, who had built a whole life on the principle that history happened elsewhere, to other people, to braver people—heard themselves answer before any wiser self could intervene:

"No. Never. Now come upstairs before the city wakes."

* * *

By full light, Liesl Voss was hidden in plain sight.

This was the one mercy of the clockmakers' quarter: it was full of oddities. A child in the workshop of an observatory assistant could be explained as a niece, a neighbor's daughter sent on an errand, a delivery boy mistaken for a girl. Jonah rehearsed the story twice while heating water on Ferencz's cold stove—Ferencz himself would not climb his stairs till nine—and Liesl listened with that unnerving adult attention, nodding at the important parts. Her name for the world would be Marta. Marta, niece of a cousin from upriver. Marta, who was shy and therefore must not be questioned. Children's fictions are simple; adults accept them because questioning children costs effort.

She drank the water. She ate half the bread from her own satchel, carefully saving the rest, wrapping it again with the same fierce precision, and Jonah's throat closed watching her do it. They put their own breakfast beside her without comment. After a moment, she moved half of theirs onto her stack, evening the accounts. Even starving, she kept books.

It was when she thought Jonah wasn't watching—when Jonah stood at the window counting the passersby on Kessler Street, cataloguing coats, hating how naturally the habit came—that the girl crept to the corner of the room where Jonah's toolbox sat, and knelt before it, and laid one finger, featherlight, on the brass escapement model Jonah had made at sixteen.

She knew what it was. Not its name, perhaps. But her finger followed the anchor's swing, tick and tock, tick and tock, and her shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time since the doorstep, and Jonah understood that someone had taught her this. That in whatever rooms she had come from, the swinging of a pendulum had meant *safe*, had meant *father nearby*, had meant *the world is behaving*.

Anselm Voss had been a railway clerk before the pamphlets, before the speeches, before the state made him a poster. Jonah knew this the way the whole quarter knew it—from the man himself, once, laughing in Jonah's mother's kitchen, his sleeves rolled, the guts of her mantel clock spread on oilcloth while he coaxed the tired mainspring back to life and refused payment three times and finally accepted only a plate of seed cake. *A clock is just a promise that keeps itself,* he'd said to fifteen-year-old Jonah, who had been pretending not to listen while sanding a cabinet. *That's why governments hate them. You can argue with a newspaper. You can't argue with noon.*

*I promise you,* Jonah had said afterward, walking him to the door, suddenly solemn with the terrible sincerity of adolescence, *if anything ever—if your family ever needs—*

Voss had laughed and rumpled their hair. *Then I'll knock on your door, little clockmaker.*

Seven years. Seven years of nothing—of the ordinary erosion of acquaintances into memories, of the quarter's quiet sympathy for Voss's politics cooling into caution as the times darkened, of Jonah telling themself that the promise had been a boy's flourish, a pleasantry, a coin of no denomination. Promises made in peacetime are paper boats. Everyone knows they dissolve.

But he had knocked. Not on the door of the boy who made the promise—he had knocked on the door of the invisible apprentice, the safe one, the one nobody watches—and the boat had not dissolved after all. It had floated seven years downstream and arrived, at first light, carrying a child.

Below on Kessler Street, a black government carriage passed, slow, its occupant reading the shopfronts through the glass. It did not stop. Jonah stepped back from the window anyway, heart hammering, and found Liesl watching them with those dark, dry eyes.

"They're looking for me," she said. Not a question. She had known since the doorstep; probably since before.

"Yes," Jonah said, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene. "And I am going to make sure they don't find you. That's—" and here the old words surfaced, worn smooth as the little carved boat, "—that was promised. A long time ago. To your papa."

For the first time, the girl's composure trembled. Her chin crumpled, held, crumpled again. She did not cry. She had promised someone she wouldn't, Jonah realized. Somewhere in the last terrible week, someone had made her promise not to cry anymore, and she was keeping it the way she kept everything—carefully, precisely, like coins gripped in a mitten.

She reached into her coat instead and produced the last item, which Jonah had missed among the satchel's contents: a watch, a man's pocket watch, cheap tin case, stopped. She placed it in Jonah's palm and folded their fingers over it.

"Papa's," she whispered. "Can you make it go?"

Outside, the ice on the Vessa groaned like a thing waking. Inside, in a rented room above a lens-grinder's shop, with a saboteur's note waiting in a coat pocket and a state carriage somewhere circling the district, Jonah Yarrow held a dead man's watch and understood that the invisible life was over.

Somewhere in the city, someone had written *Fix it, and wait.*

Jonah was beginning to understand what they were meant to be waiting for.
