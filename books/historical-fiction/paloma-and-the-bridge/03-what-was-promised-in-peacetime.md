## 3. What Was Promised in Peacetime

The girl had been asleep for six hours, and Jonah had not stopped moving since.

There was always something to do in a room full of clocks—that was the mercy of the trade. Oil to draw into a syringe, drop by amber drop. A pendulum rod to true against the flat of a knife blade. Dust to lift from forty brass faces with a brush so soft it seemed made of regret. Jonah worked through all of it twice, and then a third time, because hands that were busy could not shake.

Liesl Voss slept curled in the corner behind the regulator case, wrapped in Jonah's own coat, her breath making a small fog on the cold air that came down through the observatory's cupola. She slept the way children sleep in strange places: entirely, desperately, as though sleep were a country she had crossed a border into and did not intend to leave.

Jonah set down the oil syringe and let themself look at her.

Eight years old, maybe nine. Thin wrists. A smudge of soot along one cheekbone that no amount of sleeping would wear away. When they had found her at dawn, standing at the observatory door with a paper-wrapped bundle of coins in her fist, she had not cried. She had looked up at Jonah with her father's eyes—Jonah knew that now, knew it with a certainty that sat in the chest like a swallowed stone—and said, *He said someone would be here. He said to say the word "escapement."*

The word had gone through Jonah like a gear tooth through a thumb.

*Escapement.* The mechanism by which a clock lets its power out a little at a time, so that force becomes time instead of ruin. Anselm Voss had used it once before, years ago, in another life, when he was not an enemy of the state but simply a man with ink-stained cuffs and a laugh like a door swinging open. It had been a joke between them, once. Now it was a password, and passwords are what jokes become when the world turns cruel.

Jonah picked up the brush again. Dust from the great clock's case. The master clock ticked above them both, wounded but alive, and Jonah listened to it the way one listens to a sick friend breathing in the next room.

Somewhere across the river, in a prison yard or a pauper's grave—no one would say which—a man was dead because his ideas were larger than the state's patience. And here in the clock room, small and warm and breathing, was everything left of him.

*I promised,* Jonah thought. The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. *I promised him. God help me, I was fourteen years old and I promised him.*

* * *

Eleven years earlier. The winter of 1900, the coldest in a decade, when the river froze hard enough that men drove carts across it and children skated under the bridge arches until their mothers' voices cracked calling them home.

Jonah's mother had a clock. It was nothing—the kind of cheap walnut-cased thing sold at market fairs, its dial printed rather than enameled, its chime a single dull note like a spoon on a cup. But it had belonged to Jonah's grandmother, and grandmother was dead, and mother wound it every Sunday evening with a small ceremonial gravity, as if the winding were itself a prayer.

That January it died. Not gradually—clocks usually die gradually, losing minutes like an old man losing words—but all at once, mid-afternoon, with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Mother turned it over, shook it gently as one shakes a fainting woman, and set it on the table with her lips pressed thin.

"We'll take it to Horvath's," she said. But then she looked at the ledger of the week's money, and the thinness of her lips became something else, and she said no more about it.

They could not afford a clockmaker. Everyone in the weavers' quarter knew what a house call cost, and everyone pretended not to know, the way people pretend not to see hunger.

It was Jonah who mentioned the man. Jonah was fourteen, apprenticed already two years to Master Horvath, already possessed of that particular pride of the trade—the pride of knowing how things work—and they had a customer at the shop that week, a young law clerk or journalist or something of the sort, dark-haired, quick-eyed, who had brought in a broken watch and waited while it was mended, and while he waited he asked questions. Good questions. The kind that weren't really questions at all but a way of saying *I see you, I see what this craft is.*

Anselm Voss. That was the name. Jonah remembered it the way you remember a struck match.

"Voss," Jonah said at supper. "The one with the watch. He fixes things himself, I think. Or he knows people. He said—" Jonah hesitated, because repeating it felt like handling something hot. "He said if anyone in the quarter ever needs work done and can't pay, they should send for him anyway."

Mother gave Jonah the look that mothers give children who bring home stray philosophies along with stray dogs. "And what does a man live on, giving away his hands?"

"I don't know. Ideas, maybe. He has a lot of them."

But she sent Jonah the next day. Pride is a currency too, and hers was nearly spent; she stood at the window while Jonah went out into the blue cold with the dead clock wrapped in a shawl like an infant.

Voss kept rooms above a bookbinder's on Tanners' Lane, three flights up, a room where every surface was paper—papers stacked, papers pinned, papers weighted down with teacups and stones. A young man with a printer's ink stain on his left cuff looked up when Jonah knocked, saw the shawl-shaped bundle, and understood everything at once.

"Ah," he said. "The escapement?"

Jonah blinked. "I—it's the balance staff, probably. Or the—"

"No, no." Voss was already clearing a space on his table, sweeping papers aside with the confidence of a man whose chaos had a system in it somewhere. "I mean, why do clocks stop? People think it's the spring, the power running out. But it's almost never that. It's the escapement. The part that decides when to let go." He took the clock from Jonah's arms with a gentleness that seemed excessive for walnut and tin. "Everything that keeps time is just a machine for releasing power a little at a time. Hold everything back and you have a bomb. Let it all go at once and you have a flood. The whole art"—he set the clock down and looked at Jonah, and his eyes were quick and warm and entirely too interested—"the whole art is in the letting go. At the right moment. In the right measure."

Jonah, fourteen years old and trained never to speak in the presence of customers, heard themself say: "My grandmother's clock isn't art. It's rubbish. But my mother winds it like it's a holy thing."

Something happened in Voss's face then—not a smile exactly, more the way ice shifts before it breaks. "Then it is a holy thing," he said. "Leave it. Come back Thursday."

"It will cost—"

"It will cost nothing. Go home. Tell your mother a colleague owes me a favor and I am collecting it in advance." He waved a hand, already bending over the open case. "Tell her the favor is being repaid to her, and someday she can pass it on."

Thursday. Jonah came back Thursday and the clock was ticking on his table, steady as a heartbeat, and Voss showed Jonah what he'd done—a worn pallet stone, re-polished with a slip of boxwood and a patience Jonah had not known lay outside the guild. And Voss talked while he worked, the way masters talk and apprentices pretend not to listen, except that everything he said was wrong according to Master Horvath's curriculum and right according to some other curriculum Jonah hadn't known existed. About how a city was a clock. About who held the weights and who got to say when the hour struck. About how the men who owned the springs called order what was really only their own convenience.

"You shouldn't say these things," Jonah whispered, terrified and thrilled in equal parts, glancing at the door.

"Shouldn't I?" Voss laughed—that laugh, the door-swinging-open laugh. "Child, I write them down and print four hundred copies. Saying them aloud in my own room is the least dangerous thing I do all week." Then he sobered, and closed the clock's case, and put it in Jonah's arms, and looked at Jonah with those quick warm eyes, and said:

"There is one price. Not money."

Jonah's stomach dropped. Everything has a price; the quarter taught you that early.

"Someday," Voss said, "someone will come to you the way you came to me today. Carrying something broken. Unable to pay. Afraid." He tapped the clock case once, lightly, a jeweler's tap, a seal. "You will take it in. You will not ask the cost. That is the whole bill. Can you sign it?"

And Jonah—fourteen, invisible, a boy or girl or neither whom the city had never once bothered to notice—had felt for the first time in their life that they were being offered a signature on something real.

"I swear it," Jonah said. "Your family will never stand alone. Not while I have hands."

Voss smiled and shook Jonah's hand, formally, like a guildmaster, like a man closing a contract worth more than the paper it wasn't written on.

"Spoken in peacetime," he said softly, half to himself. "The only promises worth anything are spoken in peacetime, little clockmaker. Because that's when they're free."

* * *

The lamplight in the clock room had burned low. Jonah realized they had been standing at the bench with the brush in hand for some minutes without moving, the memory playing itself out behind their eyes like light through a lens.

Behind the regulator case, Liesl stirred and murmured something in her sleep—a word, half-heard. Jonah leaned closer, heart hammering, and caught it:

*"Papa."*

Just that. Just a child's word, aimed at the dark.

Jonah crouched by the corner, slow, the way one moves near a frightened animal, and pulled the coat higher over the girl's shoulder. Her hand emerged from the folds and closed around Jonah's finger—small, strong, absolute, the grip of someone who had decided, with an eight-year-old's terrible finality, that this stranger was the last solid thing in the world.

Jonah stayed there, crouched on cold flagstones, held captive by a sleeping child.

*I spoke it in peacetime,* they thought. *God forgive me, I thought it would stay peacetime.*

Above them the master clock ticked on, its sabotaged heart limping through the hours, and Jonah began, very quietly, to count what the promise might cost—and stopped counting, because the answer did not fit inside any arithmetic they knew.

Down in the yard below the observatory, gravel shifted under a boot. Once. Twice. Then silence.

Jonah did not move. But their heart had learned, in the space of a single night, the escapement's true lesson: how to hold everything in, and wait, and choose the exact moment to let go.
