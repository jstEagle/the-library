## 4. The Master's Silence

The bread was the first thing.

Jonah had taken to cutting it in the dark, before the observatory woke, sawing the loaf on the workbench with a knife meant for scraping shellac. Two portions instead of one. It should have been nothing—a boy eating more in a growth year, a generous mood, a mistake of habit. But Master Horvath weighed everything the way he weighed brass, and Jonah knew it as they stood at the long bench that third morning, chisels in hand, the smell of oil and cold stone all around them, when Horvath's eyes flicked—not to Jonah, but to the corner by the door where Liesl's shoes sat drying from the night's rain.

Small shoes. Brown leather, cracked at the toe, laces knitted by someone who had loved her once and could not anymore. They had been there two days now because there was nowhere else for them to be, and Jonah had not been able to make them disappear.

Horvath looked at them the way he looked at a movement that would not keep time: without surprise, without mercy, cataloguing the fault.

Then he said nothing at all.

He turned back to his wheel and began truing an arbor, and the silence came off him like heat off a stove. Jonah worked with their head down and their heart going too fast, and understood, with the clarity that dread gives, that this was worse than any question. A question could be answered. A question could be survived with a lie well-shaped. But silence meant the master had already asked it, already answered it himself, and was now deciding—stroke by stroke of the grinding wheel—what the answer would cost, and who would pay it.

* * *

The inspectors came on Thursday.

There were three of them, which struck Jonah as excessive for a broken clock, but nothing about the last week had been proportionate. They arrived in a government motorcar that coughed at the observatory gate, and they wore coats too good for the weather, and the tall one carried a leather folio under his arm the way a priest carries scripture—as if the paper inside were holy and everyone else were not.

They questioned them separately. That was the first thing Jonah learned about how the state works: it takes you apart.

Jonah waited in the corridor outside the clock room while a junior man went through the tool cabinets, lifting each file to the light as though sabotage might be written on steel in small print. Through the door, muffled, came the sound of Horvath's voice—low, even, unhurried—the old man answering questions the way he answered everything: precisely, and with no word more than necessary.

When it was Jonah's turn, they were led not to the clock room but to the chart room, where the walls were papered with barographs and tide tables and the great glass instruments that recorded what the sky did whether anyone watched or not. The tall inspector sat behind the director's desk without being invited. His name, spoken flatly by the junior man, was Brandt. He had a face built for patience: pale eyes, a mouth that did not smile so much as permit itself to remain closed.

"Jonah Yarrow. Apprentice, second year." He read it from the folio as though confirming rather than learning it. "You found the escapement damaged."

"I repaired it."

"You found it damaged," Brandt repeated, with the mild insistence of a man correcting a child's grammar. "Describe how."

Jonah described it. The pallet stones ground past any wear that honest friction makes—the angles wrong, deliberately wrong, cut by someone who knew clocks. The mainspring let down hard against the stopwork. "It wasn't neglect," they said. "Someone wanted the city to lose its time."

"And who benefits when a city loses its time?"

The question hung there. Jonah heard the trap in it and stepped around it carefully. "I couldn't say."

"Couldn't or wouldn't?"

"They're different questions," Jonah said, and then wished the words back, because Brandt's mouth moved—a fraction, a millimeter—and Jonah understood that this inspector collected exactly such fractions, filed them away in the folio, and would add them up later.

Brandt turned a page. "Your master has been here thirty-one years. You, two. Tell me, apprentice—who else has keys to this building?"

"The director. Master Horvath. The warden of lights." A pause. "Me."

"Two years," Brandt said softly, "and already a key." He wrote something. The scratch of the pen was the loudest thing in the room. "A person can do a great deal with a key."

"I fixed the clock," Jonah said. "If I'd broken it, I'd have needed to fix it worse."

For the first time Brandt looked up fully, and his pale eyes held something that was almost respect, which was somehow more frightening than suspicion. "Yes," he said. "That occurred to us too. Which is why we're still here."

He let Jonah go then, and Jonah walked out through corridors that seemed narrower than before, past windows where the river showed grey between the trees, and thought: *he knows the shape of the truth without knowing the truth itself.* Someone inside. A key. A child somewhere in this quarter matching a description that existed in a folio now. And every hour Jonah kept Liesl hidden was an hour the arithmetic grew heavier.

* * *

That evening Horvath sent the other apprentices home early—an act without precedent that fooled no one—and stayed late over the bench, doing work that did not need doing. Jonah cleaned the clock room. Liesl was upstairs in the caretaker's loft, having been taught, sternly, that between certain hours she was a piece of furniture: silent, unmoving, part of the room. She was better at it than Jonah liked. Eight years old, and she already knew how to be still the way hunted things are still.

At nine o'clock the master set down his tools and said, without turning around:

"The bread."

Jonah's hands stopped in the cleaning rag.

"You've been cutting double rations for three days." Horvath still faced the bench. The lamp threw his shadow huge across the wall, bent and enormous, like something out of a shadow-play. "And there are children's shoes by my door, Yarrow, which I am expected to believe belong to nobody. And twice this week I have heard a floorboard above the clock room take weight at hours when this building is supposed to hold only ghosts."

"Master—"

"Do not." The word came quiet and absolute. Horvath finally turned, and his face in the lamplight was the face Jonah had studied for two years across the bench—lined, severe, unreadable as a locked case—but tonight something moved behind it, deep down, like ice shifting on the river. "Do not tell me. Whatever you have decided to tell me, do not tell me yet. Do you understand what I am saying to you?"

"No," Jonah admitted.

"Good." Horvath took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, slowly, buying time the way he bought everything—deliberately. "Because the day I know a thing, Yarrow, is the day I can be made to give it up. They questioned me for four hours today. Four hours, and they know less than they think, because I know nothing—that is what saved me, and it is what will save you, if you are wise enough to stay empty." He put the spectacles back on. "Whatever is in this building besides clocks—I have not seen it. Say it back to me."

"You haven't seen anything," Jonah said.

"I have seen bread," Horvath said dryly, "and shoes. But those are not things; those are rumors, and rumors are deniable. Keep them rumors." He turned back to the bench, dismissal in his shoulders. Then, just before Jonah reached the door, without looking up: "The girl's father. Was it Voss?"

The floor tilted. Jonah gripped the jamb. "Master, you said—"

"I said don't *tell* me. There is a difference between being told and knowing." Horvath's voice was very low. "Anselm Voss mended my mother's mantel clock in the winter of '03 and refused payment, and I have remembered his hands ever since. So I know what a Voss looks like asleep in a hayloft, Yarrow. I have known since Tuesday." He picked up his burnisher. "I have seen nothing. Now go, before I see more than I can deny."

* * *

Jonah climbed the stairs to the loft with the lamp turned low and found Liesl awake, sitting cross-legged among the sacking, waiting. Children always knew. She didn't ask what the master had said; she asked, instead, in her small careful voice:

"Is he angry?"

"No," Jonah said, sitting beside her against the cold stone. "That's the trouble."

She considered this with the gravity of a much older person. Down below, faint through the floors, came the tick of the master clock, running true again, keeping the city's time, keeping their secret in its own way—every beat of it saying *nothing happened here, nothing happened here.*

"He looked right at your shoes," Jonah told her, because she deserved the truth in small amounts. "And he said nothing."

Liesl pulled the blanket up under her chin. "My papa said that's how you know," she said. "That's how you find out who's yours. Not who helps you." Her eyes were enormous in the lamplight. "Who stays quiet for you."

Jonah lay back on the sacking and stared at the rafters and did not sleep. Because it was true, and because it was not enough. Silence could be loyalty—but it could also be a debt, saved up, called in later at interest. Horvath knew. Horvath had always known. And sooner or later Inspector Brandt would come back with his folio and his patient pale eyes, and he would offer the master something, and then the silence would have a price on it, and Jonah would learn which kind of quiet an old man keeps.

Below them, the master clock ticked on into the small hours, faithful and blind, counting down toward spring.

And three days later, Brandt returned—this time asking for Master Horvath alone, and closing the door behind him.
