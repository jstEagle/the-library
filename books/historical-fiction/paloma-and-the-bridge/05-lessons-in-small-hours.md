## 5. Lessons in Small Hours

The clocks taught Jonah Yarrow to count time in small units, and so it was that the days of hiding Liesl Voss were measured not in mornings but in escapements—in the tick of the observatory's lesser regulators, in the forty minutes between Master Horvath's rounds, in the exact span of time a girl of eight could hold still before her body betrayed her with a giggle.

Eleven days. Eleven nights. Jonah counted them the way they counted everything: precisely, because precision was the only prayer they knew.

By day, Liesl lived in the clock room behind the pendulum gallery, where the observatory kept its orphans—regulators awaiting repair, marine chronometers salt-corroded from some captain's grief, a tower clock's dead heart the size of a wash basin. The room was warm from the weight-driven mechanisms and smelled of oil and old walnut, and if you lay very still among the crates you could hear thirty hearts beating at once, none of them agreeing on the hour.

At night, when Horvath locked the public galleries and the lamplighters had gone home to Vessengard proper, she was allowed to move. She learned the geography of shadows the way other children learned catechism: which stair creaked on the third step (all of them, but the third worst), which window's latch could be coaxed without sound, how long the watchman's lantern took to cross the courtyard below—ninety seconds, give or take, and Liesel gave it to the second because Jonah had made a game of it and she was a child who played games as if her life depended on the score. Which, Jonah thought more than once, watching her crouch by the window with her chin on her fists, counting under her breath, was not far from the truth.

"Eighty-eight," she whispered. "Eighty-seven. You're breathing too loud."

Jonah flattened themselves against the wall and breathed through their nose until the lantern slid out of sight below.

"Ninety," Liesl announced, triumphant, and spun on her heel. "Again."

"You'll make yourself dizzy."

"I'll make myself *good* at it." She said it with the flat certainty of a child who has already survived something that should have killed her, and Jonah's chest did the thing it did now whenever she spoke like that—a tightening, a small cold hand closing around the heart. They had stopped asking where she'd been before the door, what she'd seen, whose coat she'd worn for three days walking to the city. She would tell them, or she wouldn't. Fear sat on her lightly, like a shawl rather than a chain, and Jonah had begun to understand that this lightness was not innocence. It was practice.

* * *

It was Liesl who found the broken chronometer first.

She'd been three days in hiding, bored past endurance, forbidden even to hum, when Jonah came up to the clock room with bread and found her cross-legged on the floor before an open marine deck watch, its brass guts spilled across a cloth like jewelry off a drowned woman's wrist.

"Don't touch—" Jonah began.

"I didn't." She held up both hands, palms out, immaculate. "I looked. It's wrong here." She pointed one finger at the balance spring without touching it. "It's bent. That's why it goes funny. My papa had a watch like this. He said everything that breaks has one piece that broke first, and if you find it, you can fix anything."

Jonah knelt slowly beside her. "Your father said that?"

"He fixed things. Before." Her voice did something complicated on the word *before*—flattened itself, closed a door—and then brightened again with terrible speed. "Can I learn? Teach me the names. I hate not knowing names."

So Jonah taught her the names.

They began after midnight, when the observatory belonged to no one but the clocks. Jonah spread the tools across the workbench—the good ones, Horvath's, which was its own small treason—and held each one to the lamplight. "Tweezers. Staking set. This is a jeweler's screwdriver; it is worth more than your shoes and it will ruin the whole month if you drop it."

"I won't drop it."

"You dropped the broom."

"The broom was badly balanced."

And so it went, night after night, the two of them bent over brass under a single green-shaded lamp while the great regulators ticked around them like a parliament in session. Jonah showed her how an escapement doles out time one tooth at a time, greedy and precise, and Liesl listened the way water listens to a crack in a dam. She asked questions no apprentice asked. Why does the pallet stone need a jewel? Why does the mainspring get tired? Does the clock know it's keeping time, or does it just think it's moving?

"I don't know," Jonah said once, honestly. "Maybe it only knows the ticking."

"Then it knows the important part," Liesl said, and went back to filing her practice plate, tongue between her teeth, holding the file exactly as she'd been shown, which was to say almost correctly and entirely stubbornly.

Horvath found them there on the fifth night.

He stood in the doorway of the clock room with his lamp raised, and for one long moment he said nothing at all, which from Horvath was a whole speech. Liesl froze mid-file, guilty as a thief, the practice plate shining under her hands. Jonah rose halfway, ready to lie, ready to beg—

"Elbow down," Horvath said. "You're filing crooked. If you're going to ruin my stock, girl, ruin it properly." And he crossed the room, adjusted her grip with two dry fingers, and left without another word, though he paused at the door long enough to set his own reading spectacles on the bench within her reach, saying nothing about why.

After that, there were three chairs at the workbench. No one remarked upon it. In the clockmakers' quarter, things were acknowledged by being used.

* * *

Liesl learned patience the way the clocks taught it: by failing, and being made to start over. She ruined four practice plates and cried exactly once, silently, wiping her face on her sleeve without stopping work, and Jonah pretended not to see because they understood that pretending not to see was also a kind of teaching.

But she taught things back.

She taught Jonah how fear looks when it's worn lightly—not gone, never gone, but folded up small and put away like a tool between uses. Jonah had carried their own fear like a full bucket for eleven days, sloshing at every footstep, and here was this child setting hers down to laugh at the way a disassembled gear train looked like a flower, picking it up again only when needed. On the twelfth night she fell asleep at the bench, cheek on a half-finished escapement model, and Jonah lifted her to the cot among the crates and stood a moment watching her breathe, thinking: *this is what it is. This is what Voss meant. Not heroics. A lamp. A bench. Someone to carry you to bed.*

Anselm Voss had sat at a table very much like this one, years ago, in a peacetime that seemed now like someone else's dream. He'd been laughing about something. He'd said *your family will never stand alone, little clockmaker,* and Jonah, sixteen and immortal, had said *nor yours,* the way you agree to weather, to spring, to things that come whether promised or not.

The words had cost nothing then. They were costing everything now, and paying out anyway, and Jonah found—to their own quiet horror—that they would not have taken them back for anything in Vessengard.

On the thirteenth day, Liesl named the tools unprompted, all of them, eyes shut, and opened her eyes waiting to be praised.

"Adequate," said Horvath, gruffly, which from him was a parade.

That night she slept smiling. Jonah banked the lamp low and sat listening to thirty hearts disagreeing about the hour, and thought: whatever comes, we were a family for thirteen days. Let them write that down somewhere.

Then, at dawn, footsteps on the stairs—heavy, official, two pairs, unhurried in the manner of men carrying papers—and Master Horvath appeared in the doorway, pale as dial enamel, and said quietly:

"Jonah. There are inspectors in the gallery. And they're asking about bread rations."
