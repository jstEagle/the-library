## 6. The Informer's Ledger

Inspector Brandt kept his files the way other men kept their consciences: alphabetized, cross-referenced, and dusted weekly.

He had inherited the habit from his father, a customs clerk in Marnau who had spent thirty years stamping manifests and died with clean hands and a pension, and Brandt intended to die much the same way—only higher up the building. He was forty-one years old, a widower without children, a man whose ambitions were modest in scale and absolute in direction: a corner office on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Internal Order, a window that faced the river instead of the wall, and the quiet satisfaction of a state that functioned because men like him made it function. He did not consider himself cruel. Cruelty was inefficient. It left marks, marks invited sympathy, and sympathy was disorder wearing its Sunday clothes.

What he believed in was paper.

Paper did not shout. Paper did not bleed. Paper, handled correctly, could dismantle a conspiracy more thoroughly than any squad of gendarmes with boots and truncheons, because paper arrived at breakfast, sat politely beside the coffee pot, and asked nothing louder than a signature. Brandt had watched men survive beatings. He had never once seen a man survive a properly organized dossier.

The dossier on the observatory clock now occupied a green folder on the left side of his desk, and it was growing fat.

* * *

The sabotage report had come in eleven days after the incident, which told Brandt something already: the observatory staff were not eager talkers. The master clock—the great regulator that governed the city's official time, from the harbor bells to the railway schedules to the courthouse docket—had been found stopped at four minutes past three in the morning, its escapement deliberately jammed with a sliver of hardened steel no thicker than a fingernail. Not broken. Jammed. A breakage was accident or wear; a jam was intention wearing work gloves.

The steel sliver itself sat in an evidence envelope before him now. He turned it under the lamp. Machine-cut, he judged, and cut recently—the burrs along one edge had not yet dulled. Which meant access to a workshop. Which meant the clockmakers' quarter, where every third door opened onto a lathe and every apprentice carried steel filings home in his cuffs like a disease.

Brandt made a small note in the margin of the incident summary: *Internal hand. Someone let them in, or someone inside.* Then he underlined it twice, because marginal notes were where investigations went to die if you let them stay marginal.

There was also the matter of the timing, which troubled him more than the steel.

The clock had failed on the night of the ninth of February. On the tenth of February—between the hours when a dead clock would have been noticed and the hours when it could plausibly have been repaired—the Voss execution order had been signed, sealed, and carried out in the courtyard of the Citadel, with a promptness that several people in this city still found suspicious. Anselm Voss, printer, pamphleteer, enemy of the state, shot at dawn with less ceremony than they gave a mutinous sergeant. And for those few hours, while the master clock stood silent, Vessengard's official time had been whatever anyone said it was. The execution had been logged at 6:04 by the prison chaplain's pocket watch. No independent verification existed.

Brandt did not believe in coincidences of that size. He believed the Ministry's own account of the hour of death—but he also believed that somewhere above his pay grade, someone else had wanted the clocks blind that night, and that whoever had jammed the escapement had either known why or been paid not to ask. Both possibilities pointed downward, into the guild quarter, into the warm lamplit shops where men repaired the city's time and asked no questions about who owned it.

And then there was the child.

* * *

The child had surfaced the way children always did in his experience: through paperwork that didn't match itself.

A midwife in the Weavers' Row district, summoned to give evidence in an unrelated matter—a disputed paternity, tedious stuff—had mentioned in passing that she'd attended a woman named Marta Voss some years back. Birth of a daughter. The registry entry for that birth had been examined and found thin: father listed as "Voss, A., printer," occupation confirmed, but the parish copy missing, presumed lost in the fire of '06. Convenient fires. Brandt had a whole drawer of them.

Then a schoolmistress in the same district reported that Liesl Voss had stopped attending classes on the eighth of February, two days before her father's arrest became public knowledge. Withdrawn quietly. No forwarding address filed. And on the twelfth, a neighbor's statement—not yet sworn, but taken down faithfully—described a woman, presumably the mother, leaving the tenement before light with a child's hand in hers and a bundle too heavy for laundry.

The mother had not been found. That was normal; women with sense ran. What was *not* normal, what kept Brandt at his desk past the lamplighter's rounds, was the second statement, taken three days ago from a bargeman named Kessel who moored below the observatory hill. Kessel swore that on the night of the eleventh he had seen a figure carrying a sleeping child up the observatory path, and that the child's coat, in the moonlight, had been red wool with a torn hem.

Marta Voss's neighbors remembered a red coat. Small. Girl's.

Brandt laid the statements side by side on his desk and looked at them for a long time, the way he imagined a chess player looked at a board when he saw not the moves made but the ones about to be. A sabotaged clock in the one building in Vessengard that stood above the city and saw everything. An executed revolutionary. A vanished widow. A missing child. And an observatory staffed, according to the guild rolls, by exactly five licensed persons, one of whom was Master Emil Horvath.

Horvath. Brandt pulled the older file—thick, soft-cornered, twenty years deep—and read it again though he knew it by heart. Horvath had spent fourteen months in the Citadel himself, under the old regime, before the amnesty. Charged with what, the record declined to specify in detail; the old regime had been vague about its charges the way a drunk is vague about his debts. Released. Reinstated by guild petition. Silent ever since. A man who had learned exactly how much silence was worth, and what it cost to buy it back.

Ambitious men wanted dramatic suspects—firebrands, agitators, men with printing presses hidden in cellars. Brandt wanted Horvath, precisely because Horvath was none of those things. Dramatic men fought. Quiet men negotiated. And a negotiation required only one thing from Brandt: something the other man wanted badly enough to trade names for.

He began drafting the offer that afternoon, and he drafted it the way he did everything—slowly, in triplicate, with an eye toward how each sentence would look later, if later came, and someone asked him what he had known and when.

* * *

The observatory smelled of oil and cold brass, and Inspector Brandt disliked it immediately, which he took as a good sign. Places he liked tended to be places where he grew careless.

Master Horvath received him in the clock room itself rather than the office downstairs, which was either insolence or a message, and Brandt suspected the latter. The old man stood among his instruments with his sleeves rolled, hands stained to the wrist, as though the inspection were an interruption of honest labor—which, of course, was exactly the impression he wished to give. Two apprentices worked at benches along the far wall, heads bent. One of them—young, fine-boned, with ink-stained fingers and a way of not looking up that was itself a kind of looking up—did not move at all during the entire interview. Brandt noted the stillness. Guilt fidgeted; fear held still. He made a mental asterisk beside the boy and moved on.

"You'll forgive the mess," Horvath said. "Time doesn't keep itself."

"So I understand." Brandt set his portfolio on the nearest clear surface—a cabinet topped with green baize—and did not open it yet. Opening it early surrendered leverage. "Your master clock. Repaired?"

"Running true since the fourteenth. You're welcome to hear it." The old man nodded upward, and indeed the great pendulum swung overhead with a patience that seemed almost mocking, tick and tock, tick and tock, the sound of a city being told what time it was whether it agreed or not.

"Who had access to the mechanism on the night of the ninth?"

"Anyone I admitted. No one else. The door is locked; the key doesn't leave my chain." Horvath raised the chain slightly, an old man's small joke, iron links glinting. "You think one of my people jammed my clock, Inspector? Come. Accuse one of us by name and watch how fast I show you the error."

"I accuse no one." Brandt opened the portfolio at last and withdrew a single sheet, laying it flat with both hands, formally, like a card played face-up. "I inform. That is all an inspector does, Master Horvath—he informs the Crown of facts, and facts are such blunt things, aren't they? They have no manners whatsoever." He let the silence sit. "For instance. Here is a fact: the steel fragment used to jam your escapement was machine-cut within the last month. Here is another: the cutting pattern matches lathes registered to this quarter. Here is a third—" and here Brandt allowed himself the smallest pause, the kind he had practiced before mirrors until it stopped feeling like theater and started feeling like architecture— "a child's coat, red wool, was seen carried up this hill on the night of the eleventh. A child answering the description of Liesl Voss, daughter of the traitor Anselm Voss, executed by order of the Crown on the tenth."

He watched the old man's face with the attention other men gave to fine mechanisms. There: a flicker, gone in half a heartbeat, around the eyes. Not surprise. Recognition. The difference mattered enormously. Surprise meant innocence hearing news; recognition meant a man measuring how much the visitor already knew.

"Tragic business, the Voss affair," Horvath said. His voice had not changed at all. "I knew him slightly, years ago. He mended my mother's mantel clock once. Good hands."

"Good hands run in families, I'm told."

"Not in that one. The daughter's eight. She has a doll and a spelling book, if the neighbors are accurate, and neither is a weapon."

"No," Brandt agreed pleasantly. "She's not the criminal. She's the evidence. There is a warrant forming—I can tell you this much as professional courtesy—that will authorize a search of every licensed premises in the clockmakers' quarter. Premises, workshops, dwellings above them, outbuildings, coal sheds. It will be served at first light on a morning of the ministry's choosing, and it will be thorough, because thoroughness is my one vanity." He straightened the sheet of paper on the cabinet, squaring it to the edge. "Before that happens, Master Horvath, there exists a brief window in which matters may arrange themselves differently."

Horvath said nothing. The pendulum said it for him, tick, tock, patient as winter.

"The Crown does not want an old man in the Citadel again," Brandt went on. "Trials are expensive, the papers make noise, and frankly the ministry has larger fish. What the Crown wants is names. Who cut the steel. Who carried the child. Where the child is now. Give me those, in writing, and the file closes over your head like water. Immunity, full and documented. Your license, your shop, your apprentices—all untouched. You would go on repairing the city's time until your hands quit, and no one would ever trouble you again." He slid a second paper from the portfolio, blank except for the heading and the space for signatures, and set it beside the first. "Or refuse, and when the warrant comes—and it will come, Master Horvath, warrants are the one promise this government always keeps—we will find what we find, and you will stand in a dock explaining a red coat, and everything you built will be inventoried and auctioned by the end of the season. I don't threaten. I schedule."

For a long moment the only sounds were the pendulum and, distantly, the river ice groaning against the pilings below the hill—a long, low complaint, like a giant shifting in sleep, like something vast beginning to let go.

Horvath picked up the blank form. Turned it over once, as if checking the quality of the paper. Set it down again, unmarked, and folded his stained hands on top of it.

"You've made a careful document, Inspector," he said. "Everything accounted for. Everything signed." The old eyes lifted, mild and terribly awake. "But you've made one error of craftsmanship. You've assumed a man sells his conscience the way he sells his labor—one transaction, receipt given, done. Some of us sold ours long ago, to a worse buyer, and we remember the price. We don't sell twice."

Brandt collected his papers unhurriedly, squared them, closed the portfolio. "Then I'll leave the form with you. Forms keep, Master Horvath. They're very patient." At the door he paused, and glanced back once at the silent apprentice at the far bench—the one who had not moved, not breathed, not looked up. "Give my regards to your people. All of them."

The stairs down were dark, and at the bottom the night air hit him with the smell of thawing river—mud and rot and running water, the smell of a city about to change its mind. Brandt buttoned his coat and thought: *the ice goes early this year. Early ice means crowds on the bridge, crowds mean confusion, and confusion is when the guilty move.*

He returned to the ministry and wrote a single line in his ledger, dated, initialed:

*Suspect premises to be watched continuously. Bridge tollhouse advised. Warrant request expedited — service at first light, day of the breaking.*

Somewhere across the dark city, in a room full of ticking brass, a girl was asleep. By the time she woke, the machinery of the state would already be turning toward the bridge—and unlike the master clock upstairs, this particular mechanism, once set in motion, could not be stopped by any hand in Vessengard.
