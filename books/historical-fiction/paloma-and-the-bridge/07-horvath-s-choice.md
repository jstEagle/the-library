## 7. Horvath's Choice

Master Emil Horvath had spent sixty years learning to read silence, and the silence of his own workshop had changed key three nights ago.

It was a subtle thing. The clocks ticked as they always ticked, each one keeping its small stubborn time, but beneath them there was a new sound—or rather an absence of it, a held breath where none had been before. He noticed it first when he climbed the stairs at four in the morning, as was his habit, to oil the regulator before the day's work began. He stopped on the third step with his hand on the rail and listened.

A child was breathing in his house.

He had known for nine days now. Nine days since he had seen the small shoes set beside Jonah's boots by the door, toes pointed outward the way children's shoes always were, as if the feet inside them hadn't yet learned to apologize for taking up space. Nine days since the bread had begun to disappear faster than two people could account for, and since he had found, wedged in the crevice of the workbench, a twist of yellow hair no longer than his smallest finger.

Nine days of saying nothing.

He climbed the rest of the stairs and stood in the doorway of the long room where the clocks hung in their ranks like a congregation at prayer. Jonah was asleep at the bench, cheek against a half-disassembled striking train, a broach still pinched between ink-stained fingers. Beyond them, behind the screen of the old case that had once housed the observatory's trial regulator, a small shape curled under a blanket that Horvath recognized from his own bed—Jonah must have taken it, which meant Jonah had thought about warmth, which meant Jonah was in this deeper than fear alone would carry them.

Horvath lowered himself into his chair by the cold stove and did not wake anyone.

This was the hour in which he did his honest thinking. Not the daylight hours, when his hands were busy and his mind could hide in escapements; not the evening hours, when lamplight made everything feel provisional and forgivable. The dark hours, when a man had nothing between himself and what he'd done, and what he was about to do.

*You are an old fool,* he told himself, as he told himself every night.

And every night, another voice answered—the flat, unhurried voice of the interrogator who had sat across from him thirty-one years ago in a room with a drain in the floor. *We know you are an old fool too, Master Horvath. We have counted your fools carefully.*

He had been young then, younger than Jonah, apprenticed to a master in Maribor under the old regime, back when the guild still taught politics alongside gear trains because it had to. He had printed leaflets. That was all—leaflets, absurd little things, arguments about wages and suffrage set in ten-point type on paper so thin the words showed through like veins. For this he had spent fourteen months in a cell, and when he came out his master's shop had been seized, his tools sold, his name entered in a ledger from which no entry was ever struck.

He had learned the lesson thoroughly. He had come to Vessengard and rebuilt himself into exactly what a state could love: precise, punctual, silent. He had certified instruments for the harbor authority and the customs house. He had signed loyalty declarations in triplicate. He had kept his conscience the way one keeps a movement out of production—wrapped, oiled, boxed, stored in the deep drawer where nothing rusts because nothing moves.

For thirty years the drawer had stayed shut.

Then Anselm Voss had walked into his shop with a broken chronometer and a smile too wide for a man carrying papers that hot, and said, *Master Horvath, I'm told you're the only honest man left in Vessengard,* and Horvath had looked at him and understood that the drawer was going to open again whether he willed it or not.

Voss had never asked him for anything, in the end. That was the cruelty of it. The man had simply talked to him, twice, three times, about springs and liberty and the arithmetic of courage, and then gone off to do the thing that got him hanged, and now his daughter slept eight feet away from where Horvath sat, breathing in the dark like a small bellows keeping some essential fire alive.

The state had already decided she should not exist. He had read the notice pinned outside the guildhall: *the family of the traitor Voss having been dealt with according to law.* Dealt with. As though the child were a debt, a remainder, a rounding error to be swept from the column.

Horvath rose, quietly, and went to the cabinet where he kept the guild seal.

* * *

The seal was brass, the size of a plum, worn smooth at the grip from forty years of certifying instruments. It sat in its velvet nest among the punches and stamps, and it was, he reflected, the single most valuable thing he owned—not for its metal but for its authority. With it he could pronounce a barometer true, a chronometer seaworthy, a man competent. With it he could make a lie official.

That, he supposed, was the whole secret of power, and why the men in the ministries guarded their seals more jealously than their swords.

He turned it over in his hands. On its face, the crossed anchors and gearwheel of the Clockmakers' Guild of Vessengard, and around the rim, in letters fine enough to require a lens: *TEMPUS FIDEM FACIT.* Time makes faith. His predecessor had chosen the motto; Horvath had always privately amended it—time makes liars of us all, given long enough—but tonight the original reading seemed suddenly, urgently correct. A forged document was only a machine for making time, and time, properly built, made faith in whatever it measured.

There was a travel permit in his strongbox. There always was; the guild issued blank forms to masters for the certification of journeymen sent to provincial workshops, and the forms required only completion—a name, a destination, a purpose, a seal. The inspector, Brandt—he had met him twice, a neat young man with careful eyes and the manner of someone who had never once enjoyed his meals—had been asking questions in the quarter for a week. Questions about sabotage, about sympathizers, about a girl.

*Names,* Brandt had offered Horvath across a desk smelling of ink and wet wool, *purchase immunity. Immunity purchases continuity. Your certification marks, Master Horvath, would remain valid throughout the province.* As if the old man's life's work were a subscription that might lapse.

Horvath set the seal down on the bench beside the permit form and looked at the two objects together, and understood that he was looking at the entire question of his life rendered in brass and paper.

If he gave the name—if he gave even the rumor of the name—he kept everything. The shop, the seal, the standing, the slow dignified decay of a respected craftsman. The girl went wherever the state took girls whose fathers had been dealt with, and Horvath went on certifying barometers until his hands failed, and at his funeral the guild would speak of his exactness.

If he did not, then somewhere ahead of him there was a cell with a drain in the floor, or worse, and everything he had built in thirty years of careful silence would be confiscated, catalogued, and scattered—and the girl might still be taken anyway, and the sacrifice would purchase nothing.

The arithmetic was clear. He had always been good at arithmetic.

He dipped the pen.

But his hand stopped above the form, and he found himself listening again—that habit of sixty years—to the silence of the house, and beneath it, very faintly, the sound of the child turning in her sleep, and the smaller sound of Jonah stirring at the bench without waking, the way an apprentice learns to sleep in snatches, guardedly, the way soldiers sleep.

*What is a craft for?*

His father had asked him that, once, when Horvath was seven and struggling with a file. He had forgotten the question for fifty years and remembered it now with total clarity, standing in his own workshop at five in the morning with treason drying on a nib. His father had been a wheelwright. *Any fool can bend wood, boy. The craft is knowing what the wood is for.*

The wood was for the wagon. The gears were for the clock. And the clock—

The clock was for the people under it. Every clock he had ever built or mended hung over somebody's table, somebody's bed, somebody's deathbed, marking out the hours in which they loved each other or failed to. A clockmaker who served only the mechanism was a man sharpening tools in a burned village. And a guild that lent its seal to the hunting of children had stopped being a guild and become a weapon, and he would not be a part inside it any longer, however precisely machined.

Horvath wrote.

He wrote slowly, in the hand he used for certificates, each letter upright and unimpeachable. *Lena Hartl*, a name common enough to drown in. Age fifteen, a journeyman's daughter traveling to her uncle in Kelmburg for reasons of convalescence. Purpose: family illness. Validity: fourteen days. He hesitated only at the last line—the signature line, where the guild master attested under penalty of law to the truth of what was written—and there he paused long enough to hear, one final time, the voice from the cell with the drain in the floor, telling him how this ended.

Then he pressed the seal into the wax, evenly, with the full weight of his forearm, and held it while the wax cooled, and lifted it, and looked at the crossed anchors shining there, true and false at once, the most honest lie he had ever certified.

*Tempus fidem facit,* he thought. Let it be so, then. Let time make faith of this.

* * *

He was still standing at the bench when Jonah woke.

The apprentice came up out of sleep the way they always did—all at once, eyes finding Liesl's screen first, then Horvath, then the document in his hands. Horvath watched understanding arrive in that sharp narrow face like dawn crossing a dial: the form, the wax, the seal still warm in its nest.

"Master," Jonah said. Nothing else. The word carried the whole sentence anyway.

"The permit is good for fourteen days," Horvath said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him; steadiness, he supposed, was also a craft. "The thaw will bring traffic to the bridge—carts, drovers, the ice crowds. Papers will be checked quickly and badly." He laid the permit on the bench between them. "She travels as my kinswoman's daughter. If anyone asks, I will say so myself, loudly, which no one will expect from me."

"You'll hang for this." Jonah's voice cracked on it. "Master—you know what they did to you before—"

"I remember the room," Horvath said, "better than I remember most of my life." He began, deliberately, to roll down his sleeves, to button the cuffs, to perform the ordinary morning of an ordinary man. "I spent thirty years building something they could take from me. I find I mind it less than I expected."

"It's everything. Your shop, your mark—"

"My mark," Horvath said, "is on every instrument I ever certified, and every one of them keeps time regardless of who owns my name. That is the whole of what a maker leaves. The rest is furniture." He picked up the seal, weighed it once, and set it back in its velvet. "Besides, I am seventy-one years old. They can only hang me once, and I am told the queue is long these days."

Jonah laughed—a broken, startled sound, quickly smothered—and then, horribly, was crying instead, silently, the way apprentices cried, without moving the face. Horvath pretended to examine a pallet of screws until it passed.

Behind the screen, Liesl stirred and sat up, hair wild, watching them both with the grave attention of a child who has learned that adults' mornings contain decisions.

"Papa knew a man with a stamp like that," she said.

Horvath felt the words go through him like a cold wire. He looked at her—at Voss's jaw under the childish softness, Voss's level gray stare—and heard himself answer before he had decided to.

"Then your papa knew a man who owed him better than he got," he said. "Go back to sleep, Lena Hartl. You sail for Kelmburg tomorrow."

She lay down obediently. But her eyes stayed open a moment longer, fixed on the seal in his hand, and Horvath understood that she had memorized his face, just as he had memorized hers, and that whatever happened on the bridge, this was already done—there were now three people alive who knew what he had chosen, and none of them would ever be invisible again.

Outside, far downriver, the ice gave a long groan, the first true groan of the season, like a giant shifting in its sleep.

The thaw had begun.
