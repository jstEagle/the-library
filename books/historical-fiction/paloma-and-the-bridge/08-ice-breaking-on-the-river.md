## 8. Ice Breaking on the River

The thaw came early that year, and it came dishonestly.

Jonah heard it first through the observatory floor—a long, groaning sigh from the river below, the sound of ice shifting in its sleep and deciding to wake. They had been oiling the great escapement at midnight, Liesl handing tools with the solemn competence of a surgeon's assistant, when the sound rolled up through the stone foundations like the building itself clearing its throat.

"Is it breaking?" Liesl whispered, as if the river might hear her and take offense.

"Not yet. But soon." Jonah set down the oiler. "The ice goes out early this year."

They climbed to the observation gallery, where the dome's shutter stood open to the cold, and looked out over Vessengard. The city lay under a thin skin of thaw-fog, lamplight smearing gold across wet cobbles. The river was the thing—the broad grey sheet of it, still holding, but darkened now along its edges, weeping into channels, and far out near midstream a seam of black water had opened like a wound that would not close.

The bridge crossed that river. The bridge was the only way across for forty miles in either direction. And when the ice broke—when the floes came grinding downstream like a fleet of white ships gone mad—every carter, every merchant, every farmer who'd been waiting out the freeze would surge onto it at first light. The tollhouse would be crowded. The gendarmes would be watching the crowd.

"They'll check papers," Jonah said quietly. "When the traffic comes, they always do. It's the one day of the year nobody can cross without being counted."

Liesl said nothing. She had learned, these six weeks among the clocks, when silence was the right answer. Instead she leaned against Jonah's arm, small and warm, and watched the black seam widen.

Below them in the workshop, Master Horvath sat at his desk with a lamp turned low, and he did not look up. But Jonah saw his shoulders go still—the stillness of a man listening to a clock he already knows is running down.

* * *

Brandt came at nine the next morning, and this time he did not come alone.

Jonah was fitting a new pallet stone when the knock came—three measured raps, official even through oak. Horvath answered it himself, which he almost never did, and Inspector Brandt stepped inside followed by two gendarmes whose boots left wet prints on the workshop floor.

"Ice is going out tonight or tomorrow," Brandt said, without preamble. He was not a cruel-looking man; that was the worst of him. He had the face of an accountant, patient and gray, and he carried a leather folder the way other men carry a prayer book. "The bridge will be watched from before dawn. Every crossing logged, every traveler questioned. Standard procedure for the thaw."

"Standard procedure," Horvath agreed, in the flat tone he used for weather.

"We've also completed our inquiry into the sabotage here." Brandt opened the folder. "The mechanism was tampered with by someone with guild training. Someone in this quarter. And we have recovered a record—an innkeeper south of the city—who took payment last autumn from a woman traveling with a child. A girl of about eight. Dark-haired. A scar over her left eyebrow, from a fall off a garden wall, if the family records are accurate."

The room went very quiet. Jonah kept their hands on the movement they were repairing, because stopping would be worse than anything, and forced their breath into the shallow rhythm of concentration. *She fell off a garden wall.* Six weeks, and Jonah had never asked how the scar came. There had been no time for ordinary questions, and now the ordinary question had arrived wearing a warrant.

"The girl survived," Brandt continued. "Anselm Voss's daughter is alive, gentlemen. And someone in Vessengard is keeping her."

He let that sit. Then he drew a paper from the folder and laid it on Horvath's workbench with the care of a man setting down a chess piece.

"A warrant for the premises. I'm required to execute it within three days—I chose to give you notice, Master Horvath, because you are a man of standing and I see no reason to embarrass you." His eyes moved once around the workshop: the benches, the cabinets, the narrow stair to the clock room where a child's shoes were hidden in a flour sack behind the great regulator. "But my superiors require results, not courtesy. If nothing is found here, they will ask why I searched so late."

"And what do you want from me, Inspector?" Horvath asked. "You've said what you must say. Now say what you came to say."

Brandt closed the folder. For a moment something almost human crossed his face—weariness, maybe, or the ghost of a man who had wanted to be a schoolteacher before the Ministry found better uses for tidy handwriting.

"I want names, and I don't want them," he said. "Understand me. If the child passes that bridge while I hold this warrant unsigned, it becomes my failure. My neck. So I am telling you, old man, as plainly as I am permitted: the bridge closes at dawn tomorrow, and it does not open cleanly again until the flood season ends. Whatever conscience is sleeping in this house has one night to wake up or stay buried forever."

He picked up the warrant, tucked it away, and inclined his head with terrible politeness. At the door he paused.

"The clock runs true again," he observed. "I checked it on my way in. Whatever heart you gave it back—it keeps excellent time."

Then he was gone, and the wet bootprints began slowly to dry, and Jonah realized their hands were shaking too hard to hold a screwdriver.

* * *

Horvath locked the door behind the gendarmes and stood with his palm flat against the wood for a long moment. When he turned, he looked twenty years older and somehow more solid, the way a beam looks honest after it has held weight.

"One night," he said.

"Master—" Jonah started, but the old man raised a hand.

"No speeches. We have perhaps fourteen hours, and I have spent sixty years learning to use hours well. Sit down, both of you." He pulled the travel papers from the false drawer beneath his desk—the forged permit bearing his own guild seal, ink barely dry from three nights ago—and spread them beside a map of the river district that he had clearly been studying longer than any of them had known. "Now. Listen, and listen like apprentices, because this is the finest mechanism we will ever build, and we build it out of hours instead of brass."

The plan assembled piece by piece, and every piece was wrong in some way that might kill them, and there was no time to find better pieces.

Horvath could not leave. His face was known to every gendarme in the quarter; his absence would be noticed by morning, and his presence at the tollhouse was the alibi that would buy them hours of confusion—he would report himself present, log the workshop sealed, give Brandt a reason to trust the search results until it was too late. "I built this life out of gears and patience," he said. "I can spend one night of it buying yours."

So it would be Jonah and Liesel on the bridge. Papers: the permit named a widow's son and niece traveling south to a cousin's farm past Miren—Horvath had aged the girl's description up, called her ten instead of eight, given her a different surname entirely. "Voss is a name that gets people hanged," he'd said. "Yarrow is a name that gets people waved through. You are Yarrow's cousin's daughter, Liesel Yarrow, and you will believe it completely, because children believe what adults insist upon."

Disguise: Liesel's hair bound up under a wool cap, her face smudged, her clothes cut down from a dead apprentice's trunk and made shapeless. Jonah in their oldest coat, carrying a journeyman's tool roll—the universal costume of the invisible, the costume Jonah had worn all their life without ever guessing it would one day be armor.

Route: not straight across. The bridge's center span would be choked with sightseers watching the ice run; gendarmes would stand at both tollhouses checking papers. But the eastern footpath, the one the carters used for overflow, narrowed past the second pylon—and there, Horvath showed on the map, a coal merchant named Brenner kept a shed that backed onto the stair down to the ferry landing. "Brenner owes me forty years of favors and hates the Ministry more than he loves his own comfort. He'll look the other way while you go down the stairs. On the far bank, at the boathouse with the green door—Voss's people will be waiting. I sent word Tuesday."

"You sent word?" Jonah stared at him. "How long have you—"

"Since the third week," Horvath said mildly. "Did you think I hadn't counted the bread?"

Heat rose in Jonah's face—shame and relief tangled together so tightly they couldn't be told apart. All those weeks of careful rationing, of hiding the extra bowl, of Liesel's laughter pressed down to a whisper—and the old man had known since the beginning, had said nothing, had simply begun forging papers in the dark while pretending to sleep.

"Why didn't you stop us?" Liesl asked. Her voice was very small. "Why didn't you tell them?"

Horvath looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—Jonah thought of a man looking at a clock he had made in his youth, remembering the hands he'd had then.

"When I was younger than you are now, girl, a different government put me in a cell for eleven months because I fixed a clock for the wrong man." He said it evenly, the way he stated gear ratios. "I learned in that cell that a man's craft survives anything, but his conscience only survives if he spends it. I am eighty-one years old. I intend to spend mine well tonight."

* * *

They worked through the dark hours.

Jonah sewed the false seams into Liesel's coat pockets—deep enough for the papers, shallow enough not to bulge. Liesel practiced her new name in a whisper, over and over: *Liesel Yarrow, Liesel Yarrow,* until it stopped sounding like a stranger's. She ate the last of the good bread without ceremony, because Horvath ordered her to, saying that fear digests poorly on an empty stomach. Once, near two o'clock, she caught Jonah's sleeve.

"If they catch us," she said—not crying, just asking, in the level voice she used for asking how escapements worked—"what happens to you?"

Jonah considered lying. Decided, finally, that she had earned the truth the way she'd earned everything: by living inside it.

"Prison, probably. Maybe worse." They kept sewing. "But I promised your father, a long time ago, that your family would never stand alone. Promises aren't safe things, Liesl. That's what makes them worth having."

She nodded slowly, gravely, an eight-year-old weighing the arithmetic of sacrifice and finding it acceptable. Then she went back to winding the small carriage clock Horvath had given her to practice on, because in this house, grief and work shared a bench.

Outside, toward dawn, the river began to speak.

It started as a crack—a single sharp report, like a rifle shot, that made them all freeze—and then another, and another, closer together, until the cracks ran together into a continuous grinding thunder. The ice was going out. Black water showed in widening veins across the whole grey sheet, and somewhere upstream a floe the size of a chapel broke loose and began its long slow rotation downstream, catching the first light like a burning sail.

"It's early," Horvath breathed at the window, and there was something new in his voice—not dread exactly, but urgency sharpened into clarity. "Hours early. The crowd will come sooner, and the gendarmes will be at their posts before full light." He turned from the window and looked at Jonah, and for the first time in six weeks Jonah saw fear in the old man's face—fear, held steady the way you hold a delicate part over a flame. "We move now. Not at dawn. Now, while the watch is still forming, while the tollhouse lamps are still being lit. There won't be a second chance. There may not be a first."

Jonah looked at Liesel—at her cap pulled low, her smudged cheeks, the forged papers buttoned inside her coat over her heartbeat—and felt the strange calm of the point of no return sliding shut behind them, quiet as a case clock's door.

"Then we go," Jonah said.

And from the river below, the ice answered with a roar like the whole world's machinery starting up at once.
