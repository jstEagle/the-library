## 9. Crossing

The ice broke in the night, and the whole city heard it.

Jonah stood at the window of the observatory's clock room and listened to the Vessengard shrug off winter—a sound like a giant turning over in sleep, groans and cracks rolling upriver, the floes grinding against the bridge piers with a patience that was worse than violence. Below, lamplighters still made their rounds along the quay, but the light caught on water now where there had been white for four months.

"It sounds angry," Liesl said beside him. She had her coat already on. They both did. It was not yet three in the morning.

"It sounds like a door opening," Jonah said. "Doors sound like that when they've been stuck a long time."

She nodded as though this were ordinary knowledge, and perhaps to her it was. In five weeks she had learned escapements and oil grades and how to file a tooth true without breathing on the brass; she had learned to be quiet the way clocks are quiet, which is to say full of motion no one sees. She had learned fear and worn it lightly, the way she wore Jonah's spare scarf wound three times around her neck. But she was eight years old, and when the ice cracked loudest—somewhere beneath them, under the piers of the Karlstrasse Bridge—her hand found Jonah's sleeve and held on.

They would cross today. There was no longer any question of waiting. Brandt had come yesterday with a warrant folded in his glove like a knife kept sheathed out of politeness: authority to search the observatory, the guild hall, every workshop in the clockmakers' quarter, beginning at first light. Horvath had read the warrant twice, handed it back, and said, "You'll want daylight for that. The stairs are treacherous in the dark." And then he had gone upstairs and unlocked the drawer where he kept his guild seal, and Jonah had understood that the old man's silence all these weeks had been a decision being built plank by plank.

The forged permit lay against Jonah's chest now, inside their shirt, warm from their own skin. A travel authorization for one *J. Yarrow* and ward, bound for the mill towns downriver, stamped in violet ink that Horvath had mixed himself from pigment and patience. It would not survive close inspection. Nothing about this morning would.

"Say it again," Liesl whispered.

"The tollhouse first. You keep your eyes down and count the gears in your head—we'll pick a number before we go in. Then the span, and you don't stop on the span, not for anything, not if I tell you, not even if—" Jonah stopped. Started again. "On the far bank there's a coal yard with a green door. A man will be leaning against it smoking a pipe, and he will say, *cold morning for the fair.* And you'll say—"

"*Every morning is cold for someone.*"

"Good." Jonah crouched and straightened her collar, an excuse to look at her face one more time while it was still only a face and not yet a memory they were carrying. "And after that, whatever happens on my side of the river, you walk through that door and you don't look back. That's not a suggestion, Liesl. That's the whole trick of it."

"You'll come too."

"I'll come too," Jonah said, because the lie was kinder than the truth and they had exactly one kindness left to spend.

Horvath came down the spiral stair at half past four, dressed in his good coat, the one with the guild braid at the cuff, and carrying his walking stick with the brass ferrule worn silver from forty years of cobblestones. He looked, Jonah thought, like what he was: the senior master of the Clockmakers' Guild of Vessengard, on his way to open his observatory on the morning the ice broke, when every official in the city would be watching the river instead of each other. His disguise was simply being entirely himself.

"The tollhouse opens at six," Horvath said. "Brandt's men will come up Observatory Hill at first light, which is ten past seven today—I checked the almanac, don't argue. That gives us fifty minutes and no margin." He held out his hand to Liesl, palm up, formal as a dance. "Fräulein Voss. It has been an honor to have my workshop disturbed by someone who actually asks questions."

Liesl took his hand and shook it solemnly. "Your fourth wheel is still off-center," she said.

"I know," said Horvath. "I left it that way so you'd have a reason to come back."

He said it lightly, but his eyes went over her head to Jonah, and something passed between master and apprentice that neither of them would ever put into words—the whole ledger of those weeks: the extra bread, the small shoes by the door, the silence that had been loyalty after all, or had become it. Jonah pressed the old man's hand once, hard.

"Master—"

"Don't." Horvath picked up his stick. "Sentiment is for people with time. We have none. Come."

* * *

The Karlstrasse Bridge at dawn on the day of the thaw was less a crossing than a flood given somewhere to go.

Farm carts loaded with the first early greens came up from the south bank in a slow river of their own; drovers shouted; two men argued over a barrel of herring; a woman carried a cage of chickens high above the crowd like a lantern. And everywhere along the rails, people leaned out to watch the ice—the great plates of it tilting and shattering in the brown water, grinding past the piers, piling up at the middle arch where the current ran hardest. Every few minutes a sheet the size of a chapel roof would crack loose and ride downstream, and the crowd would cheer as though the river had performed for them personally.

Into this chaos walked a clockmaker's apprentice, a child, and an old man, moving at the pace of people who have nowhere particular to be.

The tollhouse sat at the near end of the span, a stone kiosk where a bored clerk collected a copper from carts and waved pedestrians through for free unless they looked worth stopping. This morning the clerk was not bored. Two constables flanked his window, and a third stood at the far end of the bridge, visible across the whole span, checking papers for anyone traveling south with luggage. Jonah counted them the way Liesl counted gears—one, two, three—and felt the arithmetic settle into their chest like a weight.

"Cold morning," said Horvath pleasantly, arriving at the tollhouse window ahead of them, stick tapping, guild braid catching the light.

"Master Horvath." The clerk straightened; everyone in Vessengard knew that braid. "Out early."

"The thaw waits for no one, and neither does the guild. There's a regulator on the south bank that's been losing four seconds a day, and the ferry office swears by my work." He dug in his pocket, produced coins, counted them slowly, dropped one, watched it roll toward the wheel of a hay cart, retrieved it with much bending and muttering. He was, Jonah realized with something close to awe, doing deliberately everything he had spent seventy years doing accidentally: being old in public, thoroughly and unignorably.

It worked. The clerk's attention was on Horvath; the constables' attention was on Horvath; the crowd pressing behind was watching the ice. Jonah took Liesl's hand and walked through the tollhouse shadow, eyes down, lips moving around the number thirty-six, thirty-seven, counting teeth that were not there.

Then the clerk said, "Just a moment, Master Horvath. There's a notice—"

And Horvath's stick came down on the flagstones, and his voice went up like a bell cracking: "A *notice*? I have been keeping time for this city since before your father could shave, boy, and I will not be *noticed* at my own bridge—do you know what happens to the harbor bells if my regulators run wrong? Do you know what happens to the *trains?*" He struck the ground again. Somewhere in the press of bodies a cart horse shied; a barrel went over; herring rolled; a woman shrieked with laughter rather than alarm; and in the beautiful spreading confusion of it, Jonah and Liesl were already three steps onto the span, then ten, then lost among coats and baskets and the rail-side crowd all craning toward the water.

At the middle of the bridge the ice chose its moment. A floe struck the central pier with a boom Jonah felt through the stones, and the whole crowd surged to the rails at once, and Jonah used the shove of it, let the crowd carry them forward like a current carries a leaf, Liesl's hand crushed in theirs, her scarf whipping, her face set in the expression she wore when filing a difficult gear—concentration as armor.

Halfway across, Jonah looked back.

Horvath stood between two constables at the tollhouse, arms folded, stick still in his hand, arguing—still arguing, magnificent and furious, giving them every second he could buy. One of the constables had hold of his elbow. Horvath shook it off with the dignity of a man swatting a fly, and even at this distance, even through the crowd-noise, Jonah saw him glance up at the bridge once, quickly, and nod. As if to say: *go on, then. I checked the almanac.*

"Jonah." Liesl tugged. "You're stopping."

"No I'm not." They weren't. They were moving, one foot and then the other, across stones that trembled faintly with the river's violence underneath. Thirty meters. Twenty. The third constable at the far end turned toward them, bored, and Jonah lifted their chin and put on the face of a tradesman late for a job and walked straight at him, because hesitation was the only thing that could stop them now. The constable glanced at their ink-stained fingers, their toolbag, their plain working coat, and looked past them to a gentleman behind who was struggling with a hatbox in the wind.

Ten meters. Five. The green door of the coal yard stood open below the embankment steps, and against it leaned a broad man with a grey mustache and a pipe, smoke standing straight up in the still air.

*Cold morning for the fair,* he said.

*Every morning is cold for someone,* said Liesl, in a clear voice that did not shake, and the pipe-smoker's eyes went soft and fierce at once. He knelt. Whatever he saw in her face made him take off his cap.

"She has his chin," he said to Jonah quietly. "Anselm's chin." Then, briskly, before feeling could do damage: "Wagon leaves in ten minutes, under the sacking, downriver as far as Marrenholt. There's people past that." He looked up. "You coming?"

Behind them, across the water, a whistle blew at the tollhouse—short, sharp, repeated. A constable was starting onto the bridge at a trot, and the crowd was parting for him with the reluctant courtesy of people who hoped not to be involved. Jonah's heart slammed once, hard, against the forged permit under their shirt.

"I'm coming," Jonah said, and kissed the top of Liesl's head, and pushed her gently through the green door into the dark that smelled of coal and safety. "Remember the fourth wheel is off-center. Come back and fix it someday."

The door closed. Through the gap before it shut, Jonah saw her turn, saw her mouth form the word *you promised*—and then the sacking, and the wagon, and gone.

Jonah turned and walked back up the embankment steps, onto the bridge, into the path of the running constable, and began, very calmly, to count teeth.
