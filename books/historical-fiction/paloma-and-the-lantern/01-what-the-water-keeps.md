## 1. What the Water Keeps

The tide came in before the sun did, as it always did in Saltmere, sliding gray and patient over the mudflats until the harbor woke by degrees — first the moaning of hulls against their moorings, then the gulls, then the bells of the Meridian counting house ringing the half hour in a voice that brooked no argument. Rosalind Brightwater was awake for all of it. She had been awake since the fourth bell, lying on her pallet beneath the eaves of the divers' dormitory, listening to the water rise and measuring her day against it the way other people measured theirs against clocks.

Water kept its own time. Her father had said that once, or she had decided he had said it once, after so many years of carrying the memory that the memory and the saying had grown into each other like a pearl into its shell. *The water keeps what it keeps, little fish. Your job is only to go down and ask politely.*

She went down and asked politely.

By the fifth bell she stood at the edge of the drying racks with eleven other women and girls, each of them wrapped against the morning chill in coats the color of old rope, each of them holding a diving basket and a knife and nothing else worth mentioning. The Meridian Company owned their contracts down to the last stitch. It owned the racks, the sheds, the oyster beds out past the sandbar, the very air they breathed if you counted the air pumps, which the company did, charging two coppers per dive for the privilege of breath delivered through a hose. Rosalind did not use the pump. She dove free, the way her father had taught her, the way the older women still did when the overseers weren't watching closely enough to object. Free diving meant shorter bottom time and smaller takes, but it meant the company could not bill her for breathing, and there was a satisfaction in that which she hoarded like coin.

"Line up," said Overseer Brann. He said it every morning in exactly the same voice, a voice worn smooth as a river stone from twelve years of saying the same things to the same women. "Contracts open. Baskets inspected. Anyone found with shell outside her count answers for it."

They lined up. They were always lining up somewhere in Saltmere — at the ration window, at the pay desk, at the infirmary door where the coughing women gathered in cold months. A life under contract, Rosalind had learned early, was mostly the art of standing in lines without letting your face show what standing in lines does to a person.

Brann worked his way down the row with his ledger held against his forearm, ticking names. When he reached her he paused, as he always paused, one beat longer than he paused for anyone else. Not suspicion. Something more like accounting. Rosalind Brightwater, entry forty-one in the diver rolls, daughter of Tomas Brightwater, entry nine, status: lost at sea, year twelve of company tenure. She was a line item with a footnote, and Brann liked footnotes.

"Brightwater. Deep bed today. The new ground off Gull Rock."

"Yes, overseer."

"You'll take Nettie Farrow with you. She's green and the others won't slow their counts for her."

Nettie Farrow was sixteen, all elbows and salt-cracked knuckles, and she looked at Rosalind now with the particular terror of a girl who has just learned she will be someone's responsibility. Rosalind gave her a nod, nothing warmer. Warmth was a currency, and she had learned to spend carefully.

* * *

Gull Rock stood a half mile off the sandbar, white with droppings, loud with birds, and beneath it lay the new bed the company had opened that spring — deeper water than the old grounds, colder, with a current that ran along the bottom like a hand pushing you toward the open sea. The company called it Ground Seven. The divers called it nothing at all, because naming a thing made it yours, and nothing here belonged to them.

Rosalind went down first, to see what the water wanted to say.

The world below closed over her head and became simple. Light fell in slanted columns through the green; the oysters lay in their thousands across the seafloor like a field of closed mouths. She equalized her ears, tipped forward, and let the weight of her body carry her down, kicking once, twice, then not at all, conserving everything, spending nothing. This was the part of her life that made sense. Down here there were no ledgers, no lines, no names read aloud by men who wrote in books she would never be permitted to open. There was only pressure and light and the long patience of the water, which asked nothing of her except that she come down honestly and leave before it changed its mind about keeping her.

Her father had drowned — officially drowned, the word the company used, *lost at sea*, as though the sea had simply misplaced him — when she was nine. They had never recovered a body. The company paid out a death stipend of four months' wages to her mother, deducted the cost of the funeral boat, and closed the entry. Rosalind remembered almost nothing of the ceremony itself. What she remembered instead, with a clarity that felt like cruelty, was the smell of the ink in the clerk's book, because she had been close enough to see the page when he wrote *Brightwater, T. — lost* in a hand so neat it might have been practicing penmanship rather than ending a man.

After that, her mother had begun waiting. That was the truest word for it, though no one in Saltmere ever used it aloud. Marta Brightwater sat at the window facing the harbor through six years of tides, mending nets that did not need mending, watching every boat that came in, and when Rosalind came home with pearls and salt in her hair her mother would look through her as though she were another vessel arriving without the right passenger aboard. Her mother died in the winter Rosalind turned fifteen, quietly, between one tide and the next, and the company deducted the cost of the second funeral boat from Rosalind's wages, and Rosalind swore an oath over the grave that she would never wait for anything, never hope for anything, never let the water keep something of hers that she couldn't live without.

She had kept the oath for seven years. It was not a difficult oath to keep, in Saltmere. Hope required surplus, and the company saw to it that no one had any.

She surfaced beside the boat with her basket heavy, gasping once, twice, finding the rhythm again. Nettie watched her from the gunwale with wide eyes.

"How deep?"

"Four fathom to the bed. Don't fight the current — go with it along the floor and come up where I'm standing. Count your breaths on the surface. Three times what feels enough, then once more."

"That's what my mother said." Nettie said it quickly, as if embarrassed, and began checking the ties on her basket with fingers that wouldn't hold still.

"Your mother dives?"

"Dived. She's on the infirmary list now. Chest." Nettie shrugged the way young people shrug at enormous things. "She says the water's fairer than the company. She says at least the water doesn't lie about what it takes."

Rosalind looked at the girl a moment longer than she meant to. "Go down honest and come up fast," she said. "That's all the wisdom there is."

* * *

Inventory day came at the end of the week, and inventory day was the worst day of the month, worse even than storm days, because storm days at least released you from the counting house. On inventory day the entire take was weighed, graded, and entered, and the divers stood in the shed among the drying racks while clerks moved down the tables calling numbers, and every pearl you had bled for at the bottom of the sea was spoken aloud in a flat voice and converted into somebody else's wealth.

Rosalind carried well. That was her reputation, the one thing she had built in twelve years of indenture: *the Brightwater girl carries.* She carried baskets, crates, ledgers, messages between the harbor office and the counting house, keys from one overseer to another. She carried them without dropping, without asking questions, without appearing to notice what passed through her hands. It was a skill like free diving — the art of going down into rooms full of dangerous information and coming up with nothing visible on her face. The clerks trusted her precisely because she seemed to have no interior. She had spent years cultivating that emptiness the way other women cultivated gardens.

So it was Rosalind who was sent, on inventory day, to fetch the master tally book from the counting house strong room, and Rosalind who stood waiting at the high counter while Clerk Fenwick hunted for it among the shelves, and Rosalind whose eyes — trained by years of carrying, trained to see everything and register nothing — happened to fall upon the page of the shipping manifest lying open beside the inkwell.

It was a losses column. Losses columns appeared in every manifest; she had carried hundreds of them. *Losses — barrels spoiled. Losses — gear unserviceable. Losses — hull damage, storm.* Ordinary arithmetic. But this line read:

*Losses — divers, unserviceable (7).*

Unserviceable. The word sat on the page with a smallness that made it obscene. Seven women written in the same column as spoiled barrels, in the same tidy hand, with the same total carried forward at the bottom. One of the seven, she saw, was listed by initials only — *M.F., Ground Four* — and she thought of Nettie's mother on the infirmary list, coughing into a rag, and something moved behind her ribs that she had not allowed to move in a long time.

"Brightwater." Fenwick had returned, the tally book under his arm. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a manner of perpetual mild grievance. He followed her glance to the manifest, and for a moment — half a moment — something flickered across his face that was not mild at all. Then he closed the manifest and laid his palm flat upon it, the way a man covers a card he doesn't intend to show.

"You didn't see that."

"I see whatever's on the counter, sir. I don't read."

"See that you continue not to." He pushed the tally book across to her. His voice dropped, and the drop made it heavier than any shout. "Company records are company property. A girl who reads above her station finds her contract extended. A girl who talks about what she reads finds it extended somewhere less pleasant than Saltmere. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly, sir."

"Good." He straightened, resumed his grievance, waved her off. "Mind you don't jostle the book. Master Corliss counts every page."

She carried the tally book back through the yard with her arms steady and her face empty, past the drying racks, past the weigh shed, past Brann, who nodded at her the way he nodded at furniture. Inside her, beneath the emptiness, the word sat where she had swallowed it. *Unserviceable.* She turned it over as she walked, the way her tongue sought a broken tooth. Seven divers, in a column, totaled and carried forward. She tried to remember whether anyone had told her that seven divers had died this season. She could not. She could remember the price of lamp oil and the dockage fee on the salvage barge and the deduction schedule for damaged baskets. The things the company wanted known were everywhere. The things it didn't simply vanished, and vanished so cleanly that a person could doubt they had ever existed at all.

That was the trick of it, she thought, setting the book on Corliss's desk and receiving his grunt of dismissal. Not hiding the dead. Hiding the *counting* of the dead. A body washed ashore made noise. A number in a column made none.

* * *

She went to the shallows that night, as she did most nights when the moon was kind and the patrol boats were lazy. Past the breakwater, where the sand sloped gently and the water held the day's warmth, she waded out until it took her waist, her hips, her shoulders, and then she floated, and then she breathed.

Her father's breaths. He had named them for her when she was small, sitting on the beach with her feet in the foam, making a game of what was really survival. *First the long drink* — a slow inhale that filled the belly before the chest. *Then the hold, soft, not clenched* — the body quiet, the heart slowing, the whole self agreeing to be still. *Then the long pour* — the exhale through pursed lips, measured, unhurried, giving the sea back exactly what it had lent. She practiced them now in the dark water, floating on her back with the stars wheeling overhead, and she counted, and the counting steadied her the way prayer is said to steady other people.

Twelve years. Twelve years since the entry closed, since the neat handwriting ended a man in a single line. And tonight, floating, she caught herself doing the thing she had sworn off at her mother's grave: she caught herself wondering. Whether the sea had taken him quickly. Whether he had gone down honest and come up never. Whether the water had kept him the way it kept everything — patiently, without malice, without apology.

She made herself stop. She rolled over and swam the slow strokes back toward shore, and the shallows gave her up reluctantly, streaming, and she stood dripping on the sand looking back at the black harbor where the company ships rode at anchor with their riding lamps lit, one, two, three, small orange eyes in the dark.

Tomorrow was a carrying day. Ledgers from the harbor office to the counting house, three trips, maybe four. She would carry them the way she carried everything — steadily, invisibly, seeing nothing, saying nothing. That was how she had survived. That was how she would go on surviving, until her contract ran out or the sea changed its mind about her, whichever came first.

She was halfway up the beach, wringing out her hair, when she heard the bells of the counting house ring the midnight hour — and then, wrong, out of rhythm, a fourth bell she had never heard them ring. An alarm, thin and urgent, from the direction of the harbor gate.

Down by the drying racks, torchlight bloomed. Voices rose. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, out where the dark water kept what it kept, a boat was coming in that no one had sent for.

Rosalind stood still on the wet sand, her heart going hard against her ribs, and did not yet know that the water was about to give something back.
