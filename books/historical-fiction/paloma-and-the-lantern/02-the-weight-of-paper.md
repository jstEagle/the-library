## 2. The Weight of Paper

The fourth bell had been nothing. That was what they told her by morning, and what she told herself, and what the harbor itself seemed to have agreed upon between dusk and dawn, folding the whole affair away beneath its grey skin like a stone rolled into deep water.

A signal lamp misread, said the gatekeeper, yawning. A fishing boat out of Vell, running dark because its skipper owed duties he preferred not to pay. Nothing. By the time Rosalind reached the sorting yard, the torch-scars on the sand had been raked smooth, the drying racks stood in their patient rows like a congregation after a funeral, and the only evidence that anything at all had happened was the way the overseers kept glancing toward the breakwater, quick and covetous, as if the sea owed them money.

She learned early — before she learned to read, almost before she learned to walk on dry ground without rolling her gait — that the fastest way to disappear in Saltmere was to be useful in small, unremarkable ways. So she was. She carried what she was given. She wrote what she was told. When Master Quillon's clerk called her name across the yard in that flat voice reserved for errands, she set down her bucket of pearl meat, wiped her hands twice on her apron because the clerk hated smudged paper, and went.

"The counting house," he said. "You're wanted for copying. Wear the grey coat."

The grey coat. Not her diving coat, stiff with salt and patched at the shoulders where the harness wore through, but the plain wool thing kept for indoor work, for being seen by people who mattered and therefore must not notice you. She understood the distinction the way she understood tides: not as rules but as weather, simply how things were.

* * *

The counting house of the Meridian Trading Company sat at the head of the harbor like a fat man at the head of a table, three stories of honey-colored stone with windows tall enough to let in the light but not the view. Inside it smelled the way all company buildings smelled — ink and lamp oil and the sweet rot of old paper, underlaid with something colder, something like the air off deep water, though no water had ever been permitted within these walls. Money, Rosalind thought, did not like to be reminded where it came from.

Master Aldery was waiting for her in the long copy room on the second floor, standing at the head of a table laid out with manifests the way a surgeon lays out instruments. He was a narrow man, Aldery, dry as a December reed, with ink stains up both forearms to the elbow so that he looked perpetually as though he had waded into his work past the wrists. He had been kind to her once, years ago, in the distant unrepeatable way that kindness is sometimes dispensed in such places — a copper coin pressed into her hand at her mother's funeral, accompanied by a sentence about her father's good penmanship. She had never decided whether this made him trustworthy or merely made him dangerous in a more complicated shape.

"Brightwater." He didn't look up from sorting pages. "You'll copy these manifests. Clean hand, columns true, no embellishment. You know the work."

"Yes, Master Aldery."

"Twelve folios before the evening bell. The light's good today; use it." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, exactly. A calculation wearing warmth's clothing. "Your father had a fine hand too. Company-trained."

"I've been told," Rosalind said, in the voice she kept for references to the dead, which was the same voice she kept for references to herself: level, brief, already leaving the room.

She sat at the long table by the window and began.

There is a particular trance that comes with copying, and Rosalind entered it the way other divers entered the water — deliberately, breath first. Manifests were honest in their way, or at least consistent: cargo in, cargo out, pearls weighed to the grain, duties assessed, fees assessed, assessments assessed. She copied the names of ships she would never sail on, ports she would never see, sums that could have bought the whole diving quarter ten times over and fed every family in it besides. Her pen moved. The numbers passed through her like water through a net, catching only what she needed to catch and letting the rest slide away. This was the discipline of her life, refined over twelve years of being nobody in rooms full of somebodies: take in everything, hold nothing where it can be seen.

It was late morning, four folios deep, when the private ledgers came out.

They were brought up from the strong room by two men she didn't know, men with the soft hands and hard faces of the counting-house inner circle, and they were bound in black leather rather than the company's dun brown, and they were carried the way one carries anything one does not wish dropped and does not wish seen — close against the chest, angled away from the room. Aldery received them at the far table. The copying Rosalind had been assigned continued from a separate stack; the black books were for another purpose entirely, and the room arranged itself around that purpose without anyone saying so, clerks angling their desks, eyes lowering, the whole space developing a current that flowed around the far table like water around a hull.

Rosalind copied. She did not look. Looking was the first sin and the most easily detected; a turned head is louder than a shout in a quiet room. But she had spent her life learning where things were without turning toward them, the way her father had taught her to feel a shell bed through her feet in six feet of murk — pressure, displacement, the weight of a thing where a thing should not be.

The far table was where the weight was.

She worked through the noon bell without stopping. And it happened — she would think afterward that it could not have happened any other way, that the whole architecture of the moment had been waiting for her since before dawn, since the fourth bell, perhaps since the day her father drowned — that a page from the black ledger was carried across the room to be collated with the manifest stack, and the man carrying it stumbled slightly on the worn threshold of the doorway, and the page slid free and skated across the floorboards and came to rest against the leg of Rosalind's table.

She picked it up. Of course she picked it up. To leave it lying would have been strange, and strangeness was the one luxury her life did not afford.

It was a loss record. Quarterly. The column heads were ordinary enough — vessel, port, date, category — and then the entries began, and the categories were not ordinary at all, because there, in Aldery's own cramped upright script, running down the page like a list of fish landed at market:

*Losses — divers, unserviceable.*

Unserviceable. The word sat in her stomach like swallowed seawater.

Fourteen names. She counted them without meaning to, the way her eye counted everything — fourteen lines, fourteen women's names in the leftmost column, dates clustered like birds on a wire, and in the final column, where the manifest stacks recorded assessed value, a figure per line. A figure per woman. Not a pension. Not a payment to kin. An entry against loss of asset, balanced neatly against insurance recoveries, and beside two of the names a notation in smaller script that Rosalind read twice because her mind refused it the first time:

*Depth quota exceeded — operational necessity.*

Operational necessity. Fourteen women, and the sea between Saltmere and the shoals had swallowed them one season after another, and here it all was, dried flat, ruled straight, worth exactly what it was worth per line, per throat, per pair of lungs that had held their last breath of company air in the company's service.

One of the names was Sefa Ilion. Rosalind knew the name the way you know a scar. Sefa had dived the outer beds the year Rosalind turned eleven; Sefa had laughed loud and swum like an otter and taught half the girls on the strand how to clear their masks by humming underwater. Sefa had gone down on a grey Tuesday in the autumn of a bad quota year and come up without breath in her, and they had said at the time that the sea takes who the sea takes, and everyone had nodded because what else was there to say, and now here was the sea's account book, and the sea, it turned out, took whom the company sent down too deep too fast to meet a number written by a man with soft hands in a room that smelled of ink.

"Brightwater."

She hadn't heard Aldery cross the room. That frightened her more than the page did — that he had come all that way across the boards and she, whose ears kept the watch her eyes pretended not to keep, had not heard him. His hand was out. His face was perfectly composed. Only his eyes were wrong, very still, very awake.

"You've dropped nothing," he said quietly, "that concerns you."

"No, Master Aldery." She put the page in his hand. Her fingers did not shake. Twelve years of practice lived precisely in moments like this, in the muscles of her forearm and the stillness of her face, and she felt them holding, holding, the way a diver holds at depth with the burn building in her chest and the surface a rumor overhead.

But something had cracked anyway. She felt it go, small and internal, like ice giving way under a careful foot — not a collapse, not yet, just a seam opening somewhere below the waterline of herself. Because Sefa Ilion had a sister still living on the strand, and Sefa's mother still set out a cup for her at festival times, and all those years the truth had been lying in a black book in a strong room under this floor, ruled and totaled and filed, while the strand said *the sea takes who the sea takes.*

"Good hand," Aldery said, looking down at her finished folio instead of at her face. "Your father's hand. It's a pity—" He stopped himself. Whatever the pity was, he folded it away. "Continue. The remaining folios by bell. And Brightwater."

"Master?"

"What you saw just now is arithmetic. Arithmetic has no witnesses. Do you understand me?"

She understood him perfectly. "Yes, Master Aldery."

He stood there a moment longer than he needed to, and she had the sense — faint, unprovable, like the pressure of a shell bed through murk — that he was not threatening her so much as mourning her, that somewhere behind the dry reed of him was a man who had once pressed a coin into a grieving child's hand and had watched, for twelve years, as that child perfected the art of being unseen, and recognized the craft as a waste even as he profited by it.

Then he was gone, back to the far table, and the room's current closed over the place where the black ledger lay, and Rosalind bent her head and copied shipping manifests until the evening bell rang, columns true, no embellishment, her heart going slow and heavy as an anchor paying out chain.

* * *

She did not go home after. Home was the lean-to room above the cooper's shed that she rented by the month, one window, one cot, a lock she trusted less than her own sleeplessness. Instead she walked the strand past the drying racks, past the gate where the torch-scars had been raked from the sand, past the breakwater where the sea was doing its evening business of erasing everything — and on to the shallows east of the mole, where a shelf of white sand ran out under water clear as poured glass, and no company man ever came, because there was nothing there worth assessing.

The sun was going down red behind the harbor hills. The water took the color and held it, so that she stood shin-deep in liquid copper, and the only sounds were the small talk of the tide and the far-off bells of the town counting out the hour in their proper rhythm, four bells, five, each one correct, each one accounted for.

Her father had taught her the breathing before he taught her to swim. Tomas Brightwater had been a diver before he was a ledger-keeper — before the company noticed his fine hand and pulled him out of the water and into the counting house, trading his lungs for his penmanship — and he used to say the water doesn't care how strong you are, it cares how patient you are, patience being the only currency it accepts. So he'd stand her in the shallows at evening, this same shelf of sand, and count her down.

*Breathe in slow, like drinking through a reed. Hold it soft — don't grip it, carry it. Let the bottom of the breath go last.*

She breathed in, slow, like drinking through a reed.

She held it soft. Not gripping. Carrying.

The world simplified. It always did, under the breath-hold — noise fell away, want fell away, the counting house and the black ledger and Aldery's still, awake eyes all fell away, and there was only the body and the water and the long patient arithmetic of the lungs. She waded deeper until the water closed over her head, and hung there in the reddening light, arms loose, hair rising around her like ink spilled upward, and let the bottom of the breath go last.

Down here she could pretend, for the length of a breath, that grief was simple. That her father had drowned — that the sea took who the sea takes — and that the ache she carried was the ordinary ache of an orphan of the water, no different from Sefa's sister's ache, no different from a hundred aches along the strand. Ordinary grief could be carried. She had built her whole life on carrying it lightly, invisibly, a sealed jar stowed deep in the hold.

But tonight the jar had a crack in it, and the crack had a shape, and the shape was a ruled line of fourteen names in a dry reed of a man's handwriting, and the word *unserviceable*, which meant broken, which meant worn out, which meant used up and written off — and the terrible arithmetic knowledge, rising in her like pressure in her chest, that somewhere in the company's books her father might have a line too. *Brightwater, T. Clerk. Losses.* Drowned twelve years ago on a crossing the company never fully explained, his body never recovered, his effects inventoried — she had seen the inventory once, as a child, a short list: pen, knife, coat, one ring. Everything accounted for. Everything except the reason he was on that boat.

She came up gasping, later than she meant to, the surface breaking over her face warm as blood in the last of the light.

Patience, her father had said. The water accepts only patience. But patience, she understood now, standing chest-deep in the copper water with her heart going hard, was not the same as silence, and she had been confusing them for twelve years. Patience waits. Silence buries. She had buried herself alive and called it survival, and the company had ruled the line straight and totaled the column and filed her among the living unserviceable, and Sefa's mother still set out a cup at festival times, and somewhere under the counting house the black books lay stacked like coffins with the numbers still warm inside them.

The sun went down. The copper drained out of the water and left it black and honest. Rosalind stood a while longer, breathing — top of the breath, bottom of the breath, the full length of it, greedily, in open air — and then she waded out, wrung her hair the way she always did, and started up the beach toward the drying racks and the lane beyond.

That was when she saw the lantern moving down by the racks. Not the harbor patrol's steady sweep — this light moved quick and low, hurrying, hunting, and with it came voices, two men, sharp and low, and the sound of something — someone — stumbling over the rack-lines in the dark.

She stopped at the edge of the sand, dripping, invisible, and listened.

"—went down by the racks, I tell you, Hargrave wants him found before the tide turns—"

"Him? Who's *him*?"

"How should I know? Wet through and limping, gatekeeper says. Carrying a bag he wouldn't let go of."

The lantern swung. Rosalind melted back into the shadow of the mole, her heart beating the old dive-rhythm, slow and deep, patience, patience — but it was not the water's patience anymore. It was something else. Something with a seam cracked open in it, something waking.

Below, past the drying racks, at the tideline where the sea gives back what it chooses, a shape was dragging itself out of the dark — and in its fist, clutched the way a drowning man clutches, was a satchel of letters.
