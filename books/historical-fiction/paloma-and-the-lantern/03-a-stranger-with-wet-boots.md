## 3. A Stranger with Wet Boots

The drying racks stood in their crooked rows like a congregation of the drowned, hung with nets and pearl-shell that caught the late light and threw it back in dull rainbows. Rosalind had walked past them ten thousand times. She could have done it blind, could have done it asleep, could have done it at a dead run with a ledger strapped to her chest and an overseer's voice at her heels — and often had.

That evening she was carrying nothing but herself, which felt strange, like walking without boots. Inventory day ended early when the tally clerks quarreled over a miscounted crate of mother-of-pearl, and the counting house had emptied its errand girls into the street with two hours of light still left. Two hours that belonged to no one. She had meant to spend them the way she spent all unclaimed time: quietly, invisibly, down by the low tide line where the mud gave up its smell of iron and rot and the gulls argued over fish heads.

She did not mean to find a man dying among the racks.

At first she took him for a heap of sacking. The Meridian docks bred heaps of sacking; they bred driftwood, broken oar-blades, tarred rope, and once — though she tried never to think of it — other things the sea gave back that no one wanted claimed. But this heap breathed. It made a sound like water pulling through gravel, wet and rhythmic and wrong, and as she drew closer she saw that the sacking was a coat, grey-green and stiff with salt, and beneath the coat was a man on his side with his knees drawn up and one arm flung out as if he had been reaching for something when he fell.

His boots were soaked through. That was the first detail her mind seized on and refused to release: not the blood, not yet, but the boots. Saltmere men who worked the waterfront wore boots cracked with brine, yes, but these were river boots, fine-made, the leather dark and swollen from long immersion, and there was weed caught in the lacing. He had come out of the water recently and from a distance, or off a boat that had come from one, and he had walked — crawled, maybe — from wherever he'd landed to the shelter of the racks before his body quit him.

Rosalind stood very still. Ten years of being unremarkable had taught her the first law of the port: *see nothing*. A girl who saw things got asked questions; a girl who answered questions got noticed; a girl who got noticed got used, or got rid of. She had built her whole life on the narrow footing of being worth no one's attention.

But the heap breathed, and breathing was hard to unsee.

She crouched beside him, careful to keep the racks between them and the harbor road. Up close he was younger than the coat suggested, thirty perhaps, with a clerk's pallor under the salt burn and a jaw several days unshaved. There was a tear along his ribs where the coat had been opened — not torn by rocks; cut, clean and deliberate — and beneath it a bandage gone rust-brown, seeping slow as a ledger filling line by line. His lips were cracked and moving. No sound came out. One hand, black-rimmed with dried blood at every fingernail, still clutched a strap.

A satchel. Oilcloth-wrapped, tied down tight, held against his body the way a drowning man holds a spar. Even half-conscious, even bleeding into the shell grit, he would not let go of it.

"Sir." Her voice came out flat and quiet, trained to carry no further than it must. "Sir, can you hear me."

His eyes opened. They were grey, unfocused, swimming — then they fixed on her face and something in them sharpened with terrible effort, the way a man surfaces toward air. His mouth worked. What came out was barely more than shaped breath:

"Brightwater."

The name went through her like a hook through the soft part of the hand.

Not *help*. Not *water*, not *patrol*, none of the words a wrecked man throws at the first passerby. He had looked at her — a stranger in a patched diving coat with salt-cracked knuckles and her hair bound back in working cloth — and said *Brightwater*.

"How do you—" She stopped, because his hand had moved. Fingers, trembling, fumbling at the satchel's buckle with the clumsy desperation of a man whose body was already closing its accounts. She should have run. Every instinct she had cultivated like a crop said *run*: leave him, forget him, be nobody. The harbor patrol made its evening circuit past the racks within the quarter hour. A wounded man on Meridian property, asking after Brightwaters, was a knot of trouble she could not afford, could not survive, could not—

He got the buckle open. The oilcloth fell back, and inside were letters. Dozens of them, bundled and banded, wrapped in wax-cloth against the wet, and even in the failing light she saw they were old, some of them, softened at the edges, stained, carried a very long way.

And on top of the bundle, loose, folded twice, one page lay face-up, as if it had been read so many times it had worn its way to the top.

Her father's handwriting looked back at her.

She knew it the way she knew the taste of seawater — instantly, totally, below thought. The tall ascenders leaning like masts in wind. The way he crossed his t's with a single impatient stroke that sometimes overshot the stem. The small hooked *B* he used for his initials, drawn like a little boat riding a wave. She had learned her letters tracing over his hand on scraps of sail canvas while her mother cooked; she had copied that hand before she could properly copy her own. Twelve years of ledgers in other people's hands had not erased it. Nothing would ever erase it.

*Tomas.* The letter began with the name, in ink faded brown, and below it lines she couldn't read from where she knelt but which her body recognized anyway, the rhythm of them, the slope, the living human pressure of pen on paper.

Dated — she dragged the page closer with fingers that did not feel like hers — dated three years *after* the sea took him. Three years after the company paid its condolence of six silver and a bolt of cloth and closed his account in the great books with a neat entry: *lost, diver, unserviceable.* Three years after her mother had stopped speaking above a whisper and begun the long fading that ended behind the chapel wall.

The mud flats tilted. She put a hand down to steady herself and it landed in cold grit.

"That's impossible," she said aloud, to no one, to the gulls. "He's dead. I buried my mother waiting for him. He is *dead*."

The man made a sound. She turned and found his eyes open again, fixed on her with a clarity that frightened her worse than the wound, and he lifted one finger — an enormous effort, she could see it cost him — and touched the edge of the letter.

"Not," he whispered. "Dead."

Boots on the harbor road. Voices, two of them, patrol voices with the bored menace of men carrying authority they enjoyed. The light was nearly gone; the lamps at the counting house corner would be lit; the racks threw long stripes of shadow across the shell grit, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the tide was coming in, patient as everything else that owned this coast.

Rosalind had exactly enough time to make one decision, and she discovered — with a strange calm, as if watching someone else do it from underwater — that she had already made it. Somewhere between the word *Brightwater* and the date on that page, the girl who survived by seeing nothing had seen too much to go back.

She wrenched off her own coat and dropped it over him, oilcloth satchel and all, piling the spare netting from the rack's lower bar on top until he was a shapeless heap of work-day clutter that any diver might have left for morning. Then she stood, stepped out into the open where the lantern light could find her, and became what she had spent ten years perfecting: nobody.

"Evening," she called, pitching her voice into the flat boredom the overseers liked to hear. "You'll want to give the racks a wide berth. Netting's fouled — company property, and the night clerk fined me a day's wage last month for less."

The taller of the two patrolmen slowed. Lamplight caught the brass on his collar. Behind her, under the netting, the gravel-water breathing went on, fainter now, and she kept her body loose and her eyes stupid and prayed to whatever kept the drowned that he would stay silent, that the blood would not spread past the netting's shadow, that her hands would not shake until the boots had gone.

"Fouled netting," the patrolman repeated. He looked past her at the racks, at the heaped shadows, and Rosalind smiled the empty smile of a tired indentured girl with nowhere better to be.

"Aye, sir. Smell's enough to keep you off it, if you don't believe me."

* * *

They went, eventually. Men who enjoy their authority are easily distracted by the chance to exercise it elsewhere, and a drunk was singing outside the Anchorline, which promised better sport than a cold dock and a chattering girl. Rosalind listened to their boots fade all the way to the corner before she let her breath out — a long diver's exhalation, controlled, bottom-of-the-lung, the kind her father had taught her to hold against fear as much as against water.

Then she knelt and pulled the netting back.

He was conscious still, barely, grey-lipped in the lamplight that leaked between the racks. She got her shoulder under his arm and hauled, and he was heavier than he looked, dead weight shot through with live pain, and together — mostly her — they staggered out the back way, past the tar sheds, along the drainage channel where no lamp reached, to the one place in Saltmere that belonged to her alone.

The old smokehouse. Abandoned since the company had shifted its curing yards upcoast, it leaned at the edge of the mudflats on rotted pilings, and because it appeared worthless, no one watched it. She had been sleeping there some nights anyway, lately, when the dormitory barracks pressed too close — a secret she had kept even from herself, half-formed, the first crack in the wall of her invisibility. Now it had a use.

She got him onto the old curing boards, relit the stub of candle she kept hidden in a crevice, and cut the bandage away with the small knife she used for net-mending. The wound was a slash across the ribs, deep but clean — a knife, deliberately placed, meant to warn or slow rather than kill, though infection would happily finish the job. She washed it with boiled water, packed it with the yellow sulphur powder the divers' dispensary sold cheap, and bound it tight with linen torn from her spare shift. He endured it without screaming, which told her something about him. Twice he passed out; both times he came back up like a diver surfacing, deliberate, controlled.

When it was done, he slept — truly slept, the boneless sleep of the emptied. Rosalind sat back on her heels and looked at him for a long time, the candle guttering between them, and then she picked up the letter.

She read it by inches, the way you take cold water.

It was addressed to her mother. It spoke of an island — a name half smudged, beginning with *K* — and of men who signed papers for other men's lives, and of a promise: *if this reaches you, I am alive, and I am coming home by the long road.* It was signed with the little hooked B, riding its wave.

Three years after the sea supposedly swallowed him. Written in a hand she had traced as a child. Carried across ports and years by a stranger with river boots who had known her name on sight — which meant someone had described her, which meant someone had been looking, which meant this was not accident but arrival.

Outside, the tide turned. She heard it in the pilings, the soft knock of water taking back the flats, and she thought of her father's oldest lesson, spoken in the boat before dawn: *the water keeps what it keeps, girl, and gives back only what it chooses.*

Well. The water had chosen tonight.

Rosalind folded the letter along its old creases, tucked it inside her shirt against her skin where no patrolman, no overseer, no counting-house clerk would ever think to search a girl worth noticing, and fed another inch of candle. When the man woke, she would have questions — names, places, who sent him, who cut him, how many others carried letters in a dead man's hand. And he would answer, or he would learn what twelve years of grief does to a woman's patience.

She sat down on the cold boards to wait, the letter warm against her heart, and understood that the life she had built on being unseen had just ended — not with a shout, but with a stranger's whisper and three words written in her father's hand.

*Not dead.*

Somewhere across the dark harbor, a bell rang the first watch, and Rosalind Brightwater began, quietly, to plan.
