## 4. Handwriting of the Dead

Pain woke Elias Corvane before the light did.

It came in waves, the way the sea did — a long pull outward, then a rush back in, each one trying to drag him under. He lay still and took inventory the way he'd learned to take inventory of everything: methodically, without flattery. Ribs on the left side, cracked or worse. A gash above his ear that had been bound with something rough and smelling of salt and camphor. His boots were gone. His coat was gone. The satchel —

He sat up too fast and the room tilted, whitewashed walls swimming, a single window letting in the gray of early morning over Saltmere's rooftops. His hands went to his chest, to where the satchel should have been, and found instead a folded blanket, and beneath the blanket — nothing.

Then he heard the sound of paper being turned, one leaf at a time, somewhere below him.

* * *

The stairs creaked under his weight and he had to stop twice, one hand pressed to the wall, breathing through the pain in short, shallow pulls. The room at the bottom was a fisherman's parlor, or had been once: nets hung from hooks like shed skins, a table scarred by knives, a lamp burning low against the pre-dawn dark. She sat at the table with her back to him, and she had spread his letters out in three rows, ordered not by date but by port mark — he could see it in the way she'd grouped them, the Velloro cluster apart from the Casterhal ones — and she was reading them with the total absorption of someone who had forgotten he existed.

Rosalind Brightwater. He knew her name before she gave it, though she never would. He'd spent eleven days in this city asking questions sideways, and the answer that kept surfacing, quiet as silt, was *the Brightwater girl*. The diver's daughter who carried ledgers for Meridian. The one nobody noticed, which meant the one who noticed everything.

"You're bleeding through the binding," she said, without turning around.

"I'm aware."

"Sit down before you fall down." She still hadn't looked at him. Her finger moved down a line of text, stopped, returned to the top of the page. "This one is dated the ninth of Thawmonth, four years past."

"Yes."

"Tomas Brightwater drowned off the Casterhal banks twelve years ago. I was seven years old. I stood on the pier and watched them bring up his knife and half of his coat, and the overseer told my mother the sea keeps what it takes, and my mother believed him because believing was cheaper than the alternative." Now she turned, and her eyes in the lamplight were the flat gray-green of water over sand, giving nothing away. "So explain to me how a dead man wrote a letter four years ago."

Elias lowered himself into the chair across from her. Every inch of the descent was negotiated with his ribs. "Because he didn't drown," he said. "Or if he did, he drowned very loudly and then got out of the water and kept writing."

She didn't move. Didn't blink. But he saw her hand, resting on the table beside the letter, curl slowly inward until the knuckles went white, and he understood that whatever armor this woman wore, it was thin in exactly one place, and he had just put his thumb on it.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Corvane. Elias Corvane. Eleven years a clerk for the Meridian Trading Company, most recently in the office of the Assistant Comptroller for Losses, Casterhal station." He let that sit. "Until eight months ago, when I understood what the office of the Assistant Comptroller for Losses actually does."

"And what does it do?"

"It loses things," Elias said. "On purpose."

* * *

He talked for a long time, and she let him, which he recognized as a kind of test. She fed the lamp, fetched water, wrapped a strip of dried fish and set it near his hand without comment — small hostess courtesies performed with the mechanical efficiency of someone feeding a stray dog she hadn't decided whether to keep — but she never interrupted, and her eyes never left his face while he spoke, cataloguing him the way he imagined she catalogued cargo. Weight. Condition. Likelihood of spoilage.

He told her about the ledgers.

"You've copied manifests," he said. "You know what a loss entry looks like. *Divers lost to weather, two. Divers lost to depth sickness, one.* Small numbers. Tidy numbers. Numbers a magistrate could read without asking questions." He turned one of the letters face down and tapped its blank back as though it were a ledger page. "Three years ago I was reconciling the annual returns for Casterhal and the arithmetic wouldn't close. Not badly — a rounding here, an unexplained gap there, the kind of thing you'd never see unless you'd done the same reconciliation five years running. But I had. And the gaps were always in the same direction. Always smaller than they should have been."

"Smaller," Rosalind repeated.

"The company reported fewer deaths than occurred." He said it plainly, because there was no plain way to say it softly. "Every year. Every port. I pulled the harbor registries, the burial rolls, the parish books, the chandlers' accounts — a dead diver stops being supplied, you see, so even the men who never touch a ledger leave a shadow in the supply records. When I laid them all side by side, the shadows didn't match the entries. Over nine years, across Casterhal, Velloro, and here — Saltmere — the company recorded sixty-one diver losses." He paused. "The true number is closer to two hundred."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft he couldn't feel. Rosalind's face didn't change, but something behind it did; he watched it happen the way you watch weather come in over open water, a darkening far off that hasn't reached you yet but will.

"Why," she said. It wasn't a question so much as a door held open.

"Insurance, first. Wartime premiums are paid on declared losses, and declared losses are audited, so you declare few and real ones and pocket the difference through the salvage contracts. Second — and this is the part that took me longest to believe — replacement economics. An indentured diver costs the company perhaps forty crowns a year all told. Training a new one, binding a new family, costs less than paying hazard rates to keep experienced divers alive. On paper, we are cheaper drowned than protected." He heard his own voice go dry and bitter and made himself steady it. "I was a clerk, Miss Brightwater. I thought numbers were neutral. I thought the worst thing about them was tedium. Then I spent a winter learning that every figure I'd ever entered in good faith was a body with the name filed off."

"And him." Her chin lifted toward the letters. "Tomas Brightwater. Where does a drowned man fit into your arithmetic?"

"He doesn't. That's what frightened me." Elias leaned forward, and his ribs punished him for it, and he continued anyway. "Two years ago I found a transfer record. Internal, private seal — the kind of document that isn't supposed to exist outside the comptroller's own safe. A requisition for the upkeep of six individuals, listed only by number, at a coastal holding site inside the naval exclusion zone north of here. The line item was filed under wartime security expenditures. But the upkeep figures included medical allowances. Clothing allowances. Paper and ink."

"Paper and ink," she said again. She had a habit, he was beginning to see, of taking the smallest thing he said and holding it up to the light.

"Dead men don't write letters," Elias said. "But the company has been maintaining, at expense, a small population of people who are officially dead. Witnesses, I think. Men who knew too much about how the losses were made — foremen, tally-masters, senior divers. Kept alive and kept silent, because a corpse can't contradict a ledger, but a living man who's been declared drowned can't testify either. They aren't prisoners in any way a court would recognize. They're ghosts on the company's books. Line items."

He slid the letter across the table toward her — the one dated the ninth of Thawmonth — and watched her look down at it and not touch it, the way you don't touch a thing you're afraid will vanish.

"I traced the postal marks," he said. "That letter passed through a relay station on the exclusion coast. There's no village there. No civilian traffic. Whoever wrote it was writing from inside the fence." He drew a breath that cost him. "I believe your father is one of the six."

* * *

For a long moment there was no sound in the room but the lamp and, beneath it, the sea — a slow percussion against the pilings of the drying racks outside, patient as a creditor.

Then Rosalind Brightwater picked up the letter, and Elias watched her read it, and understood that he was watching something he had no right to watch and could not have looked away from if the harbor patrol had come through the door.

Her lips moved slightly, shaping the words. Her father's words. He had read the letter himself a dozen times on the road — it was the reason he'd come to Saltmere at all, the thread he'd followed from Casterhal through Velloro to this cold northern port — and he knew what it said. *Little Rosalind, if this reaches anyone, tell them the count is wrong. Tell them I keep the true count. Tell my girl the sea did not take me, men did, and men can be made to give it back.*

"They trained us to recognize handwriting," she said at last, very quietly. "In the counting house. Signatures, especially — you learn the loops, the pressure, where a man crosses his t's. Forgery is the oldest crime in trade." She turned the page over. Her thumb hovered above the signature without landing. "This isn't forged. The slant's wrong for a forger — he leans harder on the downstrokes, always did, my mother used to say he wrote like he was digging. And the ink—" She stopped. Started again. "A forger copies what he sees. This has his mistakes in it. The dropped letters when he's tired. The way the r goes flat at the end of a word. Nobody counterfeits a man's tiredness."

"No," Elias agreed. "Nobody does."

"So either you are the most elaborate liar the company ever produced, or my father has been alive for twelve years while my mother waited on that pier until waiting killed her." She set the letter down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable when what you actually want to do is throw it. "Do you understand which of those is worse? Do you understand what you've done, walking into this city with that in your bag?"

"I do."

"My mother died believing he was coming back. Twelve years of it. She kept his coat half mended. She left the lamp lit. And I swore — I was fifteen, I stood at her grave and I swore — that I would never make her mistake. Never hope. Hope is just grief paid in installments." Her voice stayed level the whole time, which was somehow worse than weeping. "And now you've handed me a letter and told me hope has a postal route."

Elias said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't cruelty dressed as comfort. Outside, the sky was going from gray to the color of old pearl, and somewhere down the strand the first carts were rolling, and the city was waking up around the two of them and their impossible paper.

At last she looked up. "Why you?" she asked. "Eleven years a clerk. Why burn it all for dead men's names?"

He considered lying — offering her principle, conscience, some clean motive she could weigh and accept. But he had watched her all night, and he suspected she could smell varnish at fifty paces.

"Because I entered the numbers," he said. "For eleven years, I entered them. Some of those entries were lies and I didn't know it, which is no defense — ignorance is just a slower way of signing. My brother dove for Meridian out of Velloro. He's on the public rolls as lost to weather, autumn before last. Weather." He smiled, and it hurt his split lip. "I checked the harbor log afterward. That day the weather was glass calm. So no, Miss Brightwater, I'm not a good man on a crusade. I'm a bookkeeper with a debt, and the debt is written in the same hand as everything else I ever filed."

Something shifted in her face then — not softening, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of one debtor meeting another.

* * *

They worked through the morning with the shutters closed.

It was her idea, and it showed him what he'd walked into. She re-sorted the letters by postal mark and date, built a timeline on the back of a salvaged manifest sheet in a clerk's hand nearly as fine as his own, and began cross-referencing his transfer record against the harbor schedules she carried in her head — tide tables, patrol rotations, which inspectors took bribes and which took notes. She did all of it without being asked, without agreeing to anything, and when he pointed this out she said, "I'm not helping you. I'm checking your work. If your evidence is rotten, I want to know before I bet my life on it."

"Fair," he said.

"Nothing about this is fair." But she bent back over the pages.

By midday they had it: six letters over four years, all routed through the same relay, intervals shrinking as the armistice negotiations gathered speed — as if whoever wrote them could feel the window closing too. The transfer record matched a maintenance barge schedule she recognized from the manifests. Everything fit. Everything fit the way a noose fits.

"If he's alive," she said finally, laying down her pen, "then the most dangerous thing he is, is a witness. And the armistice commission—"

"—accepts testimony only until the signing," Elias finished. "Six weeks. Maybe less. After that, the historians take over, and the historians work for the victors, and the victors' ledgers say sixty-one."

She was quiet for a moment. He watched her fold his father's letter along its original creases, precisely, reverently, and slide it into the bodice of her dress, over her heart, where it would ride with her through every hour of whatever came next.

"I don't trust you, Corvane," she said. "You appear out of the sea bleeding, carrying my father's ghost in a satchel, and you want things from me I haven't agreed to give. For all I know Hargrave sent you."

"Hargrave," Elias repeated, and kept his voice level with an effort. "You know that name?"

"Everyone in Saltmere knows that name." Her eyes narrowed, reading him — weight, condition, likelihood of spoilage. "Which means you know why I should throw you back into the street."

"It also means," he said carefully, "that you know what happens if I'm caught with these papers. And you hid me anyway. Before you'd read a single letter. Before you knew anything except that a stranger was bleeding on your racks with a company seal on his bag." He leaned back, ribs screaming, and held her gaze. "So let's not pretend this is trust, Miss Brightwater. It's something better. It's two people who've both been keeping false books deciding, for once, to enter the truth."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, the gulls wheeled over the drying racks, crying like things owed money.

"Dawn," she said at last, standing, gathering the remaining letters into their oilcloth. "The counting house summons me at dawn, and I go, because invisible women survive. While I'm gone you'll stay here, you'll stay quiet, and you'll start copying everything in your head onto paper — dates, ports, figures, names — because heads get broken and paper hides." At the foot of the stairs she paused, her back to him, and he saw her shoulders square themselves like a woman stepping off a pier into deep water.

"And Corvane. If my father is alive behind that fence—" Her voice did not waver, but it dropped low, into the register of the sea itself. "Then before those six weeks run out, I am going to walk into the counting house, open their precious ledgers, and correct the count. Line by line. In his hand if I have to."

She climbed the stairs and was gone, and Elias Corvane sat alone in the lamplight with his broken ribs and his borrowed life, and began — carefully, in a clerk's finest hand — to write down every name he knew.
