## 5. The Ledger's Second Spine

The counting house smelled of tallow and old paper, a dry, closed smell like the inside of a dead man's trunk. Rosalind had worked within it for eleven years and had never once thought of it that way until now. Now every breath of it seemed to carry something buried.

She climbed the outside stair at half past six, as she had done four hundred mornings before this one, with her hair bound flat and her collar high and her face arranged into the pleasant blankness the clerks preferred. The morning fog was lifting off Saltmere harbor in rags. Somewhere below, the drying racks creaked under their burden of nets, and the first bell of the day tolled from the company chapel — a bell paid for, she now knew, by women who had drowned making someone else rich.

*Unserviceable.* The word had lived in her head for three days now, turning over like a stone in the shallows, showing its underside.

"Vasquez." Mr. Pellam stood at the top of the stair, keys already in hand, his waistcoat buttoned against a chill only he could feel. "You're wanted in the copying room. Manifests for the *Coriander*. And mind you keep your figures clean — the auditors come next month."

"Yes, sir."

She followed him down the long corridor where the light came gray through leaded windows, past the room where the junior clerks scratched away like crabs in sand, past the locked door of the Records Office, which she had passed ten thousand times without once wondering what lay behind it. That was the discipline of invisibility: not looking. She had been very good at not looking.

Now she looked.

* * *

The copying room held two desks, an ink stove, and a window that overlooked the customs quay. Rosalind took her seat, uncapped the company ink, and began on the *Coriander*'s manifests: pearl shell, dried fish, copra, mother-of-pearl buttons in lots of five gross. Ordinary freight. Ordinary numbers. Her pen moved through them the way her hands moved through water, smooth and unremarkable, and no one watching would have guessed that beneath the surface of her attention something enormous was swimming.

Elias Corvane's instructions had been simple, delivered in a whisper while she'd changed the dressing on his ribs the night before: *"Public ledgers are performance, Rosalind. They show the commission modest losses, honest wrecks, actuarial sorrow. But every trading house keeps a second spine to its books — the true count, kept for insurance and shareholders. Find where they reconcile the two. That's where the bodies are."*

She had told him she could not simply walk into the Records Office. He had said he knew. He had said, instead, watch the reconciliation.

So she watched.

At nine o'clock, Pellam came through with a leather folio under his arm and a younger clerk, Fenwick, trailing him with a stack of loose pages. They set up at the far desk. Pellam drew out the folio — dark green, brass corners, the Meridian crest stamped in gold — and unlocked it with a small key from his chain.

Rosalind copied her manifests and listened with her whole skin.

"The *Coriander* reconciliation," Pellam said. "Quarterly. Read me the public figures."

Fenwick read them. Divers lost, season of the storm year: *four*. Vessels unserviceable: *one hull*. Cargo written off: a sum so modest it might have been a widow's grocery bill.

"And the private?"

There was a pause. Rosalind heard paper being turned — heavier paper, she thought, a different stock altogether — and then Fenwick's voice, lower, careful:

"Divers lost... thirty-one."

Her pen stopped. A drop of ink gathered at the nib and fell, and she blotted it with the edge of her cuff before it could bloom across the manifest, and her heart went on beating in the ordinary way, which seemed suddenly obscene.

Thirty-one. Against four. In one quarter. In one port.

"Carry on," Pellam said mildly. "Shell tonnage variance."

She made herself write the next figure. Then the next. Her hand was steady because she trained it to be steady, because a diver's hands do not shake at depth or the water takes them, and she thought: *this is what my father taught me, without ever meaning to. How to be calm where the pressure wants to crush you.*

All morning the reconciliation continued. She copied her manifests. She memorized everything else — not the way clerks memorize, but the way divers memorize a reef: by shape, by relation, by the fixed stars above it. The green folio opened twice more. Each time, the arithmetic of the dead ran eight and nine times higher than the public count. Each time, the discrepancy was carried to a column headed only *Adjustments — Marine*, and initialled, in a hand she recognized from a thousand signatures, by Hargrave.

At noon Pellam locked the folio and left for his dinner, and Fenwick stretched, and Rosalind asked, casually, for more blotting paper, and while Fenwick fetched it she rose to close the window against the harbor damp — and saw, on the corner of Pellam's desk where he'd left it in his hurry, a single loose page from the private book, curling in the sea air.

A quarterly summary. Season of the storm year, third port annexed. And there, in a cramped column headed *Divers — Unserviceable*, thirty-one names.

Not numbers. Names.

* * *

She did not take the page. That was the discipline; taking it would raise alarms, and Elias had been explicit — *never steal what you can copy, never copy what you can remember.* But she read it. She gave herself forty seconds, standing at the window with the fog burning off the water, and she read every name on the page, and somewhere around the seventeenth line the counting house fell away and she was standing on the shingle beach of her childhood, twelve years old, watching the boats go out.

Because she knew these names.

*Maren Oduya.* Who had taught her to hold her breath behind the breakwater, laughing bubbles, when they were both nine. Maren who could free-dive to four fathoms and come up grinning with her hair like black weed. Maren whose mother had received a compensation notice of eleven pounds and had used part of it to buy her daughter a headstone, though there was nothing under it but sand.

*Sefa Ilori.* Sefa with the gap teeth and the voice like a gull, who used to sing the hauling songs louder than the men just to annoy them. Sefa had been twenty-two. The column noted her cause of loss as *cramp*, as if the sea itself were to blame, as if the sea had not been sent for her.

*Petra Voss.* Petra, who had dived beside Tomas Brightwater all those years, who had carried Rosalind on her back through the shallows when the child tired. The entry beside her name was in a smaller hand, added later: *over-depth assignment, refused bonus terms, disciplinary.*

Refused bonus terms. Disciplinary. Sent down deeper than any body should go, as punishment, and written up afterward as weather.

Rosalind stood at the window with forty seconds running through her like a tide going out, and she understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like cold water filling the ears, that these women had not died. They had been spent. Spent the way cargo is spent, deliberately, at rates calculated against premiums, over-dived season after season because the price of shell was high and the price of a diver's life, properly adjusted, was eleven pounds and a blank headstone.

The word *unserviceable* turned over again in her mind and showed her its full underside. Not broken. Used up. Used up on purpose, and then erased — the public book saying four gentle losses to the world, the private book keeping the true slaughter for the insurers, and neither book keeping anything at all for Maren Oduya's mother, who had gone to her grave believing the sea had simply been hungry.

The forty seconds ended. Fenwick came back with the blotting paper. Rosalind thanked him in her flat, pleasant voice and sat down and finished the *Coriander*'s manifests in a beautiful, unbroken hand.

Inside her, something had stopped being quiet forever.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the seawall, because she needed the sound of water and could not have said why. The harbor lay glassy and indifferent in the afternoon light, mackerel-colored, hiding everything. Boats went out. Bells rang. The world performed its ordinariness, and she understood now that the ordinariness was the crime — that the whole shining apparatus of Saltmere, the chapels and counting houses and customs quays, floated on a ledger of the dead so well kept that even grief had been filed and balanced.

Her mother had waited eleven years for news of a man the books had written off in the first week. Had lit the lamp and sat by it. Had died of waiting, quietly, of a slow collapse the doctors called *weakness of the heart*, and the company had sent a condolence card signed by Hargrave himself, with a printed verse about the mercy of the deep.

*They didn't drown my father,* she thought, walking, her hands fisted in her coat pockets. *They drowned my mother too, slower, on paper, with ink. And Maren. And Sefa. And Petra Voss, who taught me to swim.*

By the time she reached the safehouse above the cooper's yard, the grief had burned down to something harder and cleaner. Grief was for things lost. This was not lost. This was hidden, and hidden things can be found, and found things can be shown.

Elias was sitting propped against the wall with his ribs strapped and a pencil stub in his hand, pages of names around him on the floor. He looked up when she came in, and whatever he saw in her face made him put the pencil down.

"You found it," he said. "The second spine."

"I found the reconciliation. Public losses against private. Thirty-one divers in one quarter, against four declared. It runs the same every season, every port." She sat down across from him, still in her coat, unwilling yet to shed the day. "And I found names, Corvane. Not figures. Names. They keep the names in the private book — the real one — and cross-reference them to insurance schedules, like livestock."

She recited them. Maren Oduya. Sefa Ilori. Petra Voss. She recited the annotations — the causes of loss, the adjustments, the initials — and her voice did not waver because she would not let it, but by the end it had gone low and hard as ballast stone.

"They called Petra Voss *disciplinary*," she said. "She wouldn't dive past her depth for extra pay, so they sent her anyway and docked her family's account for the rescue attempt. Eleven pounds, Corvane. That was the price they settled on. My friend Maren is worth eleven pounds and a lie."

Elias was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle in the way of a man handling something that might detonate. "I've seen the same pattern in Calloway Port and Meridian Sound. Different clerks, same hand at the top. It's systemic. That matters, Rosalind — it means this isn't one cruel man. It's a method. Methods can be proven."

"I don't want it proven," she said. "I want it *read aloud*. I want it read aloud in a hall full of people who believed the four."

"It will be. If we get it into the record." He leaned forward, winced at his ribs, and pressed on. "But testimony needs witnesses, and witnesses need protection, and protection needs leverage. We need copies of the private pages themselves. Not memory — memory can be dismissed as a grieving woman's invention. Paper survives. Paper is evidence."

"Then tell me how to get the paper."

He studied her. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds along the cooper's lane, and the light came up the window in soft yellow bars and lay across the floor between them like a plank bridge.

"There's a night inventory," he said slowly. "End of the fiscal quarter — eleven days from now. The Records Office is opened, the folios brought out for audit preparation, and the office is staffed by a skeleton crew because the senior men attend the directors' dinner. One clerk, usually Fenwick, and a door ward."

"And you know this how?"

"I was a company clerk for six years. I inventoried that office myself, before I understood what I was counting." His eyes held hers. "It will require someone inside the building after dark. Someone unremarkable. Someone whose presence raises no alarm."

Rosalind looked at the lamplight on the floorboards, and thought of Maren Oduya rising through green water with her grin and her bubbles, and of her mother at the lamp, and of thirty-one names in a column headed like a shipment of spoiled hides.

"Eleven days," she said. "Then I'll be unremarkable for you one last time."

Elias nodded slowly, but he was watching her with that clerk's precision, cataloguing her the way he catalogued everything, and she saw him see it — the change in her, the thing that had cracked open and would not close again.

"Rosalind," he said carefully. "What you're feeling right now — use it. But don't let it steer. Fury makes divers go down too fast."

"My father taught me to dive," she said, rising, pulling off her coat. "He taught me that fury is ballast. You don't cut it loose and you don't let it drag you. You trim for it." She hung the coat on its nail. "Eleven days. Teach me the office layout tonight. And Corvane—"

She turned at the doorway, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and Elias Corvane later swore to himself that in that moment she looked less like a clerk's shadow than like the sea itself deciding where it would land.

"—when I bring you those pages," she said, "we stop hiding. Whatever comes."

* * *

That night she dreamed of the reef again, but it was changed. The coral shelves stood stacked like ledger pages, row upon row of them, white and numbered, and the water above them was clear as glass, and everywhere she swam there were names carved fine as nacre into the living stone. Maren Oduya. Sefa Ilori. Petra Voss. She swam the rows reading them, reading them, and the water bore her up without effort, and far above her, filtered silver through the fathoms, a lamp was burning.

She woke before dawn with her heart steady and her mind made up, and lay listening to the gulls begin their argument with the morning, and thought: *eleven days.*

Below in the cooper's yard, Elias was already awake. She could hear the scratch of his pencil, writing down names, keeping count.

Keeping the true count — the one the company feared — one name at a time.

And eleven days out, at the bottom of the fiscal quarter, the Records Office waited with its green folios and its skeleton crew, holding the second spine of the Meridian Company's books like a keel of bones beneath a painted hull.

She got up and dressed for work.
