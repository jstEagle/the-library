## 6. Orders Come at Dawn

The drum began before the gulls did.

Rosalind heard it through the floor of the loft she rented above the cooper's yard — a low, patient knocking, like a heart heard through a wall. She lay still in the grey half-dark and counted the beats the way her father had taught her to count her breaths before a dive: not with anxiety but with attention. Four strokes, pause. Four strokes, pause. The requisition drum. She had heard it only once before in her life, when she was nine, and the harbor had emptied of every boat bigger than a skiff.

She was dressed before the fourth repetition ended.

Down in the streets, Saltmere was already assembling itself into the shape obedience required. Lamplighters stood idle at their posts, watching doors open along Rope Walk and Brine Street. Women came out with shawls over their nightclothes, children pressed against their skirts, and everyone faced the same direction — toward the Meridian counting house, where a clerk had climbed onto the steps with a paper in his hand and the particular stiffness of a man reading words he has been told carry no feeling.

Rosalind did not go to the counting house. She went to the water.

It was an old habit, older than sense: when the world announced something terrible, she walked to the shallows and put her feet in it, because the sea never lied about its own weather. The tide was out, leaving the flats gleaming and exposed, ribbed with sand like the inside of a shell. Mist sat on the water in slow columns. She took off her boots, rolled her trousers to the knee, and stood ankle-deep in cold that climbed her bones like a rope.

*All divers registered under Meridian contract,* the clerk would be saying behind her, his voice carrying off the walls in that flat official cadence. *Are hereby conscripted to salvage service. Report to the salvage grounds aboard the* Vespera's Mercy *at dawn bell tomorrow. Failure to report constitutes breach of indenture and forfeiture of—*

Forfeiture. The word had a taste to it, like copper. Forfeit the contract, forfeit the debt, forfeit the small legal personhood the company allowed her — which meant forfeit everything: the right to work any port, the right to rent, the right, eventually, to be anywhere at all without papers explaining why. They could not sell her. That practice had been abolished, mostly, in most places. But they could make her unownable in a way that amounted to the same thing, passed from contractor to contractor like damaged goods, until some far station worked her lungs out at forty fathoms and wrote her down as *unserviceable.*

She knew the word now. Elias had taught it to her three nights ago, tracing it in the private ledger's margin with one grim finger. *Losses — divers, unserviceable.* As if a woman were a rope gone rotten, a pump handle cracked, a hull wormed through. As if she could be written off and replaced by morning.

The mist moved. Somewhere beyond it, the harbor bells rang the hour, and beneath them she could hear the fleet waking — hawsers creaking, the cough of cold engines, men shouting numbers to each other across dark water. A munitions ship had gone down off the shoals eleven days ago, taking its cargo and most of its crew into the green silence. Rosalind had watched the smoke from the drying racks, a black feather standing on the horizon for two days. Everyone in Saltmere had watched it and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. Ships burned. Men drowned. The war ate what it was fed.

Now it wanted divers.

She thought of the wreck as she had heard other divers describe such places: a hull on its side in the dimming light, holds full of shells and powder packed in oiled paper, bulkheads sprung, cargo shifting with every swell like cargo with opinions. Divers went down into ships like that with hooks and hand trucks and their own two lungs, and the company measured what they brought up, and if the measuring came up short, they sent them down again, deeper, longer, colder.

Her father had died — officially drowned, twelve years ago this autumn — on a contract much like the one being read out behind her in the mist. That was the story, anyway. The ledger said lost at sea, storm damage, regrettable. The private ledger, Elias's stolen pages suggested, might say something else entirely.

Rosalind stood in the cold until her feet went numb past feeling, and then she waded out and let the water take her to the waist, because the thinking was better there, the way it had always been better there. Her mother had hated that about her. *You go to the water when you're frightened,* her mother used to say, *like your father, like all of them, as if it will tell you something kind.* It never told her anything kind. But it told her true things, and true things were the only currency she had ever trusted.

What the water told her this morning was simple. If she ran, they would take the loft, the tools, the name she had been permitted to keep. She would become a rumor moving between ports, and rumors could be caught, and caught women could be made examples of. And more than that — worse than that — she would leave Elias alone in Saltmere with his satchel of impossible letters and Hargrave's men asking questions at the archives, and whatever chance existed to put the dead women's names back into the world's accounting would walk out of the city strapped to her own back and die somewhere quiet.

If she stayed, she went onto the wreck.

There was no third door. There rarely was. The company built its houses with exactly as many exits as it intended you to use.

* * *

She found Nettie at the drying racks, because Nettie was always at the drying racks before anyone else, folding nets with quick clever fingers and humming something tuneless. Sixteen, all elbows and salt-scoured braids, the best young diver the flats had produced in a decade, and terrified of exactly nothing except the deep water itself, which she conquered every day with a fury Rosalind recognized from the inside.

"They're calling us up," Nettie said, without looking around. "My aunt woke me with the drum. She says the* Vespera's Mercy* takes us out to the shoals. She says the foreman's a man called Dray, and he broke a diver's wrist at Correnport for surfacing without orders." Nettie folded another length of net, hard, as though the net owed her money. "Are you going?"

"I'm going."

"Everyone's going. Old Marta's going and she's fifty-three and her ears don't clear anymore." Nettie turned then, and her face in the flat morning light was furious and young. "They can't just — it's our *lungs*, Ros. It's not rope. It doesn't come back."

"No," Rosalind said. "It doesn't."

Nettie looked at her for a long moment. Something passed over her face — the beginning of a question, maybe, or the end of one. "You've got that look," she said finally. "That look like you're carrying something heavier than lead. You get it sometimes. I've seen you get it on the boats when the ledgers come aboard."

"I have a heavy head," Rosalind said. "It's a family condition."

Nettie snorted, unconvinced, and went back to her nets, and Rosalind walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, and climbed the stairs to her loft, and took out the pages.

* * *

Elias had copied them fair in a clerk's beautiful hand — he could do nothing else; even his apologies came out looking like contracts. Three nights' work by candle stub, while she kept watch at the shuttered window and listened for the patrol: the public loss figures beside the private ones, port by port, year by year. The gap between them was a wound in arithmetic. In the public books, Saltmere had lost four divers in the war years. In the private ones, thirty-one. Correnport: six public, forty-four private. And column after column marked in that dry administrative shorthand — *over-depth, quota pressure, unserviceable* — the vocabulary of a company describing murder in the language of inventory.

Thirty-one names from her own harbor. She had read them until dawn the first night, reading slowly, giving each one its weight. Sera Quill. Odile Marsh. The Fen sisters, both of them, a year apart. Women whose funerals she had attended as a child, standing barefoot on the shingle while the priest said *the sea gives and the sea takes away,* and the company's agent dropped a coin in each widow's palm and the widows closed their fingers around it because a coin was a coin and grief was free.

She had known, in the way you know a bone is broken before the swelling comes. Now the swelling had arrived. Now she could not close her hand around anything without seeing the columns.

Tonight she sewed.

She sat cross-legged on her bed with the diving coat across her lap — her good coat, oiled canvas, patched at the shoulders where the harness wore through, smelling irreversibly of tar and brine and years. With a sailmaker's needle and waxed thread she opened the inner lining along the left side, the seam her mother had set there years ago and Rosalind herself had restitched twice, and folded the copied pages into a packet wrapped in oilcloth against the wet. Forty-one leaves. The whole falsified architecture of a decade. She slid the packet flat against the lining where it lay over her ribs, the place a diver learns to protect first, and drew the seam closed with small tight stitches, and then she sat for a while with her palm flat against it, feeling paper where a heartbeat should be.

It was the strangest sensation of her life: carrying evidence the way she carried air. Both of them borrowed. Both of them finite. Both of them the difference between coming up and not.

A soft knock at the stair door, three beats, pause, one — their signal, invented in a week of signals. She let him in, and Elias stood dripping in the doorway with the look of a man who has run the last quarter mile, though he would deny it.

"You heard," he said.

"The whole harbor heard."

"Then you know I'll say it anyway." He came in and stood over her, hands flexing at his sides the way they did when the clerk in him wanted a desk that wasn't there. "Don't board that ship. Disappear for six weeks. I can hide you at the mission hospital — Sister Verity owes me — we keep the copies safe, we wait for the commission—"

"And my indenture?" she asked. "And the warrant? And every diver who goes out without me, diving worse because I wasn't there to argue with Dray?" She held his gaze. "You traced these losses across three ports, Elias. Tell me how many of those women ran, and how well running served them."

He didn't answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie, and he was constitutionally incapable of lying badly. He crouched instead so their eyes were level, and after a moment he reached out and touched the coat, lightly, over the hidden packet, the way a man touches a wall to confirm it is solid.

"It should be me going," he said quietly. "It's my evidence. My risk."

"It's my father," she said. "And my harbor. And my lungs, which happen to be excellent." She almost smiled. "Besides — you write like a dream and swim like an anchor. We each serve where we're fitted."

"That is not a reassuring division of labor."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He stayed while she checked the stitching, and tested the coat's weight in her hands, and packed her kit with the economy of someone who has moved often and owned little: two diving knives, the tin of salve for ruptured ears, her mother's rosary she never prayed on and never discarded. At the door he stopped, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the space between them filled with everything the week had not yet found words for.

"Dawn bell," she said.

"Dawn bell." He hesitated. "Rosalind. If the water tells you anything kind down there — listen to it."

"It never does," she said. "But I'll listen anyway."

* * *

She boarded the *Vespera's Mercy* in a rain fine as sifted flour.

The ship smelled of pitch, bilge, and the particular sourness of a vessel recently converted from something respectable — passenger berths ripped out, the scars of the joinery still pale in the stained wood. Thirty-one divers crowded the deck with their kit bags, a silent congregation of the city's least consulted citizens. Old Marta stood near the rail with her kit at her feet and her chin up, daring the sky to comment. The Fen cousins had come together, holding hands like children, aged nineteen and twenty-two. Nettie planted herself at Rosalind's elbow and did not move from it, and Rosalind did not tell her to.

Foreman Dray stood at the masthead ladder with the muster book open on his forearm, calling names. He was a broad, grey man with a voice like gravel poured slowly, and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they touched — faces included. When he reached *Brightwater, Rosalind,* he looked at her a beat longer than the list required, and she met him the way she met the deep water: level, unremarkable, a woman with nothing in her face worth writing down.

"Present," she said.

He made his mark. The gangplank came up. The hawsers went taut, groaning like something waking unwilling, and the harbor began to slide away beneath them — the drying racks, the cooper's yard, the counting house with its blind windows, all of it receding into the rain as though the whole city were being lowered gently underwater, which, Rosalind thought, in a manner of speaking, it had been for years.

Nettie leaned close. "Look at them all," she murmured. "Every one of us with a number now."

"We always had numbers," Rosalind said. "This is just the first time they've said ours aloud."

Ahead, through the rain, the shoals waited — a long low seam of white water over green, and beneath it, eleven days cold, a ship full of powder and iron and drowned men, waiting to be opened by women whose names the company already knew how to write off.

Rosalind buttoned her coat to the throat. Against her ribs, flat and secret, the paper rode with her heartbeat — every page of it a voice, every stitch a vow.

The bell rang. The divers went below to rig their gear.

And the *Vespera's Mercy* turned her bow toward the wrecking ground, into the deepening weather, carrying thirty-one pairs of lungs and one coatful of truth toward the place where the water keeps what it is given.
