## 7. The Salvage Grounds

The *Meridian Grace* was not a graceful ship, whatever her name claimed. She was a converted collier with a scarred hull and a winch that screamed like a gull caught in a door, and she rode at anchor three miles off the Teeth — a line of black rocks the charts marked politely as a hazard and every diver in Saltmere knew for what it truly was: a graveyard with good visibility.

Rosalind stood at the rail on the first morning and watched the wreck come up through the green water. The *Coriander's Pride* had gone down eleven years ago carrying naval munitions to the northern garrisons, and the company had let her lie because salvage was expensive and dead cargo was cheap to write off. Now the war's appetite had changed the arithmetic. There were brass fittings down there, copper sheeting, crates of small arms still sealed in their grease-paper, and above all the powder magazines, which the Admiralty wanted recovered intact before the armistice made them someone else's problem.

The water where the wreck lay was forty fathoms deep in the holds. Rosalind knew the number the way she knew her own heartbeat. Forty fathoms was past the edge of what a woman could do safely even once. The company's answer to that was simple: they would ask each woman to do it many times.

"Names," said Foreman Aldous Cray, walking the deck with his ledger under his arm the way a priest carries scripture. He was a narrow man, grey as driftwood, with eyes that never quite settled on anything living. "When I call your name, you step forward, you take your station, you do your work. When I call it again, you come up. Anyone who comes up without her full quota answers to me."

He read out twenty-two names. Rosalind Brightwater was ninth.

She stepped forward because stepping backward was not among the options the sea offered. Around her the other women did the same: Maren Ostrova with her burn-scarred hands; old Petra, forty-one and diving since she was twelve, whose lungs whistled now when the weather turned cold; the Ferreira twins, who dove in tandem and came up holding hands; and at the end of the line, smallest of all, Nettie Sallow, seventeen years old, who had been assigned to the *Grace* only three weeks ago and who looked at the water the way a lamb looks at a door it has heard wolves behind.

*Nettie.* Rosalind filed the name away without knowing yet why it would matter.

* * *

Diving the Coriander was unlike anything the shallows of Saltmere could have prepared a body for. At thirty fathoms the light went from gold to bruise-green to a darkness that pressed against the eyes like a thumb. The wreck lay on her starboard side, masts snapped, her hull furred with a decade of weed, and her open hatches breathed cold water up at them like the mouths of wells. Inside her were corridors. Inside the corridors were crates lashed with rotting rope, and inside some of those crates was death in dry paper packaging, patient as a spider, waiting eleven years and perfectly willing to wait eleven more.

"You feel the pressure in your teeth first," Petra told Rosalind the second morning, spitting over the side. "Then your ears, if you're stupid about clearing them. Then your chest. After that it stops talking to you and starts giving orders." She tapped her own sternum. "You learn which orders to obey and which to argue with. That's the whole trade, girl. Everything else is just holding your breath."

Rosalind learned fast, because she had always learned fast underwater, where slowness was another word for dying. She learned the wreck's geography by touch: the main hatch, the collapsed ladderway, the corridor the crew called the Throat because it narrowed until a loaded woman had to turn sideways. She learned the sound the hull made when the current swung the anchor chain across it — a long iron groan, like something enormous turning over in its sleep — and how that sound traveled differently through water than through air, arriving in the bones before the ears.

And she learned the quotas.

Cray ran them like a man running a horse toward a cliff edge. The first week the dives went to thirty fathoms, which was hard but survivable. In the second week he raised the target weight per woman per day, and the only way to meet it was deeper — into the lower hold, thirty-five fathoms, then thirty-eight. He kept a slate on the deckhouse wall with each name and a running figure beside it, chalked fresh each evening, and the women came up from their last dive of the day and looked at the slate the way starving people look at bread they cannot reach.

"He'll kill someone," Maren said quietly, on the fourth night, in the fo'c'sle where the divers slept packed like salt fish. She was rubbing liniment into her hands, and Rosalind saw that her fingers would not fully close. "You mark me. It won't be an accident, neither. Accidents need a cause. What he's asking for is arithmetic."

"Then we go slower," Rosalind said. "Come up early. Take the beatings."

Maren gave her a look that was half pity, half something older. "Petra took a beating once. For coming up early." She nodded across the compartment, where Petra lay already asleep, her breathing a thin torn whistle in the lamplight. "She hasn't beaten the deep water since. You don't fight Cray by losing him money, girl. You fight him by staying alive long enough for someone upstairs to notice what he's doing. And nobody upstairs notices anything that happens below forty fathoms."

That was the truth of the salvage grounds, and Rosalind carried it down with her every morning like ballast: the depth itself was a kind of silence. What happened under the water stayed under the water, unrecorded, unwitnessed, dissolving into blue. She thought of ledgers. She thought of entries marked *losses — divers, unserviceable.* She thought, though she tried not to, of Elias Corvane's copied pages sewn flat against her ribs inside her diving coat, riding down with her into the dark like a second heart.

* * *

It happened on the ninth day, at the turning of the tide, when the current through the Teeth ran hardest and everything underwater became a negotiation with forces larger than yourself.

They were working the aft magazine — the one Cray had declared safe after a single inspection, because the manifest suggested its crates had been offloaded before the sinking, and manifests, as Rosalind knew better than anyone alive, were documents written by men who had never been within a mile of the thing they described. The magazine's hatch had buckled inward. To reach the last accessible tier of crates, a diver had to slip through the gap and work by lantern-light in a space barely wider than her shoulders, with ten tons of settled wreckage above her and the whole drowned ship creaking around her like a coffin being built in real time.

Nettie drew the gap.

She was quick and she was small and Cray liked her for exactly those reasons, and she went down without complaint because she was seventeen and did not yet know that fear was allowed to be spoken aloud. Rosalind worked the outer corridor that day, ferrying loads to the lift line, and so she was perhaps eight fathoms from the girl when the sound came.

It was not loud. That was the terrible part. A shift of cargo somewhere above, a crate settling a hand's width, a rope finally surrendering to eleven years of rot — and then a soft, vast, structural exhalation, the sound a ship makes when a little more of her gives up. Through the green murk Rosalind saw the silt bloom outward from the magazine hatch like smoke, and saw the guide rope to the gap go slack, and understood before her mind finished the arithmetic: the gap had closed.

She did not remember deciding to move. Later she would search her memory for the decision and find nothing, only the motion itself — dropping her load, kicking hard along the corridor with her knife already out, cutting loose the safety line rather than taking the seconds to unclip it. The silt was everywhere now, a brown blindness through which her lantern made one weak yellow coin. She found the hatch frame by touch, by memory of yesterday's route, and followed it down and around to where the gap had been.

It was half shut. A crossbeam — part of the fallen ladderway — had dropped across the opening, pinning the bottom edge, and beyond it, in a pocket of trapped air and screaming darkness, she could hear Nettie.

Not words. Just breathing — the fast, shallow, panicked sawing of a girl burning through air that could not be replaced, and beneath it a thin knocking, fists on wood, fists on a hull, the oldest signal there is.

*I'm here. I'm alive. Come.*

Rosalind got her shoulder under the beam. Ten tons of wreckage above; the beam itself perhaps four hundred pounds, wedged at an angle, held by its own jammed geometry and the suction of the silt. She planted her feet against the hatch coaming, filled her chest with the last of her good air, and lifted.

Nothing. The beam ground a quarter inch and stopped.

Her vision began to prickle at the edges. She had spent too long descending; she had maybe ninety breaths left in her, and the first thirty were already spent. Somewhere above, in the sane world, the watch officer was checking his glass and counting names, but no one came down into a magazine collapse; everyone knew the rule. Everyone knew the rule, and the rule was arithmetic, and the arithmetic said one lost diver was cheaper than two.

Rosalind set her shoulder again and thought of nothing at all — no father, no letters, no ledger lines — because the mind that thinks is the mind that hesitates, and she emptied herself down to muscle and will. This time she did not lift. She turned her body, braced her back against the beam, and pushed upward with her legs, the strongest muscles she owned, the muscles that had driven her through ten thousand fathoms of cumulative water, and the beam shrieked along the hatch frame and rose the width of a hand.

A hand was enough. A body can pour through an opening the width of a hand, if the body is desperate enough and the water helps.

She reached through the gap and found Nettie in the blackness — found her by the wild grip of the girl's fingers closing on her wrist hard enough to bruise — and pulled. Nettie came through the way drowning things come through obstacles: bonelessly, violently, all elbows and terror, her mask fogged, her lips grey. Rosalind shoved her up the corridor toward the lift line, toward the light going pale and holy overhead, and kicked after her, and the last thing she saw of the magazine was the beam settling back down into the gap like a jaw closing, unhurried, willing to wait.

They broke the surface together inside the dive bell's cage, and the world was noise and gasping and the mate hauling on the winch, and Rosalind discovered she was holding Nettie's head above the water with one arm and could not remember having decided to do that either.

* * *

Cray wanted it written as a delay. Of course he did.

"A momentary fouling of the working gap," he told the mate that evening, standing at the rail with his ledger, while below them the tide turned and the wreck went on quietly killing anyone careless enough to enter it. "No structural event. The girl dawdled. Lost her bearing in the silt." His pen scratched. "Sallow loses a day's pay for the fouling. Brightwater—" and here his eyes came up, grey and flat as slate, and rested on her for the length of one slow breath, "—loses nothing. Yet."

But the women had seen. That was the thing Cray could not ledger away. They had seen the rope go slack, had seen Rosalind cut her own line, had counted the minutes she stayed down past any sane margin — four minutes, five, six, the mate's face going the color of tallow while the glass ran — and had seen her come up out of the brown water with Nettie Sallow's fist locked in her collar.

That night the fo'c'sle changed around her. Nothing was said, because nothing needed saying. But Maren Ostrova moved her sea-chest so that Rosalind's hammock hung in the draft-free corner by the stove pipe. Someone — she never learned who — left a pair of dry wool socks rolled on her kit bag, and dry wool aboard the *Grace* was currency worth more than the company's scrip. The Ferreira twins, who spoke to almost no one, saved her the last of the coffee from their private hoard, set silently on her kit bag beside the socks, and were asleep before she could thank them.

And Nettie sat down beside her, cross-legged on the deck planks, still smelling of the deep water, and said nothing for a long time. The lamp swayed with the swell. Above them, through the planks, they could hear Cray's boots crossing the deck, back and forth, back and forth, a man pacing over his sums.

"You didn't have to come in after me," Nettie said at last. Her voice was hoarse from the swallowed seawater. "Everyone knows you don't come in after. There's a rule."

"There are a lot of rules," Rosalind said. "Most of them were written by men whose daughters will never see this ship."

Nettie looked at her then — really looked, the way the young look when they are deciding something that will organize the rest of their lives — and whatever she saw in Rosalind's face seemed to settle the question. "My sister dove the Banks run," she said. "Two years back. They told my mother the water took her. Nobody came in after her either." She paused. "Nobody even looked."

The lamp swung. Rosalind felt the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where she kept the names of the women from the second ledger spine — *Ostrov, Ferreira, Sallow* — and she understood, with the slow cold certainty of a diver watching her depth gauge fall, that Nettie Sallow had a sister on that list, or near enough to it, and that this girl's quiet loyalty was now a live thing between them, dangerous and warm, like a coal passed hand to hand.

"Eat," Rosalind said gently. "Sleep. Tomorrow we go down again."

"I know." Nettie lay back in her hammock. Then, in the dark, so softly that Rosalind might have dreamed it: "I won't forget what you did. Whatever happens."

*Whatever happens.* The phrase hung in the fo'c'sle air like fog. Rosalind lay awake long after the girl's breathing steadied, listening to the hull groan beneath her, feeling through the soles of her feet the far-off grind of the anchor chain, and thinking about the pages sewn against her ribs, and about how many other sisters were down there in the company's books, folded flat between columns of figures, marked unserviceable, marked loss.

Three days later, at dawn, a company launch came alongside the *Grace* flying Hargrave's pennant — and Foreman Cray, who had never once in nine days smiled, smiled going down the ladder to meet it.
