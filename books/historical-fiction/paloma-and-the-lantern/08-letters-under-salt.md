## 8. Letters Under Salt

The Saltmere archives smelled of salt and dead men's paperwork.

Elias Corvane had learned to breathe it the way a diver breathes water — in measured pulls, never too deep, never greedy. The records hall occupied the ground floor of the customs annex, a long stone room where the harbor's damp crept up through the floor and curled the corners of everything, so that even the proudest shipping manifests arrived already softening, already halfway to being nothing. Clerks called the smell *letters under salt*. It got into your clothes. It got into your hair. After three days Elias had stopped noticing it on himself and started noticing it on everything else — the bread at the chandlery, the pillow at his rented room, the hand of the archivist who shook it and then looked faintly at his own palm as if something had transferred there that couldn't be washed off.

He worked under a borrowed name now: Corvin, an itinerant copyist engaged to transcribe damaged manifests for the Meridian Company's own legal office. The irony of it was the only warmth he allowed himself each morning — that he sat at a company desk, paid from company funds, correcting company paper with company ink, while the evidence of what the company had done accumulated in his satchel page by stolen page.

The archivist was a woman named Pell, sixty if she was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and eyes that had read every document in the room without ever appearing to look at one. She had been assigned to supervise him. She did not supervise. She drank chicory at her station by the window and let him work, and once, when he'd misfiled a folio out of exhaustion, she had corrected it silently and said only, "Paper remembers where it lives. Best not to confuse it."

He could not decide whether she knew what he was. He suspected she decided this daily, and enjoyed keeping him uncertain.

That third morning he came early, before the official hour, because Hargrave had come late the evening before and Elias wanted the room's rhythms back in his body before the man's shadow crossed the door.

Hargrave. The name alone had weight, like a stone dropped in shallow water. Meridian's fixer — though *fixer* was too kind a word for what he did, which was less repair than burial. Elias had seen him twice since arriving in Saltmere. Once across the customs yard, standing very still among moving men, the stillness itself a form of attention. Once closer, in the doorway of the counting house, asking questions of the door clerk with the unhurried courtesy of a man who already knew the answers and was merely timing how long it took others to arrive at them.

Hargrave wore grey. Not drab grey — deliberate grey, the grey of wet slate, cut well enough to signal money but muted enough to signal that money was none of your business. He was perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples in a way that looked designed, and he had a habit of tilting his head slightly when listening, as though the true content of any sentence lived just below its words, down where divers drowned.

He was looking for someone. Elias knew this the way prey knows it. A clerk had gone missing from the Veyport office — a clerk named Corvane, formerly of the loss-records division, who had asked too many careful questions about too many careful numbers before departing abruptly with certain materials that were not his. The description circulating through the ports was precise in the way company descriptions were: *middling height, dark hair going grey early, writes with his left hand, has a scar on the right thumb.*

Elias had taken to wearing gloves indoors. In summer. In a port city where no gentleman wore gloves indoors. It was the single worst disguise he owned and the best one available, and every day he expected Pell or some junior clerk to remark upon it, and every day nobody did, which frightened him more than commentary would have.

* * *

The morning's work was salvage manifests — the requisition papers for the sunken munitions ship, the *Cormorant's Pride*, whose hull had gone down off the shoals two years past with a cargo the company had insured three times over and mourned exactly once, publicly, in figures rounded generously downward.

Elias copied them with a copyist's blank diligence. And as he copied, he did what he always did: he read.

Not the words on the page. The words beneath. The way a manifest dated the fourteenth of March recorded a delivery of diving line that, cross-checked against the requisition ledger, had actually been drawn down on the second of February — six weeks before the wreck it supposedly equipped. The way the tonnage figures for the same cargo differed between the customs copy and the company copy by eleven tons, eleven tons that existed nowhere and everywhere, eleven tons of insurance money folded into thin air. The way the crew list for the salvage operation included four names that appeared nowhere else in any record Elias had yet touched — names entered, employed, paid, and discharged all within a single fiscal quarter, ghosts hired for the purpose of being ghosts.

Paper lied the way people lied: not outright, but by emphasis. By what it chose to count and what it chose to lose.

He had learned this in Veyport, in the loss-records division, where he had spent six years entering the deaths of divers into columns so neat they looked almost gentle. *Unserviceable. Written off. Loss, diver, one.* As a young man he had believed the language was callousness. He had grown older and understood it was worse than callousness — it was accounting. A diver was capital. Capital depreciated. When depreciation ran ahead of schedule, you adjusted the schedule, or you adjusted the diver, and either way the column balanced, and the column was the truth the company recognized, and anything outside the column was weather.

It had been the handwriting that broke him. That was the thing he could never explain to anyone who hadn't felt it — not the deaths, which were numbers he'd entered by the hundred, but a single letter, surfaced in a bundle of correspondence from the holding stations, written in a hand he recognized because he had copied its owner's earlier reports for years. A diver listed drowned twelve years prior, writing a complaint about his ration allotment. Alive. Complaining about salt beef. The letter had been flagged for destruction — routine — and Elias had pulled it from the burn pile with his heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

Twelve years drowned. Writing letters. To whom? Through what postal marks? Filed under what classification?

He had followed the answer across three ports, and the answer had led here, to Saltmere, to a wounded stranger hiding in a drying shed and a girl with her father's hand in hers.

* * *

Pell arrived at half past eight with her chicory and her silence, and the other clerks trickled in behind her, and the day took up its ordinary noise — nibs scratching, folios thumping, someone two benches down coughing the wet port cough that everyone in Saltmere eventually caught.

At ten o'clock, the door opened, and the temperature of the room changed.

Elias did not look up. This was the discipline: the instinct screamed to look, and the discipline said *write*, and he wrote, his left hand moving steadily under the glove, copying a figure for chain tonnage that he would later verify against three other documents because Hargrave's arrival meant the documents in this room suddenly mattered more than they had an hour ago.

Boots on stone. Unhurried. Pausing here and there — at Pell's station first, naturally; Hargrave was courteous to functionaries the way a surgeon is courteous to skin.

"Madam Pell. Still keeping the empire's secrets dry, I see."

"Damp," Pell said. "Everything in this room is damp. Secrets included."

A low chuckle, genuine-sounding, which was the most dangerous kind. Then the boots resumed, moving along the aisles, pausing behind this clerk and that one, and Elias wrote his chain tonnage and thought about breathing the way Rosalind Brightwater had described it once, briefly, reluctantly, on the second night after he'd collapsed into her life — the old diving breaths her father taught her, pull and hold and release, the rhythm that kept a body calm when the body wanted panic.

Pull. Hold. Release.

The boots stopped behind him.

He felt the attention land on his shoulders like a hand. He kept writing. The figure under his nib was a forgery of attention itself — accurate, plausible, unremarkable — and he finished it, blotted it, squared the page, and turned his head with the mild expression of a man interrupted at dull labor.

Hargrave stood over him with his hands clasped behind his back and his head tilted, reading upside down with the ease of long practice.

"Corvin, isn't it. The copyist."

"That's me, sir."

"Left-handed." Hargrave's gaze rested on the glove a moment too long. "Uncommon, in copyists. Ink smears. Most of them train it out of themselves." A pause calibrated to invite confession. "You didn't."

"My mother's doing, sir. She said a hand is a hand, and shame is optional."

Another chuckle, warmer this time, entirely false. "Mothers." Hargrave straightened. "You've been working the salvage requisitions. Tell me — does the paper strike you as clean?"

The question was a knife offered handle-first. Elias considered it with the care of a man accepting a blade. "Clean as port paper gets, sir. There's drift, here and there. Dates that don't quite sit. But I'm a copyist, not an auditor. Drift is above my pay."

"Drift." Hargrave tasted the word. "Yes. The sea is full of it." He tapped the bench once, lightly, a gesture that managed to be both friendly and proprietary, as if marking Elias's workstation as territory now surveyed. "Carry on, Corvin. Mind the damp. Paper spoils so easily in this climate — one wants to know what's worth preserving before it goes."

He moved on. Elias watched him go in the reflection of his inkwell, a small dark figure in a small dark mirror, and did not resume breathing fully until the door closed behind him.

Pell sipped her chicory. "He doesn't usually talk to the furniture," she observed, to the window.

"He seemed friendly."

"Mm." She turned a page of her newspaper. "Friendly men in grey have ruined better rooms than this one."

* * *

He found the commission notice that afternoon, and it changed the shape of everything.

It was posted — of all places — inside the customs annex itself, a broadsheet tacked to the clerks' board between a tide table and a sanitation decree, so ordinary in its presentation that most eyes slid off it like rain off oilcloth. Elias nearly slid past it too. Then a phrase snagged: *claims arising from losses sustained.* His heart began its slow heavy work before his mind had caught up, and he crossed the room with a copyist's aimless gait and read it three times.

**BY ORDER OF THE ARMISTICE COMMISSION FOR THE MERIDIAN LITTORAL.** The commission would convene in Saltmere during armistice week, at the customs hall, to hear claims and receive testimony *relating to losses of personnel and property sustained in company service during the war period.* Testimony would be received **until the date of signing and no later.** Claims submitted after signature would be referred to the company's own review board for disposition.

Signed by the commission secretary. Stamped with the victors' seal — the interlaced anchors of the Coalition, ink faded from blue toward grey by the same damp that ate everything here.

He read the last clause again. *Referred to the company's own review board for disposition.*

Meaning: after signing, the fox inherited the henhouse ledger and the hens' testimony both. Meaning: whatever wasn't on record before the ink dried on the armistice would never be on record at all. The war would end, the historians would arrive, and they would write their histories from the company's books, because those were the only books that would remain.

He made himself walk back to his bench calmly. He made himself copy another manifest. Inside, arithmetic was running wild — dates, tides, the interval between now and armistice week, which he calculated twice and then a third time because the first two answers refused to be true.

Six weeks.

Six weeks until the window closed. Six weeks to turn a wounded investigator's satchel and a dead man's handwriting and a living ghost's fragmented memory into something the commission would accept — sworn, witnessed, corroborated, *entered*. Six weeks against twelve years of falsified losses, dozens of drowned divers, an island of inconvenient witnesses inside a naval exclusion zone, and a fixer in grey who had already marked him, already tilted his head at a gloved left hand, already used the word *preserve* like a threat wearing a smile.

He thought of Rosalind. Of the way she'd held her father's letter that first night — not reading it, at first, just holding it, the way one holds something hot, something that burns and heals in the same instant. She had spent her whole life learning to carry things: cargo, ledgers, secrets, grief. She would understand immediately what a deadline meant. She had been living under deadlines her entire indenture, measuring her days in what could be carried before the orders came.

But knowing the window existed and getting through it were different trades.

The evidence was scattered. Half his copied pages were sewn into her diving coat, hidden somewhere in the divers' quarter; the originals of Tomas Brightwater's letters were wrapped in oilcloth under a loose plank in his rented room, and every night he expected the plank to be pried up when he returned. The testimony that mattered — Tomas's own account, the living voice that could confirm what paper only implied — was locked behind water and naval patrols on an island they hadn't yet located, in the mind of a man broken by twelve years of being a filed number.

And Hargrave was no longer circling. He had spoken to the furniture. He had asked about preservation. Within the week, Elias was certain, there would be a search of the archives, a quiet audit, a request for the copyist's references — and references, in the world of borrowed names, were the first domino.

Six weeks. Perhaps fewer. Perhaps much fewer, if Hargrave worked faster than the commission.

Elias packed his satchel at the official hour with the unhurried movements of an honest tired man, bade Pell good evening, received her noncommittal nod, and walked out into the harbor light, which was turning gold and bruised the way Saltmere's light did at the end of day, gilding the drying racks and the masts and the far breakwater where the patrol boats crawled like patient beetles.

The city smelled of tar and frying fish and the green rot of the tideline. Somewhere beyond the shoals, Rosalind was diving the wreck of the *Cormorant's Pride*, breathing her father's breaths in her father's sea, carrying his pages sewn against her ribs. Somewhere beyond that, an island held a man who complained about rations because complaining was the last proof of being alive.

Six weeks.

He stopped at the mouth of the lane that led to his room, and for a moment he simply stood in the crowd, a middling man with a scarred thumb and a forged name, feeling the deadline settle over him not as dread but as something almost like clarity — the strange gift of a counted time, in which every wasted hour became visible, every caution became a cost, and the luxury of waiting, which had been his armor for three ports, was gone.

There was no more drifting. The sea was full of it; he was done with it.

Tonight he would map the postal marks. Tonight he would begin pulling the thread that ran from a dead man's handwriting through the dead-drop post offices of the littoral to wherever the company kept its living dead. Tomorrow he would find a way to put a message into Rosalind Brightwater's hands — carefully, through channels Hargrave couldn't watch, because the clock was running now, audibly, like water filling a hull.

He turned down the lane. Behind him the harbor gold went out, and the first lamps of Saltmere lit one by one along the water, small flames against all that dark, each one a wager that light could be posted where it was needed.

Six weeks. He walked faster.
