## 9. The Foreman's Price

The foreman's name was Dree Halloway, and he had hands like cured leather, brown and cracked at the knuckles, that smelled of tar and tobacco no matter how often he washed them. He kept a shed at the edge of the salvage camp with a stove in it and a chair with only three legs and a window that looked out on nothing but the rope racks — a window positioned, Rosalind thought, so that whoever sat in the chair could see men coming toward him from far off and arrange his face accordingly.

She was summoned there on a Tuesday, after the second dive of the day, when her ears still rang with pressure and her fingers pruned white as drowned things. The summons came by way of a boy of perhaps ten whose whole job was carrying messages between sheds, and who looked through Rosalind as if she were weather.

"You wanted me, Mr. Halloway."

"Sit." He did not look up from the slate he was chalking. Figures marched across it in his blunt hand: tonnage, hours, air, the arithmetic of other people's lungs. She sat on the stool by the door because it was the only seat offered, and even the stool felt like an accusation, low and splintery.

For a while he let the silence work. She knew this trick. Overseers used it the way divers used weights — to make you sink faster than you'd meant to. In Saltmere she had watched clerks do it to debtors and debtors do it to their children, and she had long ago learned the countermeasure, which was simply to wait without wanting anything. Wanting was what they smelled. She folded her hands and studied the stove's rusted flank and let him have his silence like a coin he could keep.

At last he set down the chalk. "Brightwater. That's your name."

"It is."

"Rosalind Brightwater. Indenture number—" he squinted at a paper weighted under an inkwell, though she was certain he knew the number by heart; foremen always did, the way butchers knew cuts— "seven-one-four. Six years served. Four remaining."

"Something like that."

"Nothing like anything," he said mildly. "It is what it is. Six years, four remaining, and a body that's held together better than most. You dove the east face twice last week when two others wouldn't go near it. You pulled Nettie Cobb out of a collapse that should've buried her. The men talk about you like you're lucky."

"I'm not lucky. I count."

He almost smiled. It was a thin thing, his smile, more a rearrangement of cracks than any warmth. "You count," he agreed. "That's what I hear. Counts her bottom time, counts her breaths, counts the quota before the quota's announced. Company likes a counter." He leaned back until the three-legged chair complained. "Here's my trouble, Brightwater. Quotas are going up. Munitions ship won't clear itself, and the armistice people are coming, and when they come, Meridian wants that hull stripped bare and every crate logged, because whatever's down there belongs to somebody with a signature. So I need divers. Good ones. Deep ones. And deep ones break."

"Most of them," Rosalind said.

"All of them, eventually. Some just break quieter than others." He turned the slate toward her as though the figures were an argument she might be persuaded by. "Now. There's a way a woman like you doesn't have to break. Shallow shifts. Rope-tending. The tender boat, even — dry boots all winter. A body keeps itself for four more years that way, serves out her indenture, walks off at the end with her lungs intact and maybe a reference in her pocket. That's worth something. Reference from Meridian Salvage opens doors in three ports."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a winch groaned through its gears, hauling up someone's day.

"What's the price," Rosalind said, because there was always a price, and she had learned young that it went easier on everyone if you named it yourself instead of letting them enjoy naming it.

Halloway took his time. He opened the little iron door of the stove and fed in a knot of kindling, watching it catch, and the light moved on his face and made the cracks deeper.

"There's talk on the crews," he said. "Talk about the books. About losses. About who signs for the dead and who profits by the signing. Talk about papers moving between camps." He closed the stove door with his boot heel. "I'm not a cruel man, Brightwater. I don't much care what men say over their supper. But talk costs quotas, and quotas cost me, and when the commission comes asking questions I'll be the one standing in front of it answering for numbers I didn't write. So. Names. Who carries the papers. Who writes the letters. Who's been filling the crew's heads with the idea that the company owes them something beyond their bread."

"And if I don't know any such names?"

"Then you know nothing, and nothing dives deep." He said it without heat, almost gently, which was the worst of it. No rage, no threat shouted — just the plain bookkeeping of it, evil entered into a ledger in a fair hand. "Shallow shifts or deep ones. Your choice, and I'll respect either. But choose tonight, because the rosters post at dawn."

* * *

She walked back along the shore path with the wind coming off the water flat and gray, pushing against her chest like a hand that wanted her to turn around. The salvage camp spread below the bluff: tents and drying sheds, the black ribs of the munitions wreck showing at low tide offshore like something that had died mid-leap. Smoke stood up straight from the cook fires because there was no wind high enough to bend it. Everything looked orderly. That was the trick of camps like this, she thought — from a distance, order; up close, only endurance arranged in rows.

Nettie was mending line by the third fire, her fingers quick despite the burn scars across two of them. She was seventeen and built like a keel, all beam and ballast, with a laugh she spent carefully because laughter cost breath and breath was money here. She didn't look up when Rosalind sat down beside her. That was another camp courtesy: never look too directly at a person coming out of the foreman's shed, in case what they carried needed hiding.

"Well?" Nettie said to the line in her lap.

"He wants names."

"Names of what?"

"Agitators. Paper-carriers. Letter-writers." Rosalind watched the fire. "He's offering shallow water. Tender duty. Dry boots till my indenture runs out."

Nettie's hands stopped. Just for a breath, then started again, but Rosalind had seen the stop. "And you told him—"

"I haven't told him anything yet. Rosters post at dawn."

"Halloway doesn't offer dry boots out of kindness. Kindness isn't in the company stores." Nettie bit the line and worried the fray with her thumbnail. "You say yes, and next month it's not names anymore. Next month it's who spoke at the fire after lights-out. Then who looked at Hargrave wrong. Then it's just anyone Halloway wants gone, and your name's on every one of those slips, and there's no coming back from being that person. Not here. The water forgives more than a crew does."

"I know what I'd be."

"Do you?" Nettie finally looked at her, and her eyes were old in her young face, the way all the divers' eyes were old, salt-scoured. "My mother informed once. Back on Cormorant Cay, when we were small. One name, one time, to keep our hut when the company thinned the village. She never slept right again. Died at forty with her eyes open." She returned to the line. "You're not that. I've dived with you. You're not that. But I'd rather tell you now than mourn you later."

Rosalind said nothing. Below the bluff the tide had begun its slow return, sliding up the shingle with that sound like a long exhale, and she thought of her father's breathing exercises taught to a child too young to need them — *in for the count of the waves, hold for the count of the gulls, out for the count of everything you can't control* — and she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she had been practicing for this moment her whole life without knowing it. The hold. The long hold, where the body screams and you stay anyway, because surfacing wrong kills faster than staying down.

"I'll take the deep shifts," she said.

Nettie closed her eyes briefly, and it might have been grief or relief or both wearing each other's clothes. "Dawn roster will put you on the east face."

"I know."

"They'll work you until your teeth bleed."

"I know that too."

Nettie tied off the mend and sat looking at the fire, and then, very quietly, without turning her head, she said: "If a person needed a letter to get to town — a letter that couldn't go through the company post — the supply sloop comes Thursday. The cook sends ashore for pepper and dried apples because the company food's fit for pigs, and the cook owes me for two seasons of diving his share of quota when his shoulder was bad. A letter could ride inside the pepper order. Nobody searches pepper."

Rosalind turned to look at her properly, and Nettie stared hard at the fire, jaw tight, as if she hadn't spoken at all, as if the words had come from somewhere else entirely and she refused to claim them.

"Nettie."

"Don't thank me. Thanking makes it real, and if it's not real I can stop doing it whenever I want." She stood, brushing sand from her knees, and gathered her coils of line. "Thursday morning. Give it to me wrapped around the pepper list, written small. And Rosalind—" she paused, half-turned, the wind pulling at her cropped hair— "whatever you write to that clerk of yours, write it like a diver. Say the true thing once and get to the surface."

* * *

The worst shifts were worse in exactly the ways she had predicted, and worse besides in ways she hadn't. Dawn found her name chalked at the top of the east-face roster, and the east face was where the wreck leaned deepest into the channel, where the current ran cold enough to ache in the marrow and the light gave out twenty feet down so that divers worked by feel and faith. The foreman's boats put them out at first gray and hauled them in past dark. Between dives they crouched under oilcloth drinking tea brewed from leaves steeped so many times they'd given up pretending to be tea, and nobody talked above a murmur, because Halloway had posted two new watchers among the crews, men from the port with soft hands and hard eyes who laughed at nothing and wrote in little books.

Rosalind dove. She counted her breaths on the way down — *waves, gulls, everything you can't control* — and worked the dark with her hands, prying loose the wreck's crusted fittings, passing up crates whose contents were logged by weight alone because the manifest pages had long since dissolved. On the fourth day a man named Aldous Parr ruptured something in his ear on the descent and came up streaming blood-pink foam, and the tender boat took him away and his quota was divided among the rest before sundown, entered on the slate in Halloway's blunt hand as though tonnage were a fluid that merely sought its level.

On the fifth night, she wrote the letter.

She wrote it by the cook fire with her knees drawn up and the paper on her thigh, using a pencil stub Nettie had charred sharp, writing small as seed grain, in the cramped cipher of shorthand her father had taught her for keeping dive logs private — depth and time and bottom conditions rendered as symbols no counting-house clerk would bother deciphering. To anyone who snatched it from her, it would read as a girl's idle marks, a sampler half-learned. To Elias, who had spent months learning the divers' trade from the inside of their records, she trusted it would read as what it was:

*East face, five days, quotas raised twice. Watcher named Quill asks about letters and gets smiles. Halloway trades exemptions for informers — I refused; expect worse. Commission must know the rosters are built to spend us before the signing. The wreck holds crates marked with the company's own broad-arrow. We are salvaging their secrets for them. Tell me if Tomas's testimony mentions the *Vespera*. It is the ship we're breaking apart.*

She wrapped the paper around the pepper list, sealed it in wax scraped from a candle end, and passed it to Nettie at dawn without either of them looking at the other, the way you pass contraband or a live coal, eyes elsewhere, hands steady.

Thursday the supply sloop came and went. Saturday a fisherman who traded with the camp sold Nettie three mackerel and, folded inside the wrapping, delivered a scrap of oilcloth with writing on its tarred underside. Nettie brought it to her at the rope racks, and Rosalind walked to the tideline to read it alone, standing where the water could take her if anyone approached.

Elias's hand, hurried but even:

*Understood. Vespera confirmed — she was listed publicly as lost with all hands off Corren Shoals, yet her crates surface daily in your quota tallies. That discrepancy alone is a hearing's worth. Keep diving, keep counting, keep breathing. The clock runs six weeks. Do not let them spend you before I can spend these papers. — E.*

P.S., smaller, pressed hard into the tar: *Your shorthand has improved. Tomas would be proud. Forgive the presumption. I only mean that pride is owed somewhere, and I am collecting debts on his behalf until he can collect his own.*

She read it twice, then a third time, and the third reading was the dangerous one, because by then the words had stopped being intelligence and started being a voice, and the voice had stopped being Elias's and become something warmer that she had no ledger entry for. She folded the oilcloth small and pushed it deep into the lining of her diving coat, where the copied pages already rode sewn against her ribs, and she stood a moment looking at the water with her heart doing something unprofessional and uncounted.

Then she walked back up the shingle to the fires, to the lines and the slates and the watchers with their little books, and put her face on like a diving mask: sealed, clear, holding.

Sunday's roster posted at dawn. Her name was at the top of the east-face column again — and beneath it, in fresh chalk, a notation she had not seen before, a single mark Halloway had begun adding beside certain divers' names, small as a tick, ordinary as a tally. She asked Nettie what it meant. Nettie looked at it a long moment, and her face went careful and pale.

"That's the deep-water mark," she said. "They put it on anyone they mean to run past the safe tables. Past the point where a body comes back up its own self." She lowered her voice to almost nothing, under the sound of the tide. "There's a boat leaving for the commission's advance office in Saltmere on Wednesday. If your clerk means to use what we send him — he'd best start using it fast."
