## 10. Hargrave Comes Aboard

The steamer came up the channel on a morning of flat grey water, and every diver on the *Meridian Star* knew what it meant before anyone said it aloud. You could tell trouble by the way it moved through the sea — a naval vessel cut the water clean and went about its business; a company ship came slow and heavy, riding low with its own importance, trailing a wake that flattened the swell like a hand pressed down on a table.

Rosalind was in the shallows, checking Nettie's line, when she saw it. The hull was black. The funnel wore the Meridian colors — deep green, a white star — but the black hull was new, painted since the last time any supply boat came out to the salvage grounds. Ships did not get repainted for deliveries. Ships got repainted for inspections.

"Line's good," Nettie said, then followed Rosalind's gaze out across the water. "That's not the *Coriander*."

"No."

"That's not a supply boat at all."

"No." Rosalind wound the slack around her fist, once, twice, feeling the wet rope bite into her palm. "Get your coat. Get everyone above decks and looking busy. And Nettie—" She caught the girl's shoulder before she could turn. "Whatever happens today, whatever anyone asks you, you've never carried a letter anywhere in your life. You've never seen me write anything but dive logs. Do you understand me?"

Nettie's eyes were very wide and very dark, and beneath the fear in them Rosalind saw something else — the same thing she had seen the night the girl told her about her sister, that hard little seed of fury that some griefs plant instead of drowning. "I understand," Nettie said. "I'm not the one who'll break first."

"I know," Rosalind said, and found that she meant it. "That's why I'm telling you."

The satchel of pages — Elias's copies of the double ledgers, the manifests marked *losses, divers, unserviceable*, the letters with their postal marks decoded and annotated in his small clerk's hand — was sewn into the lining of her diving coat, along the spine where the canvas was thickest, where a seamstress's repair would look like nothing more than what it appeared: mending. She had done the sewing herself by lamplight over four nights, taking the stitches so fine they might have been made by the coat's original maker. It weighed almost nothing against her back when she wore it. It weighed everything when she thought about it, which was constantly, which was now, as the black-hulled steamer dropped anchor two hundred yards off and began lowering a launch.

She put the coat on.

* *

The man who came up the boarding ladder was smaller than Rosalind had imagined, and this frightened her more than a large man would have. Hargrave. She knew the name the way the divers knew weather — from Elias's warnings, from the careful omissions in his stories, from the way he said it without saying it, *there is a fixer in Saltmere whose business is making things disappear, and I am among the things*. She had built him tall in her mind, broad, loud. The man standing on the deck of the salvage barge wringing harbor water from the hem of an excellent grey coat was slight, silvering at the temples, with the mild, patient face of a schoolmaster and hands that never stopped moving — adjusting his cuffs, touching the rail, resting briefly on the shoulder of the foreman, Brannock, who had come to meet him sweating.

"Mr. Hargrave," Brannock said. "We weren't told—"

"You're never told," Hargrave said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of me." He looked out over the deck with an air of gentle appraisal, the way a man looks at livestock he does not intend to buy. Divers stood along the rails in their salt-stiffened coats, forty women and men trying very hard to be furniture. "Productive operation. Quotas met?"

"Exceeded, sir. Even after the storm delays."

"The storm delays." Hargrave smiled at something private. "Yes. We heard there was sabotage. Terrible business. Two arrests, I understand." He turned his mild face toward the divers and let his gaze travel along them, unhurried, row by row, the way a ledger travels down a column. "You all work very hard. The Company values you. I say that sincerely." He paused. "Which is why we take such care with our records. Every loss accounted for. Every name in its place."

Rosalind felt the words go through the assembled divers like cold water through cloth. Beside her, Nettie had gone very still.

Hargrave's eyes kept moving. They reached Rosalind, rested there a moment — a moment too long, or perhaps exactly long enough — and moved on.

"Mr. Brannock," he said, "I'd like to see the manifest office. And then I'd like to speak with whoever keeps your dive logs. The senior hand. Someone thorough."

Brannock's eyes flicked sideways, and Rosalind understood, with a sensation like the moment a weighted line goes taut, that she had been named already. That her reputation for exactness — cultivated for twelve years as armor, as camouflage, as the whole architecture of her invisibility — had been passed up the chain as a recommendation.

"Rosalind Brightwater keeps the logs," Brannock said. "Best hand I've got."

"Brightwater." Hargrave said the name the way a man tastes wine. "Well. Let's meet her properly."

She stepped forward before her stillness could be read as anything worse than composure. "Sir. Rosalind Brightwater."

"So you are." He studied her openly now, and she let herself be studied, keeping her breathing in the old pattern her father had taught her in the shallows when she was six years old — four counts down, seven held, eight released — the rhythm that slowed a heart in cold water and, she discovered, slowed one in front of company inspectors too. "They tell me your records are exemplary. I do love an exemplary record. So much easier than the other kind." He gestured toward the aft cabin. "Shall we?"

* *

The manifest office was a closet of a room smelling of lamp oil and mildew, one desk, one shelf, one porthole through which the grey light came sideways. Hargrave sat on the single stool himself and left her standing, which was deliberate; she recognized the technique because she had spent her life watching powerful people arrange rooms.

He took her dive log from Brannock's hand and read it. He actually read it — not skimming, not performing, but reading, his finger moving down the columns of depths and durations and recovered tonnage, pausing here and there.

"Your arithmetic is very good," he said.

"Thank you, sir."

"It would have to be. Your father was Tomas Brightwater, was he not? Chief diver. Pearl grounds, before the war."

The room went narrow. She kept her voice level by holding it the way one holds breath. "Yes, sir. He was lost when I was eleven."

"Mm." Hargrave turned a page. "Lost. Yes. There's a great deal of 'lost' in these waters." He looked up at her then, and for the first time the schoolmaster mildness shifted, and underneath it she saw the thing itself, plain and patient and interested. "You copy manifests at the counting house, I'm told. Before your conscription."

"I did copying work, sir. Shipping manifests, cargo lists."

"Did you ever handle loss records?"

"No, sir."

"No?" He set the log down and folded his small hands upon it. "It's only that someone has been handling them. In three ports now, records have been consulted that had no business being consulted. A clerk's fingerprints, figuratively speaking, on drawers that were locked for good reason. And those drawers contained, among other things, the loss schedules for the pearl fleet. Divers' names. Your father's name appears in several of them, Miss Brightwater. Posthumously, of course." He smiled again, gently. "Or should I say, allegedly posthumously? The paperwork is ambiguous. Paperwork usually is. That's its charm, and occasionally its danger."

Her heart was doing something dangerous, hammering against the count, breaking rhythm, and she brought it back the only way she knew — down into the water, into the old breaths, four and seven and eight, the cold green quiet under the surface where panic was a luxury the drowned could not afford.

"I wouldn't know anything about that, sir," she said. "I keep dive logs. Depths and hours. The dead don't need logging twice."

Something flickered in Hargrave's face — appreciation, possibly, or its close cousin, appetite. "No," he agreed. "They don't. Which is why it's so curious when someone tries." He rose from the stool, smoothing his coat. "I'll want to inspect the wreck operations this afternoon. You'll accompany me. You can explain the methodology. Consider it an honor — the senior hand showing a director of the Company how her work is done."

"I'm not senior, sir. Just thorough."

"Thoroughness is seniority," Hargrave said. "Everything else is noise." At the door of the little office he paused, half-turned, and said, without looking at her, in a tone of perfect pleasantness: "There was also, I'm told, a stranger seen near the drying racks in Saltmere some weeks ago. Wounded. Carrying papers. The patrol searched the quarter and found nothing, which I find either reassuring or extremely interesting. You were working the racks that morning, I believe."

The silence stretched. She measured it — too short would seem rehearsed, too long would seem afraid.

"I see forty people a day come through those racks, sir," she said. "Wounded ones too. The war's full of them. If one of them was carrying papers, I didn't stop to read them. Reading gets you noticed."

Hargrave laughed — a real laugh, brief and dry, startling in its smallness. "Oh, I like you, Miss Brightwater. 'Reading gets you noticed.' Write that down somewhere safe." He stepped out onto the deck into the grey light. "This afternoon, then. Wear your coat. It looks cold on the water."

* *

She wore the coat. Of course she wore the coat — refusing it would have been the loudest answer possible, and the pages rode against her spine with every step she took across the deck behind him, forty sheets of paper that weighed less than a gull's feather and could hang every diver on this barge if they were found.

The afternoon inspection was a performance, and both of them knew their parts. Hargrave watched the winches with professional detachment, asked intelligent questions about lift capacities and decompression times, nodded over Brannock's answers, and made notes in a small black book with a pencil worn to a stub. Twice he asked her to repeat a figure. Twice she gave it identically. She showed him the wreck grid — how the munitions ship lay broken across the sand at forty fathoms, how the divers worked in shifts along the collapsed hold, how the quotas were set and met and exceeded.

"Forty fathoms," Hargrave murmured, looking down at the green-black water as though he could see through it. "And the Company thanks them by name, I hope. The survivors."

"There's a board, sir. Names go up when dives are logged."

"And the others?"

She knew what he was asking. He knew she knew. The afternoon light was going coppery, and the water below the rail held no answers, only depth.

"The others are in the ledgers," she said.

"Yes," Hargrave said softly. "Everyone is in the ledgers. That's rather my whole life's work, Miss Brightwater — making sure of it."

Then a diver came up from the third shift screaming, and the performance ended.

* *

It was Nettie. Not hurt — snagged, her line fouled on a spar of twisted iron in the collapse zone, hauled up fast when she signaled, coughing blood-flecked foam into a basin, her hands shaking so badly two women had to hold the bowl. The foreman wanted her back down inside the hour; the quota was short. Hargrave stood at the rail and watched the whole scene with his head tilted, like a man at a play he had seen before and enjoyed.

"She'll dive or she won't," Brannock said to no one. "Quota doesn't wait."

"She nearly died just now," Rosalind said. She hadn't meant to speak. Her mouth had gone ahead of her, the way it sometimes did when the water rose too high. "Another ten minutes down there and her line fouls tighter, her air runs short, and you log another unserviceable loss."

The word was out before she could weigh it. *Unserviceable.* Brannock stared at her. Hargrave turned, very slowly, and the mildness on his face had become something else entirely — not anger, nothing so useful as anger, but the focused delight of a collector finding the specimen he had been promised existed.

"'Unserviceable,'" he repeated. "Now that's an interesting term from a diver's mouth. That's a ledger term. That's a counting-house term." He came closer, and up close his eyes were pale grey, the color of the water before a squall. "Where did you learn that word, Miss Brightwater?"

"On the manifests I copied," she said. "Cargo gets marked unserviceable when it's written off. I used the wrong word. Forgive me, sir — divers aren't cargo."

"Aren't they?" Hargrave said, and smiled, and the smile was the truest thing he had shown all day. Then he straightened, buttoned his coat, and became the schoolmaster again, addressing the deck at large. "Shift change. The girl rests until morning. The quota adjusts. The Company is generous — within reason." He gathered his black book, nodded to Brannock, and paused beside Rosalind on his way to the ladder, close enough that she could smell bay rum and paper dust. "You've been very helpful today. Thorough people always are, whether they mean to be or not. Give my regards to your father, if the sea returns him. Stranger recoveries have happened." He descended the ladder. "Stranger ones."

* *

The launch pulled away into the dusk, its lantern swinging, growing small, going out of sight beyond the headland. The divers dispersed to their berths in the ordinary silence of exhausted bodies. Rosalind stood at the rail a long time, running the day back through herself like line through a block, hunting for the snag.

He had come aboard hunting Elias. That was certain — the wounded stranger, the drying racks, the questions dressed as pleasantries. He had left without finding what he came for. That should have been a victory, and for one hour she let herself feel it as one.

But then she went below, took out the day's log to make her evening entries, and opened the book to the previous week to check a figure, and found her own handwriting recording the shift roster for the morning Hargrave's steamer must have been chartered — and beside it, in the column where she noted personnel, her own notation: *B., R. — counting-house detail prior, familiar w/ manifest formats.*

She had written it weeks ago, routine, invisible, the kind of note a thorough log-keeper makes. But Hargrave had read her log. He had read it the way he read everything — completely — and he had known her father's name before Brannock spoke it. Had asked about her copying work. Had said *your father's name appears in several of them* — several loss schedules, across ports, the very documents Elias had traced — and then had watched her face while he said it.

He hadn't come aboard only hunting Elias. He had come to look at her. To stand close enough to smell fear, or its absence. To leave a message wrapped in courtesy: *I know who you are. I know whose daughter you are. I know what the ledgers say about him. Show me what you do with that.*

And she had given him *unserviceable* — a word no honest dive-keeper needed, a thread pulled loose from the hidden spine of the second ledger, handed to him free of charge in a moment of anger over Nettie's bleeding mouth.

Rosalind closed the log. Above her, boots crossed the deck; the winch creaked; the sea worked patiently at the wreck below, undoing iron the way it undid everything eventually.

Fear sat in her chest like swallowed seawater. But underneath the fear, settling slowly, cooling, hardening, was the other thing — the thing the water taught every diver sooner or later: that panic kills faster than pressure, that the only way out of depth is methodical, that you ascend by stages or you die on the way up.

Very well, then. Stages. Tonight she would get a message to Elias through Nettie — Hargrave sails back on the morning tide; the window is tomorrow. Tomorrow Elias would move the archive copies out of the port before the counting house opened. And she would begin, quietly, systematically, to erase herself from her own meticulous records — the notations, the cross-references, twelve years of thoroughness turned against its keeper — because Hargrave had just taught her the most valuable lesson of her invisible life:

A ledger only betrays you if it's accurate.

She dipped her pen, and began to falsify her own history, line by careful line, and found that her hand did not shake at all.
