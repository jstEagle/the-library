## 11. What My Mother Carried

The path to the burial ground above Saltmere was not a road so much as a habit — a track worn into the hillside by generations of feet that had nothing left to carry but grief. Rosalind climbed it in the grey hour before the company's bells rang the morning shift, when the harbor below lay still as poured pewter and the gulls had not yet begun their arguments. She carried a bundle of white flowers cut from the cliff ledges where they grew wild, salt-hardened and stubborn, blooming in soil that should have refused them.

Her mother's grave sat at the eastern edge of the yard, where the first light came off the water. Maren Brightwater had chosen it herself, years before she needed it, telling the sexton with her usual practicality: *Put me where I can watch the boats come in. Someone in this family ought to know when things return.*

Rosalind knelt on the grass, which was damp and smelled of thyme gone feral between the stones, and began pulling the weeds from around the marker. The stone was cheap granite, its letters softened by twelve years of weather until her mother's name read almost like a suggestion: MAREN, WIFE OF TOMAS. No dates for Tomas. The sexton had offered to add one after the drowning, and her mother had refused with a flatness Rosalind had never heard from her before or since.

"He isn't dead," Maren had said. "Dead is a fact. This is only paperwork."

Rosalind laid the flowers against the stone and let her fingers rest on the carved letters, tracing the groove of the M the way she had once traced the letters of her first primer.

* * *

Her mother had carried things. That was the word Rosalind always returned to when she thought of her — carried, the way divers spoke of ballast, something taken on deliberately to keep you down where the work was. Maren Brightwater had carried the household accounts in a head that could multiply faster than any clerk at the counting house; carried mending for three other families besides her own because her stitches were the finest in the quarter; carried, folded small in the pocket of her apron, the letter that had started everything.

It had come on a Tuesday in the season of the red tide, eleven months after the Meridian Company's boat went down with Tomas aboard. Eleven months of Maren rowing out past the breakwater every Sunday to look at the horizon, coming back with her face arranged into patience. Eleven months of Rosalind, then nine years old, learning to read her mother's silences the way other children learned scripture.

The letter was from the company. It was very short. It expressed regret, confirmed the finding of death by misadventure, enclosed a settlement sum that would not have covered the cost of the funeral it presumed, and closed with the signature of a junior clerk whose name meant nothing to anyone. Maren had read it standing in the doorway, the light behind her, so Rosalind could not see her face — only the way the paper trembled, once, like a fish taking the hook.

Then she had folded it along its original creases, put it in her apron pocket, and said, "We'll want more lamp oil this week. The nights are getting longer."

She never wept where Rosalind could see. Not then, not in the two years that followed, not on the day the company's men came to appraise the house for debt and found nothing worth taking but the girl. On that day Maren had straightened Rosalind's collar with hands that smelled of salt and lye and said, "You'll be clever. Cleverer than me. I waited on paper, and paper lied."

And then, quieter, in the voice she used for things too heavy to say aloud: "Don't hope, little fish. Hope is what they leave you instead of the truth. It costs them nothing and it eats you whole."

Maren had died four years later — the cough, the winter, the ordinary arithmetic of poverty — and Rosalind had stood at this grave at fifteen years old and made the vow with the same solemnity other girls gave to marriage: *I will never wait for anything. I will never hold a door open in my heart for someone who is not coming through it. What is lost stays lost, and I will spend my life carrying what can be carried, and nothing else.*

She had kept the vow for seven years. It had been easy, mostly. Invisibility was a discipline, and discipline suited her. She did not keep photographs. She did not reread old letters. When the women of the diving quarter lit candles for their drowned each year on the tide-turning, Rosalind worked late at the counting house, copying figures, letting the numbers fill her up the way sand fills an empty shell.

And then a man with wet boots had fallen at her feet carrying a satchel of letters, and one of them had been dated three years after the sea supposedly took her father, and the vow had cracked down its length like ice under a boot heel.

* * *

"Your mother had good taste in views."

Elias's voice came from behind her, careful, the voice of a man announcing himself to someone who startled easily these days. Rosalind didn't turn. She'd heard him coming — he still walked like a clerk, heel-first, apologetic about the ground — but she let him cross the grass and settle himself a few feet away before she spoke.

"You followed me."

"I noticed you leaving before dawn and I worried." He said it plainly, without apology, which was somehow worse than if he'd dressed it up. "Hargrave's people watch the quarter now. A woman walking alone toward the burial ground at first light is either grieving or meeting someone. Either way, worth following."

"And which do you think it is?"

He considered the question honestly, which was another thing about him she hadn't decided whether to forgive. "Both," he said. "I think you came to tell her."

Rosalind's hands stilled on the weeds. Below them the harbor was waking — the first lighters moving out to the anchored hulls, smoke rising thin from the galley stacks, the great shape of the salvage ship riding at its moorings like a patient animal. Somewhere down there were ledgers with her handwriting in them, and pages sewn into her diving coat with her father's words, and a clock ticking toward an armistice that would seal all of it away forever if they failed.

"I don't tell her things," Rosalind said. "I visit. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"My mother believed my father was alive. For two years she believed it, on no evidence at all, against everyone's certainty, and it killed her by inches — not the believing, but the waiting. The hoping." She pulled a weed free and held it, roots dripping earth, without looking at it. "I swore at this grave that I would never do that. Never wait. Never hope. And now there's a letter in your satchel that says everything she believed was true, and I don't know how to carry that, Elias. I know how to carry cargo. I know how to carry secrets. I don't know how to carry hope. It doesn't have a weight. You can't balance it against anything."

Elias was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the investigator's precision.

"My sister hoped," he said. "After our brother went down on the *Cormorant*, she wrote to the company every month for six years. Petitions, inquiries, requests for the crew manifest, the wreck report, anything. Every month, a reply: *the matter is closed.* She died still writing them. The last petition was found on her desk, sealed, stamped, ready to post." He looked out at the water. "I became a clerk because I thought paper was neutral. I spent ten years learning it isn't. Paper is a weapon, and the company holds it two-handed. Your father's letters are the first time I've seen it turned around."

"That's why you do this. The investigating. Her name was—"

"Sefa." He said it the way people say names they've said to no one in a long time, testing whether it still works. "Sefa Corvane. She's not on any record anywhere. That's part of why. The company erased the *Cormorant*'s losses so cleanly that even the families' grief has no paperwork. You can't mourn officially if officially nothing happened."

The wind moved through the burial ground, stirring the white flowers against the granite. Rosalind looked at her mother's name and felt the strange double weight of it — the woman who had been right, and the woman who had been destroyed by being right, both of them hers, both of them unreachable.

"She used to sing while she mended," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself. "Old diving songs, from her mother. Songs about holding your breath and trusting the line. I haven't thought of them in years. I thought I'd forgotten them entirely, but I was in the shallows last night, practicing the breathing, and one came back whole. Every verse." Her throat tightened. "That's what memory does. It keeps what you didn't ask it to keep."

"Yes," Elias said. "It does."

She finally turned to look at him. He was sitting with his forearms on his knees, hat in his hands, and the early light caught the scar along his jaw — the wound that had brought him stumbling into her life among the drying racks, bleeding and impossible. He was watching the harbor with an expression she recognized, because she wore it herself most days: the look of someone counting exits.

"Elias." His name in her mouth felt different than it had a week ago. "Why did you really follow me?"

He didn't answer immediately. A gull dropped onto the wall at the edge of the yard, regarded them with one yellow eye, and thought better of it.

"Because last night, when we finished cataloguing the letters, you rolled them and tied them exactly the way the company ties dispatch bundles — the knot, the tension, all of it. And I realized I've been thinking of you as the daughter of a victim. A witness. Evidence that walks." He met her eyes. "But you're not evidence. You're the most capable person I've ever worked alongside, and I've worked alongside people who ran ports. And I find that I—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully. "I find that when you're not in the room, I notice."

The words hung in the salt air between them. Rosalind felt her heart do something she had trained it never to do — reach. Not for safety, not for alliance, not for the cold comfort of shared purpose, but for the man himself, for the specific gravity of him sitting three feet away on her mother's grass.

"Don't," she said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't be kind to me here. Not here." She gestured at the grave, at the yard, at the whole hillside of small stones. "This is where I come to remember what waiting costs. My mother waited and it hollowed her. If I let myself hope — for my father, for anything — and it goes wrong, I won't survive it the way she didn't survive it. I'm not built with reserves, Elias. I've spent everything just staying invisible this long."

"I'm not asking you to hope," he said. "I'm asking you to let me stand next to you while you refuse to."

Something in her chest shifted — not the crack of the vow breaking, but something gentler and more frightening, like a knot loosening that she had tied so long ago she'd forgotten it was there. She looked at him and he looked back, steady, asking nothing, offering exactly what he'd said: presence, not promise.

Below them, the company's bells began to ring the morning shift, brazen and indifferent, and the light over the harbor turned from pewter to gold.

* * *

They walked down together, not touching, close enough that their sleeves brushed twice on the steep places and neither of them moved away. At the fork where the burial path met the coast road, Elias stopped.

"The archive again today," he said. "There's a requisition ledger from the year of the *Cormorant* I haven't seen. If Hargrave's clerks refiled the shipping schedules, I need to know before the commission convenes." He hesitated, and then added, as though the thought had been waiting all morning for its courage: "Will you come to the grave again? While all this lasts?"

"Every seventh day," Rosalind said. "It was her rule. Visit or don't, but if you visit, mean it."

"Then I'll mean it," Elias said. "On the seventh day."

He went on ahead down the coast road, his stride still a clerk's stride, and Rosalind stood a moment longer looking back up the hill at the small white smudge of flowers against the dark stone.

*Maren,* she thought. *He isn't dead. Dead is a fact. This was only paperwork — and I am going to make the paperwork tell the truth.*

She turned toward the harbor, toward the bells, toward the salvage ship and the sewn pages against her ribs, and it was only halfway down the path that she understood what had settled between her and Elias on the hillside, unnamed and unasked: not hope, which her mother had warned her against, but something older and harder to kill.

Trust, planted in the one place she had sworn nothing would ever grow.

And trust, unlike hope, could be betrayed — which was why, when she reached the bottom of the path and saw the company launch standing offshore of the salvage grounds, flying the pennant of an official inspection, her first thought was not of the ledgers or the letters or the armistice clock.

It was of Elias, walking alone down the coast road toward the archives, visible as a wound, and Hargrave's men already asking questions at the harbor gate.
