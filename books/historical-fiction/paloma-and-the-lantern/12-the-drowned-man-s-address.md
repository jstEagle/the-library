## 12. The Drowned Man's Address

The letter had been in the satchel for three days before Elias understood what it was.

He had read it a dozen times, of course — the way he read everything now, twice, three times, hunting the second meaning beneath the first. It was an ordinary piece of correspondence by any measure. A merchant's note, dated fourteen months past, addressed to a factor in Saltmere regarding the price of copra oil. The handwriting was cramped and commercial. The content was dull enough to put a customs officer to sleep, which was precisely its genius, if it was genius at all and not simply his own exhaustion reading patterns into blank paper.

But the postal marks would not leave him alone.

Elias Corvane had spent eleven years as a company clerk before he became something else, and in those eleven years he had learned that paper was never innocent. Paper was a body that remembered everything done to it. It carried the pressure of hands, the humidity of rooms, the route of its own travels pressed into it like footprints in mud. Most men saw only ink. Elias had been trained to see the rest.

So on the third night, sitting in the back room above the chandlery where Rosalind had hidden him, with a single lamp turned low and the harbor breathing outside the shuttered window, he laid the letter flat on the table and stopped reading the words.

* * *

The marks were there, faint as old scars. A receiving stamp near the top left corner, half-struck, the sort applied when mail passed through a company sorting office rather than the civil post. Below it, two smaller impressions: a date ring, and what appeared to be a routing code — four characters, hand-stamped, the kind used on internal company routes where no public carrier went.

The date ring was legible: 14th of the Drying Month. The routing code was not. The stamp had been applied crookedly, the letters overlapping, and whoever had handled the letter had done so quickly, or carelessly, or in poor light.

Elias fetched the lamp closer. He tilted the page against the light. He breathed on it, watching the moisture deepen the older impressions while the fresher ink resisted — a trick an archivist in Vellamare had taught him, one of the few useful things he'd carried out of that burning office besides his life.

Three of the four characters resolved. An M. A numeral, either 7 or 1. And what might have been a K or an H.

The fourth character sat directly over the fold of the letter, crushed by years of being carried folded, and refused to give itself up at all.

He sat back. Outside, a patrol boat's lantern swept the water, and he counted the seconds out of habit — eleven, twelve, thirteen — until the light moved on. Hargrave's people were searching the harbor district nightly now. They had come to the chandlery once already, asking after a clerk who'd gone missing from Vellamare, and the chandler, a broad silent woman named Ossa who took payment in silence as well as coin, had told them nothing because she had seen nothing, because Elias existed only after dark and through the trapdoor to her cellar.

Rosalind came at moonrise. She always came at moonrise, slipping up the outside stair with the soundless competence of someone who had spent her life moving through spaces owned by other people. Tonight she carried a wrapped bundle that smelled of fried plantain and salt fish, and she set it down without ceremony, the way she did everything, as though kindness were just another form of cargo to be delivered without comment.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I've been reading a letter about copra oil."

"For three days."

"It's a very interesting letter." He turned it toward her. "Not the words. The marks."

She pulled a crate close and sat, and he watched her do the thing she always did first — scan the room, the window, the door, cataloguing exits. She had told him once, in one of her rare unguarded sentences, that her father had taught her to check a room the way other fathers taught daughters to pray. The thought arrived and then, as always, he set it aside carefully, because thinking about Tomas Brightwater too long made his hands unsteady, and unsteady hands were useless tonight.

"Company sorting stamp," he said, tapping the corner. "Internal route. Which means this letter didn't travel by civil post at any point. It traveled on Meridian vessels, through Meridian offices, the whole way."

"So?"

"So look at where it started." He slid the letter across. "Read the sender's mark. Not the name — the location line."

She bent over it. In the lamplight her face went sharp and still, the way it did when her mind was working, all the softness packed away somewhere deep. "'Caret Bay Depot,'" she read. "That's nothing. There are a dozen depots—"

"On the mainland coast, yes. On shipping lanes, yes. But Caret Bay isn't on any lane I ever routed cargo through, and I routed cargo through everything Meridian owned for eleven years." He let that land. "I checked the fleet registers. No scheduled vessel calls at Caret Bay. No tender service. No listed warehouse. The depot exists in the company's correspondence and nowhere else."

Rosalind looked up slowly. "A place that receives mail but no ships."

"A place that receives mail on company boats that officially aren't going there." He heard his own voice drop, the way it did when he was afraid and trying not to be. "You know what that is? That's how you run a place you don't want auditors to find. You don't erase it. Erasure leaves a hole, and holes get noticed. You just... never write it down anywhere anyone looks."

* * *

They worked through the night.

It was, Elias would think later, the strangest intimacy he had ever known — two people leaning over a dead man's letter by lamplight, decoding the geography of a prison. Rosalind brought things he could not have brought alone. She knew the harbor's unofficial geography the way he knew its paperwork: which cays had fresh water, which had ruins from the old fortification era, which islands the divers avoided because the currents ate swimmers. When he named a possibility, she could tell him in thirty seconds whether it could hold a building, a garrison, a boat landing.

"The fourth character," she said at some black hour, her chin on her fist. "Show me again."

He angled the page. She studied the crushed impression for a long time, then reached into her coat and produced a diver's knife, of all things, and used its spine to press the fold flat, working along it slowly, the way she said they smoothed kelp for drying.

"There," she said quietly. "See how the stroke breaks here, then continues? It's not a curve. It's two strokes. An L crossed by the fold."

M. 7-or-1. K-or-H. L.

"M71L," Elias said. "Or M1KL. Or—" He stopped. His mouth had gone dry.

"What?"

He was reaching for his satchel, digging past the copied ledger pages, past the false manifests, past everything they had assembled like surgeons assembling a case. His fingers found the thin folder he kept separate from all the rest — the letters in Tomas Brightwater's hand, the ones dated after the drowning, the ones that had started everything.

"Tomas's letters," he said. "The ones he sent through whatever channel he found. I assumed he mailed them from ports — I looked for port stamps, and there weren't any, and I thought that meant forgery. But look at the routing codes."

Rosalind took the letters with a strange carefulness, the way one handles relics, or wounds. She laid them beside the copra letter. Three routing stamps, three sets of four characters.

M71L. M71L. M17L.

"They're all the same island," she whispered. "All this time. He wasn't moving. He was writing from the same place and I couldn't see it because I was looking for a name instead of a code."

"And the copra letter came from the same code." Elias felt something enormous turning over inside him, slow as a ship coming about. "Whatever Caret Bay is — wherever it is — your father has been within reach of it for years. Every letter. Every one."

She stood abruptly and walked to the window, and he let her go, because he understood that grief and hope arriving together must feel like drowning, and a drowning woman needs air more than company. Her shoulders were very straight. Through the shutter slats the moon lay on the water in a broken silver road, and he watched her watch it, and he thought: *she swore never to hope, and I am about to ask her to.*

* * *

Dawn found them at Ossa's chart table in the cellar, a smuggler's chart spread between them — not the hydrographic office version, which showed only what the navy wished shown, but a working chart annotated in three generations of different hands, the kind of document that got men hanged in less interesting times.

"Exclusion zone," Rosalind said, and her finger came down on the chart like a verdict.

Elias had known it would be. Some part of him had known since the routing code resolved, and he had kept working anyway, because the alternative to working was stopping, and stopping was unthinkable. But seeing it drawn — seeing the red-inked boundary sweep across the water in a great arc north of the harbor, enclosing a scatter of islands marked only by soundings and warnings — was different from knowing it.

"No civilian traffic," he said. "No fishing rights. Patrols at intervals. Anything inside can be shelled without inquiry under the emergency statutes." He recited it flatly, the way he'd once recited tariff schedules, because flatness was the only way to say such things aloud. "And Caret Bay sits here." His finger touched a small island near the zone's heart, a shape barely worth naming, with a ruined fort symbol and a depth notation off its western shore. "Deep-water approach. Protected anchorage. Close enough to the mainland for supply runs, far enough that no one swims away."

"My father is on that rock." Rosalind's voice was level. Too level. "Twelve years. Twelve years on a rock inside a naval exclusion zone, and every letter he risked his life to send landed within sight of it, and none of us—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again the levelness had cracked just enough to show the fire beneath. "How many others, Elias? How many 'unserviceable losses' are on that island right now, alive, while their names sit in falsified ledgers?"

"That's what we're going to find out."

"How?" She gestured at the chart, at the red arc, at the whole armed weight of the state drawn in ink around one small island. "Row out and knock? The patrols—"

"Storm debris." He said it before he had fully thought it, the idea arriving whole the way ideas sometimes did after a night of grinding against a problem — not born but surfaced, like a body coming up from deep water. "The season's turning. When the storms come, the zone boundaries mean nothing for a day or two — patrol boats pull back to safe anchorage, the navy grounds its launches, and the water fills with wreckage. Wreckage drifts. Boats full of salvagers go out after it, because salvage law doesn't respect exclusion zones during declared storm emergencies — it's in the maritime code, section forty-one, I filed requisitions under it for years."

Rosalind stared at him. "You want to float into a naval prison on a raft of storm trash."

"I want to float in looking exactly like what the patrols expect to find after a storm: desperate salvagers chasing drifting cargo. It's the one cover story that requires no papers, because no salvager has papers. And we'd need—" he counted silently, hating each name he added to the risk, "—a crew of three at most. People who can row far, hold their nerve, and keep silent. People who are already lost if they're caught, so they have nothing further to lose."

The cellar was quiet. Somewhere above them, Ossa's boots crossed the floorboards, opening the chandlery for the day, and the ordinary sounds of commerce — crates, coin, a customer's voice — went on overhead like weather from another world.

"Elias." Rosalind's voice had changed. He looked up and found her watching him with an expression he hadn't seen before, something stripped bare beneath the competence. "If my father is there — if he's alive after twelve years — he may not be..." She couldn't finish it. She tried again. "The man who comes back may not be the man who left."

"I know."

"He'll be broken. Or changed. Or he'll resent us for finding him, or he won't remember—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, by force. "I've spent twelve years grieving a drowned man. I built my whole life on that grave. If we do this, and we're wrong—"

"We're not wrong." He said it quietly, and reached across the chart table, and for the first time since the drying racks, since the wet boots and the blood and the hiding, he touched her hand deliberately, on purpose, letting the touch say what his words could not carry safely. "The handwriting is yours, Rosalind. Whatever else is true, that's true. Your father wrote those letters. Something on that island kept him alive to write them."

She looked down at their hands. For a moment she didn't move, and the lamplight trembled slightly, and he understood that the trembling was the lamp, not her, because Rosalind Brightwater did not tremble; she had been built too well for that, by hard water and harder years.

Then she withdrew her hand, gently, and became practical again, and he loved her a little for it and mourned a little too, though he had no right to either feeling yet.

"Three people," she said. "Who rows, who holds, who keeps silent. I know a girl on the salvage crew who's already smuggling messages for me and hasn't asked why. Nettie. She's young, but she pulls an oar like a woman twice her size, and she hates the company more than she fears it." A pause. "And I know these waters better than any patrol pilot. I've dived every reef inside that red line since I was nine years old. I know where the water hides rocks and where it hides boats."

"You'd dive in the exclusion zone."

"I'd dive anywhere my father might be." She rolled the chart closed with quick, decisive movements, and the red arc vanished, but they both knew it was still there, waiting, patient as all borders are. "Storm season starts soon. We'll need to watch the barometer, pick our window, plan the approach along the deep channel where the wrecks drift. And Elias—" she paused at the foot of the stairs, half in shadow, "—when we find him, if we find him, he'll need to hear it from me. What happened to my mother. What I became. All of it. Don't soften it for me and don't speak for me. Promise me."

"I promise."

She nodded once and went up into the morning, and Elias sat alone in the cellar with the dead man's letter and the living woman's absence and the impossible plan lying between them like a loaded weapon.

Outside, far to the north, over water no civilian boat was permitted to cross, the sky was beginning to stack itself with cloud.

* * *

He spent the following week becoming a postmark expert.

It was the kind of work he had been made for, though he had never known it — the patient, obsessive cross-referencing of trivialities, the building of certainty from fragments no one else considered evidence. By day he worked the port archives through Ossa's cousin, a half-blind records clerk who accepted bribes in tobacco and asked no questions because his eyes were bad and his conscience worse. By night Elias matched routing codes against freight schedules, against coal requisitions, against the provisioning returns of a dozen company vessels whose logs recorded "water and vegetables delivered" to destinations that officially did not exist.

The picture assembled itself the way pictures do: not all at once but in accretion, grain by grain, until suddenly it was a face.

Caret Bay received supplies monthly from the steamer *Vigilant*, a vessel listed as a coastal survey ship. Its provisioning returns were modest — a garrison-sized ration scale, thirty to forty mouths. Its medical requisitions included quantities of laudanum and restraints that no survey crew required. And once, buried in a quarterly summary Elias almost missed, a line item that made him sit motionless for a full minute in the archive's dust-smelling gloom:

*"Special maintenance — persons, ward — 34 souls."*

Thirty-four souls, maintained. Not employed. Not transported. Maintained, the way one maintains a lighthouse or a fence — something kept in working order because its existence served a purpose, and its purpose, Elias understood with cold clarity, was silence. Thirty-four people who knew too much, kept alive precisely because dead men testified to nothing, but kept where no living voice could reach a courtroom.

Until now. Until an armistice commission announced it would hear testimony, and every soul in that ward became not a liability to be maintained but a threat to be erased, and the clock that had been ticking for twelve years began ticking much faster.

He copied the line item three times, hid the copies in three places, and burned the original in Ossa's stove, watching the paper curl and brighten and go to ash, and thought: *this is what they will do to all of it, to every record, every witness, unless we move.*

* * *

Rosalind came at moonrise again, five nights later, and he knew before she spoke that the weather had turned. He could feel it in the harbor himself — a new pressure in the air, a smell of iron and far-off rain, the water in the basin lying strangely flat and oily, the way it did when a storm was gathering its strength beyond the horizon.

"Barometer's falling fast," she said, sitting across from him. "Nettie says the foreman's pushing double shifts to beat the weather, which means the company expects it too. Two days, maybe three." She set something on the table between them: a small canvas packet, waterproofed with wax, tied with waxed cord. Inside, he knew, were copies of everything — the routing codes, the provision returns, the letters — sewn also into her diving coat, duplicated, because she trusted no single container with the truth. "If we don't come back, this reaches the commission. Nettie knows where to send it."

"You've planned for failure."

"I'm a diver's daughter. We plan for failure every time we go under. It's how we stay alive." She looked at him across the lamp, and her face in that light was both very young and very old, and he thought about the grave she had described to him, the mother who died waiting, the vow never to hope. "Say it plainly, Elias. Say what we're doing, so I can hear it said aloud once before we commit."

He took a breath. "In two days, when the storm breaks and the patrols withdraw, we take a boat north through the debris field, into the exclusion zone, to an island called Caret Bay, where thirty-four people are held prisoner by a trading company inside a naval fortress. We find Tomas Brightwater among them. We bring him home. And we do it all before the weather clears and the guns come back to the water."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere out in the harbor a bell buoy clanked, counting the swell.

"All right," Rosalind said at last, and the word was quiet and absolute, like a keel settling into deep water. "All right. Then we'd better learn to row in a storm."

Through the shutter slats, far to the north, the first lightning of the season flickered over the exclusion zone — silent at this distance, a pulse of white in the stacked black cloud, like a signal only they could read.

Hope, Elias thought, had arrived the way it always did: disguised as an impossible plan, wearing the face of certain ruin, knocking at the door of two people with nothing left to lose.

He pinched out the lamp, and in the darkness they began to count the hours.
