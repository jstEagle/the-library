## 13. Nettie's Choice

The storm came the way the harbor master said all bad things came to Saltmere: announced in advance, priced accordingly, and worse than promised.

Rosalind felt it three days before it arrived — in the ache of her old shoulder scar, in the way the gulls went silent over the drying racks, in the greenish bruise-light that settled on the water each morning and refused to lift. The divers read it the way they read everything, with their bodies first and their minds after. By Tuesday the foreman had doubled the salvage quotas anyway, because the company's arithmetic did not include weather, only tonnage.

It was on that Tuesday, in the last hour of light, that Nettie found the letters.

Rosalind was below in the forepeak of the salvage barge *Meridian Grace*, checking the stitching she'd sewn into her diving coat — Elias's copied pages, folded small as communion wafers, wrapped in oilcloth. She'd developed a ritual: check the seams, count the pages by touch, return them. Twelve copies now, plus Tomas's original testimony in its deeper pocket against her heart. She knew them the way a blind woman knows coins.

She did not hear Nettie come down the ladder.

"Those are papers."

Rosalind's hands went still inside the coat. The lamp hung from its hook between them, swaying with the swell, throwing their shadows up the curved ribs of the hull so that they seemed to lean toward each other like conspirators — which, Rosalind thought with a cold drop through her stomach, one of them now was.

"It's nothing," she said. "Ledger work. I copy manifests for the counting house sometimes. Extra pay."

Nettie was seventeen and built like a whipstaff, all leverage and no give, with a face that had been burned brown by ten years of sun-glare off open water and eyes that missed nothing above or below the surface. She'd been diving since she was nine. She could hold her breath long enough to walk the length of a sunken hull and back, and she could smell a lie faster than most people could hear one.

"You don't get extra pay," Nettie said. "Nobody gets extra pay. I've been here four years and I've never seen the company give anybody anything it didn't take back double." She came closer, boots ringing on the deck plates, and stopped an arm's length away — close enough that Rosalind could see the salt dried white in the creases of her knuckles, the fresh rope-burn across her palm from Tuesday's winch work. "Open it."

"No."

"Open it or I go up top and tell Foreman Brack there's contraband in your coat."

The words landed in the quiet like a stone into still water, and Rosalind watched the ripples spread outward through everything she had built — the careful months of invisibility, the messages passed hand to hand in wet gloves, the plan taking shape north of the exclusion line, all of it balanced on the goodwill of a girl who was now offering to sell it for her own safety.

And Rosalind understood — understood with a clarity that felt almost like mercy — that she could not even blame her for it. That was the architecture of the thing. The company didn't need loyalists; it needed frightened people, and it manufactured them by the gross. Fear was cheaper than loyalty and more reliable than love. Hargrave had built an empire on it.

"Nettie," Rosalind said slowly, "if you tell Brack, they'll hang him—" she nodded upward, toward the deck where Elias's borrowed identity lived and died by the hour, "—and then they'll come for me, and then they'll ask who else helped carry what I carried. You know how it goes. It never stops at the first name."

"I know how it goes." Nettie's jaw worked. "I watched it go when they took the Osei brothers off the *Cormorant*. First one name, then five."

"So you know what you'd be buying with my coat."

"I know what I'd be buying with my *silence* if I keep it!" The girl's voice cracked, and beneath the crack Rosalind heard something rawer than fear — something that had been carried too long in too small a space. "You think I haven't lain awake doing this arithmetic? You think I don't do it every night? Keep quiet, stay small, dive deep, come home. Except home isn't—"

She stopped. Her breath went ragged.

Rosalind waited. Below decks, waiting was a discipline; you learned it at depth, where panic spent air like a drunkard spends wages.

"There was a ship," Nettie said finally, very quietly. "Two years ago. The *Violet Anne*, out of Corvane's old station. My sister dove for Meridian. Sera. She was twenty-two and she could free-dive forty fathoms on a good day, which is not a thing the sea forgives twice." Her hands had closed around the edge of the forepeak bench, knuckles white. "The *Violet Anne* went down in the strait with eleven divers aboard. Company record says 'lost with all hands, weather.' One line. I've seen it. I copied it myself once, before I understood what I was copying."

Rosalind's blood went slow and heavy.

"And?" she asked, though she already knew — knew the way you know the shape of a thing underwater before your fingers confirm it.

"And last month," Nettie said, "when the new manifests came through and I was carrying them down to the strong room, one page slipped out of the bundle. Just one. And on it, under losses, was written: *divers, unserviceable — 11, Violet Anne, adjusted.*" She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the terrible way that eyes are dry when the crying finished years ago. "*Adjusted,* Rosalind. Not lost. Not drowned. Adjusted. Like a column that doesn't balance."

The lamp swung. Shadows tilted.

"My sister wasn't weather," Nettie said. "My sister was a rounding error."

---

Later — much later, after the lamps had been trimmed and the watch changed and the storm's first outriders began rattling the rigging overhead like dice in a cup — Rosalind sat with Nettie in the stern sheets, out of earshot of the crew, and told her everything.

Not everything. Not yet. But enough: Elias, the letters, the two sets of books, the holding island inside the exclusion zone where inconvenient witnesses were kept alive precisely because dead men testified poorly but broken ones stayed put. Enough that Nettie sat with her elbows on her knees and stared out at the black water for a long time without speaking.

"Sera used to say the company kept two kinds of paper," she said at last. "Paper for reading and paper for burning. I thought she was being clever." A muscle moved in her cheek. "She was just being accurate."

"They'll come for the record eventually," Rosalind said. "When the armistice signs, the commission closes its book and whatever isn't written down by then never happened. That's how victors write history — they don't burn it, they just stop printing it."

"And you mean to be printed."

"I mean to be *inked*." Rosalind turned to face her fully. "But I can't do it alone, and I won't lie to you about what it costs. Look at me, Nettie. I'm indentured. If this goes wrong, I don't lose my freedom — I never had any to lose. What I can lose is everyone who stood near me when it fell. So before you decide, decide knowing that."

Nettie was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her shirt and drew out a small square of oilcloth, worn soft at the folds, and opened it with the care of someone unwounding herself.

Inside was a lock of hair, dark and fine, tied with thread gone gray from salt and years.

"Sera's," she said. "They sent it to my mother in a company envelope. Postage paid. Like it was samples from a trade goods shipment." She wrapped it again, slowly. "I've been deciding since I was fifteen whether to be safe or to be somebody. Every year I picked safe, and every year safe got smaller."

She tucked the oilcloth away and met Rosalind's eyes, and in them Rosalind saw the same thing she'd seen once in a mirror at fourteen, the morning after her father's boat failed to come home — the exact moment when grief stops being a weight you carry and becomes a direction you walk.

"I'm in," Nettie said. "But not for you. For her. And for the other ten names nobody adjusted properly."

---

By Friday, the conspiracy had roots.

Rosalind hadn't planned it that way; plans made in fear grow shallow. But Nettie, having decided, decided completely — and Nettie talked the way water talks, quietly, finding every low place, filling every hollow. She didn't make speeches. She made *observations*, dropped at the right moments into the right ears: to old Marta Voss while they mended net together, that the *Violet Anne*'s loss entry had been adjusted; to Denz Okonkwo in the chow line, that the commission sitting in Saltmere would take testimony until signing day and then never again; to the whole shifting, tired, watchful community of the salvage fleet, that somewhere in the company's own ledgers their dead were listed not as people but as *variance*.

Nothing was asked. Nothing was offered. That was the genius of it, and the terror — because within a week Rosalind began noticing small changes that no one acknowledged and everyone understood.

The winch operator, a gray-bearded Salish man named Coho, started taking his smoke breaks directly beside the forepeak ladder whenever Rosalind went below, an immovable silhouette against anyone else descending. The cook began setting aside hardtack and salt pork in a canvas bag that appeared each evening on the third hook from the door, unclaimed, unremarked — provisions for a journey no one mentioned. When Brack scheduled Rosalind for the worst deep shifts, the rotation roster mysteriously rearranged itself overnight, twice, until he gave up trying to bury her and simply glared at her across the deck with the baffled fury of a man whose instruments had begun lying to him.

Even the youngest divers, girls of twelve and thirteen who had barely learned their depths, took to walking past Rosalind in pairs and touching two fingers to their collarbones — a diver's sign, old as the fleet: *breath held, heart steady, I see you.*

No one said the word *mutiny*. No one said *conspiracy* or *testimony* or *armistice*. They were powerless people, and they knew the grammar of powerlessness the way fish know current: you don't fight the tide head-on; you move beneath it, together, in silence, and let it carry you somewhere the surface-dwellers never thought to look.

On Saturday night, anchored in the lee of Gull Rock with the storm's true face still building on the northern horizon, the crew gathered in the fo'c'sle around a single lantern — ostensibly for cards, actually for nothing, actually for the simple animal comfort of being many in the dark — and Nettie stood up.

She didn't give a speech. She took out the oilcloth square and set Sera's lock of hair on the crate beside the lantern, where everyone could see it, and then she sat back down.

One by one, without a word, the others did the same. Marta Voss produced a brass diving-weight stamped with a serial number. Denz laid down a torn scrap of shirt with a name embroidered at the collar. Coho placed a child's shoe, cured leather, salt-stiff, small enough to fit a girl of eight. Eleven objects accumulated on that crate, some representing the living and some the dead, until the lantern's circle was ringed with the physical evidence of everything the company's arithmetic had failed to carry.

Then Coho spoke, for the first time in Rosalind's memory addressing the whole fo'c'sle rather than the wind.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my grandmother told me the sea keeps everything. Everything that goes down comes up again someday, changed, but it comes up." He looked around the circle. "These companies think paper drowns. Paper floats, friends. Paper always floats."

Outside, the wind rose another notch, and far to the north the lightning had begun its silent, patient counting.

Rosalind looked at the ring of relics in the lamplight — at the shoe, at the hair, at the scrap of embroidered cloth — and understood suddenly, viscerally, that the plan had changed shape. It was no longer hers and Elias's anymore, a desperate errand rowed by three frightened people toward an island full of ghosts. It belonged to the fleet now. To the powerless, the indentured, the adjusted, the forgotten. Which meant it could fail — but it could no longer be silenced, because silencing it would require drowning every soul aboard every barge in Saltmere harbor, and even the Meridian Company, for all its ledgers, had never yet managed to adjust *that much* water.

She was still turning that thought over, warm as a coal in her cupped hands, when the lookout's voice came down the hatch, tight and strange:

"Boat off the port quarter! No lights — running dark — heading straight for us!"

Through the fo'c'sle porthole, past the ring of relics and the guttering lantern, Rosalind saw it: a long, low shadow sliding across the black water, moving with the particular purposeful silence of a vessel that has business it does not wish to explain.

And lashed to its bow, catching the lightning's pulse from the northern clouds, something pale and rectangular that might have been a cargo crate — or might have been a coffin.
