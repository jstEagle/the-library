## 14. Storm Season

The storm came up from the southeast the way debts come — slowly at first, then all at once, and never when you could pay.

Rosalind felt it in her teeth two days before the barometers at the salvage camp confirmed it. The water had gone strange and sweet-smelling, thick with a green light that made the shallows look like bottle glass. The fish moved shallow. The gulls went quiet in a way that was worse than screaming. Old divers said a storm announced itself first to the body and only afterward to the sky, and Rosalind had long since stopped doubting what her bones told her.

She stood on the deck of the *Meridian Grace* that morning and watched the horizon assemble itself out of nothing — a low bruised line where there had been open sea, growing taller by the hour.

"Barometer's falling like a stone," said Nettie beside her, arms crossed against the wind that hadn't yet arrived but was already pushing ahead of itself, combing the water into long silver ridges. "Foreman won't call it, though. You watch."

She was right. Foreman Aldous Vane stood at the rail with his slate under his arm and his collar up, squinting at the same bruised horizon as if he might argue it down through sheer administrative will. He had quotas from the company office in Saltmere, and quotas did not care about weather. The munitions ship — the *Cormorant's Pride*, gone down eleven months ago with her cargo of shells and powder still nominally company property — had yielded barely half of what the manifests claimed she carried, and someone in a counting house with dry boots wanted an explanation in figures, not excuses.

"He'll run us until the masts go," Nettie said.

"He'll run us until after the masts go," Rosalind said. "He'll want the loss documented."

The wind arrived at dusk. By full dark it was a living thing on the water, and the *Grace* rolled at her moorings like a drunkard trying to remember how to stand. They were anchored off the salvage grounds in the lee of Gannet Shoal, close enough that the breakers over the shoal sounded all night like something enormous being torn steadily apart. No one slept well. In the hold where the diving crew bunked, the lantern swung on its hook and threw shadows that circled the walls like animals pacing a cage.

Rosalind lay awake with her hand pressed flat against the planking over her heart, where the oilcloth packet lay sewn into her diving coat — Elias's copied pages, the second spine of the Meridian ledger, the true count of the drowned. She had taken to sleeping with the coat folded beneath her head like a pillow stuffed with stones. It was not comfortable. It was not meant to be.

Six weeks, Elias had told her, the last time Nettie's smuggled message came back. Six weeks until the armistice commission signed and stopped listening. That had been nine days ago.

She counted them now against the sound of the storm, the way her father had once taught her to count breaths before a descent. Five weeks and five days. Five and four. The numbers would not stay still. They slipped away from her like fish in cloudy water, and every one that escaped took a little more light with it.

* * *

The storm broke over them properly near dawn, and for three days the world consisted entirely of noise and water and the groan of rope.

They rode it out at anchor because Vane would not risk the passage back to harbor with his quota unmet and his own neck consequently exposed; better, he calculated aloud on the second day, to lose gear than to lose standing. Two of the mooring cables parted in the night and the *Grace* swung dangerously toward the shoal before they got a third line down, all hands hauling in rain that came sideways and stung like flung gravel. Rosalind worked the capstan between Nettie and a broad-shouldered Salter Island woman named Maren Cole, and the three of them leaned into the bar together, boots slipping, while the sea tried its best to take the ship and give her to the rocks.

It was almost restful, Rosalind thought absurdly, hauling. There was no room in it for dread. Her whole mind narrowed to the pull of the bar and the burn in her shoulders and Nettie's gasping count — *one, two, heave* — and for those hours she was only a body doing work, and the packet over her heart was just wet leather, and the window closing over the harbor of Saltmere was just weather.

Then the storm passed, as storms do, trailing ragged cloud, and left behind a sea like a beaten floor and a salvage schedule in ruins.

Three days lost. Diving gear soaked and fouled — two of the air hoses had chafed nearly through where they'd rubbed against the rail, and one of the helmets had taken seawater down the breastplate valve and rusted shut. The barge they used for lifting had shipped water and spoiled a third of their stores. And worst of all, in Vane's arithmetic, the wreck itself had shifted: the storm's surge had rolled the *Cormorant's Pride* deeper into the sand, collapsing part of the stern section they'd spent weeks carefully opening.

Vane walked the deck the morning after, surveying the damage with his slate held against his chest like a breviary, and Rosalind watched his face do something she had not expected. It did not fall. It *brightened*.

She saw it clearly and it frightened her more than the storm had. Here was a man handed three days of ruin, ruined gear, a buried wreck — and instead of despair, relief. Because ruin, Rosalind understood in that moment, was not always a thing that happened to a man. Sometimes it was a thing that happened *for* him. Ruin explained things. Ruin covered things. A storm was a witness who could not be cross-examined.

"Company'll hear it was sabotage," Maren muttered at the rail, low enough that only Rosalind heard. She was splicing a frayed line as she spoke, her hands moving without her attention. "Mark me."

"Sabotage," Rosalind repeated. "Of what? Of weather?"

"Of whatever needs explaining." Maren bit the cord tight with her teeth. "Storms sink ships, girl, but storms don't sign papers. Somebody has to answer for the shortfall, and it won't be the man with the office."

Rosalind looked across the gray water at the foreman, still making his rounds, still wearing that small settled brightness, and felt the cold come into her that the storm had not managed.

* * *

The patrol sloop came out from Saltmere two days later, riding low with armed men, and the arrests happened before breakfast.

There was no drama in it, which was the most terrible thing about it. The patrol boat bumped against the *Grace*'s hull, a lieutenant with a wet mustache read names off a paper, and two men put down their tools and went. Tam Weller, who had dived the grounds for nine years and taught half the younger crew their signals. And Josiah Finch, a quiet rigger with a wife in Saltmere and a habit of singing hymns under his breath while he worked the winch. Sabotage, the paper said. Tampering with the air hoses. Willful destruction of company property with intent to defraud.

The frayed hoses. Of course. The ones the storm had chafed against the rail — the ones any diver could see had worn from friction, the wear marks plain as handwriting to anyone who knew rope and rubber. Rosalind had looked at those hoses herself, had helped coil them away after the blow, and had thought nothing of it because there was nothing in them to think about. Now they sat in evidence somewhere, or would, presented to men who knew nothing of chafe and everything about convenience.

"They can't mean it," Nettie said. She had gone white around the mouth. "Tam's forty years old. He's got a bad ear from the deep work. What would he even—"

"It doesn't matter what he'd do." Rosalind kept her voice level, though her hands had closed hard around the rail. "It matters what the paper says."

"But the wear marks—"

"Will be described by whoever is paid to describe them." She turned to face the girl fully. Nettie was nineteen and still believed, in some stubborn cellar of herself, that facts behaved like facts. Rosalind remembered believing that too. It seemed a lifetime ago, though it was less than a season. "Nettie. Listen to me. This isn't about Tam and Josiah. This isn't even about the hoses."

"Then what is it about?"

Rosalind looked north, toward Saltmere, invisible beyond the headland but present all the same — the counting house, the customs hall, the commission that would sit there in a handful of weeks while lawyers arranged the truth like flowers at a funeral. Somewhere in that city, men were already writing the story of this war's end: the honorable losses, the regrettable costs, the tidy ledger that would close with a signature and a band playing. Everything loose needed tying down before then. Every inconvenient voice.

And here, forty miles out, was a boatload of divers who had seen the real numbers, who had been pushed past safe depth for quotas, whose friends' names appeared in a private ledger under *unserviceable*. Voices. Loose ends.

"That," Rosalind said quietly. "It's about that. They're not punishing anyone. They're *preparing*. You don't arrest a man for sabotage you know he didn't commit unless you need him arrested. Arrested men can't testify. Arrested men are examples. And every man on this deck who sees Tam taken off in irons learns exactly what happens to people who complain."

The color drained out of Nettie's face by degrees, like tide going out. "So it's theater."

"It's theater with a hangman's budget." Rosalind made herself say it plainly, because softening it would be a kindness, and kindness right now would get someone killed. "The armistice comes, the commission signs, and after that nobody official listens to anything a diver says ever again. Between now and then, they have to make sure we're too frightened, too scattered, and too criminalized to be worth listening to anyway. The storm gave them their excuse and they're going to ride it all the way ashore."

* * *

That evening the crew gathered in the fo'c'sle, ostensibly to mend gear, actually to be near each other, the way people do after a death. The lantern hung steady for once — the sea had gone glass-calm, that exhausted smoothness that follows hard blows — and its light fell on bent needles and tarred twine and faces that had aged years in a week.

Maren Cole spoke first, which surprised everyone including, perhaps, Maren. She was not a talker. She sat with her mending forgotten in her lap and her big square hands folded, and she said, to the room in general, "I dove with Tam's brother. Off Corvo Bank, before the war. He didn't come up."

No one answered. There was nothing to answer with.

"My cousin's name is in a ledger somewhere too," said a young Salter man called Dev, who had not spoken above a murmur in the entire time Rosalind had known him. "My mother got a letter. *Regret to inform.* Four lines. He worked eight years for the Company."

"They gave my sister's wages to the office," said another voice, "on account of she owed them for her kit. Dead six years and still paying rent on a helmet."

Rosalind sat among them and said nothing, and the saying-nothing cost her more than speech ever had. Because she knew. She had seen the pages sewn against her own heart. She knew exactly which ledger, exactly which column, exactly how many names, and every day she did not speak them aloud was a day they stayed buried twice — once in the water and once in her silence. But to speak now, here, in this crowded wooden room where any wall might have ears and Vane's cabin sat twenty feet aft — to speak now would spend everything at once and buy nothing, and Elias's words rang in her: *the record, Rosalind. It has to reach the record. Otherwise it's just more drowning.*

Still. She looked around the lantern light at these faces — Maren with her drowned brother-in-law, Dev with his four-line letter, the young ones watching the older ones to learn how to grieve — and she understood that the conspiracy she and Elias had imagined building out of paper was already built. It had been built all along, out of grief, in rooms like this one, on decks like this one, by people with nothing left to lose except the chance to say so. All she had to do was stop pretending she wasn't one of them.

"The commission sits in Saltmere in five weeks," she said.

The room went still. Needles stopped mid-stitch.

"I know what I'm saying. I know what happened today and why." She let that sit. "Five weeks. After that, the signing, and then it's done and dusted and none of us were ever anything but equipment. But before that—" she met Maren's eyes, then Dev's, then Nettie's, "—there are people coming to Saltmere whose whole work is to listen. For the last time in our lives, listening is their job."

"And you think they'll listen to the likes of us?" Maren's voice was not mocking. It was genuinely, tiredly curious — the question of a woman who had been unheard so long she'd half forgotten sound was possible.

"I think they have to write something down," Rosalind said. "And I think what gets written should be the truth, if anyone is brave enough to carry it there. I'm asking if you are."

Silence, but a different silence than before — not the silence of grief but the silence of weight being shifted from one set of shoulders to many.

It was Dev, the murmurer, who finally nodded. Then Maren. Then, one by one, in the swinging lantern light, the rest — not loudly, not with oaths, just the slow accumulation of nods, like water rising, like a tide that asks no permission.

* * *

Later, alone at the stern rail with the packet pressing its familiar shape against her ribs, Rosalind watched the lights of Saltmere flickering far off beyond the headland and did her counting again. Five weeks minus four days. Less, now, with every breath.

Somewhere in that city, Hargrave was working. She was sure of it — sure with the certainty of the hunted. He had recognized her father's name on a manifest; he had boarded the *Grace* once already with his soft questions and his softer eyes. If he had found Elias, the copies were worthless and the trail led straight back to her. If he hadn't, then he was looking, and every day she stayed alive and free was a day he failed.

And now Tam and Josiah sat in some Saltmere cell, charged with breaking hoses the sea had broken, and their trial — if there was a trial, if the company bothered with the form — would happen inside that closing window too. Another way to fill the record: not with testimony but with verdicts. *Diver sabotage*, printed and certified, the perfect explanation for every short ton and every dead woman, sealed by a magistrate before the commission ever unpacked its papers.

Unless.

Rosalind's fingers found the edge of the oilcloth packet through the coat, tracing it the way she'd once traced her mother's locket. Unless the truth arrived first. Unless someone walked into that customs hall carrying the real ledger, the real names, the real account of the *Cormorant's Pride* and the over-dived banks and the island where old men who couldn't testify were kept breathing — unless all of it landed on the commission's table before the ink of the false record dried.

Five weeks. One rowboat. A coat full of paper and a city full of wolves.

She turned from the rail and went below to wake Nettie. There was a message to smuggle, and no time left to waste.
