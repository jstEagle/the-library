## 15. The Long Row Out

### Rosalind

The storm did not end so much as exhaust itself, the way a man does after a beating — still dangerous, but slower now, and easier to read. All night it had thrown itself against Saltmere like something with a grudge, tearing awnings from the fish market, splitting the *Meridian Vigil* against her moorings, and by dawn it had spent its fury on the harbor and left behind a sea of wreckage: splintered spars, drowned crates, a fishing skiff upside down and spinning slowly in the current like a dead thing turning over in sleep.

It was perfect cover. That was the terrible arithmetic of it — every loss the company suffered tonight was another boat that could go missing without being missed.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the wreck-strewn shallows and let the wind push her hair back from her face, feeling the cold work down through her coat to the pages sewn against her ribs. She had sewn them herself, three nights ago, by candlelight: Tomas's letters, Elias's copied manifests, the second spine of the ledger reduced to what could be hidden against a woman's body. They pressed there now like a second heartbeat.

"You're sure about this," Nettie said beside her. Not a question, really. Nettie had stopped asking questions somewhere around the moment she'd said her sister's name aloud — *Marisol, they wrote Marisol down as unserviceable* — and started saying things instead, flat and final, like a diver checking her line before going over the side.

"The holding island is inside the exclusion zone," Rosalind said. "Which means no patrol boats. Which means no one looking for us."

"Or no one looking for us when we drown."

"That too."

Elias came down the shingle path carrying the last of their gear — a lantern with its glass shuttered, fresh water in a corked jar, and his arm bound tight where Hargrave's men had caught him with a belaying pin two nights past. He moved stiffly, favoring the right side, and he pretended he didn't. She noticed anyway. She was noticing everything about him lately, the way you notice a change in the water's color before a squall — not because you want to, but because your life may depend on reading it correctly.

"The skiff's under the north pier," he said. "Company markings burned off. It'll look like storm debris if anyone sees it."

"It'll look like storm debris because it is storm debris," Rosalind said. "We're taking the *Paloma*."

Nettie made a sound that might have been a laugh. The little skiff had been hauled out of the mudflats after the storm cracked her loose; she was old, patched twice, named for some long-dead owner's daughter or maybe for a dove, depending on which story you believed. Rosalind had dived off her as a child. Her father had painted the name himself, she was almost certain, though the paint had faded until only the P remained legible, white and stubborn against the weathered wood.

She didn't say that aloud. Some things you carried without handing them to other people.

They pushed off through the wreckage, the three of them, oars dipping between floating planks and sodden rope, and the harbor opened around them like a mouth full of broken teeth. Behind them Saltmere smoked gently in the rain-washed light. Ahead, gray water, gray sky, and eleven miles out, an island that did not officially exist.

---

### Elias

He had crossed three ports tracing paper, and paper, he had learned, told you everything except how anything actually felt. Paper said *holding facility, coastal, naval jurisdiction.* Paper did not tell you about the moment when the shore fell away behind you and there was nothing between you and the horizon but a rotten skiff, a wounded arm, and a woman who rowed like the oars owed her money.

Rosalind rowed in the bow stroke, steady as a metronome, and Elias rowed opposite her, and Nettie steered with a broken sweep oar, correcting for the chop with small, economical movements. None of them spoke much. There was a language to shared labor that made speech unnecessary, and besides, Elias's mind was busy elsewhere — running the route again, the tide tables again, the timing again. They would make the island's leeward side by mid-morning. The garrison, if the intelligence held, was four men and a wireless set, most of whom would be hungover from storm watch or asleep in the aftermath of it. The witnesses were kept in a long shed above the shingle beach. Tomas Brightwater would be —

Would be. Eleven years of paperwork had taught Elias the difference between *would be* and *might be*, and he had learned never to promise either to anyone. But the woman rowing ahead of him had promised herself something, and he could see it in her shoulders, in the set of her jaw beneath the wet hair: she was not coming back without an answer. Whatever the answer was.

The rain came again around the third mile, fine and needling, and with it a swell that lifted the skiff and dropped her with a patience that felt almost mocking. Nettie sang under her breath — some diving song, low and rhythmic, the kind the women used to keep time in the water. He caught fragments. *Down where the light goes thin / hold your breath and count your kin.* He understood then that it wasn't a song about drowning. It was a song about surviving the deep by counting the people waiting above it. A liturgy of return.

"Corvane." Rosalind's voice, pitched under the wind. "When we get there. If my father isn't —"

"He is," Elias said.

"Don't do that. Don't hand me hope like it costs you nothing. If he isn't what I remember — if twelve years have made him someone I don't know — I need to meet that man and not the one I invented at nine years old. So don't promise me. Just get me to the island."

He looked at her back, the straight spine of her, the coat dark with rain, and he thought: *this is the bravest thing I've ever seen, and it doesn't even look like bravery. It looks like bookkeeping.* Reckoning the true cost before signing.

"All right," he said. "No promises. Just the island."

* * *

### Rosalind

The island rose out of the mist the way islands do in bad dreams — all at once, closer than expected, a black shoulder of rock wearing a cap of stunted pine. Rosalind had heard of it all her life the way sailors' children hear of such places: *that's where they send people.* No one ever said who sent them, or why, or whether they came back. The name of the place had been struck from the charts so long ago that even the fishermen called it only *the quiet side.*

They came around to the leeward shore with the swell pushing them helpfully toward the rocks, and Nettie brought the skiff in through a gap in the breakwater that she swore she'd remembered from a smuggling run two summers past, and she was right, and the keel kissed shingle with barely a sound.

Then it was hands and breath and fear: hauling the skiff up past the tideline, wedging it under a shelf of rock, covering it with storm-blown palm fronds. Above them, half-hidden in the pines, stood the long shed — whitewashed once, gray now, windows high and barred. A wire fence ran along the ridge, sagging in places where the storm had leaned on it. Somewhere beyond the trees a door banged in the wind, regular as a clock.

"No guards," Nettie whispered.

"Storm damage," Elias murmured back. "Look — the fence is down along the whole east face. Either they're understrength or they've stopped caring. Men kept this long stop trying to run."

"That's the part that frightens me," Rosalind said quietly.

They moved up through the wet grass, bent low, Elias leading now because he had the layout from the postal marks and the supply manifests — gate here, cistern there, the shed entered from the seaward end where the wood had rotted soft. Rosalind's heart was doing something strange in her chest, not pounding but stuttering, skipping like a needle crossing a scratched record, and she realized with clinical detachment that she was afraid not of capture but of the building. Of what twelve years of buildings like it did to a man.

The rotted board gave way under Elias's knife with a sound like a sigh. Cold air breathed out of the gap — air that smelled of salt, mildew, and lamp oil, and underneath those, faintly, of too many men living in too small a space for too long.

Inside: dimness. Two lanterns burning low. Bunks in rows, most of them empty — the storm had scattered the inmates to work details, perhaps, or the garrison had consolidated them elsewhere — but not all. Three figures lay sleeping, or pretending to. An old man by the far wall, breathing in whistles. A young man with a bandaged foot who opened his eyes, saw them, and closed them again with the deliberate calm of someone who had decided long ago that anything he saw was not his business.

And at the end of the row, sitting up on his bunk with his back against the wall, a man with gray in his beard and a ledger open on his knees, writing.

Writing. In the lamplight. With a stub of pencil worn down to a nub, on the back of a flour sack cut flat, in handwriting Rosalind knew better than her own name — the strong downstrokes, the way the T crossed with a single impatient slash, the numbers built like little ships, hull and mast.

"Tomas Brightwater," Elias said softly, formally, the way you address a witness. "My name is Elias Corvane. I've come to take your testimony."

The man looked up. His eyes were pale, washed-out blue, and they went first to Elias, then to Nettie, and then to Rosalind, and stopped there, and something happened in his face that Rosalind would spend the rest of her life failing to describe — a collapse and a construction at once, ruin and rebuilding happening in the same instant, like a wall falling inward and a house rising on the same foundation.

He knew her. Twelve years, and he knew her.

"You have her chin," he said. His voice was rust and gravel, a voice unused to being answered. "Your mother's eyes. My chin. God help you."

"Daddy." The word came out of her small, a child's word, absurd and enormous, and she hated it and could not unsay it. She crossed the room in four steps and knelt on the wet stone floor in front of him, and he reached out one hand — scarred across the palm, the fingers stiff, a diver's hand gone wrong somehow, the tendons shortened — and touched her cheek as if checking whether she were real, whether she were warm, whether the water had finally given something back instead of only taking.

"They told me you'd be grown," he whispered. "I used to try to picture it. I couldn't. I could only ever see you at eight, standing on the pier with your arms out because you'd just come up from your first dive and you wanted everyone to see you'd done it."

"I did it," Rosalind said, and her voice broke clean in half. "I'm still doing it."

For a long moment neither of them said anything else. The lantern flame bent in the draft through the broken wall. The old man whistled in his sleep. And Tomas Brightwater held his daughter's face in his ruined hand and wept without any sound at all, the tears simply arriving, one after another, the way they must have arrived alone in this room for twelve years, unwitnessed, unrecorded.

Then he drew his hand back, and Rosalind watched the change come over him — watched grief fold itself away with the practiced economy of a man who had learned that feelings cost energy he could not spare — and he tapped the flour-sack page on his knee.

"I can't go," he said.

* * *

### Elias

Elias had prepared for refusal. He had prepared for a broken man who could not walk, a frightened man who would not risk it, a suspicious man who might cry out for the guards. He had not prepared for this: Tomas Brightwater sitting upright on his bunk with a spine that had clearly been damaged and mended badly, one leg drawn up at an angle that explained the stick leaning against the wall, and eyes that were not broken at all but burning, banked coals under ash.

"Mr. Brightwater," Elias said carefully, "the fence is down. The garrison is depleted. We may not get a window like this again. The commission sits in five weeks —"

"I know exactly when the commission sits," Tomas said. "I know the date, the hour, the names of the commissioners, and the text of the provisional armistice terms, because I have had twelve years in rooms like this one with nothing to do but listen and memorize. You think I've been idle?" He lifted the flour-sack pages — dozens of them, tied with string, dense with pencil. "This is what I have. Fragments. Written in the dark, on scraps, hidden in the wall behind my bunk. Names. Dates. Cargo manifests recited from memory by men who loaded the ships. But it's not testimony. Do you understand? It's not *properly written*. It's not sworn, it's not ordered, it's not witnessed. If I stand in front of that commission and produce sacks and pencil nubs, they will smile at me the way they smiled at the last man who tried — yes, there was one — and they will call it the ramblings of a damaged man, and they will be right, because that is what they made me."

His voice had risen, and the young man with the bandaged foot turned his face to the wall, giving them privacy the only way left to him.

"So no," Tomas said, quieter now, and the quiet was worse than the shouting. "I will not run yet. Running comes after. First the account gets written the way it should be written — every name spelled right, every date checked, every loss entered where it belongs. On the record. In order. I was a clerk's apprentice before I was a diver, girl, before you were born — did your mother ever tell you that? Numbers are the only weapon they gave me and I mean to use every one."

Elias glanced at Rosalind. She was still kneeling, and her face — he had learned to read that face across ledgers and storm water and the space of a shared boat — her face was doing the arithmetic of love. Weighing the father in front of her against the father she needed him to be. Wanting to carry him out of here bodily, tonight, now, and understanding, with the same clarity that had made her refuse the foreman's bargain and lie to Hargrave's face, that some things cannot be carried. That they have to be walked out on their own legs, in their own time, or they break.

"Then we write it here," Rosalind said.

Tomas blinked. "What?"

"We're not leaving without you, and you're not leaving without your account finished. So we stay and we write it. Elias has copies of the manifests — real ones, from the second ledger. You have twelve years of memory. Between the two of us we can reconcile the books." She said it the way she said everything that mattered, plainly, as fact, as cargo to be moved from here to there. "How long?"

Tomas stared at her, and then a sound came out of him that took Elias a moment to identify as laughter — rusty, disbelieving, half sob. "Reconcile the books," he repeated. "Twelve years I dreamed of my daughter and she arrives proposing an audit."

"Someone has to do it," Rosalind said. "No one else will."

Elias unslung his satchel with his good arm and began laying out the copied pages on the bunk between them, smoothing each one flat, and outside the wind worried at the broken boards and the rain came fine against the roof, and somewhere down the coast the great machinery of the armistice ground forward without them, indifferent, running on schedule. Five weeks. Perhaps less.

They worked through what remained of the morning. And it was near midday, the light through the high barred windows gone from gray to a thin silver, when footsteps sounded on the shingle below — more than one pair, unhurried, heavy-booted — and Nettie, who had been keeping watch at the breached wall, turned to them with her face drained white.

"Boat coming in," she whispered. "Company colors. And Rosalind — Hargrave is standing in the bow."
