## 16. A Father Made of Paper

The fever came and went like a tide.

We learned its rhythm in the first three days on the island, Nettie and I — when to sit close with the basin and the cloth, when to keep back and let him mutter at the ceiling in a language that was half delirium and half the old diving cant of Saltmere's shallows. *Line's slack. Line's slack, pull me up.* He said it most often in the worst hours, before dawn, his hands working at the blanket as though hauling on something no one else could see. I learned not to touch those hands. The first time I did, he came out of the dream swinging, and the blow caught my cheekbone, and afterward he wept for an hour without knowing why, and neither of us pretended it was the fever alone.

The holding house had been a leper station once, then a quarantine shed for ships that carried sickness in their holds, and last of all — for who knew how many years — this: four rooms above a stone boathouse, salt-eaten shutters, a stove that smoked unless you fed it driftwood, and a company man named Pell who came every second Thursday with rice, lamp oil, and a list of questions he called a wellness inquiry. Elias had mapped all of it from the postal marks on a dead man's letters, and now we lived inside his map, waiting out the storm debris still choking the channel, waiting for my father to be well enough to do the one thing he had refused to do on the row out.

*Not yet,* he'd said, when Nettie put paper in front of him that first night. His voice had been a ruin, but the refusal in it was whole and hard as ballast stone. *Not until it's done properly. You don't set down a thing like this shaking.*

So we waited, and the tide of him came in and went out, and I kept the ledgers of his illness the way I had once kept ledgers of pearls: hours lucid, hours lost, what he ate, what he said, what he could carry.

* * *

On the fifth morning he woke clear.

I knew it before I opened my eyes, because the sound coming from the main room was wrong — not muttering, not the hauling-hands dream, but the scrape of a chair being dragged deliberately across boards, the small domestic violence of a man arranging a room to suit himself. I came out barefoot with my hair unbound and found him sitting at the table by the window, wrapped in two blankets despite the heat, and before him, squared to the table's edge with a precision that made my throat ache, lay the sheaf of paper Nettie had bought off the supply boat, a stoppered pot of ink, and three pens.

He looked older than twelve years should have made him. That was the first true sight of him in daylight, sober, and I let myself take it: the white in his beard gone yellow at the corners, the scar splitting one eyebrow where none had been, the way his left hand curled inward at rest like a gull's foot. But his eyes were my father's eyes exactly, grey-green, patient, always slightly amused at the world's expense, and they went over my face the way a diver reads water before going under.

"You have your mother's mouth," he said. "And her habit of standing in doorways deciding whether to come in."

"I'm deciding," I said, "whether the man at that table is my father or another stranger with wet boots."

Something moved behind his eyes — pain, maybe, or respect. "Fair," he said. "Sit down, Rosalind. Both answers are true, and only the paper can sort them."

I sat. He pushed a pen toward me and watched me test the nib against my thumb the way he had taught me when I was six years old, kneeling beside the inkstone in our one room, learning letters off the backs of old manifests because paper cost money and my mother's patience was finite.

"Where do we begin?" I asked.

"Not where you think." He drew a breath, and I saw him gather himself the way divers gather — one long pull, everything held. "You begin with what you copied. Tell me what the ledgers say."

* * *

So I told him.

I told him about the counting house in Saltmere, the smell of tallow and cheap tobacco, Supervisor Quill's wet lips, the manifests I had copied in my neat invisible hand. I told him about the entry marked *losses — divers, unserviceable*, and how the words had sat in my chest for weeks like a swallowed hook. I told him about the second spine of the books, the private ledger Elias had traced through three ports, and I watched my father's face while I read him names: Maren Oduya. Sef Betancourt. Liss Harrow, who had taught me to equalize my ears by humming. Anaya Voss, who used to braid Nettie's sister's hair on the pier.

At each name he nodded, once, like a man confirming a count.

"And the dates?" he asked. "Read me the dates against the tonnage."

I read them. He closed his eyes and worked sums in his head — I could see them moving behind the lids, the old arithmetic of loads and depths and air — and when I finished he was silent so long I thought the tide had gone out of him again.

"The winter entries are false even in the private book," he said finally. "They logged eleven losses in the storm quarter. It was thirty-one. They wrote the storms into the margin as cause." He opened his eyes. "There were no storms that quarter. I know because I was the one who argued against the quota, and Hargrave's predecessor told me the quota was set in Meridian City and the weather would be arranged to suit."

"Arranged," I repeated.

"Papered," he said. "Storms are convenient, girl. A drowned diver needs no inquest if the sea is blamed, and the sea cannot be cross-examined." He tapped the blank page in front of me. "That is why it must be done properly. Not a letter. Not a complaint. A testimony, dated, witnessed, specific — numbers they can check against their own hidden book, so that when their lawyers come sniffing, every line of mine opens a door in theirs. Write: *Tomas Brightwater, dive-master, Meridian Company, engaged year of the Double Harvest.* Then today's date. Then we go line by line, and you will write only what I say, and I will say nothing I cannot prove."

My pen hovered. Twelve years of questions stood behind my teeth like a crowd at a gate, and he had just given me orders as if I were still six years old at the inkstone.

"Papa." The word came out rougher than I meant it. "Before the numbers. Before any of it. Where were you?"

* * *

He didn't flinch. I'll give him that; he didn't flinch, and he didn't reach for me either, and I understood that both restraint and distance were the same discipline, the same wall he had built to survive whatever these rooms had been before we broke into them.

"The wreck of the *Cormorant*," he said. "You were fourteen. They told you I went down off the Gullet and didn't come up."

"They showed us a boot," I said. "One boot. Mother kept it on the shelf for a year before she threw it in the harbor."

"I know about the shelf." His voice went very quiet. "Pell read me your mother's letters sometimes, when he wanted to hurt me. It was the only cruelty available to him, and he was a thorough man in his small way." He looked at the window, at the light lying flat on the water beyond. "I wasn't lost off the Gullet. I was pulled off the *Cormorant*'s wreck by the company's own boat, along with four others, because we had seen the hold manifest. Munitions, Rosalind. War cargo, insured twice, written off once — and divers sent down to salvage it anyway, in defiance of every law of the sea and every contract ever signed with a diver's thumbprint. We were meant to die with what we knew. Three of us didn't drown fast enough to suit them, so they brought us here instead. Dead men who breathe are cheaper than funerals, if you feed them rice and call it charity."

"And Mother waited twelve years—"

"I know what she did." For the first time his composure cracked, just at the seam, and I saw the man inside the discipline, the one who had spent twelve years listening to a stranger read his wife's letters aloud as punishment. "I know every year of it. Don't you dare think I don't. And don't you dare think an apology from me will weigh anything against it, because it won't, and I won't insult her memory by offering one cheap."

The room was very still. Out on the water a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"So what do you offer?" I asked. My hands were steady on the pen. I was proud of that and ashamed of the pride in the same breath.

"The truth," he said. "All of it, in order, in ink, in your hand, which is the best hand on this coast and always was. That's all I have. A man who comes back from the dead owes the living everything he remembers, and I remember everything." He straightened against the chair back. "Write the heading. Then ask me anything you want between the lines — I won't refuse you a question. But the record comes first, because the record is the only thing they can't burn out of us. Ask me after. There will be time after. I intend there to be time after."

It was not forgiveness. I understood that even as I dipped the pen. It was a bargain, struck between two people who had been robbed of the years in which bargains are freely given, and it was the only honest thing on the table. I took it.

"*Tomas Brightwater,*" I wrote, "*dive-master, Meridian Company, engaged year of the Double Harvest. Testimony taken at—*" I stopped. "Where do we say?"

"Nowhere," he said, and for the first time since I'd known him grown, my father smiled, and it was crooked and tired and entirely his own. "Nowhere yet. Let them wonder. Let them spend a week trying to find this island after the commission reads it. Write *taken in witness*. They'll hate that. Good."

* * *

We worked through the morning and into the afternoon.

He spoke slowly, the way he had once called depth-markings up a line — measured, unhurried, each word placed like a stone in a breakwater. The date of the *Cormorant*. The names of the five divers ordered down past safe air. The tonnage figures, which he recited from memory with a precision that made the hairs rise on my arms, because I had copied those same figures in the counting house and knew them to the crate. The order given by the port factor — he gave the man's name, rank, and the exact phrase used, *proceed regardless of loss* — and the date the insurance claim was filed, eleven days before the last of the five was officially listed as drowned.

"Eleven days," I said, pausing. "They claimed the loss before the men were dead."

"One of them was still alive," my father said. "Me. Write it exactly so. *The undersigned attests he was living on the eleventh day, in the custody of the Meridian Company, and was thereafter recorded as deceased.*" He watched me write it. "Let their lawyers chew on that sentence until their teeth crack."

Between the lines, as promised, I asked.

What the rooms had been like, before. (Bad. He gave me one sentence and a look that closed the door.) Whether he had known, all this time, that Mother and I were in Saltmere. (Yes. Pell had made sure of it. Knowing was part of the punishment.) Whether he had written to me. (Forty-one letters, confiscated at the boat; he could describe the ink he'd used, and later, God help me, I would find the inventory slip among Elias's papers — *correspondence, prisoner, 41 pcs., destroyed per instruction* — and I would need to walk out onto the rocks and stand in the wind for an hour.)

And once, only once, I asked the question underneath all the others: "Did you ever think of us? In the bad times? Or did you make yourself forget?"

He was quiet a long moment. The tide of him seemed to go very far out, and I braced for it to come back as anger.

"I counted," he said at last. "The way you count breaths on a long dive. Not to hope. Hope uses air. I counted to keep the shape of you — a girl growing, a woman grieving, a wife waiting — fixed in my mind, so that when I came up, if I ever came up, I would know what I was swimming toward." He looked at me directly then, and his eyes were wet and perfectly steady. "I did not picture you forgiving me, Rosalind. I pictured you accurate. I hoped for accuracy. Forgiveness, if it exists, will have to be found somewhere in the margins, and I've never trusted margins."

I bent my head and wrote his sentence down word for word, because it belonged in the record too, though he hadn't meant it for the record, and perhaps that was the first forgiveness: that I kept even his unguarded words as truth.

* * *

Nettie came in at dusk with fish and rainwater and the news that the channel was clearing, that Elias judged we could run for Saltmere in two days if the weather held, that Pell's boat was due Thursday and someone ought to decide what to do about Pell. She said all this to the middle of the room, carefully not looking at the pages drying in rows across the table, and my father studied her with open interest.

"This is the one whose sister is in the ledger," he said. It wasn't a question. He had listened, these five days, even in the fever. "Anaya Voss."

"Anaya," Nettie said. Her chin came up. "You knew her name."

"I knew her laugh," my father said. "She laughed on the ladder coming up, always, even with her ears bleeding. Some people celebrate being alive that way." He inclined his head to her, formal as a captain. "Your sister will be in my testimony by name, girl, and I will not spell it wrong."

Nettie's face did something complicated and fierce, and she turned quickly to the stove, and the fish spat in the pan, and nobody said anything else for a while, because some things are said best to a fire.

After she'd gone back out to the boat, my father gathered the dried pages, squared them, and tied them with the cord from his blanket — the same knot, I noticed, that he'd used on his tool rolls all my childhood, the knot I could tie blind. He weighed the bundle in his hand.

"How many pages?"

"Twenty-two."

"And your hand held all the way through?"

"It held."

He nodded slowly, and then he did the thing I had not let myself hope for. He reached across the table, awkwardly, left hand curling and right hand stiff, and laid his palm flat on the top page — over my handwriting, over his own words rising through it — and left it there, as if feeling for a pulse in the paper.

"When we get to Saltmere," he said, "they will offer you money to stop. Hargrave's masters will offer you money, and then they will offer you threats, and then they will try to make you small in front of the commission — an indentured girl, a liar, a daughter making stories to dignify a drowned father. When they say *drowned father*, look at me. That's all. Look at me, and keep reading." He slid the bundle across the table to me. "These are yours now. I dictated them, but they're yours. A father can give a daughter little enough after twelve years. Give me the chance to watch you carry this one ashore."

I took the testimony and held it against my chest, twenty-two pages warm from the sun and smelling of ink and driftwood smoke, and outside the window the channel glittered, clear and open, all the way to Saltmere.

Two days, Elias had said. Two days, and then the customs hall, and then whatever Hargrave had already set in motion against us — because Nettie had seen the signal flags on the supply boat that evening, and one of them, hoisted where no honest boat had reason to hoist it, was the company's private mark for *search in progress*.
