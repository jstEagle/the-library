## 18. Fire on the Water

The counting house burned the way everything in Saltmere did — quickly, greedily, with the harbor wind feeding it.

But I am ahead of myself. Begin where the night began: at the water stairs behind the old customs shed, where Hargrave had named his terms and Nettie stood between two company men with her wrists already bound, her chin up, her eyes finding mine across thirty feet of black water and refusing to apologize.

"You came," she said. Not to me. To Elias. As if she'd half expected us not to, as if she'd made her peace with that. She was sixteen and had been diving since she was nine, and she knew better than anyone alive what it meant when a thing was too good — an easy breath, a fair price, a rescue without a cost.

"We brought your papers," Hargrave said pleasantly. He was a small man who wore largeness like a borrowed coat, standing on the lowest stair with the tide lapping at his polished boots. "Every page you stole from my archives, Corvane. Every copy you've made since. In exchange for the girl, unharmed, and no further inquiry into how she came to be carrying your messages." He smiled at me then, and it was the smile of a man who believed he had already counted this transaction twice and found it balanced. "And the diver girl keeps her name off any list."

*Any list.* I understood him perfectly. There were lists and there were lists, and I had spent my whole life learning which kind a person could end up on.

Elias stood beside me with the satchel against his chest, both arms wrapped around it the way a man holds a child in deep water. The copies inside represented four years of his work — manifests, loss records, the second spine of the ledger, pages in Tomas's hand, pages in mine. Everything we had built our case from. Everything except the one thing Hargrave didn't know existed: my father's testimony, written in his own failing hand during those lucid intervals on the island, sewn flat against the left side of my diving coat where the lead weights usually sat.

"Give them over," Hargrave said, "and walk away. Both of you. There will be an armistice in eleven days. Men of sense are already choosing their sides for the peace."

"And the divers who died?" Elias asked. His voice was steady, but I could feel the tremor in him through the space between our shoulders. "The women listed as unserviceable? Their families?"

Hargrave's expression did not change at all, and that was the most terrible thing about him. "Are a rounding error," he said, "in a war much larger than they were."

I felt something go very still and very cold in me, the way the water goes below forty feet, where the sun stops mattering and only the pressure speaks. I had heard men talk like that before. Overseers. Clerks. My whole life I had heard it, and my whole life I had swallowed it, because swallowing was how you survived.

Nettie looked at me across the water. She didn't say *don't*. She was too proud, and too knowing. She just looked, and her look said what she'd told me once on the wreck grounds, laughing, spitting saltwater: *We're worth more than they say. Say it back to me.*

"Rosalind," Elias murmured.

I reached out and put my hand flat on the satchel. Not taking it. Resting it there, the way you rest your palm on a door before opening it onto a room you can't take back.

"The papers," I said to Hargrave, "for Nettie. But she walks past us first. Past us, into the boat, and we push off together, and your man on the wall doesn't raise that lantern signal until we're clear of the pilings."

Hargrave sighed, a man inconvenienced by the haggling of peasants. "You're in no position to negotiate terms."

"No," I agreed. "I'm in the position of holding the only thing you want. That's a different position than most people get."

For a moment — one long swell of the tide — I thought he would refuse. Then he nodded to the man holding Nettie's arm, and she was released, and she walked toward us along the wet stones with her bound hands and her spine straight as a mast, and I thought, foolishly, in that last soft minute, *we might actually come through this.*

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong at sea: all at once, and because of something small.

Later I learned the shape of it. One of Hargrave's men — the one on the customs shed roof — had been given a second set of orders, the kind a fixer always writes in case the first set goes soft. When Nettie passed the midpoint of the stones, when she was close enough that I could see the rope burns on her wrists, he raised the lantern anyway. Two flashes. And down the quay came the harbor patrol, six men with truncheons and a warrant with Elias's name on it, and Hargrave's face did something I had never seen it do: it lost its pleasantness entirely.

"Take the satchel," he said, no longer negotiating, no longer pretending. "Shoot the clerk if he runs."

Elias ran. Of course he ran — not away but sideways, along the waterline, drawing them after him, and I saw the whole geometry of it in a heartbeat: he was giving me the moment, spending himself to buy it, and every instinct in my body screamed against letting him pay that price while I stood safe with the true testimony strapped against my ribs.

I threw the satchel.

Not to him. Into the fire barrel.

There was a brazier by the customs shed — the watch kept it burning against the fog — and I don't know that I even decided. Four years of Elias's work, three ports of evidence, every copied page we had: it arced through the dark trailing loose sheets like a bird molting in flight, and hit the coals, and caught.

The sound it made was almost nothing. Paper doesn't scream. It flares white at the edges and curls and goes to ash in seconds, and the wind took the burning sheets up off the top of the pile and scattered them across the black water like a migration of dying stars, each one carrying some dead woman's name or some falsified tonnage out into the harbor, glowing, sinking, gone.

Everyone stopped. Even the patrol. Six armed men and a company fixer stood watching the evidence of their crimes burn itself to light on the water, and in that held breath — that one astonished, stupid, human pause — Nettie tore free of the dazed man beside her and dove.

Straight off the stones into the harbor, bound hands and all, the way she'd dive off the wreck grounds, clean as a needle. I went in after her. The cold closed over my head and the world became lantern-light shivering on the surface above and the great blind dark below, and I found her by feel — her hair, her shoulder — and got my arm around her chest and swam for the pilings with her kicking hard beside me, freed hands and all; she'd worked the rope loose against a barnacle-crusted piling underwater, of course she had, she'd been born to untie knots in the dark.

Behind us, shouting. A shot, flat and enormous over the water. Then another, and a cry that wasn't Nettie's and wasn't mine, and I knew whose it was by the way my chest seized, some knowledge older than thought.

* * *

We pulled Elias into the drainage culvert under the fish market an hour later, and by the light of a shielded candle I saw what the second shot had bought.

His left side, below the ribs. Through and through, the ball having crossed him on its way to somewhere near where my head had been. He lay on the wet stone with his shirt soaked black and his face gray as old rope, and he was — this is the part I cannot make gentle — he was smiling.

"Saw it," he whispered. "Saw the pages burn. Best — best thing I ever watched."

"Don't talk." I had his coat off, wadded, pressed hard against the entry wound, and my hands were doing the work while the rest of me stood somewhere far away screaming. "Nettie — the fishmonger's stairs, there's a woman, Maren, she owes me, she'll have needle and spirits—"

Nettie was already gone, running bent-double through the culvert dark, and I was alone with him and the candle and the drip of the harbor coming through the stones.

"Rosalind." He caught my wrist. His grip was shockingly weak. "Listen. The copies were — the commission would have called them copies. Copies can be disputed. Forged. Dismissed." Each word cost him visibly, breath by breath. "What's in your coat is his hand. Original. Witnessed by a notary on the island — you told me. It was always — the only thing that mattered. I just — carried the decoys."

"You carried four years of your life," I said, and my voice broke on it, and I hated that it broke, and let it.

"My life." He laughed, and winced, and laughed again anyway, softer. "My life was a ledger room and a cough and a window facing a wall. You gave me — " He stopped. Looked at me with eyes going bright and unfocused, and I pressed the cloth harder and willed the blood to stay in him by sheer hatred of losing anything else. "Eleven days," he whispered. "Commission sits in eleven days. Whatever happens to me — you walk in that hall. Openly. Like you said. Promise me the hall, not the surgeon. Promise me the record."

"I promise you both," I said. "I'm done choosing. Do you hear me? I'm done. I spent my whole life believing safety and truth were different doors and I could only knock on one, and I am *done*—"

He'd gone faint again, and I put my mouth near his ear and talked to him the way divers talk in the dark, low and rhythmic, breathing words instead of air: about the shallows at dawn, about the way light comes down through green water in bars, about the first time I ever held my breath on purpose and felt the world open instead of close. Diver's talk. Stay-with-me talk. Somewhere in it his hand found mine and held on, and held on, and held on.

* * *

Maren came with her needle and her raw spirit and her river of curses, and between the three of us women we dragged Elias back from the edge by lamplight, the bullet out, the wound packed, fever banked like a fire in wet wood. By the gray hour before dawn he slept, shallow but real, his breath whistling a little in the wound side.

Nettie sat against the culvert wall with her wrists wrapped, watching the thin line of light grow above the drain grate. Her sister's name — Sefa, listed unserviceable at nineteen — was ash on the harbor now, along with everything else. She'd watched it burn from the water like I had.

"I keep thinking I should feel worse," she said. "Four years of his work. Gone in a minute."

"It wasn't work," I said. "It was a copy of work. The work is—" I touched my own ribs, where the oilcloth packet lay flat and patient against my heart, twelve pages in my father's hand, dictated in his cracked voice while I transcribed and he corrected, corrected, corrected. "The work is in here. It was always going to come down to this. One original. One voice. Everything else was scaffolding, and scaffolding is meant to burn."

Nettie was quiet a while. Then: "They'll be looking for us now. All three. Properly looking."

"Yes."

"And the patrol saw your face at the water stairs. Hargrave knows you were there. He knows the satchel was yours to throw."

"Yes."

"So it's not hiding anymore." She said it not with fear but with a kind of wonder, turning the fact over like a pearl of strange color. "Whatever's left of invisible — that's gone."

I looked at Elias sleeping. At Nettie with her rope-burned wrists. Out through the grate at the graying sky over Saltmere, where the customs house roof would show scorch marks by full light and the whole city would know someone had finally, openly, struck at Meridian.

Twelve years I had made myself unremarkable. Carried the ledgers, carried the cargo, carried the secrets, kept my eyes down and my name small. I had believed invisibility was survival. Standing in that culvert with ash on my hands and blood under my fingernails, I understood at last that it had only ever been survival's shadow — a way of being present in the world without being *in* it, a drowning practiced so long it felt like swimming.

Let them look for me, then. Let Hargrave send his men and the patrol send theirs. Eleven days. My father's testimony against my ribs, his voice in my memory correcting each line, his living hand waiting in a safe house across the city for whatever came next.

"Nettie," I said. "Go to the crew. Everyone we trust, everyone who lost someone to those ledgers. Quietly. Tell them in eleven days the commission sits in the customs hall, and tell them—" I paused, and found the words were easier than I expected, as if they'd been waiting in me all along the way ballast waits, low and steady, keeping the boat upright. "Tell them I'll be reading names aloud. Their people's names. Into the record. And ask them if they want to stand in that hall and hear it done."

She grinned at me through the dark — fierce, salt-caked, sixteen years old and unafraid in a way no one should have to learn so young.

"And you?" she said. "What will you be doing?"

I touched the packet beneath my coat one more time.

"Walking in the front door," I said.
