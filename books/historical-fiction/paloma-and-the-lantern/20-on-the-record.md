## 20. On the Record

The customs hall of Saltmere had been built to impress the sea, and it still tried. Its ceiling rose in white arches ribbed like a whale's belly, its windows ran floor to glass in long panes that caught the harbor light and broke it into moving coins across a floor of black-and-white marble worn smooth by a century of boots. On ordinary days the hall smelled of tar, tobacco, and the salt sweat of men arguing freight rates. Today it smelled of ink. Ink and wet wool and something else beneath it — fear, maybe, though no one would have named it that. Fear dressed in good coats and seated behind tables.

They had set the commission at the far end, on a dais draped in bunting from three nations whose negotiators were even now dividing the world in a room we would never see. Three commissioners: an admiral with a face like a closed ledger, a civil jurist from the capital with kind, tired eyes, and a third who never spoke at all but wrote constantly, his pen scratching like a rat inside a wall. Behind them sat the Meridian Trading Company's counsel — six lawyers in charcoal wool, arranged like organ pipes — and at their center, exactly where he had placed himself, Hargrave.

He had shaved. He had a new coat. When our eyes met across all that marble, he did not glare or sneer; he smiled very slightly, the way a man smiles at weather he has already decided to walk through. That frightened me more than any threat ever had. A man who smiles is a man who believes the ground beneath you belongs to him.

I had walked into this hall openly for the first time in my life. I want to record what that was like, because I will forget otherwise. It was like surfacing. Twelve years of walking through Saltmere with my eyes down, making myself narrow, making myself furniture — carrying ledgers, carrying cargo, carrying other people's numbers past other people's eyes without leaving a dent in the air. And now I walked up the center aisle with my father's arm in mine, Tomas Brightwater, alive and upright by sheer will and Nettie's broth and whatever the doctors had done to him in the mission infirmary, and every head in that hall turned, and I let them look.

My legs shook so badly I could feel it in my teeth. I let them look anyway.

* * *

The clerk called the session to order with a bell, not a gavel — a small brass hand-bell, absurdly domestic, like something to summon a maid. The admiral read the day's docket in a voice sanded flat by years of command: remaining petitions for testimony, final sitting before the armistice instruments were signed at week's end. Final. The word went through me like cold water through cloth.

"Petition of Rosalind Brightwater," the clerk read, "diver, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, contract number—"

"I am here," I said, "to speak against that company."

The hall's murmur rose and fell like tide over gravel. The jurist leaned toward the clerk; papers were passed; somewhere behind me a lawyer coughed with the precision of a man clearing his throat to object before anything existed yet to object to.

"You are indentured," the admiral said. Not a question. His eyes found my wrist, where the company's tally-mark scar sat under my skin like a fishhook buried years ago. "You understand your standing. An indentured person's testimony may be received only as—"

"As information," I said. "I've read the commission's rules. All forty-one sections. I can recite them if it helps."

Someone laughed — one short bark from the back of the hall, quickly strangled. The jurist's tired eyes flickered with something that might have been sympathy.

"And the document you intend to submit?"

"It isn't mine." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It's my father's. Tomas Brightwater. Master diver, Meridian contract, listed drowned twelve years ago at the Vane Shoals. He is sitting beside me. You may verify that he exists, which is more than your records allow."

I heard it then, the sound I had imagined a hundred times in a hundred sleepless rehearsals and never once gotten right: the sound of a room full of powerful people understanding, all at once, that something they had agreed to consider closed was open again. It was quiet. A specific quiet, dense as deep water. Somewhere in it, Hargrave uncrossed and recrossed his legs.

* * *

Tomas stood to be sworn. It took him longer than it should have — his hands had a tremor the mission doctors said might ease and might not, and the oath card rattled faintly against the rail until I put my hand over his and we held it still together. When he spoke the words, his voice was rough and low and true, and I thought: *this is the voice that taught me breath. This is the voice that counted me down into the dark.* Twelve years they had written him into nothing, and here he was, taking up space, taking up sound, refusing with his whole ruined body to be a loss column anymore.

The reading took two hours.

I had transcribed his account myself, in the infirmary, over eleven nights, my pen keeping pace with a voice that moved like a man wading — steady, then stuck, then steady again. Now I stood at the witness table and read it aloud, and he confirmed each line as I reached it. "Confirmed." "That's correct." "Confirmed." Two words each time, mostly, delivered like stones laid in a wall.

I read the numbers first. The public ledger losses against the private ones — the second spine, the books within books. Nineteen divers at Vane Shoals recorded as nine. The over-diving quotas of the war seasons, when the company had pushed bottom-time past every limit the old masters honored, because the naval contracts paid by the ton and dead men cost less than slow ones. The salvage crews sent onto wrecks that were known graveyards. The holding island — and there the murmur in the hall changed key entirely, because holding witnesses off the record inside a naval exclusion zone was not a clerical crime, it was a different species altogether.

Then I read the names.

I had asked Tomas for them one night when he was lucid, and he had given them slowly, in the order they died, some going back twenty years, names he had carried the way a shell carries the sound of the sea. I read them out in that hall of white arches: women I had grown up watching surface, gasping and laughing in the light. Women whose funerals had been ledgers' footnotes. Maren Oduya. Sefa Brightwater — no kin, but she'd taken the name the way harbor folk did, borrowing family where the water had left none. Little Ansa, who was sixty-two and dove shallows nobody else could read. Names the company had rendered as *unserviceable*.

By the fourth name, people in the galleries were standing. By the tenth, the hall had gone so silent I could hear the harbor bells outside, marking the hour, indifferent and beautiful. I read every name to the end. My voice did not break until the last one, and then only once, and I made it carry anyway, the way my father had taught me to make breath carry at depth: don't waste it, don't fight it, give it to the water and trust.

* * *

Hargrave rose at the hour mark, as I'd known he would. He had waited, patient as rust, until fatigue had begun its work on the room.

"With respect to the commission," he began, and his voice was honey poured over wire, "counsel for the Meridian Trading Company does not dispute that this woman's father lives. We rejoice at it. A drowned man restored — it's a mercy, and the company extends its condolences for the years lost to misfortune at sea." He turned, and the whole turn was aimed at me like an instrument. "But the commission should understand precisely what it is receiving. This witness is an indentured servant of the company she accuses. She stands to have her debt voided by the very inquiry she requests. She has spent months in the company of—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—a dismissed former clerk currently wanted for theft of corporate documents. Her testimony is not merely interested. It is purchased. Every line of that paper was written by her own hand, from a source too damaged to know what he confirms and too grateful to refuse. Gentlemen, this is not evidence. It is a daughter's grief wearing her father's name as a mask."

He sat down. The lawyers around him did not smile, but the room felt them wanting to.

And I stood there in the ringing silence and understood that everything now depended on what happened in the next hundred heartbeats, and that I had prepared for every weapon except contempt, because contempt was the water I had swum in all my life and I had forgotten it could be used against me.

It was Tomas who answered first.

"Sir." He had risen, gripping the rail, and the tremor in his hands made the wood knock softly, and he let it. "You say my mind is broken. Perhaps. Eleven nights I dictated and my daughter wrote, and if I mistook a year or a tonnage, correct it — bring your own ledgers, the real ones, the second spine, and lay them beside my words. But do not stand in a hall built with pearl money and tell these men that the dead cannot speak through the living. I watched nineteen divers die at Vane Shoals. I carried four of them up myself. Your company wrote me drowned rather than hear what I saw." His voice cracked down the middle and he drove through the crack. "I am broken. What I witnessed is not."

The jurist spoke for the first time. "The commission will hear corroborating testimony if any exists outside this document."

Nettie was already standing.

She came down the aisle in her diving coat with the patches showing, seventeen years old, chin up, and she swore the oath in a clear voice that didn't rattle at all, and she told the commission about the salvage grounds. About the quotas. About the foreman's price — exemption offered for informers, worst shifts dealt to those who refused. About her sister, and the entry she had seen with her own eyes in the counting house: *losses — divers, unserviceable*, and the date that matched the day the sea gave back nothing of her.

Then, because courage is contagious among people who have been breathing the same water, the others came. Old Sefa, gray-braided, who had survived the Vane season and never spoken of it in twelve years, stood and said, "I was there. Everything the girl read, I can confirm, and more that she doesn't know." And Doss, and Marta Ilves, and the boy Fen with his rope-scarred palms — one after another they rose from the gallery like divers coming up in sequence, spaced along the line, each confirming the one below, until the commission's clerk stopped writing notes and started writing pages.

Hargrave objected twice more — chain of custody, hearsay, the character of Elias Corvane — and each objection landed softer than the last, because the room had changed around him. You cannot drown a thing once the whole harbor has seen it floating.

* * *

Near dusk the admiral called a recess, and the commissioners conferred at the dais in voices too low to carry, while the lawyers bent together like reeds and the hall emptied slowly, people drifting out into the lamplit evening carrying the names with them. I heard a woman on the steps telling another, already, the way news travels: *Nineteen at Vane. They wrote nine.* The story was loose now. It was in the air of Saltmere, and no lawyer could file it back into a drawer.

When the bell rang us back, the jurist spoke for the commission.

"The testimony of Tomas Brightwater, as recorded and confirmed, together with the corroborating depositions taken this day, will be entered into the commission's permanent record." A pause; his tired eyes found mine. "Entered over the objection of counsel, and noted as such. The record will reflect that this testimony was received on the final sitting day, hours before the signing of the armistice instruments, and that the commission therefore directs it be appended to the annexes governing reparations claims." He looked down at his papers. "It will survive the signature, Madam Brightwater. Whatever else happens, it will survive the signature."

Whatever else happens. I held those words the way you hold a breath at depth — knowing you'll need every part of them later.

The clerk's pen scratched. The rat in the wall, writing it all down. Outside, the harbor bells rang the change of watch, and the light through the tall windows went from gold to copper, and my father's hand found mine on the rail and gripped it, hard, both of us trembling now and letting anyone see.

Hargrave was gathering his papers. He did it slowly, unhurried, and as he rose he paused beside our table just long enough to speak, low, pitched for me alone.

"You've won a paragraph in an annex," he said. "Companies outlive commissions. Ask your father how well the company forgets."

Then he was gone, charcoal coat swallowed by the crowd, and I stood in the wrecked golden light of the customs hall with the record sealed and my name spoken aloud in a place of power, feeling the triumph hit me full price — every coin of it bought with names, with years, with blood in the water — and I turned to tell Tomas it was done, it was finally done —

— and saw Elias standing at the back of the hall, gray-faced, one arm bound and slung, leaning against the doorframe as though he'd crossed the whole city wounded to get there, and the look on his face was not joy.
