## 21. What Cannot Be Carried

The rain began the morning after the signing, as though the sky itself had been holding its breath through the hearings and could finally let go.

Rosalind stood in the doorway of the customs hall and watched it fall on Saltmere — a soft grey curtain drawn over the rooftops and rigging, over the counting house whose windows had been shuttered since Tuesday, over the harbor where the *Meridian Grace* rode at anchor with her colors struck half-mast in a gesture of mourning so carefully calculated it might have been drafted by lawyers. Perhaps it had been. Everything else about the company's contrition had been.

Behind her, the hall smelled of wet wool and ink and the ghost of lamp oil. The commissioners' table stood bare now, the great ledger — *the* ledger, the restored one, the one with Tomas's testimony sewn into its spine like a keel — already crated for transport to the capital. She had touched its cover one last time before they closed the lid. It had felt warm, absurdly, the way a stone feels warm after a day of sun, holding what it held.

"You're still here."

Tomas's voice. He came down the aisle slowly, one hand on the backs of the benches, because his legs were not yet to be trusted on smooth floors after six years of uneven ones. He had gained back flesh in his face; his eyes had gained back something harder to name. When he reached her he did not take her arm — he never did anymore, having learned, she suspected, that she flinched when people steered her — but stood beside her and looked out at the rain.

"I keep thinking they'll come back," she said. "The lawyers. That someone will stand up and say the record is void, that an indentured diver has no standing, that we made it all up between us." She turned her father's words over, tasting their shape. "An indentured liar. Hargrave's phrase. It's still in my ears."

"He wrote it down," Tomas said. "That was his mistake. Everything Hargrave did, he did on paper. Men who live by paper die by it." He smiled faintly. "You should know. You carried enough of theirs."

Outside, two company officers crossed the square under a single umbrella, moving fast, heads bent together. She recognized neither, which meant nothing; there had been so many names read into the inquiry rolls this week that she had stopped keeping count. Director Ambrose Vell had resigned on Wednesday, his statement read aloud by counsel while he sat with his hands folded like a man at a funeral for someone he didn't much like. Two clerks from the second-spine office had been taken into custody Thursday. A third had fled on the night tide aboard a coal barge, or so the dock gossip said, leaving behind a wife and a very neat set of books that told on everyone above him.

And Hargrave.

Hargrave was simply gone.

* * *

They learned the shape of it on Friday, from Nettie, who heard everything before anyone else because the whole waterfront told her things the way water tells things to sand.

"Gone clean," Nettie said, dropping onto the bench beside Rosalind in the customs hall's anteroom, where witnesses waited to be called. Her sister's name — Maren, listed as *unserviceable loss, salvage grounds, third quarter* — had been read into the record two days ago, and Rosalind had watched Nettie's hands in her lap, watched them open slowly like something unfolding after years closed. "Left his house standing. Left his tea in the pot, Mrs. Oduya swears it. Took nothing but a leather case and the fast packet south." Nettie pulled a face. "The commission says he'll be sought. The commission says a great many things."

"He'll be found," Rosalind said, "or he won't."

"That's it? That's all you've got to say?"

Rosalind considered. She had spent the week learning what victory actually looked like, and it looked like this: partial, grainy, incomplete as a net hauled up with half its catch spilled back. Vell resigned. Clerks arrested. The record amended — forty-one names restored to the rolls, forty-one women and men who had gone into the water for Meridian and been subtracted from history like errata. Forty-one. And the private ledgers, Elias's copies and Tomas's memory together, suggested more than twice that number across three ports, most of them documented nowhere at all, most of them recoverable only if someone walked every coast from here to the Corren Straits asking old questions of older people.

Forty-one names, and it had nearly cost her father, and Elias, and Nettie, and herself, and it was not even half.

"They gave him time to run," she said at last. "Someone warned him. Someone always warns them, Nettie. That's how it works — the ones who keep the books look after each other." She flexed her fingers, feeling the old ache in them, the cold-water ache that never quite left. "I wanted him in a dock. I wanted him to sit where I sat and feel the room look at him. Instead he gets a fast packet and a new name somewhere, and I get—" she gestured vaguely at the hall, the crates, the amended record, "—this."

Nettie was quiet a moment. Then: "Maren got her name back. On paper. In front of everyone." Her voice caught and steadied. "My mother heard it read out. She's been dead nine years and I still had to tell her somehow, so I went to the wall at the chapel and I said it out loud to her stone." She shrugged, fierce-eyed. "It isn't everything. But it isn't nothing."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It isn't nothing."

That was the arithmetic of it, she thought, and no one had warned her that justice came in fractions. The company's charter would be reviewed; the diving contracts rewritten; the indenture clauses — hers among them — struck from the standard forms going forward. A clerk had brought her the paper on Thursday afternoon: *Indenture of Rosalind Brightwater, entered service of the Meridian Trading Company, age eleven — declared void, all obligations discharged, compensation awarded for term served beyond lawful limits.* Compensation. Eleven pounds, four shillings. She had laughed when she saw the figure, and then, without any warning at all, had cried for the first time since the hearings began — not for the smallness of the sum but for what it measured, twelve years priced against a pearl diver's wage, every carried crate and copied page and held breath summed up and stamped paid.

She had taken the money anyway. She was done refusing what was owed, however small.

* * *

Saturday the rain cleared and the light came in hard and white off the water, and Saltmere emerged blinking, hung with bunting for the armistice celebrations it hadn't dared hang during the hearings themselves. Now that the signing was done, now that peace was official and profitable, the flags bloomed overnight along the seawall like something seeded there.

Rosalind walked the harbor road with the voided indenture folded in her coat pocket, touching it through the cloth every few steps like a talisman, or a wound. Tomas was resting — the doctors insisted, and for once he obeyed, perhaps because dictating his account had spent something in him that would take longer than weeks to refill. He slept most of the day and woke at odd hours wanting tea and talk, and she gave him both, greedily, hoarding conversations the way she'd once hoarded breath below the surface.

She found Elias at the end of the north pier, standing where the drying racks used to be. They were gone — dismantled last month for the requisition effort, their poles repurposed, the space swept bare. Only their shadows seemed to remain, printed in salt on the planking, and he stood inside one as though it still sheltered him.

"You walk differently," he said, without turning.

"Do I?"

"Like someone who owns her feet." He glanced back at her, and she saw he was in uniform — not Meridian grey but the plain dark of the commission's investigative corps, the buttons dull, the cut unforgiving. Her stomach performed a slow, cold turn, the way it did ten fathoms down when the light changed without explanation.

"The commission keeps you," she said.

"The commission sends me." He took a folded paper from inside his coat and held it out. Orders, she saw. Official script, commission seal, the works. "Corvane, E., reassigned to the port archives at Corren Landing. There are records there — shipping manifests, hospital rolls, burial registers for the straits stations. If the losses ran through three ports, the other two kept books too, and someone has to go find them and read them properly." He paused. "Someone has to go find the names."

"How long?"

"A year. Possibly two. Possibly the rest of my life, if the archives are anything like the ones here." He tried to make it a joke and mostly failed.

She looked past him at the water. The harbor lay calm, glass-green in the shallows, deepening to that blue-black further out where the salvage grounds began. Somewhere under it lay the munitions ship, and the trench where Nettie had nearly died, and the ledge where Rosalind had first learned that the sea keeps everything and surrenders only what it chooses. Twelve years she had given this water. She had thought, walking down the pier just now, that she felt free of it. She discovered instead that freedom and grief weigh almost exactly the same in the chest.

"When do you sail?"

"Tide after next."

* * *

They walked, after that, without deciding to. Out along the seawall, past the chapel with its wall of names — the original forty-one now carved beneath the weathered stone, fresh-cut letters still pale as scars — and up toward the point where the harbor light stood. Tomas's light, soon. The commission had confirmed his appointment that same week: keeper of the Saltmere lamp, a post for a man with bad legs and good eyes, a salary, a cottage, a reason to climb stairs slowly every evening. He had wept when the letter came, briefly and furiously, and then pretended he hadn't, and Rosalind had let him.

At the point the wind picked up, carrying the smell of kelp and tar and the far-off smoke of celebration cookfires. Below them the tide was turning, the water sliding out over the flats in long silver sheets.

"There's a thing I should say," Elias began.

"Don't." She said it gently. "If you start making promises, I'll have to start believing them, and I've seen what happens to women in this port who believe promises made by men with orders in their pockets."

He shut his mouth. Then, visibly gathering himself: "Then let me say true things instead. True things aren't promises. They're just — inventory."

That startled a laugh out of her, short and real. "Inventory. You would put it that way."

"It's the only language we share." He faced her fully, and the wind pushed his hair back from his forehead, and she saw how tired he was, how the wound he'd taken shielding her at the exchange had healed crookedly, how young he looked with all the purpose stripped away and only the man left standing. "I don't know what I'll find at Corren Landing. I don't know if I'll be sent somewhere worse after. I know that when I think of anywhere worth being, it's a pier in Saltmere with you on it. I know that your handwriting is the best thing I ever learned to read. And I know that if you told me to stay, I'd stay, and be wrong to, because the names need finding, and you'd despise me within a year for staying." He exhaled. "That's the inventory. All of it. No promises."

Rosalind looked at the sea for a long moment. Ten years ago — no, more, twelve, a lifetime, another girl entirely — she had stood on these flats and sworn never to hope, because hope was the thing that had killed her mother by inches, hope deferred until it curdled into waiting and the waiting into silence. She had built her whole life on that vow. Unremarkable. Invisible. Carrying nothing that couldn't be dropped.

But she had testified. She had stood in a hall full of company lawyers and put her voice on the record, and the world had not ended; it had merely changed shape, the way the harbor changes at slack water, everything rearranged and recognizably itself. If she could do that, she could survive the truth about Elias Corvane, which was simply that she loved him, and that love was not a cage unless both people agreed to lock it.

So she would not ask him to stay. And she would not pretend she felt nothing. Both of those refusals were acts of the same new courage.

"Here's mine, then," she said. "I'm not going anywhere either. Not waiting — I want that understood. I'm building something. Nettie and I have the lease on the old Oduya shed on the east flats, and come spring we're taking contract dives independent of any company, and teaching girls to read water and ledger both, because the drowning started with women who couldn't check their own accounts." She heard her own voice steady and strong and marveled at it. "I'll be busy. I'll be busy for years. And when your work brings you through this port — if it does, when it does — you'll find me at the east flats, or at the light, or in the water. And you'll write to me, Elias Corvane, proper letters, in that clerk's hand of yours, and I will answer every one, and none of it is a promise because none of it needs to be."

Elias looked at her the way he had looked at the private ledgers the night they'd opened them: as though the truth were larger than he'd braced for, and heavier, and worth every ounce.

"All right," he said softly. "No promises."

"No promises."

* * *

They went back down into the town as the afternoon slanted gold, and at the corner by the fish market they parted — not dramatically, not like a scene from one of the novels she'd once secretly read in the counting house storeroom. He touched her hand; she held it a moment longer than necessary; then he walked on toward the commission offices and she walked on toward the pier, and the space between them widened at exactly the rate of two ordinary strides, which was somehow the most bearable way she could imagine losing anyone.

Nettie was waiting at the pier head with the lease papers and two mugs of terrible coffee, and Rosalind drank hers looking south along the coast, where the water ran on and on toward Corren Landing and all the unrecovered names sleeping in its archives.

"Spring," Nettie said. It wasn't a question. It was a heading.

"Spring," Rosalind agreed.

Far out past the mole, a ship was making ready — the fast packet, or one like it, canvas rising pale against the brightening sky. She watched it a moment, then turned away, toward the town, toward the light on the point that her father would kindle tonight for the first time, toward the work waiting with its sleeves rolled up.

There was so much that could not be carried out of those years: the drowned who stayed unnamed, the ledgers burned, the man on the fast packet growing smaller with every wave. She set them down, one by one, deliberately, the way a diver sheds weight to rise — not forgetting a single thing, only choosing what her hands were for.

The tide was coming in. She had letters to write.
