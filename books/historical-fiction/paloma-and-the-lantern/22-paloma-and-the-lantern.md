## 22. Paloma and the Lantern

The lamp above Saltmere harbor had been dark for eleven years before my father came home to light it.

I learned that from the harbormaster's widow, who remembered the night the light went out — the same week the company's boats carried my father's name into the column marked *losses*. She said the whole harbor seemed to hold its breath afterward, as if the water itself were waiting to see whether anyone would notice. Nobody did. The company noticed; the company noticed everything that touched its ledgers. But a lamp is not cargo. A lamp is only a promise, and promises were never line items anyone audited.

Now the lamp burns again. My father lights it at dusk, every dusk, climbing the ninety-one steps of the old tower with a patience that still astonishes me when I let myself look at him straight on. He was returned to me half-broken, a man made of paper and pauses, and there are evenings when his hands shake too badly to strike the match. On those evenings he waits at the top of the stairs until I climb up beside him, and we strike it together, two hands cupped around one small flame, and neither of us says anything about which of us is steadying the other.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a year of living has taught me that the order of things matters — that testimony is nothing but memory set down in the right sequence, and memory without sequence is only grief wearing a ledger's coat.

So: a year later. Let me begin where the day begins.

* * *

The tide goes out before dawn in the cold months, and the girls come down to the shallows while the water is still the color of pewter and the air smells of salt and kelp and woodsmoke from the cook-fires behind the drying racks. There are nine of them now. Last spring there were three. Nettie says by next season there will be fifteen, and she says it the way she says everything — flat, certain, already counting — because Nettie has never once in her life underestimated what the women of this harbor can do when someone finally tells them they are allowed.

Our cooperative has no grand name. We call it the Brightwater School, mostly as a joke at my expense, though Nettie keeps a real ledger now, bound in oiled canvas, and on its first page she has written the terms of membership in her careful clerk's hand: *Every diver owns her own catch. Every diver learns her letters. Every diver's name is written down.*

That last clause was mine. Nettie argued for it harder than I did, actually — she stood in the sand with her arms crossed and told me that if I thought names were sentimental, I could go back to carrying other people's paper for the rest of my life. I signed it. I have never regretted signing anything less.

We teach them both, the water and the ledger, because I learned too late that they are the same lesson. The water teaches you what can be held and what must be let go; the ledger teaches you what must never be let go no matter how much it costs you to hold it. A girl who can read a depth gauge but not a contract will be robbed by noon. A girl who can read a contract but not the water will drown by evening. So we do both, every morning, in the cold and the light coming up over the breakwater, and the smallest of them, a sharp-elbowed child named Paloma whose mother dives mussels off the north spit, has taken to reciting the safety breaths like psalms.

Paloma. It was her idea to name the school after herself, or rather it was her idea to ask why we couldn't, and Nettie laughed so hard she had to sit down in the surf, and somehow after that it was simply decided. My father approves. He says a dove is a good patron for divers — a bird that comes back. He says most things briefly these days, and means all of them.

This morning Paloma surfaces from her first unassisted dive holding a shell she believes contains a pearl, and it does not, it never does, and I watch her open it with the particular heartbreak of the young and say nothing, because the finding-out is also a lesson, and because last month she found one that did.

"You'll write it in the book?" she asks, spitting out brine, holding the empty shell up to the grey light like an accusation.

"I'll write it in the book," I promise. "Empty shells count too. They teach the water you're paying attention."

She considers this, decides it is wisdom, and goes back under. Behind us on the beach, Nettie has the older girls working sums — catch weights against shares against the cooperative's fund for widows' oil and winter coal — and their voices carry over the water in a low industrious murmur that sounds, if I am honest, more like the future than anything I ever imagined for this shore.

There is a moment, most mornings, when I stand hip-deep in the shallows with my coat heavy with cold and the sun not yet fully committed, and I feel twelve years fall away and then settle back, differently. Not lighter. Nothing about those years became lighter. But settled, the way silt settles after a storm — no longer choking the water, just lying on the bottom where the light can reach it, where things can grow through it.

My mother waited eleven years for a husband declared dead, and died of the waiting, and I swore on her grave that I would never hope, because hope was the company's best harvest — it grew wild and cheap and they reaped it from us every season. I meant the vow. I kept it for years. And then a man with wet boots fell at my feet carrying letters in a dead man's hand, and I learned that a vow made against hope is still a wager placed on despair, and despair pays out worse.

I don't tell the girls any of this. Not yet. Some of them will need it someday — the ones whose fathers don't come home, whose brothers sign company papers with their eyes down — and when they need it, I will tell them, the way my father told me, in intervals, in sentences with room inside them to breathe. For now I teach them breaths and sums and the reading of currents, and once a week I teach them something else, though I don't call it a lesson.

Once a week, I read them names.

* * *

It began in the autumn, after the commission published the restored record. The hearings had ended the way such things end — not triumphantly, not cleanly, with companies fined and officers retired rather than hanged, with Hargrave gone ahead of the constables to some port where extradition moves slowly — and the record itself arrived printed on cheap paper, four hundred pages, most of them tables. But the tables were the point. The tables had names in them. *Losses — divers, unserviceable*, corrected line by corrected line, each falsified entry struck through and replaced with what it should always have said: a name, a date, a depth, a cause. Marisol Vane, crushed by shifting cargo, 41 fathoms. Odile Ferran, ruptured lung, ordered down twice in one day. Petra Sallow, listed as runaway, actually drowned, actually recorded, actually erased.

Nettie's sister was in there. Third page of the second table. We sat together on the floor of the schoolhouse the night the record came, and Nettie read the entry aloud twice, once fast and once slow, and then she took her pen and copied it into our own ledger in her careful hand, and she said, "Now it's true in two places," and that was all she said, and it was enough.

I couldn't stop thinking about the other places. The record existed in the customs hall archive, under government seal, and in the gazette that ran excerpts for a week before the war news crowded it out, and in perhaps forty private copies held by families who had requested them. Forty copies. For four hundred pages of the dead. I lay awake doing the arithmetic I could never escape: paper burns, ink fades, archives flood, commissions dissolve, and the men who falsified the first books were not men who accepted defeat; they were men who waited.

So we made more places. That is the whole of it, and it is nearly the least of it, and it has become the center of my life, and I would not trade it for the pearl beds of my childhood dreams.

Every seventh evening, after the diving day and the supper of fish stew and bread, the girls who want to stay gather by the stove, and I read from the restored record — a page, sometimes two, no more, because grief is a depth and even freedivers must ascend slowly. The girls copy the names into their practice books, learning their letters from the dead, which sounds grim when I say it aloud and is not grim at all when you watch it happen. They ask questions. Was she your friend? Did she dive the north beds? What's a rupture? Could it happen here? And I answer everything honestly, because these children were raised on honesty about the sea and deserve honesty about the rest, and because the whole catastrophe I lived through was built on answers withheld.

"Are they really dead, or just written down?" Paloma asked once, in her merciless way.

"Really dead," I said. "Written down is how we make sure being dead isn't the end of mattering."

She nodded, satisfied, and copied *Marisol Vane* in her round uncertain letters, and got the V backward, and fixed it, and I felt something in my chest that I have no better word for than peace, though peace is a word I distrusted for most of my life and am only now learning to pronounce.

Because here is what I understood finally, standing at that commission table with my father's hand on my arm and Hargrave's lawyers smiling their thin smiles: remembrance is not mourning. Mourning looks back and stays there. Remembrance is a kind of tending — a lamp trimmed nightly, a wick changed, oil carried up ninety-one steps — and it requires a keeper, and keepers must live, must eat, must love, must get up in the morning and put their coats on wet with dew. The company taught us that the drowned were cargo, written off and forgotten. Our answer was never going to be a monument. Our answer is a school. Our answer is a girl copying a name correctly. Our answer is a light on the water every single night, lit whether anyone looks up or not.

Mostly nobody looks up. That's fine. Lamps aren't for being watched. They're for being there.

* * *

Some evenings, Elias walks me home from the schoolhouse along the breakwater, and we talk about the ordinary enormous things: the price of lamp oil, whether the north spit beds will hold another season, whether Nettie's proposed rules for shared equipment will cause the fight everyone can see coming between the mussel divers and the pearl divers. He is better at the equipment disputes than I am. A year of being a clerk turned investigator turned — what? The commission offered him a permanent post. He declined it in writing, politely, and framed the receipt of his declination, which is the most Elias thing I know.

He stayed, is the thing. After the hearings, when his orders elsewhere finally came through — the navy wanting him, the archives wanting him, everyone wanting the man who could untangle a falsified ledger in an afternoon — he sent back a letter declining all of it, and then he stood in my doorway holding his hat and looking, for the first time since I'd known him, like a man who had run out of official business and didn't know what remained.

"There's a lamp needs trimming," I said, because neither of us was ever going to be eloquent about this, we were people of records and receipts, and some hearts only speak in maintenance.

He trims the lamp now, three evenings a week. My father pretends to supervise. The two of them have worked out a companionship built almost entirely on silence and small technical corrections, and I have watched them from the schoolhouse door and understood that my family, broken and reassembled with visible seams, has grown by exactly one person, and that no one will ever say so out loud, and that this is how the best things in Saltmere are done.

We do not speak of marriage. We speak around it, the way divers speak around a wreck — noting the currents, testing the holds, knowing exactly where it lies. There will be time. We have both learned what time costs when you spend it waiting, and what it yields when you spend it choosing. He is writing a book, actually — a proper history, not the commission's tables but the human account, the salvage grounds and the foremen's prices and the storm-season arrests, all of it sourced from testimony he gathered himself, from divers who trusted him because I vouched for him, which is the debt I hold over him gently and forever. He reads passages to me on the breakwater and I correct him when he gets the diving wrong, which is often, and he takes the corrections with the humility of a man who once nearly drowned in four fathoms of water and was saved by a girl half his size.

"That's not how buoyancy works," I'll say.

"It's how it works in my notes."

"Your notes are wrong."

"My notes are wrong," he agrees, and writes it down, and the gulls argue overhead about something, and the light goes gold and then copper and then gone, and I think: this. This is what they couldn't put in the ledgers. This is the entry that was never theirs to make.

* * *

Tonight is the anniversary, though I am the only one keeping count, and perhaps not even I — the commission hearings ended in a blur of dates, and the record's publication came weeks after, and grief keeps its own calendar anyway. But a year ago tonight, roughly, I climbed these tower steps for the first time with my father, and we lit the lamp together, and he said, "Your mother used to watch for it," and I said, "I know," and we left it at that, because we had already spent a year saying the hard things and had earned a season of saying the easy ones.

He is at the top now when I come up, as he always is, wrapped in the coat I had made for him from sailcloth, because he feels the cold the way the convalescent feel it, in the bones the sea never quite gave back. Ninety-one steps. I take them slower these days on purpose, so as not to arrive breathing hard in front of a man who remembers being strong.

"The wick wants changing tomorrow," he says. "It's burning yellow on the left side."

"I'll bring a new one up at dawn."

"Mm." He strikes the match — steady tonight, his hands cooperating — and the flame takes, and he turns the great lens a quarter-degree with the touch of a man adjusting a jewel, and the beam swings out over the harbor and finds the water and lays its long white road across the swells toward the open sea.

Below us, Saltmere glitters and murmurs. The taverns, the drying racks, the customs hall with its new plaque that half the town pretends not to read. Out past the breakwater, the moored fishing boats rock on the evening swell, and beyond them the dark begins, and the light goes out into it and does not flinch.

"Papa," I say. "Tell me about the first year. The year the light went dark."

He doesn't turn from the lens. He never does; he says the turning away helps him say the turning-toward things. "They moved us at night," he says. "Eleven of us, in the hold of a coastal freighter, told we were being transferred for salvage work. The lamp went out that week because the keeper resigned — he refused to log false soundings for the naval charts, and the company bought his post and left it vacant. I remember looking back from the rail and seeing the harbor black for the first time in my life, and thinking, well. Now they've taken even the noticing."

"And you kept the list anyway. In your head. Eleven names, then thirty, then all of them, through everything." I have heard this account perhaps a hundred times — I transcribed it, after all, sentence by sentence, in that dim room on the island, my pen scratching while he dictated in intervals — but hearing is different from transcribing. Transcription is work. Hearing is receiving. I am still learning to receive.

"Forty-one names," he says. "Forty-one, and I'd have kept forty-one thousand. It wasn't courage, Rosalind. You'll write that in your book someday and I'll deny it. It was spite, at the start. Then it was arithmetic — someone had to know the true sum or the false one would stand forever. Then, near the end—" He stops. The flame gutters slightly in the draft and steadies. "Near the end it was the only thing I owned. They could keep my labor, my health, my years. The list was mine. A man will defend the last thing he owns with whatever he has left."

"With breath," I say.

"With breath," he agrees, and almost smiles.

We stand together in the lamp room while the beam makes its slow rotations, and the wind combs in off the sea smelling of salt and distance, and below us a late boat comes in, running its own small lamp up the harbor road our light has paved for it. Somewhere down there, Nettie is closing the ledger and banking the stove. Somewhere down there, Paloma is asleep with her practice book open to a page of names, some of them backward-lettered, all of them correct in the way that matters. Somewhere out there, in the wide indifferent dark, are the waters that keep what they keep — my mother among them, and Odile Ferran, and Petra Sallow, and the eleven who rode the freighter into the night, and every diver who ever went down on a company contract and came up into a world that declined to write them down.

The lamp cannot call them home. I know that. I have known it since I was a child watching my mother watch the water. The lamp calls to no one; it only shines, steadily, stupidly, faithfully, on the chance that shining matters — that somewhere in the dark a boat, a swimmer, a grieving woman at a window, will lift her head and see that the light is lit, that someone kept the promise, that the noticing resumed.

"Rosalind." My father's voice, quiet under the rotation of the beam. "You're staring at the water again."

"I'm allowed."

"You are," he says. "You're allowed." And then, because he is my father, and because a year of returned years has taught him that the moments must be seized while the hands are steady: "Come. Help me trim it properly. If we're going to burn for them, we'll burn clean."

I take the scissors he offers. Together, keeper and daughter, we trim the wick, and the flame stands up straight and white, and the beam goes out across the water longer and farther than before, all the way to where the dark gets honest, and I stay a while after he has gone down the stairs, alone with the light, doing the only arithmetic that has ever mattered:

One lamp. Every night. Forty-one names, and the ones still unrecovered, and the ones not yet lost, and the girls asleep below with salt in their hair and letters in their hands —

and morning coming, as it does, over the breakwater, over the beds, over the schoolhouse roof,

over all of it, all of it, all of it.
