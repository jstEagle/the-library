## 2. Letters Worth Stealing

The post came up from Ashcombe village on Tuesdays and Fridays, carried by a boy on a bicycle who rang his bell at the lodge gate as though the house might otherwise forget it existed. I collected it at nine, sorted it in the motor room — bills to the left, personal correspondence to the right, anything with a solicitor's crest or a bank's franking straight into the leather case I kept under the seat — and delivered the whole arrangement to Mrs. Callow by half past.

That was the official route. The unofficial one took eleven minutes longer and happened in the motor room, door shut, engine cold, with my thumb breaking the seal of whatever letter seemed worth the risk.

I want to be precise about what I was, because later accounts would make me out to be either a villain or a saint, and I was neither. I was a man who read other people's letters. Not for money. Not, in the beginning, for any reason I could have defended under oath. I read them the way a man reads a newspaper left behind on a train: because it was there, because someone else's life was spread open like a map, and because reading cost me nothing and gave me everything I didn't have — entanglement, consequence, the sense that things were at stake somewhere.

My own life had no stakes. It had wages, a room over the garage, a tin of tobacco, and a Wolseley I maintained better than most men maintain their marriages. I had driven for Mrs. Marchmont fourteen months and in that time she had spoken to me perhaps four hundred words, three hundred of which concerned the condition of the roads. This suited us both. She required a chauffeur the way she required a roof: present, functional, unremarked. I required her the same way.

But letters — letters were where people told the truth, or at least the version of truth they couldn't say aloud, which is more interesting than truth anyway. And Edith Marchmont received more mail than the vicar, the doctor, and the postmistress combined.

* * *

That Friday in late September there were eleven items. Nine went into the ordinary piles. Two went under my thumb.

The first was from a firm called Pargeter & Luce, Solicitors, of Chancery Lane, and its envelope had the heavy, expensive feel of paper meant to survive litigation. The second was thinner, hand-addressed, from a Mrs. H. Quill, Torquay, and I nearly let it go — until I saw that the handwriting had been pressed so hard into the page that the words stood up on the reverse side like braille, which is the handwriting of either rage or fear, and both are worth eleven minutes.

I opened the solicitor first. Business before pleasure; that was my rule, though I'd never examined why.

> Dear Madam,
>
> Further to our conference of the 14th inst., we write to confirm that Mr. Ambrose Marchmont has instructed us formally to contest the provisions of the late Mr. Edmund Marchmont's will as they pertain to the Ashcombe estate and the associated trust instruments. Our client maintains that the 1904 codicil was executed under conditions rendering its validity doubtful, and we are further instructed to raise the question of your client's capacity at the time of execution.
>
> We would draw your attention to the enclosed schedule of assets said to be held in trust, and note that our client considers the matter of the southern holdings — specifically the passage arrangements connected therewith — to be material to the dispute.
>
> We await your instructions and remain, madam, your obedient servants,
>
> PARGETER & LUCE

I read it twice. The first time for the shape of it, the second for the word that snagged like a fishhook: *passage*. Passage arrangements. A solicitor in London did not use the word *passage* about an estate in Devon unless he meant a ship.

I sat in the cold motorcar with the letter in my hands and felt the particular pleasure that I imagine burglars feel — not greed, exactly, but the vertigo of standing inside someone else's walls. Ambrose Marchmont. I knew the name the way you know a name carved on a gatepost: the dead husband's brother, who lived near Bath and sent a card at Christmas with a signature like a spider drowned in ink. He had never visited. Now he was instructing solicitors to question whether Edith Marchmont had been sane when she signed a piece of paper nine years ago, and somewhere in the dispute sat a ship's berth like a plum in a pudding.

I refolded the letter along its original creases. This is a skill, incidentally — refolding. Most amateurs leave a tell, a second crease, a corner turned slightly wrong. I had taught myself to seal wax with a heated knife blade and reseal it so cleanly that even a suspicious eye found nothing. Fourteen months of practice. I don't know when I'd decided it was worth practicing. These things creep up on you.

Then I opened Mrs. Quill's letter, because complicity is a staircase and I was already two steps down.

> Dear Mrs. Marchmont,
>
> You will forgive an old woman's plain speaking, since no one pays me to be polite. My niece Clemence wrote to me last week in a state I can only call rehearsed — she is always rehearsed, poor child, she learned it at your knee — saying she intends to come to Ashcombe before the winter and "make peace." Make peace! In nineteen years she has not made the journey for Christmas, for your illness in '09, nor for the boy's anniversary, and now suddenly, with lawyers circling and the autumn coming on, she finds peace urgent.
>
> I do not know what Ambrose has promised her, if anything. But I know how that family counts, and I know you count the same way, God help you all. If there is something in your keeping that others want, madam, decide now who deserves it, because they will not wait to be asked twice.
>
> Yours in thirty years of friendship,
>
> Harriet Quill

*Clemence.* The daughter. I had heard the name exactly once, from Ruth Callow, and then only sideways, the way you mention a hole in a floor so guests avoid it. I put the letter down on my knee and looked through the windscreen at the wet cobbles of the yard and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that the household I served was not a household at all. It was a board upon which a game had been running for years, and every player had been waiting for the season to turn.

And I had just made myself a player. That's what reading does. You think you're watching; you're not. You're holding the cards.

* * *

At eleven I drove Mrs. Marchmont nowhere, because she was not going anywhere, and instead I carried the post tray up to the morning room where she conducted her empire from a desk that had belonged to her husband and still bore his ink stains, which she had never sanded out, though she sanded out everything else.

She was dictating when I came in. Her secretary — a pale, competent young woman named Miss Ferris whom Edith treated with a courtesy so exact it functioned as a wall — sat with her shorthand pad while Edith stood at the window looking down the valley as though the valley owed her money.

"—and tell him that sentiment is not a legal instrument," she was saying. "If Ambrose wishes to dispute the codicil, he may hire all of Chancery Lane and I will bury him in affidavits. But he will not have the southern holdings, and he will not have the passage, and if he raises the question of my capacity I shall be delighted to be examined by doctors, since I am the only person in this family whose mind anyone has ever doubted who also happens to be right."

Miss Ferris's pen scratched. I set the tray on the desk. Edith did not look at me — she rarely looked at servants, or rather she looked at them the way she looked at furniture, confirming placement without interest — but she said, "Is that all of it?"

"All of it, ma'am."

"Nothing from Bath?"

"No, ma'am." The lie came out level and warm as tea. Fourteen months of practice.

Something moved behind her face — not suspicion; I'd have recognized suspicion, I lived off it — something more like disappointment, quickly filed away. "Very well. Miss Ferris, take that down: *To Mr. Pargeter, acknowledge receipt, instruct him to proceed on all fronts, and inform him that the passage remains as arranged and will not form part of any negotiation.*"

The pen scratched again. *As arranged.* So there it was, confirmed from the other direction: a berth on some vessel, fixed, reserved, non-negotiable, worth hiring lawyers over, worth a daughter's sudden return, worth — I understood this only dimly then — whatever it was Edith Marchmont was really doing when she sat at that desk every morning keeping her accounts.

"Vane," she said, as I reached the door.

"Ma'am."

"The car will be wanted Thursday. Ashcombe and back, and I'll want you to wait. There's a parcel from the stationmaster's office that mustn't sit in the damp."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Vane—" Now she looked at me, fully, and I had the sensation of being weighed on scales built for heavier goods. "If any letter comes from Bath, you will bring it to me directly. Unopened. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly, ma'am."

She turned back to the window. I closed the door softly behind me and stood in the corridor with my heart going like a cheap watch, because she had said *unopened*, and you only say *unopened* to a man when you suspect he opens things.

Or — and this thought arrived later, walking back down to the motor room, and it arrived the way bad news arrives, all at once and dressed as arithmetic — or she suspected nothing at all, and the word was aimed at whoever else might touch the post between the gate and the desk. Ruth. Miss Ferris. The boy on the bicycle. Everyone was a reader in this house. Perhaps that was what she was really keeping accounts of: not debts, but readers.

* * *

That evening I did what I always did with stolen knowledge: I filed it. In my head there was a room — I had built it deliberately, years ago, out of boredom — with drawers for each member of the household. Edith's drawer was the fullest. Into it went: *Ambrose contests the 1904 codicil; questions her capacity; wants the southern holdings and the passage. The passage is separate from the estate. It is reserved, arranged, and she will not negotiate it. A daughter exists, named Clemence, estranged nineteen years, expected before winter, motive suspect per Mrs. Quill of Torquay.*

I closed the drawer. And then, for the first time in fourteen months, the room bothered me.

Here is the trouble with detachment: it works beautifully right up until the moment it doesn't, and then it works against you, because you've had no practice carrying anything. I had spent my adult life perfecting the art of not mattering to anyone, and I had succeeded so completely that when knowledge finally landed on me with weight attached, I had nowhere to put it down. I couldn't tell Ruth — telling Ruth meant admitting I read the post. I couldn't tell Miss Ferris — Miss Ferris reported to Edith the way a barometer reports weather, faithfully and without mercy. I couldn't tell the vicar; I didn't even dislike the vicar enough for that.

So I sat in the motor room with the lamp turned low and the rain starting against the windows — real rain, steady, the kind that in this valley went on for days and lifted the river — and I told myself the story I always told myself: *you are a bystander. You drive the car. What these people do with their inheritances and their passages and their nineteen-year grudges is weather to you. It passes over.*

But I could hear the lie now, and hearing it changed nothing, which is the definition of complicity. I had taken the letter's weight into my hands and my hands had remembered it. Somewhere south of here a steamer waited with one berth held empty, and somewhere north a brother hired lawyers, and somewhere on a train a rehearsed young woman was already packing, and I knew all of it, and none of them knew I knew, and that asymmetry — that was power. Small, dirty, delicious power. The first I'd ever had.

I got out the ledger I kept for the motor expenses — petrol, oil, tyres, a column of figures in my neat clerk's hand — and I looked at it for a long time without writing anything, because I was thinking about ledgers, about how a woman like Edith Marchmont would keep hers, and about the phrase *as arranged*, and about what kind of arrangement survives a family contesting it in Chancery Lane.

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the village the river would be coming up over its stones, brown and quick, and the bell on the old bridge — they rang it in flood season, one stroke for rising water, three for closing — hung silent in the dark.

It would not stay silent long. I remember thinking that, quite clearly, before I blew out the lamp: *that bell will ring soon, and when it does, everyone in this house will discover which side of the water they're standing on.*

I just didn't yet know that I'd counted myself, wrongly, among the safe ones.
