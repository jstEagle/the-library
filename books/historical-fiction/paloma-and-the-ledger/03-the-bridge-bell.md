## 3. The Bridge Bell

The bell rang at half past six in the morning, three strokes, and I knew before my feet touched the floor that the week was over.

Three strokes meant closing. One stroke meant the water was up; you could hear it all season, a kind of dull metronome under everything, and after a while you stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But three strokes — three strokes came once a year, sometimes not at all, and when they did, every errand in Ashcombe Vale suddenly had a deadline stapled to its back.

I dressed fast. Not because there was anything to do yet — there never is, at first; the bell is only a bell until someone makes it mean something — but because I wanted to be downstairs before anyone else could arrange their faces. A household is like an orchestra tuning: if you arrive late, you hear only the finished performance, all the discord smoothed away. I preferred to catch the tuning.

Ruth Callow was already in the kitchen, of course. Ruth was always already anywhere, as though she'd been installed with the plumbing. She stood at the long table with a pencil and a sheet of brown paper, writing.

"Lists," she said, without looking up. "Before you ask."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to stand there looking useful, which is worse." She tapped the pencil against her teeth. "Post office for Mrs. Marchmont's letters to go south — they'll take the mail van over tomorrow morning, last run before the bridge board shuts it. Druggist. The ironmonger wants paying. And Tobias needs new boots before the winter, so he's coming into the village with us whether he likes it or not."

Tobias was twelve that autumn and growing out of everything he owned at a rate Ruth described as ruinous. He sat by the stove now, half asleep, hair flattened on one side, eating bread and dripping with the concentration of the young.

"The bridge board," I said. "How certain are we of the date?"

"Certain enough." Ruth's pencil didn't pause. "Hodgett rang the three strokes himself, and Hodgett doesn't ring three until the water's over the second marker. That gives them two days of arguing about it, then it's shut till the thaw." She looked up then, and something crossed her face — quick, gone again, like a fish turning under brown water. "You'll want to get the motorcar over before then too."

"It stays on this side."

"It's no use on either side if it can't cross."

She was right, and I hated being told things I'd already worked out, which was most of the time, which was perhaps why I liked Ruth better than I admitted to myself. The garage stood on the near bank with the house; the village, the station, the doctor, the whole functioning world lay across the bridge. For eight months of the year this made no difference whatsoever. In flood season it turned a car into a very expensive piece of garden furniture.

"Mrs. Marchmont's correspondence," I said. "Anything urgent?"

"There's always something urgent. That's what makes it correspondence." She folded the list along its crease and held it out to me. "Take the boy. He's been indoors three days sulking and I can't bear the smell of it."

Tobias perked up at that with the shamelessness of his age. "Can I steer?"

"You cannot steer," Ruth and I said together, which was the first thing we'd agreed on all month.

* * *

The rain had settled into that steady grey persistence that doesn't fall so much as arrive, from every direction at once, as though the valley were being slowly posted to itself. The river had come up over its stones the way I'd imagined it the night before — brown, quick, muscular — and where in August you could see the gravel beds through six feet of clear water, now the water ran opaque and full, carrying fence rails, a dead sheep, half somebody's henhouse roof downstream toward the sea.

The bridge itself was old, older than the road, older probably than the parish: five stone arches with a wooden deck, and hanging from a bracket at the near end, the bell — a ship's bell, they said, salvaged from some wreck generations back, though nobody agreed which wreck. It gave a flat bronze note that carried the length of the vale. Every child in Ashcombe could tell you the code. One stroke, rising water. Three strokes, closing. Nobody ever needed to explain why. You learned it the way you learned your own name, and with roughly the same understanding of what it meant.

Tobias walked beside the car down the hill because I refused to let him ride on the running board and he refused to admit defeat quietly. At the bridge end Hodgett himself stood under an oilskin, watching the water with the proprietary air of a man who has personally threatened it for forty years and considers its behavior a professional matter between them.

"Second marker's gone under," he said to me, nodding at the river. "Third'll go tonight or tomorrow. Board meets Thursday."

"And after Thursday?"

"After Thursday, nobody crosses but the fish." He spat, with feeling, into the current. "Your mistress will want her post sent today. Mail van comes at eleven."

"It's seven now."

"Then you've four hours to remember everything you've forgotten." He said it cheerfully. Hodgett's cheerfulness had the texture of gravel, but it was genuine; disaster was, for him, simply weather with better conversation.

Tobias had drifted to the parapet and was dropping twigs over the side to watch them vanish downstream.

"Away from the edge," I said.

"He's fine," Hodgett said. Then, lower, just to me: "Water like that takes grown men. Keep him off."

I looked at the boy — wiry, dark-haired, his mother's chin and nobody's patience — and felt nothing much, I told myself. He was a fact of the household, like the boiler or the damp patch in the morning room. Facts don't require feelings. That was my system and it had served me well for thirty-one years.

I called him away from the parapet anyway.

* * *

The village at nine o'clock was a village performing urgency. The draper's had a queue. The ironmonger had sold out of lamp oil twice since Monday and was selling candles at prices that would have embarrassed a pirate. Everyone spoke in the same register — brisk, slightly too loud, the voice people use when they're frightened but have agreed among themselves not to be.

"Last chance for the south post," the postmistress said, stamping Edith Marchmont's letters with more force than the stamps required. "After tomorrow, nothing leaves the vale till spring. You might as well write to the moon."

"Some people do," I said, and she smiled because she thought I was joking.

I wasn't, entirely. I'd spent years reading other people's letters — I won't pretend otherwise, not here, not in my own account of things — and a fair number of them were addressed to people who might as well have been on the moon: the dead, the departed, the ones who never wrote back. Edith Marchmont wrote perhaps a dozen such letters a year to a man named Halloran who lived somewhere warm and answered perhaps twice. I read them all. They were less correspondence than ritual, letters posted into the past.

There was one letter in the batch that morning that was different, though I didn't know it yet. Thick envelope, solicitor's crest, addressed in that careful copperplate that law firms use when they're about to ruin somebody's Christmas. I noted it the way I noted everything: filed, unopened, waiting.

Boots next. The cobbler measured Tobias's feet and pronounced them "half a size from ruin," which seemed to delight the boy enormously.

"When the bridge shuts," Tobias said, while the cobbler marked the leather, "we're stuck up there with her."

"Tobias."

"Well, we are." He wriggled his toes with the frankness of the very young. "Mum says she eats alone even at Christmas."

"Mum says a great many things," the cobbler said comfortably, "that are none of our business. There. Don't grow any more till Easter."

But walking back, the boy was quiet, and I caught him looking up the valley toward the house — a grey smudge under the grey sky, windows catching what little light there was — with an expression I couldn't file under any of my usual categories. Not sulkiness. Something almost like dread, though a boy his age had no business dreading that house; he was staff's son, beneath its notice, safely outside all its weather.

I assumed, then, that he dreaded boredom. Winter shut in with nothing but his mother and a silent employer. A reasonable fear, for a twelve-year-old.

It was the wrong fear entirely. But I wouldn't learn that for weeks, and by then the water would be doing its own arithmetic.

* * *

Ruth Callow counted the change twice, because counting twice was how Ruth loved the world: precisely, skeptically, expecting to be cheated and determined not to be.

She'd sent Mirabel and the boy ahead with the parcels and stayed to settle the ironmonger's bill herself, because Mrs. Marchmont's money passed through Ruth's hands and Ruth trusted neither banks nor chauffeurs further than she could watch them. This left her a quarter hour alone in the village, which she spent the way she always did — standing outside the bakery window, not buying anything, looking at the iced buns she'd stopped allowing herself in 1904 when Thomas died and the wage became one wage.

Thomas. Even now, eight years on, the name arrived in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. You waited for it to hit bottom. It never quite did.

He'd worked the mill, Thomas, down on the flat by the river, and he used to say the same thing every flood season, standing in the yard with his collar up: *water's patient, Ruthie. That's what people forget. Water can wait longer than we can.* And then he'd gone and proved himself right in the stupidest way imaginable — not drowned, not swept off, but taken by the fever that came up the valley with the floods two winters running, the one the doctor called river fever and everyone else called the flood sickness, the one that filled the churchyard every other autumn and spared nobody's opinion of it.

Tobias had been four. He remembered his father as a hat and a laugh, which Ruth privately considered about the right amount.

So when the bell rang three strokes, something in Ruth went tight and quiet, the way a rope goes tight before it holds or parts. She hadn't said anything to Vane. What was there to say? *I don't like flood seasons* — well, who did. The lists kept the fear in its pen. Lists were how she prayed: flour, oil, boots, letters south. As long as there was a list, there was a plan, and as long as there was a plan, the water stayed outside.

Except.

Except that Tobias had been flushed around the eyes since Sunday, and sleeping badly, and complaining of his head in a way he never complained of anything. She'd told herself it was the damp, the change of season, boys being boys. She'd told herself it very firmly, several times a day, the way you tell yourself anything that must be true.

River fever started like that. Everybody in the vale knew that. Flush, headache, bad nights — and then the third or fourth day, the burning, and after the burning, either the crisis or the churchyard, and no list in the world would help you then.

The mail van was loading at the post office. Eleven o'clock, Hodgett had said, last run before the bridge board met. Ruth watched the sacks go in and felt the season tilt under her, the way a floor tilts when a house settles.

If the fever took Tobias — when, she corrected herself, when, because hope was a luxury item and she'd been off luxuries since 1904 — the doctor was across the bridge. Everything was across the bridge. The whole world worth reaching was across that bridge, and in two days' time the bridge would close like a book, and she and her boy would be pressed inside the covers with the Marchmonts and their ledgers and their silences until spring let them out — if it let them out.

*Water's patient.*

She bought two iced buns from the bakery, breaking her rule of twenty-two years, and ate hers standing in the rain, tasting nothing at all.

* * *

We drove back with the parcels smelling of leather and lamp oil and the boy chewing his bun in the manner of one who expects it to be confiscated. On the far side of the bridge I stopped the car — I don't fully know why; habit, perhaps, the driver's superstition of checking the road behind — and looked back at the village, and then up at the water.

It had come up another foot since morning. The third marker — a white-painted post on the middle pier — showed maybe eight inches above the surface, and the rain hadn't slackened. I did the arithmetic Hodgett had already done: marker under by nightfall, board meets Thursday, bridge closed Thursday evening or Friday morning at the latest. Call it three days of the old world left.

Three days. Enough time, if you were organized, to do everything that mattered.

That was the assumption sitting underneath the whole day, I realize now — in Ruth's lists and Hodgett's gravel cheer and the queue at the draper's and Edith Marchmont's letters sealed and stamped for the south. Everyone in the vale was making the same quiet wager: that whatever needed doing could be done before the water finished its argument with the bridge. That the important things were portable. That we'd all be standing on the right side of whatever closed.

I believed it too. I want that on the record. I stood there in the rain with the engine idling and believed it as thoroughly as anyone.

Up at the house, Edith Marchmont was at her desk — I saw her through the study window as we came up the drive, upright as a mast, the lamp already lit against the grey afternoon — and beside her elbow, where it always sat, the ledger. I didn't think anything of it then. A woman of her sort kept books; that's how her sort got to be her sort.

I parked the car. I carried in the parcels. I gave Ruth the change, counted twice, and she counted it a third time, which was her way of saying thank you.

And upstairs in the hall, passing the study door, I heard Edith Marchmont say to the empty room, in a voice pitched not for servants or daughters or God but for the page in front of her:

"Thursday, then."

As if the bell had been ringing for her alone.
