## 5. Ruth's Boy

Ruth Callow had learned to read a house the way other women read a face. The Marchmont house spoke in small languages: the creak of the third stair, which meant Mrs. Marchmont was awake and thinking; the particular silence of the pantry when Cook was sulking; the way Mirabel Vane's boots sounded different on the flagstones depending on whether he wanted to be seen. She had catalogued all of it over eleven years of service, and she trusted her catalogue more than she trusted most people.

So when she came up the back stairs at half past six in the morning with the warming pan and heard the sound from Tobias's room — that thin, dry cough, twice, then a pause, then again — her body knew before her mind did. Her hands went still on the handle. The pan's warmth bled through her apron into her hip, and she stood in the corridor like a woman listening to a door she did not want to open.

He was flushed. That was the first thing. Not the healthy flush of a boy who'd been running through the orchard, but a high color sitting on his cheekbones like paint laid on too thick, and beneath it a grayness around the mouth that Ruth had seen before, years ago, in the village below, in the winter the fever came up from the tanneries and took the Hendersons' two girls inside a fortnight.

"Tobias." She set the pan down and put the back of her hand against his forehead. Hot. Dry heat, not the damp heat of a cold. "Tobias, love, look at me."

His eyes found hers slowly, as if they were traveling a long way to get there. He was fourteen. He had his father's jaw and his father's habit of making light of things, which he demonstrated now by saying, "I'm fine, Mum," in a voice sanded down to a whisper.

"You're fine," Ruth said. "Of course you are. You're so fine we'll have the doctor look at how fine you are."

She said it steadily. Inside her chest something had begun to move — not panic yet, panic was a luxury — but a kind of counting. She was very good at counting. Eleven years in this house had taught her to count everything: candles, coals, days between deliveries, the exact number of steps from the kitchen door to the gate. Now she counted the things that mattered. The bridge: closed since Tuesday, the river over its deck, the bell still tolling sometimes at night when the water shifted the chains. The pass: open, but only just; the first snows had dusted the high track a week ago and the post-car driver had stopped coming over it on anything but the clearest mornings. And the motorcar: one, black, kept in the coach-house by a man who polished it more faithfully than he spoke.

The doctor would come up from the village if he could, but the village road was under three feet of water where the lane dipped past the mill, and Dr. Penhale was seventy and had bad knees and a bicycle. Ruth did the arithmetic standing at her son's bedside, and the sum came out wrong no matter how she arranged it.

* * *

By nine o'clock she had made her decision, and by ten she had located Mirabel Vane, which took some doing because locating Mirabel was like locating weather. He was in the coach-house, naturally. He was always in the coach-house when he wasn't driving or eating, and Ruth sometimes thought that if the house caught fire he would carry the car out before he carried himself.

She found him lying on his back under the chassis with his legs sticking out into the November air, a wrench in one hand, looking for all the world like a man repairing the world itself from underneath.

"Mr. Vane."

"Mrs. Callow." He didn't come out. His voice echoed slightly off the underside of the car. "If you've come about the petrol, I ordered more before the bridge shut. It's coming round by the pass road Thursday, God willing."

"It isn't the petrol."

The wrench stopped moving. That was the thing about Mirabel — he listened with his whole body, even when he pretended otherwise. Ruth had watched him do it at servants' dinners, watched him go quiet and still while everyone else talked, hoarding words the way misers hoarded coin. She had never decided whether she liked him. He was useful, punctual, and clean in his habits, and he had never once asked after her private life, which in a household full of people who asked after your private life in order to use it against you later was almost a virtue. But there was something closed about him, something that watched the world through glass. She had seen him read Mrs. Marchmont's letters once, two years ago, sorting the post in the morning room with the window light behind him, and she had seen his eyes move along the lines of a letter that was addressed to his employer, and she had said nothing, because it was none of her business what a man did with other people's secrets so long as he didn't bring them to her door.

Now she was bringing one to his door herself.

"I need you to drive my boy over the pass," she said.

Silence under the car. Then the wheels creaked as he rolled out, and he sat up in the grit with the wrench across his knees, blinking at her, his hair full of dust. He looked, she thought, exactly like what he was: a man interrupted.

"The pass is forty miles," he said. "And the top of it's had snow since All Saints'. I'd not take the car up there now for Mrs. Marchmont herself."

"He's not asking you to take him for Mrs. Marchmont. He's fourteen and he's got a fever, and the fever's in the valley, Mr. Vane — it's been in the valley since Michaelmas and it's climbing, and this house sits at the top of the climb like a dinner waiting to be eaten. There's no doctor can reach us and no medicine worth the name, and the bridge won't open till the floods drop, and the floods won't drop till Christmas if they drop at all. But the pass is open today. Today it's open. I watched the sun on it this morning from the wash-house window."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow it might snow."

"So you want me to drive him out on a maybe."

"I want you to drive him out on the only maybe there is."

Mirabel looked at her. He had gray eyes, Ruth had noticed long ago, the flat gray of river water, and they gave nothing away. Most men, told a thing like this, would have shown something — pity, reluctance, fear of their employer. Mirabel showed nothing at all. He turned the wrench over in his hands as though the conversation were happening somewhere off to his left.

"Mrs. Marchmont keeps the car," he said at last. "I drive it where she says."

"Mrs. Marchmont is asleep. Mrs. Marchmont takes her letters at eleven and her walk at two and doesn't remember either of us exists between the two. This would be done and back before she finished her second cup."

"That's not the point."

"Then what is the point, Mr. Vane? Tell me the point, because I've been racking my brain since six o'clock this morning and I can't find one."

He stood up, brushing the grit from his trousers with quick, precise movements, and Ruth saw that his hands were shaking — not much, just a tremor at the fingertips, the kind a man gets when he has been holding himself very still for a very long time. She filed that away. She filed everything away. It was how she had survived.

"The point," he said quietly, "is that if I take the car without leave and anything happens — a broken axle on the pass, a drift, anything — then there's no car in this house at all. For anyone. For whatever comes. You'd be trading a certainty for a chance, and the certainty is that while the car sits here, it can carry someone to the steamer berth if the berth ever opens up."

Ruth went cold. She hadn't known — couldn't have known — but the word *berth* went through her like a fishhook, and she understood in one lurching instant that Mirabel Vane knew about the ledger. Knew about the steamer. Knew about the thing Mrs. Marchmont kept locked in the escritoire and spoke of to no one, the thing that had hung over this house since September like smoke you couldn't see the source of.

"How do you know about—" She stopped herself. "Never mind. Of course you know. You know everything in this house, don't you. You read it all."

Mirabel's face did something complicated and brief, and then it was smooth again.

"I sort the post," he said.

"You sort the post," Ruth repeated. "Well. Then you'll have sorted the letter that came Tuesday, the one from the shipping office, and you'll know the steamer goes south on the ninth whether the roads hold or not, and you'll know that whoever's name is written in that book has a chance and my boy has none unless somebody does something. So here's what I'm asking, and I'll ask it plain, because plain is all I've got."

She stepped closer. The coach-house smelled of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of the pine woods beyond the wall, and somewhere above them a jackdaw complained from the guttering.

"Drive him over the pass today. Take him to my sister's at Kirkby Lonsdale — she'll keep him, she's got room and she owes me, and I'll pay the petrol out of my own wages, every shilling, till it's paid. And if Mrs. Marchmont asks, tell her the truth, or tell her a lie, I don't care which, so long as my son wakes up breathing on the far side of that mountain instead of burning up in the room next to mine. That's the ask. That's the whole of it."

Mirabel was silent for a long moment. A long time for a man who lived by silence anyway.

"If I say no," he said finally, "you'll think I'm a coward."

"No," Ruth said. "I'll think you're a man who's spent so long watching other people's lives go by that he's forgotten he's got one. There's a difference, Mr. Vane. Cowards are afraid of what happens to them. You're afraid of what happens to anybody. It's worse."

Something moved behind the gray eyes then — she saw it land, whatever it was, saw it strike home the way a stone strikes still water. He looked away, at the car, at the doors, at the pale strip of sky between them where the pass road climbed out of sight toward the snow.

"I'll need to check the axle," he said. "And the chains. If the top of the pass has ice, chains are the difference between arriving and going over the edge."

Ruth let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since six o'clock.

"Is that a yes?"

"It's an inspection," Mirabel said. "Ask me again in an hour."

But Ruth Callow had read houses for eleven years, and men for longer than that, and she knew a yes being assembled when she heard one. She put out her hand — rough palm, knuckles scarred from thirty years of laundry work — and after a moment, looking at it as though it were a document requiring careful study, Mirabel Vane took it.

"One more thing," she said, turning to go. "You'll want to know why I'm asking you and not going to her direct. It's because she'd say no. Not cruelly — she'd say it kindly, with reasons, the way she does everything, and the reasons would all be true, and my boy would still be in that bed tonight getting hotter. Kindness that ends in a dead child is just arithmetic with better manners. You understand arithmetic, Mr. Vane. I've watched you doing sums in your head since the day you came."

She left him there among the tools and the oil-smell, and she climbed the back stairs two at a time, and outside Tobias's door she stopped, and pressed her hand flat against the wood, and listened to her son breathe.

It was a little faster than it should have been. A little too shallow. She counted the breaths the way she counted everything, and then she went in and sat down beside him and began, in a low steady voice, to talk about nothing at all — about the price of mutton, about the jackdaw in the gutter, about spring — while downstairs a man who had spent his whole life refusing to be part of any story stood in the coach-house with a wrench in his hand, deciding whether he was about to become one.
