## 6. The Daughter Returns

The motorcar told me before anyone else did.

I had taken the Daimler down to Ashcombe for petrol and a new fan belt — Edith's orders, delivered through Ruth with the usual economy of words: *Before the roads go, Mr. Vane. I don't pay for a car that sits.* The garage man, Hobbes, was fitting the belt when a telegraph boy came pedalling up from the station with a wire for Marchmont House. I signed for it, because signing for things is what invisible men do, and because the alternative was letting Hobbes read it first, and I had already decided on the drive over that I didn't care for Hobbes's face.

*ARRIVING THURSDAY 2.15 TRAIN STOP DO NOT TELL MOTHER STOP C.*

I stood in the yard with the flimsy in my glove and felt the particular cold that comes off a piece of paper when it changes everything. Thursday was tomorrow. The 2.15 came up from London through Bexley Junction and stopped at Ashcombe Halt for exactly ninety seconds, long enough for one trunk and one person if they were quick.

Do not tell mother. As though Edith Marchmont were a thing that could be kept from knowing. As though the house itself wouldn't tilt on its foundations the moment her daughter set foot on the gravel.

I folded the wire into my inside pocket, where I kept other people's secrets, next to my tobacco tin, and drove home in the rain thinking about what I knew of Clemence Marchmont, which was nearly nothing and therefore everything.

Here is what an invisible man collects. Not gossip — gossip is for kitchen maids and colonels' wives. What I collected was structure. Over two years of reading Edith's correspondence — and I will not pretend I hadn't; I read every letter that passed through my hands on the drive up from the village post office, steaming them open when they arrived sealed, which most did — I had assembled the Marchmont family the way an engineer assembles a machine from its parts catalogue. There had been a son, James, dead seven years now, of what no letter ever said plainly. There was a husband, long gone, whose name appeared only in solicitors' letters and always in the past tense even while he lived. And there was Clemence, who wrote twice a year, at Christmas and on Edith's birthday, letters so correct they might have been typed by a bank. *Dear Mother. I trust you are keeping well. London is damp as ever. Your affectionate daughter, C.*

Two years of those. And two years of Edith's replies, dictated to Miss Pryce in the morning room in a voice like a chisel: *Your letter received. The garden is under snow. I remain, as ever, your mother.* No love in it, and no absence of love either — just the ledger-keeping of two people who had run out of things to say and refused to stop saying them.

The last letter from Clemence had come in September. It had been different. Shorter. And it had asked a question — I remembered the sentence because sentences that ask questions are rare in that family — something about *the arrangements Mama has made for the future*, phrased delicately, the way you'd ask a stranger whether a house was for sale.

Edith had not answered it. She had dictated three pages about drainage.

So when I parked the Daimler under the carriage arch that evening and saw Ruth Callow beating dust out of the blue guest-room rug with the fury of a woman punishing the rug for existing, I understood the household better than it understood itself. Someone had told Ruth. Or Ruth had read the same weather I had. Either way, the house was preparing for a visitor it had not been told to expect, and pretending, in the English way, that preparation is not anticipation.

"You'll want the car out early tomorrow," Ruth said, not looking up. "The 2.15."

"Will I," I said.

She beat the rug twice more, then stopped, and looked at me across the cinders with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Miss Clemence," she said. "Coming home."

"Is it home?"

Ruth considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, including how many lumps went in the doctor's tea. "It's where her mother is," she said finally, "and that's near enough to home for anyone." Then she went back to the rug, and the conversation was over, because conversations with Ruth were always over whether you'd finished or not.

* * *

Thursday came up grey and windless, the river high and brown under the bridge, the valley smelling of wet stone and coming winter. I drove down to the halt with the Daimler gleaming despite the mud — I had spent an hour on it, which I told myself was professional pride and knew was nerves — and stood on the platform among milk churns while the train sighed in from the east.

She stepped down before the guard reached the door. That was the first thing. Everyone else in that county waited to be handed down; Clemence Marchmont dropped onto the platform like a woman getting off a bus, trunk already signalled, hat pinned at an angle that would have made her mother write three pages of dictation. She was perhaps thirty, dark where Edith was grey, with the same jaw — that was unmistakable, the Marchmont jaw, a jaw built for closing arguments — and eyes that did a thing I recognised instantly because I did it myself every day of my life: they swept the platform once, cataloguing everyone in it, and dismissed everyone in it, all in under two seconds.

Then they came back to me, and stayed.

"You're the driver," she said. Not a question either. This family didn't ask questions; they issued findings.

"Vane, ma'am."

"Mirabel Vane." She said my name as though checking it against a list. "You've been with her two years."

"Two years in the spring."

"And before that?"

"A garage in Reading. Before that, a school that asked too much money for what it taught."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would be filed for later. "You answer questions honestly," she said. "That's unusual in this house. Don't let it become a habit." And she turned to supervise her trunk, which was being wrestled off the guard's trolley by a porter who had already lost the argument.

I drove her up the vale with the glass down an inch because she asked for it — asked, mind, not demanded; there was a courtesy in her that sat oddly on top of the armour, like lace on a mail shirt — and she looked at the river the whole way, at the brown water climbing the willows, and said, "How bad is the bridge?"

"Closes within the fortnight, they say. Bell hasn't rung yet."

"And after the bell?"

"After the bell, nobody crosses but boats, and there aren't any worth the name."

She nodded slowly, watching the water, and I watched her watch it, and I thought: she isn't here to reconcile. Reconciling doesn't require a timetable of floods. She is here the way a buyer attends a sale — to view the goods before the hammer falls.

I should say, in fairness to myself, that I didn't dislike her for it. That was the trouble. I recognised the machinery. I had spent two years telling myself that reading Edith's letters was harmless, a private vice, a spectator's sin — and here was a woman doing precisely what I did, only openly, only with her chin up, and some traitorous part of me wanted to applaud.

* * *

The meeting happened in the hall, because Edith refused to stage it anywhere else. A drawing-room reconciliation could be prepared for; a hall ambush could not, and Edith Marchmont had chosen ambush, which told me she was frightened. Frightened people choose ground.

Miss Pryce had been sent away for the afternoon — a kindness or a tactic, depending on your reading — and Ruth stood at the service door with a tray of nothing, and I stood by the coat rack with my cap under my arm, invisible, which is to say watching everything.

Edith came down the stairs at half past three precisely, in the black dress she wore for everything short of funerals, and stopped on the third step, so that she and her daughter met at a height neither had chosen. For a moment neither spoke. The clock in the study ticked through four full seconds, which in that hall was a geological age.

"Mama," said Clemence.

"You got my wire, then," said Edith.

Clemence blinked. First blood. "What wire?"

"The one I sent this morning. Saying you were expected." Edith descended the last steps with terrible calm. "I have been reading your aunt's correspondence since August, Clemence. You wrote to her asking when you might visit. I thought I would spare us both the theatre of surprise."

I felt the wire in my pocket like a coal. *DO NOT TELL MOTHER.* Sent yesterday. Read by nobody but me. Which meant Edith was lying — lying magnificently, lying with the whole architecture of the house behind her — and Clemence, standing in the draught with her gloves still on, was working through the same arithmetic, because I saw it cross her face: *if Mother knew, how did she know? Who reads whose mail in this house?*

Their eyes met, and for one unguarded instant both of them looked, not at each other, but at the room — at the doors, the servants, the shadows where a man with a cap might stand — each wondering what the walls had heard.

"You're welcome," Edith said, "to stay as long as you like. Ruth has the blue room ready. Dinner is at eight." She turned toward the study, then paused, and added without turning round: "You've lost weight. London doesn't feed you."

"No," said Clemence to her mother's back. "London doesn't feed you."

And that was the reconciliation. I have seen duels with more warmth and less precision.

* * *

That evening I carried wine up from the cellar and heard, through the study door, the sound of Edith dictating — but slower than usual, with pauses, the rhythm of a woman choosing words rather than spending them. I set the decanter on the hall table and did not knock, and as I turned to go, the door opened and Clemence came out of the morning room opposite, also carrying nothing, also going nowhere, and we stood in the corridor together in the lamplight like two burglars surprised by the same house.

"She's writing to the solicitors," Clemence said quietly. "I heard Pryce's pen going. Do you know what she tells them?"

"I drive the car, ma'am."

"Yes," she said. "You do. You drive the car, and you carry the post up from the village, and you sign for wires at Hobbes's garage, and you have a very good memory, I should think, for a man with no position." She wasn't accusing me. That was the unnerving part. She said it the way one musician compliments another's fingering.

"I couldn't say, ma'am."

"No," she agreed. "You couldn't. Nobody in this house can say anything. It's why I left." She glanced at the study door, from behind which the chisel-voice went on measuring out words. "Do you know what I learned, growing up here? That everything is accounted for. Every favour, every slight, every shilling. My mother keeps books on people the way other mothers keep photographs. And the books always balance." Her voice dropped further. "Except lately. Lately something in the books doesn't balance, and she won't say what, and that frightens me more than the fever in the valley does, if you want the truth."

I wanted to tell her — God knows I wanted to, standing there in the corridor with the forged-nothing-yet weight of what I'd seen in that leather ledger sitting in my chest — I wanted to say: *there's a berth on a steamer, and a page of debts, and your name written nowhere at all.* But I had survived two years in that house by knowing which silences to keep, and besides, a man who trades in stolen knowledge learns quickly that spending it devalues it.

"Good night, ma'am," I said instead.

"Good night, Mr. Vane." She moved past me toward the stairs, then stopped, and said over her shoulder, lightly, almost kindly: "The blue room faces the drive. If a car leaves this house at night before the roads close, I shall hear it. I hear everything. It's the family complaint."

She went up. I stood in the corridor a while longer, listening to the pen scratch behind the study door, and understood that the careful equilibrium of Marchmont House — two years of it, Edith and Ruth and Pryce and me, revolving around each other like well-oiled gears — had just acquired a fifth wheel, and the fifth wheel was made of looking-glass.

Because that was the thing about Clemence Marchmont, the thing I turned over as I banked the boiler and checked the Daimler's lamps and did all the small mechanical devotions that let a man avoid thinking. She read people the way I read letters. She had walked into that hall and in ten seconds had found the seam in me — the honest answers, the good memory, the man with no position who nevertheless knew things — the way I had found the seam in her mother's correspondence.

Fellow readers recognise each other. It's the oldest law of our trade.

And as I climbed the back stair to my room above the garage, the rain starting again against the slates, one more item filed itself in the catalogue, unbidden and unwelcome: the fever was in the village below — Ruth's boy had a cough she was calling a cough — the bridge would close within days — and now, into this sealed and counting house, had walked the one person alive with a stronger claim on that leather ledger than mine.

At eight o'clock tomorrow we would all sit down to dinner together, and pass the salt, and not say a single true thing.

I lay in the dark listening to the river rise, and wished — for the first time in two comfortable years — that I were the sort of man who couldn't hear everything.
