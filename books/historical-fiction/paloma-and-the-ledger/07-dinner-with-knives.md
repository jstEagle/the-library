## 7. Dinner with Knives

There is a particular silence that falls over a dining room when three people who dislike one another agree to pretend otherwise. I had heard it before — at funerals, at weddings, at the annual dinner of the Ashcombe Vale Ratepayers' Association — but never with such concentration as on the Thursday evening after Clemence Marchmont's arrival.

I stood at the sideboard with the claret and listened to it.

The dining room at Marchmont House was built for twelve and set for four: Edith at the head, Clemence at her right hand — a placement that was either a courtesy or a wound, depending on how you read it — Ruth Callow at the foot, because Edith insisted the housekeeper dine with them while the bridge was closed, "since we are all prisoners together," a sentence she delivered in the tone of a woman granting clemency to people she had personally condemned. My place was standing. It always was. A chauffeur eats in the kitchen, ordinarily, but with the flood season on us and the cook gone home to her mother in Tiverton until the water dropped, the household had contracted into something strange and intimate, like a family portrait painted by someone who hated all of them.

I was not family. I was furniture that poured wine. This suited me admirably.

"More lamb, Mrs. Callow?" I said, because someone had to speak first, and it was safer coming from me.

"Thank you, Mr. Vane." Ruth's voice was steady. Her hands were not; I noticed she kept them folded in her lap like two birds she was preventing from escaping. Her boy was upstairs with a cough that had lasted four days now, and every hour of this dinner was an hour she wasn't sitting with him. She'd dressed for the occasion in her good grey dress, armor for a battle she hadn't been told the terms of.

Clemence ate the way her mother must have eaten at twenty: quickly, efficiently, with an air of getting the meal over with so the real business could begin. She wore London the way other women wore perfume — you couldn't see it, exactly, but the whole room knew it was there. Her dress was some dark silk that probably cost more than my annual wages, and she'd put it on, I suspected, specifically to make Ruth's good grey look like what it was: a uniform.

"You've done wonders with the lamb, Mrs. Callow," Clemence said. "Given the circumstances."

"The circumstances being the bridge, ma'am, or the cook?"

I nearly dropped the gravy boat. Ruth Callow, it turned out, had knives of her own. They were just very small, and sheathed in politeness, and you only found them when you sat down at her table.

Edith laughed — one short bark, genuine, gone immediately. "You see what I've lived with for eleven years."

"I see what you've *kept* for eleven years," Clemence said, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Mama never keeps anything she can't use."

"And yet here you are," Edith said.

The silence came back. I refilled glasses. Claret for Edith, who drank like a man and held it like one. Water for Ruth. Clemence had asked for whatever I was having, which was a performance of democracy I didn't trust for a moment, and so I gave her the claret too, and watched her decide whether to be flattered or insulted by the assumption that she could hold it.

She could hold it. That was the trouble.

* * *

Let me tell you how a dinner like this actually works, from the servant's side of the room, because nobody writes it down and somebody ought to.

You learn the rhythm of a conversation before you learn its content. The pauses tell you where the mines are. When Edith paused after saying the word *arrangements*, there was a mine. When Clemence let a full five seconds pass before answering anything about London, there was a mine. When Ruth said nothing at all for ten minutes and then, precisely, asked whether anyone had heard the latest from the doctor about the fever in Millbrook — that was not a mine. That was a grenade, rolled gently onto the tablecloth.

"Fever?" Clemence set down her fork. "What fever?"

"There's typhoid in the lower valley," Edith said, in the voice she used for weather reports. "Three cases in Millbrook as of Tuesday. The medical officer has closed the bridge by order — not merely the season, mind, the *order*. We're quarantined in effect, though nobody official will say so until someone important dies."

"Mama."

"It's a fact, Clemence. Facts don't improve with euphemism."

"But you've known this — how long?"

"Since Tuesday."

"Since *Tuesday*." Clemence turned the word over like a coin she suspected was foreign. "And you didn't think to mention it when I arrived Wednesday? On the train, through the valley?"

"You arrived through the valley already. Mentioning it afterward seemed redundant."

I poured the wine. My hands were perfectly steady, which surprised me, because inside I was doing arithmetic. Tobias Callow had had a cough for four days. Fevers travel up valleys the way gossip travels up staircases. And the last mail steamer south — the berth I had read about in the ledger, the unnamed berth, the one fact I carried around like a swallowed stone — departed Saturday morning from the dock road, if the river allowed it, and the river was rising.

Three days. Possibly fewer.

"Mr. Vane," Edith said suddenly, without looking at me. "You drive past the churchyard on your errands. Have they begun digging?"

"They've dug six graves since Monday, madam. The sexton says he's run out of boards."

Ruth made a small sound, quickly suppressed. Clemence looked at me for the first time all evening — really looked, the way you look at a piece of furniture that has just spoken Latin — and something moved behind her eyes that I recognized, because I saw it in mirrors: calculation. She was revising me. Servants were scenery; servants who answered questions with sexton's counts were instruments, and instruments could be played.

I gave her back nothing. Blankness is a skill. I have spent years perfecting it, and I'll thank you not to ask what I was blanking out, because it was the image of a boy of nine lying in a narrow bed under the eaves, coughing in the dark while his mother performed civility downstairs.

"Six graves," Clemence repeated softly. "How wonderfully organized we are, even in catastrophe."

"That is the English genius," Edith said dryly. "We queue for death."

* * *

The fish course had come and gone — smoked trout from the river that was currently trying to kill them all, which even I thought was a bit much — and the talk had circled, the way talk does when everyone knows the real subject and no one will name it. They discussed the war clouds over the Balkans. They discussed the price of coal. Clemence described a play she'd seen in London, and Edith said plays were how the middle class avoided thinking, and Clemence said thinking was how Mama avoided feeling, and Edith raised her glass slightly, as if toasting a decent shot.

Then Clemence leaned back and did it.

"I suppose," she said, examining her nails, "the steamer still runs Saturday."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed — Ruth's ears went to it like a dog's, I saw it happen, her whole body tilting toward the sound of her son turning over in his bed.

"If the river permits," Edith said. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason. Curiosity. One hears things in London." Clemence picked up her glass, turned it slowly. "One hears, for instance, that Grandfather's estate is finally settled. That the Chancery business concluded in September. One hears that there is money — actual money, not the mortgaged sort — and that certain arrangements were made regarding passage south. Passage, Mama. Singular."

Where had she heard? Not from Edith; I'd stake my wages on it. From the solicitor's clerks, then, or from letters intercepted, or — and this thought arrived coldly, unwelcome — from the same instinct that had sent me reading other people's post for years. Some families breed readers. We recognize each other the way cats do.

Edith put down her fork. The sound it made against the plate was very small and very final, like a bolt sliding home.

"My arrangements," she said, "are my own."

"Your arrangements involve a berth on the *Aurelia*, Mama. Cabin class. Booked in August, paid in October, name left blank." Clemence smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it somewhere, though I couldn't have sworn whose. "The booking clerk at the steamship office is the brother of my hairdresser. You'd be astonished what leaves this house in confidence and arrives in London wearing a different coat."

Ruth had gone absolutely still. I watched her understand, in real time, that the thing she had perhaps only half-known existed — the escape route, the berth, the arithmetic of lives — was now on the table between the candles, next to the salt.

"And what," Edith said, each word laid down like a stone in a wall, "do you imagine that berth is for?"

"I imagine it's for whoever you love best." Clemence's voice stayed light, feather-light, and that was the frightening part; underneath it was something with weight enough to sink the house. "I'm simply asking to be considered. For once. In the running. You've spent my whole life telling me I wasn't."

"You walked out."

"You locked the door."

"I locked the door after you'd already—"

"*Ladies.*"

It was Ruth. Ruth Callow, housekeeper, standing up from her chair at the foot of the table with her napkin still in her lap, her face white as the linen, her voice cracking across the room like the ice on the mill pond.

"Forgive me," she said, and did not sound as though she forgave anything. "But there is a child upstairs with a fever, and a river rising, and a steamer leaving Saturday, and the pair of you are fencing over it like it's a *legacy*. Like it's *yours*." Her breath caught. Steadied. "Somebody in this house is going to need that berth, and it won't be either of you, because neither of you is dying, and neither of you is nine years old."

The silence afterward was total. Even the clock seemed embarrassed.

I stood at the sideboard holding the decanter and understood, with terrible clarity, that I had just watched the stakes be named aloud. Not the inheritance. Not the reconciliation. The berth. One seat. Three claimants in the room and a fourth upstairs coughing, and a fifth — me — pretending to be a decanter stand.

Edith recovered first. Of course she did; she'd been recovering from worse than this for sixty years.

"Mrs. Callow," she said quietly, "sit down, please. You are quite right, and I am ashamed, and neither of those facts requires you to remain standing."

Ruth sat. Her hands had resumed their trembling. She looked at me — one glance, quick, across the candles — and in it was everything she'd said to me on the back steps two nights ago, *you're the only one with a motorcar, Mr. Vane*, wrapped now in apology for having spoken it aloud in company.

Clemence, meanwhile, was watching her mother with an expression I couldn't parse, and it bothered me all the more because I pride myself on parsing. It wasn't triumph. It wasn't hurt. It was — recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who has just discovered that the game she came to play is being played for higher stakes than she'd brought chips for.

"Well," Clemence said at last, lightly, lifting her glass. "To family."

Nobody drank.

"To family," Edith echoed, and drank alone, and set the glass down with a click like a key turning.

* * *

I cleared the dessert plates. I brushed crumbs. I lit the candles down as the ladies rose, and I did all of it with the smooth vacancy of a man who has heard nothing, because that is the servant's covenant: you are ears without a mouth, eyes without a tongue.

But as I passed Clemence in the doorway — she was lingering, pretending to admire the tapestry, waiting for the room to empty — she spoke without turning her head, low, pitched for me alone.

"Six graves since Monday," she murmured. "You count well, Mr. Vane. Chauffeurs who count are useful people. We should talk."

And she touched my sleeve — barely, a fingertip, a signature on a document — and went up the stairs to bed.

I stood in the dining room with the dead candles and the cold claret and the salt and the knives, all the knives, laid out plain on the white cloth where anyone could see them, and I thought about a boy coughing under the eaves, and a ledger with a blank line in it, and three days.

Two, now. By the time I blew out the last candle, the river had risen another inch, and somewhere below in the dark I could hear it working at the bridge like a patient creditor.
