## 8. Fever in the Vale

Ruth Callow had learned to read a house by its silences, and on the morning after the dinner with knives, Marchmont House was silent in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

She stood at the kitchen window at half past five, before the lamps were lit, watching the rain come down off the fells in grey ropes. The river had swallowed the lower meadow overnight. The mill race roared like something alive and starving. And under all of it, from the room above the scullery where Tobias slept, she could hear it — not coughing, exactly, not yet, but that small wet catch in his breathing that every mother in that valley knew the way farmers knew the smell of snow.

He'd come to her bed at three o'clock, which he hadn't done since he was seven. He'd said his dreams were loud. She'd put her palm against his forehead and found it warm — not burning, not yet, but warm the way a stone is warm after a long day of sun, holding heat it ought to have given back.

*It's nothing,* she told the window. *It's a cold. It's the damp coming into the walls like it comes in every winter.*

The window did not believe her either.

She built the fire anyway, earlier than she needed to, and put water on for porridge, and set out the tray for Mrs. Marchmont's breakfast with hands that were perfectly steady because she made them be steady. That was the whole trick of service, Ruth had found: you could grieve, you could panic, you could stand in a burning building so long as your hands kept their appointments. Her mother had carried her father's coffin with dry eyes because there were forty people watching. Ruth stirred porridge with a face like any Tuesday.

Tobias came down at seven, wrapped in his coat though the kitchen was warm, and sat where he always sat, at the end of the scrubbed table near the door.

"You look terrible," she said, which was how they said good morning to each other.

"Feel fine," he said, and then ruined it by coughing into his sleeve — twice, three times, a racking little sequence that went through him like a shudder and left him blinking and slightly pink around the ears.

Ruth turned back to the stove so he wouldn't see her face.

"Fifteen," she said, when she could trust her voice. "Fifteen years old and lying to your mother over porridge."

"It's just the weather."

"The weather." She set the bowl down in front of him harder than necessary. "Your father used to say the same. Every fever he ever caught was 'just the weather,' right up until the one that wasn't." She heard herself say it and stopped, but it was already out, hanging in the steam between them. Tobias looked at his bowl.

"I'm not going to die," he said, with the enormous confidence of fifteen, and ate two spoonfuls, and then pushed the bowl away and put his head down on his arms.

Ruth stood with her hand hovering over his hair, not quite touching it. Touching would make it real. Not touching was a kind of prayer.

* * *

The doctor came up from Ashcombe village at ten, which meant he'd been walking since eight, because the bridge road was already under water at the ford and his motor had drowned itself trying the second crossing. Dr. Penhale was sixty and round as a barrel and had delivered most of the valley into the world, and he arrived dripping in the kitchen doorway looking like a man who'd lost an argument with the river.

"Don't get up," he said, when Ruth rose. "I'll only drip on you worse."

"How is it down there?" she asked, and watched his face do what faces did — the small professional rearrangement, the curtain drawn.

"Bad," he said, because it was her, and because he hadn't the strength left for lying. "It's in the village proper now. The Hollis girl died Tuesday night. Old Mr. Tregowan yesterday. There's four more in bed at the George and I'd wager my bag on at least two of them." He accepted the tea she pressed into his hand without appearing to notice giving it. "And the bridge is shut."

"Shut by the water or shut by order?"

"Both." He drank. "The county engineer came down Monday. There's a crack in the middle span they don't trust, and now the flood's testing it hourly. There's a constable posted on the far side and a notice board. No crossing for foot traffic, no crossing for anything. Quarantine's being talked about officially — if the fever crosses the river into the market towns, they'll seal the whole vale, roads and rail alike." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've said too many times. "You're on this side of the water, so you've got the house, the stores, the well. You could be worse situated."

"We're not sick," Ruth said. "We're not sick yet."

Penhale looked at her over the rim of his cup, and she saw him decide, deliberately, not to correct her tense.

"Who's in the household?"

"Mrs. Marchmont. Miss Clemence, arrived from London day before yesterday. Mr. Vane, the chauffeur. Tobias. Myself."

"And the boy?" He set the cup down. "How is the boy?"

"He's fine," Ruth said. "A cold. A touch of—"

"Ruth." Gently. He'd known her since she was a girl carrying milk cans; he'd set her own broken wrist when she fell off the hayrick at fourteen. "Let me see him."

* * *

She wanted to refuse. She understood, standing outside Tobias's door while Penhale washed his hands in the basin she'd brought up, that everything inside her wanted to say *he's sleeping, come back tomorrow, he's better already this morning* — and that wanting to lie was itself a kind of confession.

The boy lay propped against his pillows, reading a penny dreadful upside-down-ish, his eyes tracking without much conviction. He flushed when the doctor came in, the way boys did, mortified at being examined.

"Morning, young man. Breathe deep for me. Again. Again." Penhale listened a long time, front and back, his stethoscope cold enough that Tobias flinched. He looked in the boy's throat. He pressed fingers along the jaw, under the arms, feeling for the knots that the fever planted first. He asked Tobias to touch his nose, walk three steps, follow the lamp flame with his eyes.

Then he straightened up, and Ruth watched him do it slowly, buying seconds, arranging what he was going to say, and she thought: *he's doing arithmetic, and I'm going to hate the sum.*

"Well?" she said.

"There's nothing I can call it yet," Penhale said. "No rash. Lungs are clear — a bit of congestion, no more than any boy breathing this damp. His glands are up, but glands come up for a dozen reasons." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his handkerchief, which he never did unless he was stalling. "But he's got the look. You know the look, Ruth. The fever's been through this valley often enough that we both know the look. Two days, maybe three, and we'll know one way or the other."

"What do you give him?"

"Quinine for the shivers if they come. Broth. Rest. And—" he met her eyes, and dropped the doctor's voice for the neighbor's voice, the man who'd buried half her family's dead, "—and I'll come up every day, whatever the roads do. I promise you that. But Ruth — listen to me. If it takes hold, there's nothing in my bag that matters. Nothing in anybody's bag. What saves them is their own blood fighting, and getting them out of these river valleys where the miasma sits. The sanatorium at Torquay has taken cases. Sea air. But that means getting over the bridge or down the coast road before the weather finishes closing both."

"The bridge is closed by order."

"The berth isn't," Penhale said quietly.

And there it was. The thing she'd been not-thinking about since the night of the dinner, since she'd stood behind Clemence Marchmont's chair pouring wine and listening to a family carve up a seat on a ship as if it were the last joint of meat in the larder — which, God help her, it was.

She said nothing. Penhale gathered his bag.

"Two days," he repeated at the door. "Watch the temperature morning and night. Write it down — you're a good hand at writing things down. And if his breath goes short in the night, send Vane's motor for me even if he has to drive it into the river."

When he'd gone, Ruth sat down on the edge of her son's bed. Tobias had gone pale again, and was pretending to read.

"Mum," he said. "Am I going to get it?"

"No," she said.

"You crossed your fingers," he said. "Behind your back. I saw."

She looked at her hands. She hadn't known she'd done it.

"Then let me tell you the truth instead," she said, "since apparently I can't be trusted with lies. The truth is that you might, and the truth is that I will sit in that chair until you don't, and the truth is that if this fever thinks it's taking my boy out of this world, it has picked a fight with the wrong woman." She tucked the blanket in hard at his side, a small violence of love. "Now drink your broth and don't argue with me."

* * *

In the afternoon she went down to the kitchen and began, methodically, to count.

This was what Ruth Callow did instead of praying. She counted. Stores: flour enough for six weeks if she stretched it, sugar, salt, tinned things, a ham, potatoes in the clamp, apples going soft in the loft. Lamp oil. Coal to last the season. Medicine: quinine, iodine, laudanum under lock, the mustard for plasters. She wrote it all in her own book — not Mrs. Marchmont's leather ledger with its debts and favors and its single blank line that Mirabel Vane probably believed nobody else knew about, but her own fat kitchen book, where the household lived in columns.

At the bottom of the page, under the coal, she wrote: *One berth. S.S. Cormorant, Falmouth, sailing when the roads allow.* Then she crossed it out. Then she wrote it again.

Because here was the arithmetic Penhale wouldn't do aloud and nobody in the great rooms upstairs would do at all: the steamer was leaving from the dock road below the mill, on this side of the broken bridge, and it was the last boat south before the river finished its argument with the roads. One berth, unnamed. Edith Marchmont had bought it months ago, before anyone knew why they might need it, and had spent the autumn refusing to write a name on the line.

Clemence wanted it. That was plain; the dinner had made it plain as a knife on white cloth. And Clemence was blood, and blood had claims, and Ruth had served this family nineteen years and knew precisely how much a servant's claim weighed in this house, which was to say: it weighed whatever Mrs. Marchmont's conscience decided it weighed, on the day it decided it.

But Clemence was not coughing under the eaves.

Ruth stood at the kitchen table with her columns of flour and coal and oil, and felt something shift in her chest that frightened her more than the fever did. It was a cold, patient thing, settling in beside her love like a second tenant. It said: *you have served these people faithfully for nineteen years. Faithfulness is a debt owed to you. You have a column too.*

She didn't like the voice. She recognized it, though. It was the voice that had kept her upright at her husband's funeral. It was the voice that got things done when everything softer had failed.

She closed the book on it.

* * *

Mirabel Vane found her there at dusk. He came in the back way as he always did, stamping rain off his boots, and she noticed — she noticed everything about him now, since the dinner, since *we should talk* — that he moved like a man entering a room he'd already memorized.

"How is he?" Mirabel asked. No preamble. He'd heard the doctor come and go; of course he had.

"Warm," Ruth said. "Not hot. Warm."

"That's bad?"

"That's the question, isn't it. Warm is either nothing or the beginning of everything, and nobody can tell you which until it's done deciding." She poured him tea without asking, because he looked like a man who'd been standing in weather. "Dr. Penhale says the bridge is closed by order now. Constable on the far bank. If they declare quarantine, the whole vale's sealed — roads, rail, all of it."

"I know. I rode down to the ford this afternoon." He wrapped his hands around the cup. "There's a red notice board. Official language. *By Order of the County Sanitary Authority.* Very impressive lettering. Somebody in Truro is very proud of it." He drank. "Mrs. Callow. About the other matter we discussed—"

"Tobias is fine today," she said, too fast, and hated how fast.

"That's what worries me," Mirabel said quietly, "is everyone telling me how fine everyone is. This house has started sounding like a bank statement."

She almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a sob, and she covered it by turning to the range, and when she turned back he was watching her with those still grey eyes that missed nothing, the eyes of a man who read other people's letters and had the decency to feel bad about it.

"If it comes to it," Ruth said — and her voice came out level, which cost her everything she had — "that berth. On the boat. I want you to know where I stand, Mr. Vane, because I think you're the only person in this house who stands anywhere honest. If my boy gets sick, truly sick, I will not be polite about that berth. I will not wait my turn and I will not defer to blood. I have earned things in this house that were never written down, and if it comes to it, I will collect."

The kettle ticked. Rain worked at the windows.

"I believe you," Mirabel said.

And then, from the passage above, faint through the floorboards, came the sound that stopped both their hearts mid-beat: Tobias, coughing. Not the dry morning cough. A long, tearing, wet succession of them, one after another after another, and then silence, and then Ruth was moving, book forgotten, tea forgotten, up the stairs two at a time with the cold patient tenant in her chest whispering *now, now, it begins now.*

Behind her, she heard Mirabel Vane set down his untouched cup, very carefully, and follow.
