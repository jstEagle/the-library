## 9. Clemence's Bargain

She was waiting for me in the garage.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I have left to offer anyone, including myself. She wasn't waiting in the way that a person waits — shifting her weight, checking a watch, letting her impatience show. Clemence Marchmont did not wait. She occupied. When I came down the gravel path with the crank under my arm and my collar up against the drizzle, she was sitting on the running board of the Hupmobile as though she had been born there, smoking a cigarette in a holder with the unbothered elegance of a woman who had decided that everything in Ashcombe Vale, including the weather, existed for her convenience.

"You keep it very clean," she said, nodding at the car. "Most men let the inside go."

"Most men don't drive for Mrs. Marchmont."

"No," she said. "I imagine they don't survive it." She drew on the cigarette, and the ember lit the underside of her face, so that for a moment she looked like something from one of those new moving pictures — all shadow and cheekbone. "Do you know what I did all morning, Mr. Vane?"

"I couldn't say, miss."

"Neither could my mother. That's the point of me. She sent Ruth up twice to find out where I'd gone, and both times Ruth reported that I was resting. Resting." She laughed, a small dry sound like a page turning. "I was in the study."

The garage smelled of petrol and oiled leather and the cold iron of winter coming on. I set the crank down by the wheel with exaggerated care, because my hands wanted to do anything but be steady, and steadiness, I had learned long ago, was mostly a performance you gave with your fingers.

"Looking for something?" I asked.

"Looking for everything." She flicked ash onto my clean floor. "You'd be amazed what people leave lying about when they think no one reads. A ledger, Mr. Vane. A leather ledger with a berth booked on the *Coromandel*, sailing from Southampton — or what remains of Southampton's schedule once the roads fail, which Mother's correspondence suggests will be soon — and no name written beside it. No name at all. Isn't that extraordinary? My mother has reserved a seat off the face of the earth and hasn't told a soul who's meant to sit in it."

I said nothing. This is a skill. People mistake silence for agreement, for stupidity, for deference, for a great many useful things. I stood there being all of them at once.

"But here's the part I find genuinely interesting." She stood up from the running board, and I noticed she had dressed for the country in clothes too fine for it — a walking skirt that would never survive the mud, boots bought for photographs of country walks rather than country walks themselves. "Here's the part that made me come out into this charming drizzle to find you. Because I went looking through the wastepaper basket in the morning room, you see, and I found drafts. Drafts of letters, torn into eighths, the way careful people tear things. And I put them back together, because I am my mother's daughter, whatever else I am. And do you know what those drafts were?"

"Correspondence," I said.

"Correspondence," she agreed, "in a hand I didn't recognize. Not my mother's. Not Ruth Callow's prim schoolroom copperplate. Not the secretary's — he prints, poor man, like a child punished for his handwriting. A hand I didn't recognize at all. Which means either my mother has acquired a ghost, or—" she pointed the cigarette holder at me, pleasantly, the way one might point out a bird of unusual plumage, "—my mother's chauffeur writes her letters for her, and then tears up the evidence, and thinks himself invisible."

The rain ticked on the roof of the garage. Somewhere up the valley, water moved in the ditches with a sound like whispering, like a crowd deciding something.

"Miss Marchmont," I began.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you deny it badly and we both pretend the denial worked. It's insulting to both of us, and I've had a week of being insulted by this house. I've been insulted by the marmalade, Mr. Vane. I would like some honesty."

I looked at her. That was my first error, if errors can be ranked, and I believe they can: I looked at her directly, which is something I almost never do with anyone, because looking is how you get seen, and I had built an entire life on the principle that being unseen was the same as being safe.

She saw me look. She smiled like a woman drawing a winning card from her own sleeve.

"There it is," she said softly. "There's the actual man. Do you know, everyone in this house looks *through* you. My mother talks over your head as though you were a hat stand with opinions about carburetors. Ruth thanks you without seeing you. Even the secretary nods at your chest. But you look, don't you. You look all day long. You read every letter before you seal it. You listen at the edge of every conversation you're paid to stand outside of. You know more about this family than any of us know about ourselves, and you file it all away in that tidy invisible head, and you tell yourself it doesn't count, because you never spend it. Am I close?"

"Is there a version of this conversation," I said, "that happens somewhere warmer?"

"No. There's only this one, and it's going to happen now, in your garage, among your smells of petrol and virtue." She dropped the cigarette and ground it under that impractical boot. "So here is my bargain."

And she told me.

It took perhaps four minutes. She spoke quickly and precisely, the way people speak when they have rehearsed in front of a mirror, and I recognized the rehearsal because I rehearse too — every sentence I've ever delivered to Edith Marchmont has been drafted, redrafted, torn into eighths, and reassembled better than the original. Clemence's bargain was this: I would use whatever access my position gave me — and she was clear-eyed enough not to ask exactly what access that was; she simply assumed it was total, which flattered me more than I liked — to ensure that her mother took the next train out of the valley, south, alone, before the roads closed. The excuse could be anything. A doctor's consultation in London. A solicitor's summons. The inheritance dispute itself, which Clemence knew about, which she had known about longer than I had, apparently, because there are depths to that woman that make the flooded river look shallow. Once Edith was safely on a train to town, the household's attention would scatter, and the berth on the steamer — the unnamed, unclaimed, immaculate berth — would pass to Clemence, who would present herself at the dock with a trunk full of her mother's authority and sail south before anyone thought to question it.

"And in exchange," she finished, "you get money. Real money. Enough that you never have to hold a crank in the rain again. Enough that 'invisible' becomes a word you chose instead of a word that was done to you."

The rain kept ticking. The ditch-water kept whispering. I became aware, distantly, that Tobias Callow had coughed twice at supper, and that Ruth had pretended not to hear it, and that I had pretended not to notice her pretending, and that all of these pretenses were stacked up somewhere in the valley like dry timber waiting for a spark.

"May I ask a question?" I said.

"That's what bargains are for."

"When you say my mother — forgive me, *your* mother — takes the next train. What train is that?"

Her smile flickered. Just at the edge, just for a heartbeat — a lamp in wind. "There'll be one. Before the bridge goes."

"The bridge bell rang yesterday, miss. The bridge goes within days. The last train out of the vale runs Thursday, and Thursday is the day after tomorrow, and the road to the station is already standing water in three places. So when you say the next train, you mean the last train, and when you say your mother leaves alone, you mean she abandons this house, this valley, the people in it, and the ledger she cannot stop writing in — and you mean for me to make her do it." I paused. "For money."

"For freedom," she corrected, and her voice cracked on the word, just slightly, just enough that I understood I was not looking at a villain. Villains don't crack. "You don't know what it's like being her daughter. You've read the letters, so you know the *facts* of it, but facts aren't weather, Mr. Vane. I grew up in that woman's atmosphere. Every affection was an entry in a column. Every kindness came due later, with interest. She has been keeping score of me since I was six years old, and I have lost every single year. You think I want the berth because of what's at the bottom of the sea route or whatever romantic nonsense you've constructed. I want it because it's the one thing in the world she can't take back, and taking it is the only sentence I've ever gotten to write in her ledger myself."

I should have said no immediately. I want to record, for whoever eventually reads this account — and someone will, because I am writing it down now, and writing things down is what I do — that I understood the whole architecture of the wrongness within seconds. Blackmailing a chauffeur to displace a matriarch. Money changing hands over a berth while a boy upstairs coughed in his sleep. Every term of it was rotten.

But here is what I have not confessed to anyone yet, and confessing it is the entire purpose of setting this down:

For one long, luminous moment, I was not invisible.

She had seen me. Completely, precisely, mercilessly seen me — the reading, the listening, the filing away, the philosophy of detachment I wore like a chauffeur's coat. Twenty-nine years of standing just outside the frame of other people's lives, and here was a stranger describing the picture from memory, accurately, down to the gilt on the frame. It was the most intimate thing that had ever happened to me. More intimate than any touch, because touch is common and being *known* is not. She held me up to the light and turned me around, and I felt — God help me — *grateful*.

That is what blackmail is, when it's done well. It isn't violence. It's intimacy with the invoice attached. She offered me money for my soul, yes, but first she told me exactly what my soul looked like, and no one had ever bothered to do even that much.

"Yes," I heard myself say.

Clemence went very still.

"I'll consider it," I said, correcting course before the word *yes* could finish landing. "I won't lie to you, Miss Marchmont — you'd catch the lie anyway, you're far too good at this. I'll consider it. Thursday is two days off. Give me until tomorrow night."

"Why tomorrow night?"

Because Tobias coughs, I did not say. Because Ruth stopped asking nicely behind her eyes, I did not say. Because your mother keeps a ledger with a blank line where a human being should be, and I have not yet decided whether I'm the sort of man who fills in lines or erases them, I did not say.

"Because tomorrow night I'll know," is what I actually said.

She studied me for a long moment, head tilted, and I had the unpleasant sensation of being read in return — of being the letter, opened and skimmed, contents assessed, value estimated. Whatever she concluded, she pocketed it.

"Tomorrow night, then." She picked her way toward the garage door, then stopped, half in the grey light, and looked back at me. "Mr. Vane. One thing, freely given, no charge. My mother is going to choose someone for that berth eventually — she can't help herself, choosing is the only power she has left. And when she chooses, she will choose blood, or she will choose duty, and either way she will sleep beautifully afterward. Don't build her a cathedral in your head. She isn't worth it. None of us are. That's not cynicism. That's just the view from where I stand."

Then she was gone, footsteps crunching away through the wet gravel toward the house, toward the lighted windows where dinner was being laid and civility was being rehearsed.

I stood alone in the garage with the smell of petrol and the sound of the ditches.

I had been seen. Truly seen, for the first time in my life — catalogued, appraised, priced. And the price had been named, and the terrible thing, the thing I turned over and over as I cranked the motor and drove it round to the front of the house through the thickening rain, was not that she had named a price for my soul.

The terrible thing was that until she did, I hadn't known it was for sale.

Inside, the house smelled of roast fowl and lamp oil. Ruth met me in the hall with my coat brush, and as she brushed the rain off my shoulders she said, quietly, without looking up: "Tobias's fever came back this evening. Doctor's been sent for. In case you wondered why supper might run late."

Her voice was perfectly level. Her hands were not.

Upstairs, behind a door I would soon have business with, Edith Marchmont sat with her ledger. Downstairs, a boy burned. At the dock, a ship waited with one empty chair. And somewhere between them all stood a man who had until tonight believed he was only passing through the story.

Thursday was coming. The bridge bell would ring its final warning. And I had twenty-four hours to decide what kind of author I intended to be.
