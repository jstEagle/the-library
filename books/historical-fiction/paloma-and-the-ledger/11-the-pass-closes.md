## 11. The Pass Closes

I told myself I was going up the mountain to check the road for Ruth's sake — to see whether Tobias might yet be driven out before winter finished its argument with the valley. That was the story I gave her, and it was even true in the way most convenient stories are true: I did want to check the road. I also wanted, more than I admitted, to learn that it was still open.

Because if it was open, then the ledger on Edith Marchmont's desk was not the only door out of Ashcombe Vale. If it was open, there was a version of events in which no one had to be chosen at all — in which the berth on the *Coromandel* remained an abstraction, a line in a dead woman's son's name, and Mirabel Vane could go back to being furniture.

Furniture does not choose. Furniture is not asked what it deserves.

The Ford had been sulking since Tuesday — a wet cough in the engine, a smell of hot oil I didn't like — but I fed it the last of the good petrol and coaxed it round to the yard while the light was still going. Ruth came out with a lantern held high, snow already settling in her grey hair so that she looked briefly like a woman made of ash.

"You'll turn back at the first sign," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd learned by now how to phrase orders so they sounded like weather reports.

"At the first sign," I agreed.

"And you'll tell me true when you come down. Not the kind thing. The true thing."

"I only know one kind of telling, Mrs Callow."

"No," she said, looking at me a moment too long. "You know two. You just pretend the second one doesn't exist." She handed me a flask of tea laced with enough brandy to be medically defensible. "For the cold. Go on."

Clemence watched from the drawing-room window. I felt her watching the way you feel a draft under a door. Edith did not appear at all, which was its own message: the mistress of Ashcombe had decided the mountain's opinion did not require her attendance.

The village was already white to the knees. I passed the Bell, where old Haskin stood in the doorway with his arms folded against the cold, and he called out — "You'll not get past the quarry bend, Mr Vane! Post sledge turned back this morning!" — and I lifted a hand as though his information were merely a greeting. The post sledge turning back meant the mail route was dead. It meant the last letters anyone in this valley would write or receive until thaw would have to be carried by hand, by fools, by me.

I should have turned around at Haskin's doorstep. I noted this fact later, filing it among the other facts about myself I preferred to store rather than examine.

* * *

The road climbed through the beech wood in long switchbacks, and for the first four miles it was merely difficult — rutted, glazed where meltwater had frozen in the wheel-tracks, the branches overhead carrying snow like a congregation carrying candles. I drove with both hands strangled on the wheel and the windscreen wipers useless, clearing my view with a gloved fist every thirty seconds, and I thought, in the idiotic serenity of a man doing something dangerous, *this isn't so bad.*

That's the trap of mountains. They let you believe for miles.

Past the quarry the trees thinned and the wind arrived all at once, the way bad news arrives — not gradually but entirely, a wall of it driving the snow horizontal, filling the world with a white static in which distance ceased to exist. I dropped to second gear. Then first. The Ford coughed its wet cough and I spoke to it aloud, the way one speaks to horses and dying men: *not now, not now, hold together, old girl.*

At the quarry bend I got out to walk ahead and test the drifts, because Haskin had been right about everything else. The snow came to my thigh. Beyond the bend, where the track cut between the fell and the drop into the next valley, I could see nothing — not the track, not the drop, not the sky. Just white, and the sound of the wind like a train that never arrives.

There was a marker post every hundred yards along that stretch. Iron posts, painted red. In summer you barely noticed them.

I found the first one by falling over it.

I lay in the snow for a moment, cheek against the drift, and considered the mathematics of my position. A man on foot, in failing light, in a whiteout, on a road whose edges were a cliff. A motorcar behind him that might or might not start again after standing in this cold. A boy in the valley below burning with fever, whose single chance overland was currently face-down in a snowdrift taking inventory of iron posts.

I got up. I walked to the second post. I found the third only by luck and the fourth not at all, and somewhere in that white blindness I understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that the pass was closed. Not officially. Not with a sign or a bell or a man in a uniform. Closed the way a throat closes — absolutely, silently, without negotiation.

Every door but one.

I turned the Ford where the drift allowed, which took twenty minutes of shoveling with the spade from the tool box and ten of rocking her free, and I went down the mountain with the engine braking and my heart going like a telegraph key. All the way down I was aware of a feeling I couldn't at first name. Relief? No. Something worse than relief. It was the feeling of a man who has tested the lock on a cell and found it genuine — who is now, at least, spared the responsibility of escape.

Claustrophobia is supposed to feel like walls closing in. What it actually feels like, I discovered that night, is doors closing elsewhere — each one with a small, civil click — until only a single exit remains, and everyone in the room knows it, and everyone in the room is pretending not to count who fits through.

One berth. One steamer. One way out of Ashcombe Vale until spring.

And five people who knew about it.

* * *

Ruth met me in the yard with the lantern again, and I told her the true thing, as instructed. "It's shut. Properly shut. Drifts over the quarry bend past the markers."

She received it the way stone receives rain. "Then it's the boat."

"It's the boat."

"Or nothing."

"Or nothing," I said.

She looked past me at the darkening fells as if she could see the snow still falling up there, sealing us in stitch by stitch. Then she said, quietly, in the voice of a woman laying a card face-down on a table: "Thank you for trying, Mr Vane. Truly." And she went inside, and I stood in the yard a while longer, because I had heard something underneath the gratitude that I liked very little.

Ruth Callow had stopped asking.

* * *

Inside, the house had gone strange. Snow-sealed houses do — the silence changes texture, becomes upholstered, as though the whole building has been wrapped in cotton wool and posted to itself. The lamps were lit early. Clemence sat in the drawing room doing nothing with great attention, a book open on her knee that I would have wagered my wages she hadn't read a page of. When I came in to report, she closed it.

"Well?" she said. "Is the mountain passable?"

"Not for love nor money."

"Mm." She studied me with those London eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "So we're all in the same boat now. Figuratively speaking." A small smile. "Some of us more literally than others."

"Clemence—"

"Don't." She said it lightly, almost kindly, raising one hand. "I haven't asked you anything today, have I? I'm merely observing the weather. Like everyone else in this house." She reopened her book. "Do give my mother my regards. And do tell her —" she paused, choosing the words with a jeweler's care — "that I've been thinking about what a shame it is, a berth going empty. On a ship that will sail regardless. Empty berths are such a waste. Don't you find?"

I gave Edith her regards. I did not deliver the sentence about empty berths. Some messages, once carried, cannot be unread, and I had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not delivering things — a skill, I realized as I stood dripping in the hall, that was rapidly becoming obsolete. In a sealed valley, everything gets delivered eventually. Every secret finds its address.

Edith was in the study with the ledger open in front of her and a pen in her hand that she wasn't using. She looked up when I entered, and for a moment — just a moment — she had the expression of a woman interrupted at prayer.

"The pass?" she said.

"Closed. Until thaw, if the thaw is kind."

She nodded slowly and looked down at the ledger, and her thumb moved along the edge of the pages the way a blind woman reads. I knew what page it lay open to. I had read it often enough myself by now, in stolen minutes, in the blue hour before the household woke: the column headed simply *South*, and beneath it the single entry, in ink gone brown with age — *Berth, R.M.S. Coromandel, 14th — for J.M.* — James Marchmont, dead three years in a hospital bed in Singapore, and his reservation kept alive ever since like a flame under glass.

"There's a storm coming besides," Edith said. "Haskin's boy brought word. Barometer's fallen off the shelf, apparently." She smiled faintly at her own figure of speech. "When it comes, the steamer office will move the sailing forward. They always do. Ships don't wait on grief, Mr Vane. Only on tides."

"No, madam."

"So whatever is to be decided—" She stopped. Her hand flattened on the ledger as though holding down a lid. "Whatever is to be decided will be decided soon."

I should have left then. Instead I heard myself say: "Must it be decided by you alone?"

Her eyes came up, and there was something in them I had never seen there before — something naked and appalled, the look of a general shown the casualty lists. "Who else, Mr Vane?" she said softly. "Who else keeps the books?"

* * *

I didn't sleep. At two in the morning I dressed and went down to the kitchen, ostensibly for milk, actually because the house felt too small to breathe in, and found the lamp still burning in the servants' hall and Tobias curled on the settle under three blankets, flushed scarlet, breathing in short shallow pulls like a man climbing stairs forever.

Ruth sat beside him, mending something that did not need mending.

"He shouldn't be down here," I said.

"Doctor says cool air helps. Doctor says a lot of things." She didn't look up from the sock she was darning — a sock, at two in the morning, in a house where every door was shutting. "He asked for his father tonight. Asked if his dad had ever been snowed in anywhere exciting."

"What did you tell him?"

"That his father once drove cattle through worse than this, over the top of the fell, and sang the whole way so as not to be frightened." She pulled the wool tight with her teeth and snapped the thread. "It's even true. Near enough."

Tobias stirred and opened his eyes, and found me across the lamplight, and smiled — a hot, bright, terrible smile. "Mr Vane," he whispered. "Did you see the snow up top?"

"I did."

"Was it deep as the mill wheel?"

"Deeper."

His grin widened, delighted, and he burrowed back into the blankets, and within a minute he was asleep again, that fevered shallow sleep that isn't rest so much as a truce. Ruth and I sat without speaking. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us the wind found the chimney and moaned down it, testing the house the way I had tested the pass.

"Mrs Callow," I said at last, because someone had to say it, "if the fever goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. There's no doctor closer than the vale mouth now, and the bridge is shut by order. The boat sails on the fourteenth, or earlier, if the storm—"

"I know what sails and when." Her voice was level. Her hands, folding the mended sock, were not quite. "I've known since the day I found you reading that ledger, Mr Vane. You think I haven't? You think a woman who has cleaned this house for nineteen years doesn't know which pages get moved and which get read?" She set the sock down and looked at me, and there was no anger in her face at all, which was far worse. "I've been patient. I've asked nicely. I've begged God, who doesn't answer, and Edith Marchmont, who hasn't yet."

"Ruth—"

"But patience runs out when the roads do." She rose, gathered her sleeping son against her shoulder — he was nearly too big for it, fourteen years old and all elbows, and she lifted him anyway, the way the sea lifts anything it has decided to take — and paused at the door.

"One berth," she said. "One way out. Five of us in this house who know it exists. Do your arithmetic, Mr Vane. You're fond of other people's sums."

She carried him upstairs. I sat alone in the servants' hall with the hissing lamp and did the arithmetic she'd set me, and every total came out the same, and none of them were numbers I could live inside.

Upstairs, faintly, Tobias began to cough — deep now, racking, wrong — and did not stop.
