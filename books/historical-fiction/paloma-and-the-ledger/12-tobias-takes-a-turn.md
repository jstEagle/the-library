## 12. Tobias Takes a Turn

Ruth Callow had learned long ago that fear was a thing you carried in the hands, not the chest. You could feel it in your palms first — a dampness, a restlessness — before it ever reached anywhere that mattered. Her mother had taught her this in another valley, another fever year, walking Ruth through the sickrooms with a basin of vinegar water and saying, *Keep your hands busy, girl. Hands that are busy don't shake.* Ruth had been eleven. She had kept her hands busy for thirty years since.

Now they were busy again: wringing flannels, folding them into cool rectangles, laying them across Tobias's neck and wrists and the shallow bowl of his throat where the heat pooled like standing water. The boy was fifteen and had shot up that spring until he'd outgrown every shirt he owned, all elbows and appetite, and now the fever had burned him down to something small and strange, a stranger wearing her son's face, his lips cracked and his eyes glassy with a light she recognized from other bedsides, other valleys, other mothers' hands going busy.

"Water," he said, or tried to. It came out as a dry click.

"I know, love. I've got it here."

She propped him up and held the cup to his mouth, tipping it slowly because fast water made him retch, and retching cost him strength he hadn't got. Outside the attic window the rain had stopped at last, but the light through the glass was the color of dishwater, and somewhere below she could hear the river running high and brown under the bridge it had already swallowed. Three days since the doctor's order closed it. Three days since the word *quarantine* had been spoken aloud in Edith Marchmont's drawing room as though naming a weather system rather than a cage.

Tobias drank half the cup and sagged back against the pillows.

"Mum," he said. "The spots."

She had been avoiding looking at his chest. She looked now. Under the damp linen of his nightshirt, the rash had spread overnight — fine red points crowding together along his ribs, beginning to merge into blotches. She had seen that pattern once before, in the epidemic of '98, when she was a girl helping the district nurse carry basins door to door. She knew what it meant when the points began to run together. It meant the fever had stopped being a visitor and had decided to move in.

"It's nothing," she said. "It's coming out nicely. That's how it goes — it has to come out before it can go."

That was even true, or true enough. What she didn't say was what came after the merging, on the fourth or fifth day in the bad cases: the cough that sounded like tearing cloth, the delirium, the terrible quiet at the end that was worse than any noise.

*Keep your hands busy.*

* * *

She went down to the kitchen to boil more water and found Mirabel Vane there before her, filling the big copper kettle as if he'd read her mind, which — she thought, watching him — perhaps he had. The man missed nothing. That was the trouble with Mirabel. He stood at the edge of every room like furniture that listened.

"How is he?" he asked.

"The same." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a door closing. "Better. He's better."

Mirabel set down the kettle and looked at her. He had one of those faces you couldn't read and couldn't stop trying to read — pale, watchful, a scar of an old pockmark near his temple that gave him the look of a man who'd survived something and never mentioned it. He didn't say *you're lying*, which most men would have. He said:

"Mrs. Callow. If there's anything—"

"There isn't anything anyone can do," she said, "except keep out of the way and let me work."

It was unkind, and she saw it land, and she did not take it back. Kindness was a luxury rationed by the hour now, and hers was all spent upstairs.

But when she turned away she felt him still watching, and she understood something then that she would have called impossible a fortnight ago: that Mirabel Vane was not furniture. Furniture didn't look at a woman like that. Furniture didn't know.

*He knows,* she thought, pumping water, watching the level rise in the kettle. *He knows about the berth.*

Of course he knew. He drove Edith everywhere; he read her letters off the seat beside him — Ruth had caught him at it twice and said nothing, because a household runs on what people pretend not to see. And the ledger — everyone in the house had some sense of the ledger, the way you have a sense of a locked room. Debts and favors banked like coins. And the one thing in it that mattered now, the single berth booked on the last mail steamer south out of Ashcombe Quay, unnamed, reserved, waiting like an empty chair at a table.

Edith Marchmont could not choose who sat in it. So no one sat in it, while the river rose and the pass filled with snow and a boy's rash ran together on his ribs.

Ruth pumped the handle harder than the kettle needed.

* * *

By evening Tobias was talking to someone who wasn't there.

She sat by the bed listening to him argue softly with his father — dead four years, drowned in the same river that was now eating the bridge, drowned hauling timber in flood season because a man with two children couldn't afford to wait for calm water. Tobias had been eleven when it happened. He didn't remember much, which was its own kind of wound, and now the fever was giving him back conversations he'd never had, his father's voice reconstructed out of longing the way a blind man builds a face from touch.

"He says the boat's coming, Mum," Tobias said, lucid for a moment, turning his burning eyes to her. "He says we're to be ready."

"We will be," Ruth said. Her voice held. Her hands, busy folding flannel, held. "You sleep now."

When he slept she went to the window. Below, the drive lay dark, and beyond it the vale, and beyond that, faint against the last grey of the sky, the line of the hills where the overland track climbed toward the pass. Snow smoke on the summits already. A day, maybe two, and that road shut too. Then the steamer berth would be the only door left open in the whole world, and it was written in a ledger in a locked drawer in a locked study, and the woman who held the key could not bring herself to turn it.

Ruth stood at the window and felt the change come over her. It did not feel like rage. It felt like arithmetic.

She had spent her life being the good one. Loyal housekeeper — twenty-two years in this house, longer than Clemence had lived in it, longer than the dead son had lived in it. She had buried her husband's wages in patience and her grief in work, and she had asked for nothing, and asking for nothing had been presented to her, all her life, as a kind of virtue. As though virtue were what you got instead of things.

Her hands were steady. That was the frightening part. All this time she'd expected that if she ever broke, she would break — shaking, weeping, undone. Instead she felt clear as cold water. Upstairs her son was arguing with a dead man. In the study downstairs a book listed everything owed to everyone in this valley, and one line in it was worth a boy's life, and the owner of the book was dithering over it like a woman choosing between teacups.

No. Not quite fair. She made herself say so, because fairness mattered even now — especially now. Edith Marchmont was not dithering from cruelty. Ruth had seen her at the boy's door twice these past days, standing just inside the threshold, that great iron woman of the suffrage platforms gone small and helpless, holding her hat in her hands like a girl called before the headmistress. She wanted to save him. She simply could not decide, and her inability had become its own kind of violence.

Well. Ruth had decided.

*If she won't choose,* Ruth thought, *then I'll choose for her. And God forgive me, I'll choose mine.*

* * *

She found Mirabel in the garage at nearly midnight, sitting in the motorcar with the lamp off, doing something with a wrench and a carburettor that he probably didn't need to do. Men went to engines the way she went to flannels. Busy hands.

She closed the garage door behind her. The sound of it made him look up.

"You'll drive us," she said. Not a question. She heard that herself and let it stand.

"Drive you where?" He set the wrench down carefully. "The bridge is closed. By order, not just season."

"The steamer. Saturday morning, out of Ashcombe Quay. There's a berth."

Silence. The rain started again against the roof, soft as ash.

"Mrs. Callow," Mirabel said at last, very quietly, "you shouldn't say such things to me."

"Why not? Because you'll tell her?" She almost laughed. "Go on, then. Tell her I begged you to steal her boat ticket. Let her turn me off without a character after twenty-two years. I'll walk to the quay barefoot if I have to."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm not going to tell her anything."

"No," Ruth said. "I didn't think you were." She came closer, close enough to see his eyes in the dark of the garage, and lowered her voice to the register she used for talking sense into frightened girls in service. "Here's what I've worked out, Mr. Vane, and you can correct me where I'm wrong. That berth exists. It's booked. It's for nobody yet, because she can't bear to name anybody, because whoever she names gets saved and everybody else doesn't, and she'd rather the river decide than do it herself. Am I wrong?"

He said nothing. His silence was answer enough.

"So the river will decide," Ruth went on. "And the river decides for the strong and the lucky and the ones with money, and my boy is none of those. He is fifteen years old and his father is under that water and he is up there right now talking to him, and if that fever turns tonight the way it turned for the Halloran girl last week, then there will not be a decision left to make. Do you understand me? I am done asking nicely. I have asked nicely my whole life."

"What is it you want from me?"

"Nothing illegal. Nothing violent. I want you to drive us to the quay Saturday morning, and I want to stand on that dock when the gangplank comes down, and I want to put my son on that boat if I have to explain myself to the captain, to the coastguard, to her ladyship herself. That's all. I'm not stealing. I'm collecting a debt." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and pushed through. "Twenty-two years, Mr. Vane. This house has run on my hands. Every meal, every fire, every sickbed, every secret swept under every rug. If there's a ledger in this house — and there is, don't tell me there isn't, I've dusted around it for ten years — then what's owed to me is written in it whether she wrote it down or not."

Mirabel sat motionless in the driver's seat. When he finally spoke, his voice was strange — not shocked, not refusing. Almost relieved, as though she'd said aloud a thing he'd been carrying alone.

"And if she refuses at the dock? If she stands on the quay and says no?"

Ruth looked at him steadily.

"Then she'll have to say it to my face, with my boy standing there, and live with having said it." She paused. "And if she tries to stop me physical — if anyone lays a hand on that child — then yes, Mr. Vane. Then I'll take it by force, and I'll answer for it after, gladly, in any court in England. But it won't come to that."

"Why won't it?"

"Because I've watched that woman for twenty-two years," Ruth said. "And underneath all the iron she is a mother who lost her son, and she has been keeping that berth empty for him, and some part of her — the part that stood at my boy's door with her hat in her hands — is praying someone will make the choice for her. Take it out of her hands. She's too proud to beg and too honest to steal, so she's frozen between them. Somebody has to move first."

The rain drummed. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the vale like furniture dragged overhead.

"You've thought about this a great deal," Mirabel said.

"My hands have been busy," Ruth said. "There's nothing else for them to do."

He looked down at the steering wheel, both pale hands resting on it, and she saw — clearly, unmistakably — that he was not deciding whether to help her. He had already decided. What remained was only the shape of it, the method, the how. And there was something in his stillness that told her the method had already occurred to him too, something to do with pen and ink and a leather ledger, though he said nothing of it and she asked nothing either. A household runs on what people agree not to see. Perhaps it also ran on what they agreed not to say.

"Saturday," he said at last. "Before dawn. I'll have the car ready. Tell no one — not even the boy."

"Not even the boy," Ruth agreed.

She turned to go, hand on the door, and he spoke once more behind her, so quietly she almost missed it beneath the rain.

"Mrs. Callow. For what it's worth — I hope it comes to force. I mean—" a breath, almost a laugh, bleak as November. "I mean I hope you're wrong that it won't come to it. I hope she fights you. Because a woman like that, fighting — that's the closest she'll ever come to telling you the truth."

Ruth stood a moment with her hand on the latch, considering this, deciding whether it was wisdom or madness, deciding it might be both.

"Goodnight, Mr. Vane," she said, and went out into the rain.

Halfway across the yard she heard, above the drumming on the cobbles, the sound of the garage door opening behind her, and Mirabel Vane crossing the gravel at a pace no chauffeur keeps unless the house is on fire or the die is cast — and when she turned, he was already at the kitchen door, holding it open, his face in the lamplight stripped of everything except purpose.

"Come quickly," he said. "It's the ledger. She's taken it out of the drawer — she's at her desk right now with a pen in her hand."

Ruth's heart went once, hard, like a fist against a door.

"She's writing in the name," Mirabel said. "And Mrs. Callow — from where I stood in the hall — the ink is still wet, and I'd swear on anything you like it isn't your boy's."
