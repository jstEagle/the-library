## 13. The Forged Page

The house slept, and Mirabel Vane did not.

He lay in the narrow room above the garage with his boots on and his coat folded over the chair, listening to the rain rehearse for the storm everyone said was coming. Below him the motorcar sat in its dark like a patient animal, and beyond it, down the drive and through two locked doors, Edith Marchmont's ledger waited in her study like a confession nobody had asked for yet.

He got up at half past one because there was no use pretending to sleep past that hour anyway. He dressed without lighting the lamp — he knew the geography of this house better than its owner suspected; he had carried post through it for three years, and an invisible man learns a house the way water learns a channel — and he went down the back stairs with the particular silence of a man who has practiced being unheard until it became a profession.

The study smelled of ink and cold fire. Edith's pen stood in its holder where she'd left it at ten o'clock, capped but not cleaned, which told him she meant to come back to whatever she'd been writing. The ledger sat on the desk under the green-shaded lamp she never fully turned off, as if darkness might be kept out of the accounts by habit alone.

He did not touch it at first. He stood over it the way he used to stand over letters, letting himself feel the weight of what he was about to do, because he had learned long ago that the only way to do a dishonest thing cleanly was to look at it honestly first.

*I am not stealing anything,* he told himself. *I am correcting an ambiguity.*

It was a beautiful sentence. He examined it from all sides and found it held no weight at all, and he proceeded anyway.

* * *

The trouble — the whole trouble, he thought later, though of course there were other troubles, larger ones, waiting in the wings — began with the paper.

Edith Marchmont kept her accounts on heavy stock, cream-colored, with a faint ribbing you could feel under a thumbnail. It had been made for her by a stationer in Bath who had died, Mirabel happened to know, in 1909, taking his particular watermark with him into whatever stationers' heaven received meticulous men. The ledger pages matched nothing else in the house. There was no spare stock. There was no possibility of adding a page, or replacing one, or doing any of the tidy things a forger does in novels while the orchestra plays.

There was only the page itself, and the ink already on it, and the fact that the entry — the berth, the single reserved passage on the *Coromandel*, sailing south from Ashcombe Quay when the steamer made its last call of the season — read:

*One berth, S.S. Coromandel, 14th inst. Passenger: unnamed.*

*Unnamed.* Not *transferable*. Not *assignable at bearer's discretion*. Unnamed, which in the language of shipping offices meant one thing and one thing only: whoever's name went into that blank at the quay would be the passenger, and the name would be checked against the booking letter, and the booking letter lived in the same ledger, and Edith Marchmont's word was the only key that opened either.

Mirabel crouched at the desk and read the entry eleven times. On the twelfth reading he stopped seeing words and started seeing surfaces, which was the trick of his trade. Three years of reading other people's mail had taught him that every document is two documents: the one it says, and the one its materials admit. Paper remembers. Ink remembers. A hand remembers most of all, even the hand of its own owner, especially at night, especially tired.

Edith's hand was distinctive — a tall, imperious script with a downward slash she made of her crosses, as if every *t* were a small assertion of will. He could imitate it. He had been imitating hands since he was fourteen years old, when he had discovered that a schoolmaster's signature opened the stationery cupboard, and that the world was full of cupboards, and that nearly all of them were guarded by nothing but handwriting.

That was the skill nobody knew about. That was the secret under the secrets. People believed he read letters because he was nosy, or lonely, or small-souled, and let them believe it, because the truth was worse: he read letters the way a locksmith reads locks, professionally, and he had never once needed to force anything. Every seal he had ever lifted had gone back down looking untouched. Every envelope he had ever opened had closed again believing it had never been open.

And now, for the first time in his life, he was going to write instead of read.

* * *

He worked out the chemistry of it before he touched the book.

The ledger's ink was iron gall — he could smell the faint vinegar sharpness of it when he leaned close — and iron gall has one virtue and one vice. Its virtue is that it bites into paper; it becomes part of the page rather than sitting on top of it, which is why Edith's accounts would still be legible when the house itself was dust. Its vice is that fresh iron gall sits on top of old iron gall like a stranger at a funeral. No matter how well you match the color, the new ink glints. It hasn't sunk in. Under lamplight, under a suspicious eye, the alteration shines.

So he could not write on the page. Not directly. Not a stroke.

What he could do — what the page itself, in its arrogance, invited him to do — was subtract.

Iron gall fades where you want it to fade, if you know the trick, and the trick is oxalic acid, which every chauffeur in England keeps in his kit for removing rust stains from chromium, and which eats iron compounds with a patience that looks like innocence. A bleached line of iron gall doesn't vanish; it goes the color of weak tea, the ghost of itself, visible if you hunt for it and invisible if you merely glance. And a glance is all anyone gives a ledger. Ledgers are trusted the way gravestones are trusted: nobody checks the arithmetic of the dead.

His plan assembled itself with the smoothness of a plan that has already decided to happen. He would lift the word *unnamed*. He would bleach it back to shadow. Then, above the pale scar, in a hand indistinguishable from Edith Marchmont's own because it would *be* Edith Marchmont's own — her pen, her ink, her angle of wrist — he would write the amendment she should have written herself, the amendment any careful woman of affairs would have written given a winter of plague and uncertainty:

*Passenger: unnamed, transferable at holder's discretion.*

Not a forgery, then. An *interpretation*. A gloss. The kind of thing a solicitor would call a clarification and a magistrate would call something else entirely, if anyone ever looked, which no one ever would, because the person with the strongest interest in checking the ledger was the person whose authority the forged line borrowed.

He was laundering the sentence through her handwriting, and the beauty of it made him slightly sick, and the sickness made him steadier. That was the discovery of the night, the one he would carry forward into everything that came after: that his conscience and his craft ran on the same current, and that feeling horrified made his hand more level, not less.

* * *

He fetched his kit from the garage — the little japanned box with its stoppered bottles, its glass rod, its swabs of lint — and he came back into the study and set the lamp low, because a job like this wants dim light. Bright light is for honest work.

The bleaching took forty minutes and felt like forty years.

He wetted the lint with the acid, barely wetted it, and laid it along the strokes of *unnamed* the way you'd lay a compress on a wound, and then he sat with his wrist suspended and watched the word die. It didn't die all at once. It faded the way a face fades from a window when the carriage pulls away — by degrees, each degree a small argument against stopping. At the halfway mark the word was still legible enough that he could have quit, put the acid away, gone upstairs, and woken tomorrow as a man who had merely considered a crime in detail. He looked at that halfway word for a long time.

*Tobias,* he thought. The boy had coughed twice at supper and Ruth had gone white as the tablecloth both times, and nobody else had appeared to notice, and Mirabel had noticed, because noticing was his whole nature and now it was going to be his whole ruin.

He finished the bleaching.

Then came the part he had told himself would be administrative, and which refused, even as he did it, to stay administrative. He uncapped Edith's pen. He tested the flow on blotting paper until the ink ran the way hers ran — a hair thick, with the smallest bead at the end of a downstroke — and then he wrote, in her hand, in her voice, above the ghost of the erased word:

*— transferable at holder's discretion, this being my settled intent, E.M.*

He added the initials because initials are what make an amendment real. Anyone can add words; only authority adds initials. He drew the *E* with its high crossbar, the *M* with its deep-cut second valley, and he gave each letter the slight rightward lean of a woman writing quickly at the end of a working day, and when he set the pen down his own hand was shaking so finely that he had to press it flat against the desk to stop it, and he found he was smiling, and he found he wanted very badly not to be.

*Transferable.* Two words of wet ink. He had just rewritten what the berth *was*. Not who held it — that decision still belonged to Edith, God help them all — but what it could become. A berth fixed to a name is a verdict. A berth fixed to a discretion is a door. He had changed the grammar of escape, and grammar, he understood with a clarity that arrived too late to be useful, is where all power actually lives.

He blotted the line, closed the ledger, squared it on the desk exactly one finger's width from the desk edge, the way she kept it. He rinsed the pen and recapped it. He carried the acid bottle back to the garage and buried the used lint in the ash bucket and stood for a moment in the cold with his breath smoking, feeling the night air try to tell him something.

Here is what he told himself, walking back: *I haven't chosen anyone. I've only made choosing possible. The moral weight is still hers.* And here is what the house said back, in the voice houses use at two in the morning, which is your own voice with the warmth removed: *You built the door, Vane. Whoever walks through it walks because you built it. Authorship doesn't end at the printing.*

He reached his room. He did not sleep. He lay listening to the rain and turning the sentence over — *transferable at holder's discretion* — admiring it the way you admire a knife you've just used, and somewhere around four o'clock he admitted the other thing, the thing he had known since the halfway mark of the fading word and had declined to say even to himself.

He had not done it for Tobias. Not only. He had done it because Clemence had looked at him across the conservatory two nights ago and said, in the tone of one collector appraising another, *You read her letters, don't you?* — and because the money she'd offered sat in his coat pocket like a stone, unspent, unforgiven — and because a forged line in a dead woman's stationer's best cream paper was also, incidentally, the only move on the board that kept *his own* name available for that blank at the quay.

A door built for the boy that anyone with discretion could walk through. Including its builder.

He had made himself the keeper of a discretion. And keepers of discretions, he thought as the sky went the grey of dirty wool, are the first people other people come to see.

Which was why, when he finally drifted off as the birds started, his last waking thought was not of Ruth or Tobias or Edith or the money in his pocket.

It was of Clemence Marchmont, awake somewhere in this house, in some room he couldn't place, and whether she was asleep at all — and whether she, too, had spent this night thinking about a certain ledger, and a certain blank space, and whose hand had last touched it.

Because when he passed her door on his way back up the stairs, it had been open.

And the room beyond it had been empty.
