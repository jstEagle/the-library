## 15. Night Watch

Ruth Callow did not ask me to sit with her son that night. She simply stopped pretending she could do it alone.

I found her in the kitchen at half past eleven, asleep sitting up, her head tipped against the dresser where the good china lived, a bowl of cooled water in her lap and a cloth fallen from her hand onto the flagstones. The lamp had burned down to a stub. Upstairs, through two floors of old house, I could hear Tobias breathing — that wet, laboured sound like someone working a bellows full of gravel — and beneath it, every few minutes, the small involuntary cry he made without waking.

I stood in the doorway for longer than I would admit to anyone. My whole life I had built myself out of doorways: the man who delivers, who drives, who reads and says nothing. Doorways were safe. You could stand in one forever and be blamed for nothing.

But the boy cried out again, and Ruth's head jerked even in sleep, her hands closing on nothing, and I understood that if I turned around now I would hear that sound for the rest of my life.

I touched her shoulder. "Go to bed," I said. "Two hours. I'll wake you."

She came up out of sleep fighting, as mothers do, and for a moment I thought she'd strike me. Then her eyes cleared and she saw the lamp, the chair already dragged to the bedside, the basin I'd filled at the pump in the scullery, and something went out of her face that I had never seen there before — not gratitude exactly. Surrender. She looked at her son, then at me, and said only: "The cloth goes behind his knees when the shaking starts. Not the forehead. Behind the knees."

"I'll remember."

"And if—" She stopped. Mothers don't finish those sentences. She pressed her lips together until they went white, took the candle, and went.

* * *

Tobias Callow was fourteen years old and I realised, sitting down beside his bed, that I had never once spoken to him.

Three months I had lived under this roof. I had driven his mother to market twice a week, carried her parcels, read her letters over their sealed flaps by lamplight while she believed herself unobserved. I knew her handwriting better than my own. And her son — the reason she worked herself grey, the whole engine of her — was a shape on the stairs, a pair of boots by the back door, a name mentioned in passing. *Tobias is after the rabbits again. Tobias has got himself a place at the joiner's come spring.* I had filed him away the way I filed everything: as context. As furniture in other people's stories.

Now the furniture was burning up with fever and looking at me with eyes gone glassy-bright, and saying, in a voice scraped down to almost nothing:

"Are you the motorcar man?"

"I'm the motorcar man."

"Mum said someone'd come." His eyes drifted shut. "Thought she meant the doctor."

"The doctor's been called. Roads are bad." This was true, in the way that all our truths had become true lately — technically accurate and entirely useless. Dr. Penhale was confined to the village below by the quarantine order; the bridge was closed; the pass was filling with snow. Every road out of Ashcombe Vale now led nowhere, except the one that led onto the last steamer, and that road ran through Edith Marchmont's ledger, which lay locked in her study upstairs like a heart in a chest.

I wrung out the cloth and laid it behind his knees, as instructed. He shivered violently and then, horribly, smiled.

"That's better," he said. "That's proper better."

"Good."

"You're Mr. Vane. Mum says you drive Mrs. Marchmont like the horses used to but faster." A cough took him, deep and rattling, and bent him nearly double, and when it released him he lay gasping, and I held the basin because I'd guessed what was coming and I was right. When it was over he was ashamed — I saw it cross his face, fourteen years old and furious at his own body — and I pretended not to have noticed, which is a kindness I learned from no one and practised all my life.

"Mr. Vane," he said, when his breathing had settled. "Could you talk? Only when it's quiet I can hear my own chest going, and I don't like it."

So I talked. I told him about the roads I'd driven — the coast road at Torquay in fog, the hill at Cheddar where the engine had boiled over and a farmer's bull had taken an interest in the radiator. He liked the bull. He asked questions a mechanic would ask, good ones, and I answered what I could, and somewhere past midnight the talking wore out and the fever climbed instead, and the shaking started, and I did the cloth behind the knees, and the clock in the hall struck one, and then two, and the house creaked around us like a ship.

It was during the third hour that I opened the satchel.

* * *

I should explain about the satchel.

Every letter Edith Marchmont received passed through my hands first — that was the arrangement, unspoken and absolute, since my second week in her service. I steamed or slit or simply slid a knife along the flap of anything interesting, read it by the light of the garage lamp, resealed it with a skill I do not intend to describe here, and delivered it the next morning untouched. For years this had been my secret sustenance. Other men drank. I read other people's mail. It made me feel — I told myself — informed. Detached. Safe.

The letters I had copied or kept were in the false bottom of my tool satchel, because a man who hides things keeps them where no one looks twice, and no one looks twice at a chauffeur's tools. There were perhaps forty of them. Solicitors' letters. A lawyer's threat from a cousin in Bristol. Three years of Edith Marchmont's private weather, folded into squares.

I had never intended to read them aloud to anyone. They were mine the way sins are yours — kept precisely so that no one else could touch them.

But Tobias was past talking now, drifting in and out, whimpering at things in the room that weren't there, and the doctor was a day's drive and a closed bridge away, and there was nothing in the house for his fever but willow bark and cold cloths, and I thought: *a voice might anchor him*. People say that. Voices anchor the drowning. And the only voice I had was my own, and the only words in the house at two in the morning, besides scripture, were the ones I had stolen.

I took out the first letter and felt like a burglar in a sickroom.

"This isn't very interesting," I told him, though he couldn't have heard me. "It's about drainage rights on the south meadow. Honestly, you're better off asleep."

He stirred. "Read it," he said, or dreamed he said it, and so I read it — the whole tedious business of culverts and easements, in the flat voice of a man reading a laundry list — and it worked, is the thing. The drone of it settled him the way a settling snow settles. His breathing slowed. His hands stopped plucking at the blanket.

So I read the next one. And the next.

Somewhere in the fourth letter — a long, furious, magnificent letter from Edith to her solicitor regarding the inheritance dispute, all steel and contempt on the surface and, underneath, if you read it right, terror — Tobias surfaced enough to ask, "Who's writing all that?"

"Mrs. Marchmont."

"She sounds angry."

"She's frightened," I said, before I could stop myself. "Angry is just frightened wearing its boots."

He considered this with the gravity the fever gave him. "Mum's like that," he said. "When Dad died she didn't cry till the funeral dinner. Then she broke a plate." His eyes closed. "She never broke another one. That's how I knew it was finished coming out."

I sat with that. Outside, the wind had begun to work at the eaves, the front edge of the storm the barometer had been threatening all week, and the lamp flame leaned and righted itself, leaned and righted, like a small persistent courage.

"Tobias," I said. "Your father. What was he called?"

"Alfred." The word came out round and careful, a stone from a pocket he carried everywhere. "Alfred Callow. He drove the mill cart. Before the mill—" He stopped. Everyone in the valley stopped at the same word these days. The mill still stood, but the river had been eating its foundations all autumn, and everyone knew whose account the flood season would settle.

"He wrote you letters?" I asked. "Before?"

"One." His eyes opened, bright and unfocused. "Mum's got it. She reads it to me every year on his birthday. I'm not supposed to know she cries after."

And there it was — the specific, unbearable weight of it. Because I knew about Alfred Callow's letter. Of course I did. It had come through my hands eighteen months ago, forwarded from the shipping office in Southampton, and I had read it in the garage with the door shut, and I had resealed it and delivered it and told myself it was none of my business that a dying man three hundred miles away had written to a son too young to remember his face.

It was in the satchel. I had copied it entire, in my own fair hand, because some letters you cannot put down unread and some you cannot put down at all.

I am not going to pretend I deliberated nobly. I sat there with the paper in my hands for the length of ten breaths, and what I felt was mostly fear — the old fear, the doorway fear, the fear of being seen holding something that proves you were never a bystander at all. Bystanders don't keep copies of dead men's letters. Witnesses do. And I had been a witness for years, secretly, greedily, telling myself it was detachment when it was the opposite: it was appetite. I had wanted history the way misers want coins, and kept it, and spent none of it.

The boy shivered. The wind leaned on the house. I unfolded the page.

"There's a letter," I said. "From your father. I — your mother showed it to me once." The lie came easily and tasted like ash, and I let it stand, because the truth would keep and the boy might not. "Would you like to hear it?"

"Yes," he whispered. "Please."

* * *

*Tobias —*

*I have been a long time writing this, which you must not take for reluctance. It is only that everything I write seems either too big or too small, and a father ought to get it exactly right.*

*You are four. You will not remember me properly, and I want to tell you that this is not a tragedy. Memory is a stingy landlord and evicts the best tenants. What you will have instead is your mother, who remembers everything, and who will give it back to you piece by piece across the years, and you must let her, even when it makes you sad, especially when it makes you sad, because grief shared is grief halved and joy shared is joy doubled, and your mother contains more of both than any man I ever met.*

*Be kind to her. Not dutiful — kind. There is a difference and you are young enough to learn it properly.*

*I leave you nothing but this advice, having failed to leave you money: when you come to a choice between the safe thing and the true thing, count how many people are standing with you. If the answer is one, choose the true thing anyway. One is enough. I had one, and I was the richest man in this valley and nobody knew it.*

*The doctor says weeks. I say he flatters me.*

*Goodbye, lad. Mind the river in spring.*

*— Your father*

I got through it. I don't know how; my voice behaved as though it belonged to someone else, someone steadier, and I let that man do the reading while I sat in the corner of myself and shook.

When I finished, the room was very quiet. The wind had dropped for a moment, the way wind does, gathering itself. Tobias was looking at the ceiling with tears running sideways into his hair, and he wasn't making any sound at all, and I understood that this was the crying his mother had taught him without meaning to — the silent kind, the kind that breaks plates later, years later, in kitchens, alone.

"He says one is enough," Tobias said finally.

"Yes."

"Do you think that's true?"

I thought about the ledger upstairs. About a woman who had counted everything — debts, favours, daughters, sons — and could not make one berth equal one person, because arithmetic fails at exactly the point where love begins. I thought about Ruth asleep on her feet for six nights. About Clemence arriving tomorrow or the day after, smelling inheritance like smoke. About myself, forty-one years old, who had chosen the safe thing so many times it had worn a groove in me like a cart-track, and where had the groove led? To a garage. To a satchel. To a locked room full of other people's lives.

"It's true," I said. "I think it's the truest thing anyone ever wrote him."

"He sounds like he knew he was going to die and wasn't scared."

"He was scared. He just loved something more than the fear." I wiped the boy's face with the cloth, gently, the way you'd handle a document you can't replace. "Sleep now. I'll be here."

He slept. Properly, deeply, the fever breaking not all at once but in degrees, like ice going out of a river — and I sat in the chair with the dead man's letter in my lap and the storm rebuilding itself against the windows, and I did something I had not done since I was nine years old.

I wept. Quietly, in the dark, in the corner of myself where no one could see — and then, noticing that no one could see, and that it made no difference to the truth of it, I let it move into the rest of the room.

* * *

Ruth came down at dawn — I heard her on the stairs and met her at the door — and I gave my report like a nurse: temperature down since four, took broth at five, slept soundly, the cloth behind the knees, three times. She pushed past me to the bed and laid the back of her hand against her son's cheek and stood there a long moment, and her shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time in a week.

Then she turned and looked at me, really looked, the way you look at a stranger you've suddenly discovered has been in the room the whole time.

"You read to him," she said. "He told me. Half-asleep. Said the motorcar man knows Dad's letter."

I had prepared a lie on the stairs. It dissolved in my mouth like sugar.

"Mrs. Callow," I said, "there are things in this house I know that I shouldn't, and I've told myself for three years that knowing is harmless. Last night I stopped believing it." I handed her the copy of Alfred's letter, my own fair hand, ink still faintly smudged. "This is yours. I've no right to have kept it, and no right to give it back, and I'm doing both."

She read the first lines. Her hand went to her mouth. When she looked up her eyes were dry and terrible and awake, and I saw in them the thing I had seen building all week, the thing I had hoped was only exhaustion.

"Mirabel Vane," she said quietly, "you know about the ledger."

It was not a question. Downstairs, faintly, the hall clock began to strike seven, and above it, on the landing, a floorboard creaked under a deliberate weight — Edith Marchmont, awake early, dressed, moving toward the stairs with the step of a woman who has made a decision in the night and means to serve it with breakfast.
