## 16. Edith Decides

I had dressed in the dark so as not to wake the house, and found that dressing in the dark is its own kind of honesty. One cannot flatter oneself without light. The grey wool, the black brooch, the small pearl at the throat — I put them on by touch and by memory of how they had looked on better mornings, and when I came to the glass at last there was an old woman standing in it whom I recognized the way one recognizes a stranger met twice.

The decision had come to me somewhere between two and three o'clock, not as a revelation but as a settling, the way silt settles in a jar of river water and leaves it suddenly clear. All week I had watched the water roil — Clemence's careful laughter, Ruth's silence growing teeth, the boy coughing behind the green baize door, Vane moving through the rooms like a man carrying something breakable he refuses to name — and I had told myself I was weighing. I was not weighing. I was postponing. A woman who keeps a ledger for forty years learns every trick her own hand can play, and postponement written in a fair copy looks exactly like deliberation.

But at three o'clock the water cleared, and what lay at the bottom of the jar was this: a berth on the last steamer south, purchased four years ago under a dead boy's name, and three claimants, and me, who had promised herself she would never again be the woman who chooses which child survives.

I went down early because a decision served cold goes bad. I wanted it hot, at the table, with everyone present, so that no one could later say they had heard it secondhand or softened it in the retelling. If I was going to wound this house, I would do it in daylight and on my own crockery.

* * *

The dining room smelled of toast and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the river, which had been in everything since Tuesday. Mrs. Callow had laid the table for five — herself included now; that was new, and I noted it, because Ruth Callow does nothing new without a reason, and the reason sat upstairs with a damp cloth on his forehead. Tobias had turned eleven in September. He had a way of looking up at you sideways, as if asking permission to exist, and I had caught myself avoiding him all week, which is a cowardice I will name here if nowhere else: I avoided him because he made the berth real.

Clemence came down at half past eight, dressed for London in the middle of a flooded valley, which told me everything about where her body intended to be even while her mouth spoke of reconciliation. She kissed my cheek. Her perfume arrived a full second before she did.

"Mother, you look terrible," she said, with love.

"I look decided," I said. "It's often mistaken for it."

Vane poured the coffee. He has been with me six years and in that time I have learned precisely three things about him: that he reads faster than any man I have known, that he believes himself unobserved, and that he is wrong about the second. I let him pour. I have never once mentioned the letters. There are debts between us too, though his page in my ledger is blank, and blankness, in accounting, is only a debt you haven't yet priced.

Ruth came in last, from the kitchen, wiping her hands, and did not sit until I nodded. She stood by the sideboard instead. She has eaten standing in that corner for nineteen years, but this morning the posture read differently — less service than sentry.

"Sit down, all of you," I said. "Mrs. Callow, you as well. Today you eat at my table whether you like it or not."

She looked at me then, and I saw her understand that something was coming, because Ruth understands everything before it is said; it is why she has run my house better than I ever ran my life.

"The steamer leaves Southampton on Saturday," I began, "or it would, if the roads ran to Southampton. The dock road is open — the river hasn't taken the coast yet — and Mr. Vane can put us at the quay in nine hours if the ford holds. The berth is paid for. It has been paid for since 1908." I set down my cup. "You all know, or guess, whose name it carries."

Clemence's fork stopped halfway to her mouth. Ruth did not move at all.

"I bought it for Edward," I said. My son's name still comes out of me level and flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "He was to take it after — after the quarrel, after Australia, after everything cooled, which I assumed it would, because everything always cools if you simply refuse to warm it again. He died before the cooling finished. The berth has sat in my ledger ever since, empty, and I have told myself for four years that I was keeping it vacant out of grief. That was another lie written in a fair hand." I looked at each of them in turn. "I have kept it vacant because filling it means choosing, and I swore at his graveside I would never choose between children again."

"You don't have children to choose between," Ruth said quietly. "You have one daughter and a servant's son."

And there it was. Nineteen years of standing in corners, and she spent it all in one sentence.

Clemence said, sharply, "Ruth—"

"No." Ruth set down her napkin with terrible care. "Begging your pardon, ma'am, but no. You called us to the table. You'll hear it at the table." Her hands were flat on the cloth and I noticed they were shaking, and that she was pressing them down to stop it, and that the pressing was worse than the shaking. "My boy is fourteen miles of bad road from dying, and every hour we sit here discussing inheritance he gets further along it. Fever doesn't wait on family feeling. You want to mend something? Mend that."

"This is not a discussion of inheritance," I said.

"Then what is it, ma'am?"

"It is a decision." And I gave it to them plainly, because I had rehearsed it in the dark and knew that ornament would kill it. "The berth goes to Clemence."

* * *

I had expected the room to go quiet. It did not. It went loud in the particular way rooms go loud when nobody raises their voice.

Clemence's cup rattled against its saucer — surprise, genuine surprise, and some third thing underneath it I could not price. Vane stood very still by the sideboard with the coffee pot in his hand, and I saw his eyes move to Ruth and away again, quickly, the way men look away from accidents.

"You're sending me away," Clemence said.

"I am putting you where the world cannot lose you," I said. "You are the last of the blood. Whatever you think of me — whatever I deserve you to think of me — the Marchmont line does not end in a flooded valley because its mother was too proud to act."

"The Marchmont line," Ruth repeated. She laughed once, a short ugly sound I had never heard from her. "Four years I've cooked in this house and buried a husband in its churchyard, and raised my son under your roof, and it comes down to blood. Not who's sick. Not who's young. Blood." She pushed back from the table. "Edward wouldn't have gone, you know. He'd have given it to Toby himself and laughed at you for asking."

"You will not speak of my son."

"Somebody has to. You keep him locked in a ledger."

I felt it land, because it was true, and true things land hardest from the people you have underpaid. I kept my voice level, which cost more than shouting would have. "Mrs. Callow, you forget yourself."

"No, ma'am." She was crying now, steadily, without noise, the way women cry who have done it in kitchens their whole lives. "For the first time in nineteen years I know exactly who I am and where I stand. I'm the one whose son you've just weighed against a name, and found light."

She left the room. None of us moved until we heard the kitchen door, and then the baize door, and then nothing.

* * *

"Well," said Clemence.

"Do not say well."

"What would you like me to say, Mother? Thank you? I've spent four years telling myself you'd sooner die than admit I existed, and now you're shipping me south to preserve the species." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Do you know what I thought, coming down those stairs? I thought: she's finally going to give it to the boy. I thought that's why the summons, why the full table, why the speech. I had already — " she stopped. Recalculated. I watched my daughter decide not to finish a sentence, and understood that she had learned that from me.

"You had already what?"

"Nothing."

"Clemence."

"I had already decided to refuse it," she said, too fast, and I knew it for a lie the moment it left her, and worse, I suspected she knew that I knew, and that we had arrived at the place our family has lived for thirty years: two women telling the truth with false bottoms in it.

Vane cleared his throat. It was the first sound he had made all morning, and both of us turned on him like dogs.

"The car," he said. "If the berth's decided, I should check the ford before the water rises again. Nine hours is nine hours only if the road holds."

"Go," I said.

He went, but not before I caught his face for half a second — and I have read ten thousand letters, forged none, intercepted most, and I know a man composing a sentence he does not intend to send. Something was wrong in that face. Something had been wrong in it for days, and this morning it had sharpened, and I filed it where I file everything, and hated myself a little for filing it.

When he was gone, Clemence and I sat in the wreckage of breakfast.

"Why?" she asked finally. Not bitterly. Genuinely. "After everything. Why me?"

And here is the thing I have not confessed to anyone, and will confess now only to this page: I did not choose her because she was my daughter. I chose her because choosing Tobias would have meant admitting the ledger was wrong — that lives are not entries, that Edward's death cannot be balanced against anything, that four years of careful accounting had produced nothing but a ticket no one should have to inherit. Choosing Clemence let the ledger stand. It was authority wearing apology's coat, and I put it on in the dark and called it duty.

What I said aloud was: "Because you are my daughter, and I have failed you publicly enough. Let me fail you usefully for once."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the rain had begun again, softly, like something trying doors.

"You haven't asked what I want," she said.

"It would change nothing."

"That," said my daughter, "is the truest thing you've said all morning." She stood, gathered her gloves, paused at the door. "Toby took a turn in the night, Mother. Ruth won't tell you because she's stopped asking nicely — I heard her talking to Vane through the pantry wall. Whatever you think you've decided, decide again. Quickly."

And she was gone, and I sat alone at my own table with the coffee going cold, listening to the river, and understood that the water in the jar had clouded again, and that this time the sediment at the bottom might be something no fair hand could write clean.

From the hall came the sound of the front door opening — Vane, back already from the ford? No. Too soon. Then footsteps, quick ones, and Ruth's voice rising through the floorboards, cracked and urgent, saying a word I could not catch, and then the word again, louder:

"—gone. He's gone, ma'am. Toby's climbed out the scullery window and taken the punt toward the bridge—"
