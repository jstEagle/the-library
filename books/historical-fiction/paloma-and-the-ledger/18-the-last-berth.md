## 18. The Last Berth

The rain had been falling for two days without conviction — not the great storm yet, only its herald, a grey drizzle that made the dock road shine like pewter and turned the valley into something drowned and waiting. The steamer's departure had moved up. Everyone knew it now. The telegram had come that morning, and Edith Marchmont had read it aloud in the hall with the flat voice she used for weather and death: *Sailing advanced to tomorrow's tide. Berth must be claimed by nightfall.*

By nightfall.

So we went down the dock road together, all of us, because there was nowhere else to go. Ruth walked ahead with Tobias wrapped against her shoulder, the boy too weak now to walk the half-mile himself, his head lolling against her collarbone like a cut flower. Clemence followed with her collar up, carrying nothing, which was itself a kind of statement. And behind them all came Mrs. Marchmont and myself, side by side on the wet gravel, while the river beside us ran brown and swollen past the ruins of the mill, carrying fence posts and a drowned hen and one white chair leg toward the sea.

I had the ledger under my coat.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the thing I could not get over — three days ago, when I had opened it on my bed with the lamp turned low and my heart going like a boy's, I had expected it to be heavy. A book that assigned human lives ought to weigh something. Instead it was thin calfskin, cracked at the spine, smelling of tobacco and camphor, and the forgery itself had taken me less than an hour. My hand had barely shaken. Years of copying signatures off envelopes will do that; years of being nobody teach you how to write anybody.

*Berth reserved, S.S. Cormorant, south passage, one place.* I had added beneath it, in Edith's own cramped italic — I had practiced it forty times on the backs of her old bills: *Transferable at holder's discretion. Assigned: T. Callow, minor, accompanied by guardian R. Callow.*

Administrative, I had told myself. Not moral. A clerical amendment. The words had held me up for three nights like a rope bridge holds a man who refuses to look down.

Now the road bent, and through the rain I could see the dock — a wooden finger thrust into the flooded estuary, the *Cormorant* riding at anchor beyond it with her lights blurred yellow in the murk, her funnel smoking already. A launch waited to carry passengers out. Two sailors stood under an oilskin tarp, checking a list. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanged, slow and idiotic, keeping time with nothing.

Ruth stopped at the edge of the planking and turned around.

She looked at me first. Not at Edith, not at the ship — at me, as if I were the one who owned the berth, and perhaps in the arithmetic of desperate mothers I was. "Mr. Vane," she said. Her voice was scraped raw from three nights of sitting up. "Tell me you've thought of something."

"Ruth." Edith's voice came level across the rain. "There is one berth. There has always been one berth. It was never meant—"

"It was meant for your son," Ruth said. "And your son is dead, ma'am, God forgive me for saying it plain, and mine is dying on my shoulder, and I am done asking nicely."

Tobias stirred against her. His eyes opened halfway, found the water, the ship, and closed again. Eleven years old. He had carried messages for me all last summer, run down to the village for petrol and cigarettes, called me *Mister Mirabel* in that piping way children have when they decide a servant is really a gentleman. He had asked me once whether the motorcar could outrun a train. I had said yes, because it pleased him, and because lying kindly is the one vice I'd always permitted myself.

"I've thought of something," I said.

Every face turned to me. Rain ticked on the oilskins of the sailors twenty feet away; the launch engine idled with a small patient cough. I reached inside my coat and brought out the ledger, and the rain spotted the calfskin immediately, dark coins of water blooming across the cover, and I held it out flat on my palms the way a server presents a dish.

"Mrs. Marchmont keeps accounts," I said. "Debts owed. Favors banked. Every kindness in this house entered in ink, priced and filed. I know, because I've read it. I've read everything in this house for six years — every letter, every bill, every telegram. You all knew that, or half-knew it, and let it be, because an invisible man is useful." I looked at Edith then, straight on, which I had never once done in six years of driving her. "You wrote down what people owed you, ma'am. But you never once wrote down what you owed them. So I've taken the liberty."

Edith did not reach for the ledger. She stood very still, water running off the brim of her hat, and her eyes — those pale, unappeasable eyes that had stared down magistrates and bishops and half the House of Commons — fixed on the open page as though it might bite her.

"Read it," I said.

She read it. I watched her lips move slightly over the forged line, watched her recognize her own handwriting in it — recognized it, I was certain, instantly, because no one knows their own hand better than a woman who has spent a lifetime making other people sign things. Her jaw tightened. The rain filled the silence.

"This is not my writing," she said quietly.

"No," I said. "But it should have been. That berth was bought for a dead man. A dead man cannot sail, ma'am. Someone had to amend the entry."

"You forged my hand."

"I did. Badly, probably. You'll want to check the descenders." Something in me had gone strangely calm, the way I imagined a man feels stepping off a ledge once he's committed — no more rope bridge, just air, and the surprising discovery that he can breathe in it. "Here's the truth of your ledger, Mrs. Marchmont, since you've never let anyone total the column. Ruth Callow has kept this house alive for nineteen years on wages you haven't raised since the Coronation, and she banked every promise of better and believed none of them. Your daughter crossed the ocean to stand in your rain, and you've spent three weeks pricing her motives instead of reading her letters — yes, I read hers too. And your son—" I heard my own voice catch, and let it. "Your son died in a country you disapproved of, doing work you called foolish, and you bought him a berth home that arrived four months late, and you have kept it empty ever since because filling it means admitting he isn't coming."

"Mirabel." Edith's voice had dropped to almost nothing. It was worse than shouting.

"You taught me this," I said. "You taught me that history is kept by whoever writes it down. Well. I'm writing it down now. The berth goes to Tobias Callow, with his mother as guardian. It's in the book. It's in ink. And in about ten minutes, if the launch leaves, it'll be further from your reach than any correction you can make."

The bell buoy clanged. Somewhere up the valley, faint under the rain, thunder rolled — the real storm arriving fashionably late, as storms do.

"You'll hang for forgery," Clemence said. She said it lightly, almost conversationally, but her eyes weren't light at all; they were moving between me and the ledger like a woman counting cash. "They do still transport people, don't they? Or is it prison now. Mother, are prisons improved?"

"Clemence," Edith said.

"He's right about one thing," Clemence went on, and her voice cracked suddenly down the middle, all the London polish splitting to show something younger underneath. "That berth was Harry's. And Harry would rather die twice than watch a sick child be left on this side of the water so that his sister could take a cruise. You know it. You've known it for weeks, and that's why you couldn't choose — because choosing anything except Toby would have made you the thing Harry died arguing against." She laughed, one short ugly note. "So the chauffeur chose for you. Isn't that always the way? The servants see everything."

"The servants hear everything," I corrected. "Seeing requires being looked at."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — nobody moved. The rain came down. Tobias shivered against Ruth's neck. The sailors by the tarp consulted a pocket watch and each other, and one of them shouted something lost to wind: fifteen minutes, maybe less.

Then Edith Marchmont did the last thing I expected. She took off her glove — slowly, joint by joint, the way she did everything — and held out her bare hand to Ruth Callow.

"Guardian," she said. "Get the boy aboard before I change my mind, because I assure you I shall want to, and I would rather do the decent thing quickly, while it's still cheaper than the alternative."

Ruth didn't take the hand. She was past ceremony; she simply looked at Edith for a long second, and whatever passed between them passed wordlessly, some debt acknowledged at last in a currency neither of them named. Then she turned and went up the gangplank with Tobias pressed to her, and the sailors took her name on the list — *Callow, guardian and child* — and the pen scratched, and it was done. It was done. Three weeks of dread settled in sixty seconds of ink, and I felt my knees go strange, and I put a hand on the dock rail and pretended it was the rain.

"Mister Mirabel." Tobias's voice, very small, from over Ruth's shoulder. He had lifted his head. His cheeks were bright with fever but his eyes were open, and he was looking at me. "Are you coming?"

"Not this time, old chap," I called back. "Someone's got to mind the car."

He considered this with the gravity of the seriously ill, nodded, and laid his head back down. The launch crew began casting off lines. Ruth did not look back, which I understood completely — looking back is for people who can afford to.

* * *

The launch pulled away into the murk, smaller and smaller, until the ship's lights swallowed it, and the four of us who remained stood on the dock road like mourners after a burial — which, in a sense, we were. We had buried my exit. I felt oddly light about it. Grief, I discovered, comes later; first comes the vertigo of having finally done something.

"You understand what you've cost yourself," Edith said. She had not moved. Water sheeted off her hat brim; she seemed built for weather, granite dressed in tweed. "The roads close within the week. The fever's coming up the valley. There may be no way out of Ashcombe until spring. You may die here, Mr. Vane, in a house where you were never more than furniture."

"Yes," I said. "Though I'd point out that furniture doesn't usually choose its room."

"Why?" And there it was — the question I'd been waiting for, asked without softness, because Edith Marchmont had never in her life softened a question she could sharpen. "Six years invisible. Six years reading our mail and saying nothing. Why spend all that neutrality on a housekeeper's boy?"

I thought about lying. It would have been easy; it was practically my native language. I thought about telling her it was duty, or decency, or some principle out of her own pamphlets, and watching her file it away in whatever drawer she kept other people's motives.

"Because I sat up with him Tuesday night," I said instead. "And he asked me to read to him, and the only paper in the house was your correspondence, so I read him a letter his father wrote you in 1904, asking — begging — for work on the estate, and offering to fix the mill gate for free, and you wrote back declining and enclosed half a crown. Half a crown, ma'am. I read that to a dying child and watched him be proud of his father anyway. And I understood that I had spent six years collecting the evidence of everyone's debts and doing precisely nothing with it. Reading isn't neutral. It never was. It was just cowardice with good posture."

Edith studied me for a long time. Rain ran down the deep lines of her face and dripped off her chin, and for once she made no attempt to look invulnerable, and the strangest thing happened: I saw her age. Not weaken — age, the way a cliff ages, honestly, by losing a little more of itself to the water every year.

"Harry used to say something like that," she said finally. "About reading. About who gets to tell it." She picked up her skirts and started up the road, then paused. "You'll want to destroy that ledger page before anyone else sees it. Or you won't. I find I genuinely don't care which, and that, Mr. Vane, is the most expensive lesson I've learned in seventy years."

She walked on. Thunder rolled again, closer, and the first true gusts of the storm came down the valley and bent the reeds along the water, and somewhere far upstream the river took something else with a sound like a door slamming.

Which left Clemence, standing beside me at the rail, soaked through, watching the empty anchorage where the ship had been.

"You forged her handwriting beautifully," she said. "The descenders were fine, by the way. I checked." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone deciding something. "You know what's funny, Mirabel? I offered you money for that berth. A fortune. And you gave it away for free to a boy who calls you Mister Mirabel." She tilted her head. "Unless you're holding out for something better."

"I'm holding out for spring," I said.

"And the original page?" Her voice had gone very quiet, very pleasant. "The one with the berth unassigned. The one that proves the whole thing a forgery, if it ever surfaced. The one still sitting in your coat, because you're sentimental about documents — I've seen your desk drawers." She held out her hand, palm up, rain pooling in it. "Give it to me, and I'll keep your secret forever. We readers of other people's mail have to protect one another. Don't you think?"

The storm arrived in earnest then, the rain turning horizontal, the bell buoy clanging faster as if alarmed, and I put my hand inside my coat and found the folded original page and understood that the next thirty seconds would decide what kind of man the last six years had made me.

I gave it to her.

She looked at it, and at me, and tucked it away — and struck a match.
