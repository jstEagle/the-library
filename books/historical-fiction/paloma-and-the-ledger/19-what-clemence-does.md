## 19. What Clemence Does

Clemence Marchmont had spent thirty-one years learning that the truth was a currency like any other — it could be hoarded, spent, counterfeited. She had learned it in her mother's house before she ever learned it in London, and so when she came around the corner of the linen passage at half past six in the morning and saw Mirabel Vane standing over the open ledger with a pen still wet in his hand, she did not gasp, or drop her candle, or do any of the things a person in a novel would do.

She stood very still and watched him finish the stroke.

He didn't see her at first. The lamp on the desk threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and his face wore an expression she recognized because she had seen it in her own mirror a thousand times: the concentration of a forger, which is to say, of someone who has finally stopped pretending they are only passing through other people's lives. His collar was unfastened. There was ink on the side of his right hand where he'd braced it against the page. He looked, for once, exactly like what he was.

She should have coughed. She should have let her heel click on the boards. Instead she watched him turn the ledger page with two fingers, delicately, the way you handle something alive, and read back what he had written, his lips moving slightly, checking his own work the way a schoolboy checks his sums.

The berth on the *Coromandel*, which had sat in Edith Marchmont's ledger for four years under no name at all, now carried one. Not Clemence's. Not Mirabel's either, she noticed, though God knew he had earned it more honestly than anyone. Tobias Callow. Guardian: Ruth Callow. Transferable by order of E. Marchmont, dated the eleventh of December — three days ago, when Edith had been asleep with laudanum and the house had been nothing but wind.

Clemence had come downstairs for brandy. That was the whole truth of her position at that moment: she had been awake since four with the storm battering the shutters and her mother's voice from breakfast still ringing in her ears — *the berth goes to Clemence* — and she had wanted a drink the way drowning people want air. She had meant to take the bottle back up to her room and drink it alone, which was the family method.

Instead she had found this.

She could end him with one sentence. That was the extraordinary thing about power; it was always smaller than you imagined. One raised voice, one word — *forgery* — shouted into the storm-dark house, and the chauffeur would be finished, and the berth would revert to its rightful holder, which was herself, by her mother's declaration at breakfast, witnessed by Ruth Callow and the doctor's locum and God. She would be on the *Coromandel* by Thursday. She would be in Southampton by Friday. She would be free of Ashcombe Vale, free of the river, free of her mother's ledger and her mother's silences and the particular way her mother said her name, as though reading it off an inventory.

All she had to do was speak.

She stepped forward instead, and her heel clicked on the boards after all, too late, and Mirabel Vane turned around with the pen still in his hand like a man caught holding a knife who has decided not to pretend it's a fork.

"Mrs. Marchmont," he said.

"Don't." She set her candle down on the desk, next to the lamp, and folded her arms. "Don't do the servant thing. You've never done the servant thing once since you arrived and I won't have you start now, it would be like watching a wolf apologize."

"I wasn't aware wolves apologized."

"They don't. They run." She nodded at the ledger. "Show me."

He hesitated. She watched him calculate — she had watched men calculate against her all her life, in drawing rooms and solicitors' offices and once, memorably, across a baccarat table in Monte Carlo, and she knew every version of the pause. This one was different. This one wasn't weighing whether to lie to her. It was weighing whether lying would even work.

He turned the book toward her.

She read it twice. The hand was good — better than good, it was her mother's hand, or near enough that her mother herself might doubt it, the small cramped capitals, the way the E tucked its tail. He had even got the ink right, the brownish fade of the older entries, because he must have tested it, mixed it, aged it somehow. She thought of all those letters he'd carried for her mother and wondered, not for the first time since her arrival, how many he had actually opened.

"You've given my berth away," she said.

"It isn't yours yet."

"It is now. Mother announced it. At breakfast. In front of witnesses, which for her is practically a sacrament." She heard her own voice go flat and hard and recognized her mother in it and hated that. "A dying woman's declaration. You've forged over a dying woman's declaration."

"She hasn't signed it."

"The ledger doesn't need signatures. The ledger *is* the signature. I grew up in this house, Vane, I know what the ledger means better than you do. She writes a thing in it and the thing becomes true. That's her whole religion. Debts become payments, favors become obligations, and a blank line becomes—" She stopped. The blank line. Four years of blank line. "Who told you it was meant for Edward?"

"No one told me. I read it."

Of course he had. She almost laughed. "You and I are the same animal," she said, "do you know that? You read her letters and I read her face, and we've both spent years telling ourselves we were only observing. Bystanders. Historians. And then the water rises and suddenly we're holding pens."

Mirabel set the pen down. Carefully, aligned parallel to the ledger's edge, as though neatness could make the act administrative rather than criminal. "The boy is fourteen," he said. "He weighs nothing. He couldn't lift the wood basket yesterday. Ruth has stopped asking and started planning, and if you expose me, the berth reverts to a healthy thirty-one-year-old woman whose worst danger this winter is boredom, and a child dies in this house within the week. That's the arithmetic. I've done it every way there is to do it. It comes out the same."

"And you've decided it's your arithmetic to do."

"Yes," he said.

It was the first time she had heard him say anything without qualification, without irony, without the little self-erasing shrug he wore like livery. She studied him. Rain came off the shutters in sheets. Somewhere above them Tobias was sleeping, or failing to sleep, in the narrow room under the eaves that Ruth had kept warm with hot bricks and prayer.

"You know she'll find out," Clemence said. "My mother finds out everything. It's not cleverness, it's appetite. She will look at that page within a month and she'll know the ink is wrong, because she knows her own hand the way a singer knows her own throat."

"Then she'll know."

"That's it? Then she'll know?"

"What else is there?" He said it simply, and it landed on her like a blow, because she had built her entire life on the opposite principle — that there was always something else, always a second envelope behind the first, always an exit, a berth, a train south. Her mother had taught her that too, in her way. Two women, one catechism.

Clemence reached out and took the ledger.

*

She hadn't decided anything. That was what she would tell herself later, in the weeks after, when she tried to reconstruct the moment: that she had merely picked it up to read it again in better light, and the light had changed her mind for her. But that was a lie, and she was trying, that winter, to stop telling lies, even the small comfortable ones, even the ones made entirely of silence.

She carried the ledger to the fireplace.

There was a fire in the study grate — Ruth kept fires in every room now, fever weather, the doctor's orders — and it burned low and orange, and the heat hit her face as she knelt in front of it with the book open across her knees. She found the page. Her mother's original entry, four years old: *One berth held. S.S. Coromandel, 14 Dec. Purpose reserved.* No name. Never a name. And beneath it, in the margin, in pencil, so faint she had missed it in every previous reading and understood it completely now:

*E., if you are reading this and still cannot choose, then the choice was never yours to make.*

Her mother's own hand, writing to her future self. Begging her future self, in the smallest possible letters, to put the pen down.

"Oh," Clemence said. Quietly. The fire popped.

Behind her Mirabel said, "What is it?" and she didn't answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a confession, and she was about to make enough confessions for one morning.

Here was the thing she had not told anyone, not in London, not in the letters she wrote and burned, not in the dark of the train down from Paddington: she had come home for the inheritance. Everyone knew it, including her mother, including probably the cook. She had told herself a better story on the way down — reconciliation, filial duty, the storm as providence — but the true ledger of Clemence Marchmont read otherwise. She had come because Edward was dead and the money was unassigned and she had learned in London that love, unpaid, accrues interest like anything else. Thirty-one years of her mother's cold accounting, and she had come to collect.

And her mother, knowing this — knowing it the way she knew everything — had stood up at breakfast with her ruined heart hammering under the grey wool of her dress and given her the berth anyway. Not as a gift. As an apology. As a payment on a debt so old neither of them could name its origin. *The berth goes to Clemence,* Edith had said, in the voice she used for closing arguments, and Clemence had felt the strange double movement she always felt in her mother's presence: triumph, and grief that it should feel like triumph.

If she spoke now — one word, forgery, exposed — she would win. She would sail out of the vale on Thursday with the debt collected and her mother's apology banked, and she would spend the rest of her life knowing that she had taken a dead boy's place from a living one, and that her mother had known, and forgiven, and been right about her after all. That was the price. Not scandal. Certainty. Certainty that she was the person her mother's ledger had always implied she was: an entry, a claimant, a daughter who came home only when the estate was in probate.

Tobias coughed somewhere upstairs. A small sound, worn thin, through two floors and a storm.

Clemence tore the page out.

It came away with a soft ripping sound, and Mirabel made a noise behind her — half protest, half relief — and she fed it to the fire corner-first, the way you burn a letter, because everyone in her family knew how to burn a letter, it was practically the coat of arms. The paper curled. The old browned ink flared green for a moment, copper in it, and then the margin note went last, the pencil words lifting off the burning page like smoke before the smoke itself took them: *the choice was never yours to make.*

Except it had been hers, Clemence thought, watching it blacken. It had been hers all along, and she was making it now, kneeling on a hearth rug in her dead brother's childhood house, choosing a sick stranger's son over her own escape, and finding — this was the astonishing part, the part she would keep turning over for years like a stone with something bright underneath — finding that it didn't feel like loss. It felt like putting down a weight she had mistaken for her spine.

"There," she said. Her voice cracked on it. She cleared her throat. "Now there's only your version. Your forgery is the only truth left in the book. Congratulations, Vane. You've achieved what historians only dream of."

"That's not funny."

"It's extremely funny. It's the funniest thing that has ever happened in this family, which is saying something, because this family once staged a séance to settle an argument about a horse." She stood up. Her knees ached from the rug. She brushed off her dressing gown with more force than the task required, because her hands needed occupation or they were going to shake, and she refused, absolutely refused, to shake in front of the help. "The question is what happens now."

"Now," Mirabel said slowly, "we get them to the dock road before the water takes it. Tonight. The steamer moved up — the whistle boat came through yesterday, departure's tomorrow dawn, and the ford will be gone by midnight if the rain holds. Which means Ruth has to pack a household's worth of hope into one trunk, and you—"

"Have to tell my mother I'm staying."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him arrive at the same understanding she had just burned into ash: that the forged page was only half the crime. That the other half was hers to commit, and it was worse, and it couldn't be done with ink. Renouncing a berth was paperwork. Renouncing the story of yourself you'd carried for thirty-one years — that was blood work.

"She'll think it's a trick," Clemence said. "She'll think I'm declining in order to be asked twice. She'll think there's a second envelope. She thinks there's always a second envelope, because I taught her that. Every bluff I've ever run on her, and now the one time I'm not bluffing—"

"Then don't tell her," Mirabel said. "Not yet. Let her believe the forgery gave Tobias the berth and cost you nothing. Let her carry that. She's carried worse for you."

It was, she would think later, the kindest strategic advice anyone had ever given her, and it was also the first truly generous thing anyone had done for her in a decade: he offered her a lie to hide inside, the way you offer a coat in the rain, and asked nothing for it.

Above them, a door banged, and feet on the stairs, fast, uneven — Ruth's tread, but wrong, stumbling, and then Ruth's voice cracking through the house like the ice on the millpond:

*"Mr. Vane! Mr. Vane, come quickly — it's Tobias—"*

Clemence set down the ledger. Mirabel was already moving, taking the stairs two at a time, the chauffeur vanishing and the man remaining, and she followed, candle guttering, up into the storm-dark house where the real arithmetic waited — and she understood, climbing, that whatever happened at the dock road tonight, she had already chosen her side of the water.
