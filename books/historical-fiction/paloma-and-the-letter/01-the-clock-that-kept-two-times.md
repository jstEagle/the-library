## 1. The Clock That Kept Two Times

The observatory sat on its hill like a man who had stopped expecting visitors.

Emrys Fairweather climbed the switchback road from the ferry landing with his tool chest cutting into his shoulder, pausing twice to look back at the town of Marrow Bend spread along the river below—tannery smoke, the mill race, the slate roofs going blue in the November light. Twelve years an apprentice clockmaker and he had never once been up this road. Nobody went up it. The keeper was a woman named Paloma who had come from somewhere else thirty years ago, and the townspeople spoke of her the way they spoke of the weather: often, vaguely, and with a settled conviction that nothing about her concerned them.

But the letter that had summoned him was precise. *The master clock loses its mind at midnight,* she had written. *Come before the ice closes the bridge.*

He had read that sentence four times on the crossing and still didn't know what it meant. Clocks didn't lose their minds. Clocks lost seconds, gained minutes, drifted with heat and cold and neglect—all of which could be found, measured, corrected. A clock either had a reason or it had a fault, and both were fixable. What a clock did not have, what no apprentice in the workshop of Master Hollis had ever been taught to look for, was intent.

The door was unlocked. He let himself into a stone vestibule that smelled of lamp oil and dried herbs, and there, seated in a wheelchair beside the great case of the master clock, was Paloma.

She was thinner than any living person he'd ever seen. Her hands lay folded in her lap like something already packed away for moving. But her eyes were black and unhurried, and they took him in from boots to collar with the thoroughness of an appraiser.

"You're young," she said.

"I'm competent," Emrys said, then wished he hadn't. It sounded like a boy's answer. "Ma'am. Emrys Fairweather, of Hollis and Sons."

"There are no sons."

"No, ma'am. There's just me."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. She gestured at the clock case with her chin. "It's all yours. I'll be watching."

And she did. That was the first strange thing, and Emrys would remember it later as the moment he should have understood everything: she didn't leave. Keepers didn't hover over repairmen. She positioned her chair three paces off and watched him work with the fixed attention of a woman supervising surgery on someone she loved.

***

He opened the case and stood inside the sound of the clock.

It was a regulator movement, weight-driven, the finest work he'd ever been allowed to touch—a deadbeat escapement with a mercury compensation pendulum, made in Geneva by hands long dead, kept so clean that the brass threw light back at him like water. Somebody had maintained this machine with devotion. He checked the pallets, oiled the pivots, verified the beat against his watch. Everything was true.

Except the time.

The clock ran two minutes fast. Not drifting toward fast—*at* fast. Two minutes and zero seconds ahead of his chronometer, steady as a debt, and it held that margin through the afternoon while he cleaned and measured and found nothing. He tested the pendulum length against the temperature tables. Correct. He checked for a second weight, a hidden auxiliary train, anything that might add drive to the escape wheel. Nothing. The movement was honest. The movement was perfect. And the movement insisted, with all the authority of fine Swiss gearwork, that two o'clock came at 1:58.

"Have you had other men look at it?" he asked, not turning around.

"Two," Paloma said behind him. "One from Ashford, ten years ago. One who drank."

"And?"

"The drunk one told the truth. The Ashford man lied." She said it without heat, the way you'd report the price of flour. "He adjusted my pendulum and charged me double and swore it was cured. It wasn't cured. It was never sick."

Emrys turned around. "Then what's wrong with it?"

"Nothing is wrong with it." Her voice was quiet and level, and it did something to the air of the room. "That's what I'm paying you to understand."

"Clocks don't run fast on purpose."

"Don't they?" For the first time she looked away from him, up at the dial, where the gilt numerals caught the last of the daylight. "Every clock in this valley keeps the time this town agreed to keep. Someone decided when noon was, when midnight was, when the mill whistle blows and the courts sit and the graves were dug. Everyone winds those clocks and believes them. This is the only one in forty miles that tells a different time, and you're the first person in thirty years to ask me why instead of reaching for the pendulum."

His hands had gone still inside the case. "Why, then?"

"Not yet." She wheeled herself closer, close enough that he could see the mapwork of veins on her temples, the fever sitting bright behind her eyes. "You'll find it yourself or you won't. That's part of the test, Mr. Fairweather. If you can't find what isn't meant to be found, you can't carry what is."

"Carry it where?"

She closed her mouth and folded her hands again, and gave him nothing else until dark.

***

He worked by lamplight through the evening, and the longer he worked the less sense it made and the more sense it made, if that could be believed. Because here was the thing that kept snagging him, the splinter in his thumb: the error was too clean. Accidental errors wander. Temperature, wear, dirty oil—they make a clock gain unevenly, sloppily, a few seconds here and a few there. This clock gained exactly nothing beyond its two minutes. It was as though the mechanism had been tuned to a different standard altogether—as though somewhere in the mathematics of the thing, someone had chosen a new zero.

A clockmaker could do that. It would take a clockmaker. You couldn't shift a clock two minutes and hold it there by accident; you'd have to do it deliberately, invisibly, in a way that no inspection would catch—and Emrys sat back on his stool at half past nine, wiping his fingers, and admitted to himself that he had no idea how it had been done. Which meant whoever had done it was better than him. Or had had thirty years to think about it.

Behind him, Paloma coughed. It was a deep, wet sound, and it went on too long, and when it finished she stayed bent forward over her lap breathing carefully, like a woman carrying water across ice.

"You should be abed," he said.

"I've spent most of the year abed. It's dull." She straightened. "Your master taught you to listen to clocks?"

"He taught me every clock has a voice. Beat, strike, tick—you learn them like people."

"Mm." She studied him across the lamplight. "Then listen to this one at midnight. Not before. Don't wind it, don't touch it, don't correct it. Just listen."

"To what? It strikes twelve like any—"

"At midnight," she said, "listen to *when* it strikes."

He wanted to argue. He was tired, his back ached, and the bridge would be closing within days—the ferryman had said the first ice was coming down from the shallows, and after that there would be no crossing until thaw. He had a job, a diagnosis he couldn't finish, and a dying old woman speaking in riddles about a clock that was perfectly, provably wrong.

But he was also, underneath the fatigue, awake in a way he hadn't been since apprenticeship. Twelve years he had spent learning machines that obeyed. Here was a machine that defied—or rather, a machine that obeyed someone so thoroughly, so secretly, that its defiance looked like madness.

He ate the supper the housekeeper left—bread, cheese, pickled onions, all of it excellent—and took up his post beside the open case at eleven, with his chronometer in front of him and Paloma silent in her chair, watching.

The clock ticked. The room ticked back. Outside, the wind came off the river with a smell of iron in it, the smell of coming frost, and somewhere down in the town the church bell rang eleven with the comfortable certainty of a lie.

At five minutes to twelve, Paloma spoke one last time.

"When you hear it," she said, "you'll want to fix it. Every clockman does. It will feel like a wound in the world. Resist that. Ask instead what the wound remembers."

Emrys didn't answer. His eyes were on his chronometer, on the minute hand walking toward the numeral that everyone in Marrow Bend would call midnight, the hour the church bells had already confirmed, the hour the almanac printed, the hour the town had agreed upon—

The clock struck.

Twelve strokes, deep and even, filling the tower like water filling a well. And Emrys Fairweather, holding his own watch up to the lamplight, saw that the hands of his chronometer read 11:58.

The master clock had struck midnight two minutes early. Exactly two minutes early. As it did, he realized, every single night—faithful to a time that no other clock in the valley kept.

Paloma was watching his face. Whatever she found there seemed to satisfy her.

"Now you know it's deliberate," she said softly. "Tomorrow, I'll show you where I hid the proof of what happened at that hour—and then, Mr. Fairweather, we'll see whether your nerve is worth as much as your skill."
