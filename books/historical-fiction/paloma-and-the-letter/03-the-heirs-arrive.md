## 3. The Heirs Arrive

The ice came in the night, and it came all at once.

Emrys woke in the narrow room above the observatory's workshop to a silence he didn't recognize, and it took him a full minute to understand what was missing: the river. For six days it had murmured under his window like a conversation held just out of earshot. Now nothing. He dressed in the dark, went down the iron stair with his boots in one hand so as not to wake anyone, and let himself out into air that bit through wool.

The Verrow had frozen from bank to bank.

Not the polite lace of frost along the shallows that he'd known every childhood winter, but a solid sheet of gray-green plate stretching across the whole width of the channel, ridged where the current had fought it and lost. Downstream, at the ferry landing, old Hessel's boat sat locked in place, tilted on its keel like a beetle pinned to a board. A lantern burned in the ferryman's window—the daughter's silhouette moving behind the glass, awake early, already staring at the water she could no longer work.

Emrys stood on the observatory steps and felt the geography of his situation settle over him like snow.

Two weeks ago he'd crossed the bridge from Millhaven with his tools on his back, expecting ten days' work on a master clock and a warm bed at his master's shop by month's end. The bridge was a quarter mile downstream, an iron span raised by the founding families themselves—paid for, Paloma had said, with money that smelled of smoke. It was the only crossing for thirty miles in either direction. And now the river had closed around its pilings like a fist.

No clockmaker came to this town after the ice closed. No clockmaker left.

He should have felt trapped. Instead, standing in the blue dark with his breath smoking, Emrys found himself thinking of the letter. It was upstairs again—returned to the case two nights past once the striking train was clean, wedged behind the great wheel exactly as he'd found it, wrapped now in oilcloth against damp. Paloma hadn't asked him about it since. She watched him wind the clock each evening with those hooded eyes and said only, *You know where it sleeps,* which was not a question and required no answer.

He went back inside before his fingers failed him and put the kettle on.

* * *

Paloma was worse.

He saw it the moment he carried her tea up to the observation room, where she insisted on spending her mornings in the chair by the long telescope even though she no longer climbed the tower stairs without resting twice on the way. Her color had gone the yellow of old paper. Her hands, folded in her lap, trembled in a rhythm too slow to be cold.

"You heard the river close," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone in Verrin's Landing would have heard it—that deep groan just before dawn when the last of the open water gave up.

"I did."

"Then you understand." She turned her face toward the window, toward the frozen channel. "Nothing comes in now. Nothing goes out. Until thaw."

"Except on foot," Emrys said. "The river will bear a man by tomorrow, maybe tonight."

Her eyes came back to him, and there was something in them he hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but a kind of grim accounting, a merchant totaling a bill she'd known was coming. "Yes," she said. "Except on foot. I've been waiting for them forty years, Mr. Fairweather. It seems they'll be walking."

"Who?"

She lifted her teacup. Her hand shook; she steadied it against her knee with the other hand, an act of will so ordinary and so effortful that Emrys looked away to give her privacy. "Drink your own tea," she said. "And keep your tools close today. All of them."

* * *

They came at dusk.

Emrys was at the bench in the workshop, fitting a new pallet stone under lamplight, when he heard voices at the front door—not the knock of a neighbor but the measured ring of someone who expects doors to be opened for them. He heard Marta, the keeper's housekeeper, admit three people out of the cold, heard the stamping of boots and the low murmur of courtesy, and then the voices were in the hall and coming toward the light of the workshop doorway, because of course they were. Men like these did not wait to be received.

There were three of them.

The eldest was perhaps sixty, tall and dry as a November stalk, with a lawyer's stillness and a coat worth more than everything Emrys owned together. He stood just inside the door as though the room were a document he was reading. Beside him, a woman in her forties with her brother's same long face softened by fashion—fur at the collar, a brooch at the throat that caught the lamplight and threw it back in small green fires. The third was younger, thirty or less, broad-shouldered and restless, already drifting along the tool wall with his gloved hand behind his back, looking at the lathes and the files with an appraiser's eye rather than a guest's.

"Mistress Paloma Verrin," the eldest said, to the room in general, "is an institution of this valley. When word reached us of her illness, we came at once. Family owes family that much, whatever the years between."

Emrys set down his tweezers very carefully, because his hands wanted to shake.

He knew the names. He had read them four nights ago by candlelight, in ink gone brown with age, at the bottom of a page that began *I, Paloma, being of sound mind if not sound body, set down what was done at Harrow's Mill.* The signatures below the account were not hers. They belonged to three men who had sworn before God and the district magistrate that no violence occurred at the mill that night, that the fire was an accident of carelessness, that the eleven dead—including a girl of nine whose name appeared nowhere in any record but Paloma's letter—had died in their beds of fever. Those men were dead now. Their grandchildren were standing in the workshop doorway, bringing the cold in with them.

"Aldous Crane," said the eldest, extending a hand that Emrys had no choice but to take. It was dry and cool and perfectly controlled. "My sister, Odile Vane. Our cousin, Tobias Merle. We are the descendants of the founders, young man—you'll forgive us for arriving unannounced, but the season made ceremony impossible."

"The season closes the bridge," Emrys said. "It doesn't stop a man walking the ice."

Something moved behind Aldous Crane's eyes—a flicker, quickly smoothed, like a ripple passing over standing water. "Resourceful," he said. "You are?"

"Fairweather. The clockmaker's apprentice. I'm repairing the master clock."

"Ah." Aldous smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, warm at the edges. "Then the town owes you too. That clock has kept Verrin's Landing honest for eighty years." He paused, just long enough. "Though I hear it's been keeping two times lately."

Emrys felt the words go through him like a probe finding a wound. *They know. Of course they know—a town talks, a bell is heard by everyone.* "A fault in the strike train," he said. "I'm correcting it."

"Don't hurry on our account," said the cousin, Tobias, from the tool wall. He had a pleasant voice, loose and friendly, and his eyes never stopped moving. "Some faults are interesting. Some faults tell you things."

"Tobias collects curiosities," Odile Vane said, with a small laugh meant to make it charming. "Clocks, maps, old papers. He's a terrible magpie. Pay him no mind."

*Old papers.*

Marta appeared at the door with the announcement that Mistress Paloma would receive them in the morning, when she was stronger, and something in the housekeeper's flat voice suggested that this message came verbatim and that its sender had enjoyed composing it. Aldous received the rebuff as gracefully as a man accepting a seat at table. "Of course. Tomorrow. We would never press upon a lady's rest." He drew off his gloves finger by finger. "We'll stay at the Anchor Inn, then. If it's standing room." His gaze traveled the workshop once more, unhurried—the bench, the lamp, the tall shape of the master clock rising into the dimness above the stairs—and returned to Emrys. "You'll be here all winter, I expect. The river sees to it. Perhaps we'll become acquainted, Mr. Fairweather. A town this size offers few distractions."

They left the way they'd come, taking their cold with them. Through the window Emrys watched three figures cross the frozen road toward the lights of the inn, their shadows long and orderly on the snow, like punctuation marks written across a blank page.

* * *

In the tower room afterward, Paloma listened without interrupting while he told it—the arrival, the names, the smile, the cousin's wandering eyes, the sister's laugh, the sentence about two times delivered like a blade wrapped in velvet.

When he finished, she was quiet a long moment. The lamp guttered; he trimmed the wick.

"You read the letter," she said at last. Not accusing. Simply establishing, the way a surveyor drives a stake.

"Yes."

"And you know who they are."

"I know who their grandfathers were."

"Grandchildren are not grandfathers," she said. Then, before hope could form in him: "But they did not come here to honor me, child. No one crosses ice at dusk to honor a dying woman they have not visited in forty years. They came because word travels faster than rivers freeze. Someone wrote to them that I was dying, and that my papers were unaccounted for." Her breath caught; she rode it out. "Tomorrow they will sit by my bed and speak softly, and they will ask—oh, so gently—to help me put my affairs in order. And every hour they are under this roof, they will be searching."

"Then we move it," Emrys said. "Tonight. I know places in the mechanism no stranger would think to look—"

"No." Her voice had an edge he hadn't heard before. "You misunderstand me entirely. Let them search the clock. Let them tear the case apart board by board—they will find oilcloth and dust, and they will learn nothing except that I am cleverer than they hoped." She gripped the arm of her chair and leaned toward him, and up close her eyes were astonishingly clear, fever burning away everything but the intent beneath it. "The letter must be *findable*, Mr. Fairweather. That is the whole design. Not hidden in some crack that dies with me—but in plain sight, in the heart of the thing every soul in this valley sets their day by. If they destroy it, it must cost them something. If they leave it, it must haunt them." She sank back, spent by the effort, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and more. "They have come to bury the truth one more time. Very well. But this time it will fight back."

Emrys stood in the lamplight, his apprentice's caution warring with something larger that he couldn't yet name, and understood that whatever he had come to this town to repair, it was not the clock.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the master clock gathered itself and struck the half hour—one note too soon, two minutes ahead of the true time, patient as ever.

Paloma closed her eyes and smiled.

"There they are," she whispered. "Right on schedule."
