## 4. Paloma's Account

The fever came at dusk, the way it always did now, rising through me like water climbing a stair.

I had spent the day pretending to be a woman who would see spring. I sat up in my chair by the window, wrapped in two shawls, and supervised nothing, and let Emrys Fairweather believe that his presence in my observatory was a matter of gears and oil. He was careful with the master clock the way young men are careful with anything they do not yet understand — hands slow, eyes everywhere, questions swallowed before they could become words. I had watched apprentices for forty years. I knew what an unasked question looked like. His had been sitting behind his teeth since the moment he pulled the letter from the striking train and set it on the bench as though it were something hot.

I had not planned for him to find it so soon. But then, I had not planned much of anything anymore except the clock, and the clock was finished. The rest was only waiting to be believed.

The fever made the room swim, but fevers also make honest women of us all, so when the light went blue over the river I rang for him — twice, the signal we'd settled on — and when he came up the stairs with his sleeves still rolled and grease at his knuckles, I said:

"Sit down, boy. It is time you heard why your two minutes are not broken."

* * *

My father kept the mill accounts. That is where every story of this town must begin, because every story of this town begins with paper.

You have seen the monument, of course. Every child in the valley has seen it — the granite shaft by the bridge, the carved sheaf of wheat, the inscription: *In memory of the founders' courage, and the peace their wisdom secured.* You learned it in school along with your letters. The Founders' Peace. A quarrel resolved, a season of trouble ended, a town made whole by the wisdom of five families whose names are still on the bank, the mill office, the church pew ends.

There was no peace. There was a night in October, thirty-one years ago, when the mill race ran red enough that the ice formed brown the next morning, and my father stood in this very room with ink on his hands that he tried for the rest of his life to scrub away.

He was the keeper here before me. My mother died young; it was always the two of us and the stars. He taught me the clock, the transit instrument, the patience of watching. And because he was an honest bookkeeper before he was ever a keeper of time, he also taught me the ledgers he kept hidden inside the false back of his desk — the real accounts of the mill, the ones that showed what the founding families were doing to the workers they called their neighbors.

Wages cut three times in two years. Rent charged against pay for company houses that leaked. Fines levied for lateness, for singing, for a man's daughter being seen reading. The families called it prudence. The workers called it the mill yoke. In the autumn of that year the workers did what workers do — they stopped. They gathered at the mill with lanterns and a petition, four hundred souls, asking for what any Christian would call bread.

And the five families answered with men.

They told the town afterward it was a mob, that the workers burned first, that the militia — bought and paid, though no record says whose money — fired only to protect property. Fourteen dead, the official history allows. My father's ledgers and my own ears say otherwise. There was no fire until after the shooting. The workers had no weapons but hammers and the petition itself. They fired volleys into a crowd holding candles, and when some of the men ran for the sluice gates to drown the machinery and stop the work that was killing them, the families' men followed them down and finished it there, in the dark and the water, where no one could count.

Twenty-six. That is the number my father wrote, name by name, in the ledger I still keep under the floor beneath the transit pier. Twenty-six, including two boys who had gone along carrying their fathers' lanterns.

The families could buy the militia. They could buy the coroner, who recorded eleven deaths and called the rest "unaccounted drift." What they could not buy was my father's hand — or rather, they could, and did, and he never forgave himself for how cheaply it came.

They came to him a week later. Not with threats, he told me. With kindness, which is worse. Aldous Vane's grandfather sat in our kitchen and spoke gently about my father's debts, about my schooling, about how a widow's son — I was not quite orphaned then, but they already counted my mother's frailty as good as a grave — needed a town at peace to grow up in. Then they set a document in front of him. An account of the night, written in his name, attesting to the mob and the fire and the regrettable necessity. They required only his signature.

He signed. He told me he looked at the pen for a long time and thought of my face, and signed. And then he came up these stairs and he did not sleep for a year, and when he finally slept, he dreamed of the sluice gates, and when he woke from those dreams he worked.

That is what he built instead of a confession he could not publish: the clock.

* * *

Do you know what a striking train is, Emrys? Of course you do — you've had your fingers inside mine for three days. The gearwork that counts the hours and lifts the hammers. Most people think a clock strikes what the dial says. But the strike is its own little machine, with its own count wheel, and a man who understands count wheels can make a clock say one thing with its hands and another thing with its voice.

My father altered the strike. Two minutes. The master clock runs two minutes fast, forever, and no adjustment will cure it, because the error lives in the count wheel — he cut and pinned it himself, and then he filed his marks smooth so no repairman would ever find the trick and correct it. At midnight by the sun's true reckoning — the reckoning this observatory exists to keep, the reckoning I have spent my life defending — the clock reads 12:02. And when it strikes, it does not strike twelve.

It strikes fourteen.

Fourteen blows, two minutes past midnight, every night, into a town that has been told the official death toll was eleven. The extra four strokes fall into the silence after midnight like stones into a well. Most people never hear them. Most people are asleep, and the ones who wake assume the old clock is mad. Old clocks are allowed to be mad. It is the one license grief is granted.

But the clockmakers know. Every apprentice in the valley who has ever serviced this movement has had to ask why the count wheel delivers fourteen, and every master has given the same shrug — eccentric old mechanism, don't touch it. My father counted on their professional superstition. Clockmakers do not fix what is not broken, and a count wheel that strikes true fourteen is not broken. It is *testifying*. Every midnight, without fail, without ink, without a signature anyone can forge or burn, the truth of October stands up in the tower and speaks.

Fourteen strokes. Eleven acknowledged dead, and the four who drowned in the sluice race whom the coroner erased — twenty-six minus the twelve the families grudgingly admitted in private settlements, if you want the arithmetic, and I know you do, because you have the look of a boy doing arithmetic. Do it slowly. Let each number cost you what it cost my father.

When he died, I took over the keeping of it. People assumed I maintained the error out of sentiment — a daughter preserving her father's last quirk. Sentiment is a fine disguise. For thirty-one years I have wound that count wheel the way other women wind their grief into needlework, and I told myself it was enough. The truth was being spoken nightly into the dark. Surely someone would listen. Surely someday the town would count.

No one listened. That is the lesson of my life, Emrys: a truth that requires an audience to decode it is a truth held hostage. So when the historians wrote to me last spring — the district society, compiling a new county history, asking for the observatory's records, asking whether I had anything relevant to the founding era — I understood that the window had opened at last, and that windows close. Old keepers die. Fires happen. Count wheels get "corrected."

So I wrote the letter. My father's ledgers, my father's signature coerced, my father's fourteen strokes, all of it laid out page by page in my own hand, sealed, and hidden where only a clockmaker's hands would ever go — behind the striking train, in the one place in this town the founding families would never dare send clumsy fingers. A letter inside the machine that testifies. If I died tomorrow, the letter would wait for the next honest pair of hands.

I did not expect the hands to arrive before the ice did.

* * *

He was quiet a long while. The fever made everything distant — his face, the lamp, the sound of the river locking itself shut below us. I watched him turn his cup in his hands and understood that I was asking him to carry something heavy across a frozen bridge, and that he was calculating the weight, as any sensible person would.

"You could have sent it downriver in the autumn," he said at last. "Before winter closed the road."

"I could have," I said. "I nearly did, a dozen times." I pulled the shawl tighter, though the fever had me sweating. "But a letter sent is a letter that can be lost, and a letter lost is a confession that dies quietly. I wanted it delivered by a living witness. I wanted someone to stand in front of the historians and say, *I saw the clock. I read the letter. I heard her tell it.* A testimony needs a witness more than it needs a courier." I smiled, and felt how thin my face had grown around the smile. "And then the ice came early, and I got my witness whether I wanted one or not."

"The heirs," he said. "Vane, and the Merrick woman, and the young Corliss. They'll come. Won't they. Once they learn the historians wrote to you."

"They're already coming, boy. Why do you think I asked for you in November instead of spring?" I let that settle on him. "Aldous Vane writes to me twice a year, dutiful as a tax collector, asking after my health. This autumn his letters changed. He began asking after my *records*. When I mentioned the society's inquiry, his reply took nine days instead of three — which means it went to the family first. They know the window is open too. They will come up that hill wearing black gloves and sympathy, and they will search this house down to the cellar stones, and when they find nothing they will wait for the thaw with me, because as long as I breathe, the letter might exist anywhere. They cannot leave. They cannot risk it."

"And when you die?"

"Then they will ransack again, and burn whatever they find, and go home and sleep beautifully." I closed my eyes. The fever had turned the lamplight to gold water. "Unless the truth is already out of this house and out of their reach before I draw my last breath. Unless someone carried it across the ice to the district seat the moment the bridge opens — or before."

"There's no crossing before the thaw. The ferry's iced in."

"The river has paths, Emrys. It always has paths. The ferryman's daughter knows them better than any of the Vanes ever will." I heard him shift, heard the small reluctant creak of a young conscience being fitted for a burden. I opened my eyes and made my voice as level as I could with the fever hammering in it.

"I am not asking you to promise tonight. I watched you hold the letter and put it down, and I honor a man who won't swear what he hasn't weighed. Weigh it. But weigh it quickly." I nodded toward the window, toward the tower, toward the great case where the count wheel waited with its patient arithmetic. "Because tonight is midnight, and tonight you will stand under my father's clock and count fourteen strokes, and you will understand that this town has been lied to every single night for thirty-one years by the very machine it sets its Sundays by. After that, boy, you may promise me or not — but you will never again be able to tell yourself you didn't know."

Below us, faint through the floorboards, the river groaned — ice forming, plate by plate, closing the world's last door.

And somewhere down in the town, in warm parlors I could not see, I would have wagered my remaining breath that lamps were being lit, horses saddled, and black gloves drawn on — three heirs beginning, that very hour, the climb toward my hill.
