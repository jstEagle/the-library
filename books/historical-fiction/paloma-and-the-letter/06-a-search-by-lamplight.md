## 6. A Search by Lamplight

The errand came the way traps come—wrapped in kindness.

"Emrys," Aldous Vane said that morning, folding his gloved hands on the kitchen table as though he'd been sitting there all his life instead of two days, "the household's out of lamp oil. And Dr. Prewitt tells me Paloma should have fresh willow bark if she can keep it down. You know the river paths, I'm told. The ferryman's daughter could show you where to cross."

It was phrased as a courtesy. It was not one. There were three of them and one of him, and the ice had closed the bridge behind them all.

"I'll go after I've finished oiling the escapement," Emrys said.

"You'll go now," Aldous said pleasantly. "The clock will keep. Clocks always do."

* * *

The river at Hallow Ford in deep winter was a gray-white plain with a wind moving over it like something herding. Maren Ostrow met him at the bank with her father's hand-sled and a face that asked no questions, which was one of the reasons he trusted her more than anyone else in the town that had raised him.

"You're being sent away from the house," she said. Not a question either.

"The house is short on lamp oil."

"And long on Vanes." She kicked the sled runner free of snow crust. "My father says Aldous bought a round at the Anchor last night for men who've never bought him so much as a wink before. He's buying the town, Emrys. Cheap, too. Winter prices."

Emrys pulled his scarf higher and said nothing, because there was nothing safe to say on an open river with the wind carrying voices like a pickpocket carries watches.

They crossed in silence broken only by the creak and groan of the ice—a sound he would hear in his sleep for years afterward, a vast slow breathing beneath their boots, as though the river were turning over in its bed and finding them insignificant. At the trading post on the far bank he bought oil, willow bark, tallow candles, and, without letting himself think about it too precisely, a coil of waxed sail-thread and a tin of gun grease.

Maren watched him pay for the last two items and raised one eyebrow.

"Clockwork," he said.

"Right," she said, in the tone people use for weather they don't believe.

On the walk back she stopped him once, out on the white plain where no eavesdropper could live, and said quietly: "Whatever you're carrying that makes those men sleep badly—you should know my father kept the ferry logs from 'twenty-one. All of them. He never let anyone burn them, not even when the Vanes offered." She looked out across the ice. "I'm just saying some things are already saved. So whatever it is, it isn't only yours to lose."

He wanted to tell her everything. He told her the ice was thinning near the eastern channel and they should bear west, and she let him have the cowardice without comment, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

He knew before they reached the bank.

Smoke stood straight up from the observatory chimney, which was normal. But lamplight moved behind the observatory windows in a way lamps do not move when a house is merely lived in—swiftly, from room to room, carried at arm's length by people who want to see everything at once.

Maren saw it too. "Your errand took rather long," she said.

"I stopped to buy thread."

"Mm."

At the door, Cassian Vane opened it before Emrys could knock—young Cassian, the handsome one, whose smile in the letter's account had been described as *the smile he wore while the mill burned*. That was unfair, Emrys thought wildly. No one could help their face. But then Cassian stepped aside with a flourish, and Emrys understood the smile had been an invitation to witness.

"We were worried," Cassian said. "You were gone such a time."

Inside, the observatory had been turned inside out with terrible politeness. Books re-shelved slightly wrong. Drawers emptied onto tables and left in neat squares, because these were people who ransacked the way bankers count: thoroughly, and leaving everything looking respectable. The smell of lamp oil was everywhere; they'd used his own purchased stock, he realized, burning through the house while he was sent to fetch more.

Aldous sat by the cold stove with a ledger open on his knee—the observatory's visitor log, Emrys saw with a lurch, going back thirty years. Every name Paloma had ever recorded. Every historian who had written ahead to announce a visit. Every colleague who had ever corresponded.

"We found nothing, of course," Aldous said, not looking up. "Which is itself informative."

"Is it?"

"A dying woman keeps a house like this"—he gestured at the instruments, the star charts, the meticulous order—"for fifty years. She keeps *everything*. And yet the one thing three grown men cannot find is the one thing we're quite sure exists." He closed the ledger. "So either she destroyed it—and dying women with confessions don't destroy them—or someone moved it."

Emrys set down the oil can with a steady hand and thanked every god he didn't believe in that he had spent twelve years learning to make his hands lie.

"I've been here nine days," he said. "Mostly inside the clock case."

"Yes." Aldous finally looked up. "The clock. Tell me about the clock, apprentice Fairweather."

"It runs two minutes fast. I've corrected it twice. It drifts back."

"To what time?"

"To eleven fifty-eight."

Something passed across Aldous's face—not fear, Emrys realized. Recognition. Like a man hearing a song from his childhood and hating that he knows the words.

"Curious," Aldous said softly. "Well. If the keeper has hidden her paper somewhere in this house, it will be found. If someone has taken it off the property—" he rose, joints cracking, an old man made older by fury held under glass, "—then it is in the hands of someone in this household. And households, unlike papers, can be questioned."

Behind him, at the top of the stairs, Emrys heard a floorboard creak. Old Marta the cook stood in the shadow of the landing with an armful of Paloma's linens, frozen there, listening, her knuckles white around the cloth.

She had been here forty years. She had taught Emrys to make gruel when he first arrived, half-starved, apprenticed at eleven because his father owed money to everyone including the clockmaker who took him in payment. She had sung to him through the fever winter of 'forty-one.

Her eyes met his over the heads of the Vane brothers, and in them was a question: *What did you bring into this house, boy?*

He had no answer he could give her. He looked away first.

* * *

That night he lay in his narrow cot in the clockroom and listened to the house breathe.

The letter was gone from the clock case—had been since the third night, when he'd woken at two in the morning with his heart hammering and done the work by feel alone, no candle, because a candle seen under a door kills faster than any blade. He had unfastened the false back of the great regulator's weight cabinet, greased the channel, wrapped the letter in waxed cloth, threaded it through, and sealed it behind a plate that would take another clockmaker a full day's disassembly to find—and even then only if he already suspected it was there.

No one would find it. That was the problem. No one would ever find it, which meant it might as well be ash, which meant all of this—the search, the terror, Marta's white knuckles—had bought nothing but danger distributed among the innocent.

And now the innocent were being counted.

Through the wall he heard low voices in the kitchen: Aldous, and Dr. Prewitt, who had come again "to check on the keeper." He heard coins. Not many—he heard the particular small dry music of silver, not gold, which meant the doctor was cheap and Aldous knew it.

Then footsteps on the stairs. He shut his eyes and slowed his breathing to the rhythm he'd learned as a boy, hiding in the clockmaker's loft while creditors pounded below.

The door opened. Lamplight fell across his eyelids. Someone stood there a long moment—long enough that pretending became its own confession if they knew—but the light withdrew, and the door closed soft as a book.

He lay awake until the house stilled. Then he got up, crossed to the master clock in the dark, and put his ear against the case, listening to the escapement tick toward midnight—to eleven fifty-eight, the hour the town's histories had erased, the hour the clock refused to forget.

Two minutes fast, forever. Two minutes of truth a night.

Tomorrow, he thought, he would have to move it again. And tomorrow, or the day after, someone in this house would pay for where he'd hidden it.

Below, faint through the floors, he heard the kitchen door open and close, and footsteps going down toward the boathouse—toward the ferry landing, where the Ostrows' boat was tied.

Where Maren's family slept.
