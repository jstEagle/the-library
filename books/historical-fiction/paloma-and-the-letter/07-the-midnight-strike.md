## 7. The Midnight Strike

Aldous Vane had not slept in a bed since the bridge closed. He told himself it was the cold, the strange house, the smell of camphor and old brass that clung to everything in the observatory like a second skin. But that was a lie he kept in his coat pocket alongside the others, and by the third night even he had stopped reaching for it.

The truth was simpler. The truth was that somewhere in this building was a piece of paper with his grandfather's name on it, written in a dead man's blood, and Aldous could not rest until it was ash.

He sat now in the observatory's front room, in a chair too small for him, listening to the house settle. Below, the river groaned under its new ice—a sound like a giant turning over in its sleep. His brother-in-law Corwin had gone up to bed an hour ago, defeated by drink. His cousin Maren sat across from him, mending a glove she had no intention of wearing, her needle moving in small precise strokes, her eyes on nothing.

"She's worse tonight," Maren said.

"The doctor says she has days."

"The doctor says what you pay him to say."

Aldous let that pass. He had learned, in forty years of managing the family's affairs, that the most dangerous sentences were the ones worth answering, and so he answered none of them.

What troubled him was the clock.

They had searched the observatory twice—once openly, under the pretense of helping the keeper put her papers in order, and once at two in the morning while the apprentice boy was supposedly asleep. They had gone through her desk, her correspondence, the lining of every trunk. They had found sermons, seed catalogs, forty years of weather observations recorded in a hand so small it might have been a fly's footprints. No letter.

And the clock case itself—Corwin had held the lamp while Aldous ran his fingers along every panel, every joint, behind every weight. Empty. Clean. The movement gleamed like something newly born.

Which meant either Paloma had never written the letter at all—and Aldous did not believe that, because a woman did not summon a clockmaker from three towns away to repair a clock that needed no repairing—or someone had moved it.

The boy, then. Emrys Fairweather. Twenty-two years old, if that. Hands like a surgeon's, eyes that went still when you spoke to him, the way a hare goes still. Aldous had bought men like that before. You did not buy them with money; you bought them with futures. Every apprentice in the valley dreamed of escaping the bench, and the Vane firm could make dreams into contracts.

Tomorrow, he would make the offer.

Tonight, there was only the waiting, and the clock above them ticking through the dark like a patient heart.

Maren looked up. "What time is it?"

Aldous consulted his pocket watch out of habit, though the answer was everywhere around them. "Eleven fifty-eight."

"Then we'll know soon enough whether your repair holds."

"It isn't my repair."

"No," Maren said, threading her needle. "It's hers."

They waited. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, they could hear the nurse moving about the sickroom, the small sounds of a dying woman being made comfortable. Aldous found himself counting the clock's ticks the way he had counted votes once, long ago, in a room where the founding families had decided what the town's history would say.

At midnight exactly, the master clock began to strike.

But it did not strike twelve.

It struck one. Then two. Then three, four, five—the great bronze voice of it rolling down through the floorboards, out the windows, over the ice, unhurried and enormous, each blow separated by a silence wide enough to walk through. Six. Seven. Maren had stopped sewing. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve—

Thirteen.

Fourteen.

Fifteen.

Aldous stood up without deciding to. The strikes went on, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, each one landing in his chest like a fist, and he understood—not gradually, but all at once, the way ice understands it is breaking—that the clock was not broken. It had never been broken. It was striking the true time of the massacre at the mill, the hour the histories had shaved down and sanded smooth, and it struck that hour every midnight for whoever cared to count, and it had been striking it for years, and no one had ever listened.

Or rather: no one had ever *counted*.

But a clockmaker would. Any clockmaker in the valley—a dozen of them, fifty, apprentices and journeymen and masters, men who counted strikes the way priests counted beads—any one of them called to this house, summoned to this bench, would open the case, find the train set deliberately, unmistakably wrong, and ask why. And beside the striking train, wedged where only a craftsman's hand would think to look—

"The letter is in the mechanism," Maren said quietly. She had arrived there faster than he had. She always did. "Not hidden from the clockmaker. Hidden *for* him."

Aldous crossed to the window. Down in the town, lights were coming on—one, then another, doors opening, figures spilling onto the frozen street to stare up at the observatory hill, because a clock striking nineteen was not a malfunction, it was an omen, and towns were never more superstitious than when they were guilty.

"He repaired it," Aldous said. "The boy. He found whatever she hid in there and he *repaired* it, and now—" He stopped. Now the confession rang out over the valley every midnight, built into bronze and steel, immune to fire, immune to theft, readable by any craftsman who ever touched the movement. You could burn a letter. You could not burn an idea that lived inside gear teeth.

"He didn't repair it," Maren said. "Look at him."

Emrys Fairweather stood in the doorway of the front room, coat half-buttoned, woken by the ringing like everyone else in the house. His face was pale and composed, and his eyes—those still, hare's eyes—went first to the clock, then to Aldous, and in them Aldous read something that turned his stomach slowly over.

Not fear. Not guilt.

*Accuracy.* The boy was counting the last strikes with his lips moving slightly, confirming the number against some internal standard, and finding it correct.

"You knew," Aldous said.

"I suspected." The boy's voice was level. "I told you I couldn't find a mechanical fault. There wasn't one. The error was deliberate. I said as much the first day."

"You said two minutes fast."

"Two minutes fast at noon," Emrys said, "is nineteen strokes at midnight. It depends which time you're measuring. Hers or the town's."

Downstairs, distantly, the last echo of the nineteenth stroke came back off the ice, and the clock resumed its ordinary ticking, innocent as anything.

Aldous looked at Maren. Maren looked back at him, and between them passed the whole inheritance—not the mills and the land and the name, but the other thing, the older thing, the thing their grandfathers had signed and buried and paid to keep buried. It had been a story once. It was becoming a debt.

"She cannot be allowed to recover," Aldous said.

It was not a plan. It was not even a decision, quite. It was more like a stone finally loosed from a slope that had been holding it for sixty years, and having said it, Aldous felt the terrible lightness of a man who has stopped pretending.

Maren put down her sewing. "The doctor comes at eight."

"I know when the doctor comes."

"And the boy?"

Aldous looked at Emrys Fairweather, who stood very straight in the doorway with the whole cold night behind him, and made his second mistake of the evening: he smiled.

"The boy," Aldous said, "is going to accept my offer."

* * *

Emrys did not sleep either. He lay on the narrow cot in the clockmaker's room with his boots on, listening to the house, and what he heard frightened him less than what he understood.

He understood the nineteen strokes. He had known since the third day, when he'd traced the striking train and found the count-wheel filed by a careful, loving hand—filed to strike the true hour of the massacre, the hour the histories had shortened by five minutes to spare the founding families' blushes. Paloma had shown him the passage in the town history herself, tapping the page with one thin finger: *the trouble at the mill, brief and regrettable, resolved by nightfall.* Resolved by nightfall. Nineteen strokes said otherwise. Nineteen strokes said the trouble lasted until nearly quarter past, and what resolved it was not negotiation.

She had built the truth into the one place no heir would ever think to search and no historian would ever fail to consult: the mechanism. Confession by escapement. Testimony in bronze. And she had let him repair the clock knowing exactly what his repair would do—knowing that a working striking train would announce her secret to the entire valley, midnight after midnight, until someone counted.

He had been her instrument. He had wound the spring and pulled the trigger both.

Now, lying in the dark, he heard what he had been dreading all evening: footsteps on the stairs, unhurried, and then a knock at his door that did not wait for an answer before Aldous Vane opened it, lamplight behind him, his face arranged into benevolence.

"You should be asleep," Aldous said. "Big day tomorrow. I'd like to talk terms—your future, the firm, all of it. Morning suits you better than midnight, I imagine."

Emrys sat up slowly. "Midnight seems fitting, sir. We've just heard it."

Something flickered across the old man's face—annoyance, quickly mastered. "Nineteen strokes," Aldous said softly. "You counted them."

"Someone always counts them eventually. That was her design."

"Her design is a dying woman's fancy. What matters is what happens next." Aldous stepped fully into the room, and the lamplight fell across the workbench, across Emrys's tools laid out in their neat rows, across the empty space in the corner where the letter had been before Emrys moved it. "I'm offering you everything your trade can't give you. A bench of your own. Your name over the door. You're what—twenty-two? You could be master of your own shop by twenty-five, in any town you like, with money enough that no one in this valley could ever call you *boy* again. All it costs you is a piece of paper you claim not to have."

"And if I don't have it?"

"Then you have nothing to sell me, and we're wasting each other's time." Aldous's smile returned, thinner now. "But you moved something today, Mr. Fairweather. I've watched hands for thirty years. Yours were busy."

Emrys met his eyes and gave him the only gift he could afford: the truth, bent just enough to hold weight. "If I had such a paper, selling it to you wouldn't save anyone. It would only prove it exists. And if I burned it, you'd never believe it was burned. You'd wonder about me for the rest of your life." He paused. "So the offer doesn't work, sir. Not because I'm honest. Because you aren't."

For a moment the room was perfectly still, and Emrys heard, beneath the silence, the master clock ticking away downstairs—steady, faithful, already gathering itself toward the next midnight, and the next, and the next after that, ready to strike nineteen over this house until the ice broke and the world came asking questions.

Aldous straightened his cuffs. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet and pleasant, which was how Emrys now knew to measure the danger.

"Eight o'clock," Aldous said. "The doctor comes at eight. Do try to get some sleep, Mr. Fairweather. Tomorrow will be a long day for all of us."

His footsteps faded down the stairs. Emrys blew out his lamp and lay back in the dark, and did not sleep, and listened to the clock below keeping its two times at once—the town's time and the true one—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that by tomorrow's midnight one of those times would belong to a dead woman, and the other would belong to whoever was left to tell it.
