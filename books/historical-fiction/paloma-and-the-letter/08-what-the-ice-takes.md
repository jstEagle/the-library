## 8. What the Ice Takes

Paloma fell on the landing of the stair, halfway between her bed and the clock room, and Emrys heard it from below—the soft, wrong weight of a body meeting wood, the sound a clock never makes.

He found her crumpled against the banister, one hand still closed around the rail as if she'd been climbing toward the movement, toward the letter's hiding place, toward some last instruction she meant to give him. Her face had gone the color of the river ice. Her breath came in shallow pulls, each one costing her something.

"Doctor," the housekeeper cried from the kitchen doorway. "Send for Doctor Halloway."

"He's already here," said a voice from the front hall.

Emrys turned. Aldous Merrick stood in the doorway with his gloves still on, snow melting off his shoulders, and behind him came Doctor Halloway—a soft, gray man Emrys had seen a hundred times in town, the man who had set his broken wrist when he was nine and never once asked how it happened.

"Stand aside," Halloway said, and knelt, and did the things doctors do: the wrist, the chest, the eyelids peeled back to the lamplight. Emrys watched his hands and knew, within a minute, that they were wrong. Too slow. Too gentle. A man performing an examination the way a clerk copies a ledger he has already balanced.

"Well?" Aldous asked.

"Her heart," Halloway said. "It's failing. It has been failing for some time. Rest, and quiet, and—" he paused, delicately, "—and we must not agitate her. No visitors. No excitement. I'll leave a tonic."

Emrys had watched his own master die of a failing heart, four years ago, in a room that smelled of oil and brass. He knew what a failing heart looked like, and it did not look like this. Paloma's lips were blue at the edges, yes, but her pulse, when Halloway's fingers left her wrist, was still there. Still stubborn. Still keeping her own time.

"You're not giving her anything," Emrys said. "No laudanum, no digitalis. Nothing."

Halloway did not look at him. "The tonic will address her symptoms."

"The tonic is water and sugar." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange, the voice of a man reading an invoice. "I've ground enough drugs for apothecaries to know what a preparation looks like. You're letting her die in front of us."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Aldous removed one glove, finger by finger.

"Mr. Fairweather," he said, "grief makes us all say foolish things. The doctor has served this town for thirty years."

"Thirty years," Emrys said, "of being paid by the same three families."

He expected anger. What he got was worse: Aldous simply looked at him with a kind of tired patience, the look of a man watching a boy overreach at cards, and said nothing at all. And Emrys understood, in that moment, that the silence was the message. *We do not need to threaten you. You already know what happens to people in this town who make noise.*

They carried Paloma to her bed. Halloway left the tonic—a brown bottle, mostly empty of anything that mattered—and Aldous paused at the door on his way out.

"She's an old woman," he said quietly. "Old women die. When she does, the house will be searched properly, and whatever she hid will come to light, and no one will be blamed for it. Least of all you." He settled his hat. "Think about which side of that morning you want to stand on."

Then the door closed, and the house was very quiet, and upstairs Paloma breathed in her shallow, stubborn rhythm, and Emrys stood in the hall holding a bottle of sugar water and understanding, fully, the price of the cleverness he'd been proud of. He had moved the letter. He had emptied the clock case. And now every person in this house—the housekeeper, the girl who came with the milk, old Tomas who split the wood—stood inside the blast radius of whatever Aldous Merrick decided to do next.

He had bought himself time with a hiding place, and the interest was coming due in other people's blood.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in the clock room with a single candle and the letter open on the workbench, and he copied it.

He had promised himself he wouldn't read it until he could promise to deliver it, and he had already broken that promise a hundred times over in the dark, and so he read it now properly, all of it, every line, his pen keeping pace with Paloma's hand. The mill. The night in November, forty years ago, when the founding families had decided the striking hands were asking for too much and had answered with fire and iron. Her father's signature at the bottom of the account that followed—signed with a pistol on the table beside the inkwell, she wrote, though the histories would say *witnessed*. The historian from the capital, paid in land. The schoolbooks. The statue in the square, cast from the same foundry that made the mill's new bell.

*They did not erase us,* Paloma had written. *They reprinted us. And a reprint can be corrected.*

Emrys copied every word in his own careful apprentice's hand, the hand he'd been trained to use on engraved dials, and when he finished, near three in the morning, he held both pages up to the candle and found he could not tell which hand was which. That frightened him more than anything Aldous had said. He had spent twelve years learning to make his work indistinguishable from his master's, and now the skill had a new use: forgery. Or testimony. The same hand; the difference was only in what it served.

He folded the copy into his shirt, against his chest. The original he wrapped in oilcloth, the way he'd wrap a movement for shipping, and then he sat and thought about who could carry it.

Not the housekeeper—Aldous had already been through her, Emrys was sure of it, had already found whatever soft thing she loved and priced it. Not Tomas. Not the milk girl. Not the doctor. Not anyone whose name appeared on a ledger in this town, because everyone's name appeared on a ledger in this town. That was the whole architecture of the place. That was what Paloma's letter was actually about, under the massacre and the signatures: a town built as a ledger, every soul entered, every debt collectible.

It had to be someone with no entries.

He thought of her standing at the river's edge that first morning, when the ice was still new—Maren, the ferryman's daughter, who poled the crossing in the last open water of autumn and walked the ice shelf in winter like it was a hallway in her own house. Who was seventeen and owned nothing, whose father's ferry license had been revoked the same year the histories were reprinted, and who therefore owed the founding families nothing but contempt. Whom Aldous Merrick could not buy because she had already priced everything he owned and found it worthless.

She came at dawn, as she always did, with the milk cans, walking the river path.

Emrys met her at the woodshed, out of sight of the house.

"You look like you haven't slept," she said.

"I need you to take something across the ice."

She looked at him for a long moment. Maren had a way of looking at people that made them feel itemized. "The letter."

"You know about the letter."

"Everyone with eyes knows about the letter. The clock strikes two minutes wrong at midnight and three men from the founding families moved into a dying woman's house in the same week." She shifted the milk cans to her other hip. "I know about the letter the way the river knows about ice. It's been coming all season."

He held out the oilcloth bundle. She didn't take it.

"Where?"

"The historian in Carrow. Delacroix. He's coming when the thaw opens the road—he's the one they paid off forty years ago's successor, the one who actually reads things." Emrys heard his own voice speed up. "His office is on the mill road. You know the river paths better than—"

"I know them better than anyone, yes." She still hadn't taken the bundle. "Say the rest."

"The rest?"

"The rest of why me. Say it out loud so we both hear it."

Emrys looked at the bundle in his hands, and then at her—seventeen, unbuyable, and about to become the most dangerous person in the valley—and said it.

"Because they can't threaten you with anything you want. And they can't ruin you because you're already outside the ruin. And because if it goes wrong, you're fast, and you know the ice, and they don't." He swallowed. "And because I can't carry it myself. Because if I'm caught with it, everything stops here. Because I'm a coward with a good arm, and you're not."

Maren took the bundle then, and weighed it in her hand like a fish.

"Coward," she said. "Maybe. Or maybe you're the one who stays and signs his name at the end. That's the expensive job. Carrying paper is easy. Staying is what costs." She tucked the bundle inside her coat, against the milk can, where the cold would keep the oilcloth stiff and the shape unremarkable. "If they search me, I'm carrying milk. If they press me, I don't know what's in my coat. If they catch me on the ice, I fall through and it's gone, and I'll say it was gone before I ever had it."

"And if they offer you something?"

She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "They'd have to offer me something I don't already have. They don't make it."

She turned to go, then stopped.

"Paloma," she said. "How is she?"

"The doctor is killing her. Slowly, by doing nothing. And everyone in this house can see it, and no one can say it, because the man paying for the silence is standing in the hall with his gloves on."

Maren nodded slowly, and Emrys watched her understand the shape of it—the whole ledger of it, the price being paid in full view, in a sickroom, by an old woman whose only crime was keeping a clock two minutes honest.

"Then hurry," she said, "both of us," and she went down the river path with the milk cans swinging, and the bundle against her heart, and the ice holding her the way it holds everything—until it doesn't.

Emrys stood in the woodshed until she was a dot on the white river, and then he went inside, and up the stairs, and sat by Paloma's bed, where her eyes opened a crack and found him.

"Is it gone?" she whispered.

"It's gone. Both of it. The copy and the original, on two different rivers."

Paloma's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Good," she breathed. "Now they have to decide what kind of monsters they are. With witnesses."

Downstairs, the front door opened, and Aldous Merrick's voice asked the housekeeper, very politely, whether the milk girl had come by yet—and Emrys knew, with a cold certainty that had nothing to do with clocks, that the search had already begun, and that the ice under Maren's feet was not the only thing about to break.
