## 9. Before the Ice Breaks

Aldous Merrick came for me at dawn.

Not with the housekeeper's key or a lamp held high—he came alone, without his brothers, and he knocked on the door of the clock room like a man calling on a neighbor to borrow a tool. That was the worst of it. The courtesy. He had learned it from his father's ledgers, I supposed: every theft dressed as a transaction.

"You're up early," he said, when I opened the door with the winding crank still in my hand.

"Clocks don't keep banker's hours."

"No." He looked past me into the room, at the great case standing against the eastern wall, its pendulum swinging in the gray light like a slow metronome counting something down. "They don't. May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He stepped inside, brushed snow from his shoulders with two precise movements, and stood regarding the movement through the glass as though appraising livestock. Up close he smelled of cedar and coal smoke and something underneath both—lamp oil, maybe, from long nights spent reading documents by flame. I thought of the letter. Of Maren somewhere on the river paths with it sewn into her coat lining. I kept my hands loose at my sides and my face doing nothing at all, which is the first thing an apprentice learns and the last thing he unlearns.

"I'll be plain with you, Fairweather," Aldous said. "You're twenty years old, you've no master but a dying woman, and your trade is one that towns this size let starve politely. I've made inquiries. Your parents took the mill's money when the works closed. This observatory hasn't paid a wage since before you were born."

"Is there a question in there?"

"There's an offer." He turned from the clock and looked at me directly, and I will grant him this: he did not flinch from what he was doing. Some men lie to themselves while they buy other men. Aldous Merrick looked at the purchase plainly. "The family firm keeps a workshop in the city. Twelve apprentices, a contract with the railway for station clocks, a name that opens doors. You'd have a bench of your own within the year. Wages. A room over the shop with a window on the street. In ten years you could be a master in everything but title, and we'd arrange the title too."

"And in exchange."

"The copy," he said. "And whatever notes you've made transcribing it. And your silence after, which I believe will come free once you're a man with something to lose."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. I had expected them not to be.

"Paloma is dying," I said.

"Yes."

"She has perhaps a week. The doctor says so, and whatever else Dr. Halloway is, he reads a fever correctly."

"Yes," Aldous said again, softer now, almost gentle. "Which means this need not cost her anything. She won't know. No one needs to tell her she was beaten; she'll be past caring. You can take her secret into the ground with her, where secrets do no harm, and take a life out of it for yourself. That's not corruption, Fairweather. That's arithmetic."

Arithmetic. My father had used the same word, standing in the yard of the closed mill, telling me why he signed the severance papers without complaint: *arithmetic, boy, the town does arithmetic and calls it peace.* Every silence I had ever kept had been arithmetic. The time I saw Mr. Pell shortchange the widow Crane and said nothing, because Pell bought flour from my mother. The time I watched the older apprentices lock young Tobias in the drying cupboard and said nothing, because I was small and they were not. Twelve years of keeping my head down and my hands busy and my mouth shut, and here was a man offering to pay me, at last, the going rate for all of it.

"I'll need to think," I said.

"Of course." He set a card on the bench beside the crank—a heavy card, cream-colored, embossed. "Three days. The ice gives us all time we didn't ask for." At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "For what it's worth, I read the town histories as a boy. The founding of the valley, the brave stand at the mill. I believed every word until last month. I'd rather keep believing it. Most people would. Remember that when you decide who you're really punishing."

Then he was gone, and I stood alone with the clock, listening to it tick, and I understood that he had just taught me the shape of my own trap better than any threat could have. He wasn't afraid of the truth. He was afraid of being *seen* receiving it—and he assumed everyone was, because he was.

* * *

I went up to Paloma's room after. She was propped against pillows, gray as February, but her eyes tracked me across the floor the way they always had, sharp as a seconds hand.

"He offered you the firm," she said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because it's what I would have offered, if I were buying a boy instead of a conscience." She coughed, wet and long, and waved off the cup I reached for. "What did you tell him?"

"That I'd think about it."

Her laugh turned into another cough. When she could speak again she said, "Good. Never answer a Merrick quickly. It spooks them—they think speed means honesty." She studied me. "But you're not thinking, are you. You're already past it."

I sat on the edge of her bed. Downstairs a door closed; one of the younger heirs, moving through the house like a debt collector. Three days, I had. Maybe less—the ice groaned in the night sometimes, a sound like a giant turning over, and the ferryman said the thaw was early this year.

"If I give him the copy," I said, "he burns it, and the original dies with Maren's courage or survives on luck. If I refuse, he ruins me, and maybe worse, and the copy is still out there on luck. Either way it comes down to luck unless—" I stopped.

"Unless," Paloma agreed.

"Unless there are witnesses. Real ones. So many that burning one letter doesn't matter, because the story is already loose in the town's mouths."

She was quiet a moment. Then she said, "There's a reason I built it into the clock, boy, and not into a drawer. A letter can be burned. A mechanism, once seen working, cannot be unseen." Her hand found mine, cold and light as a bird's foot. "The whole valley's clockmakers could read that striking train. I meant it for after I was gone—for some honest journeyman, someday, taking the movement apart. But you're an honest journeyman *now*, and the town is right downstairs, and Sunday is tomorrow."

"Tomorrow isn't Sunday."

"It's better than Sunday." Her grip tightened, surprising strength in it. "First-of-the-month winding day. Every soul in this town who owns a watch brings it to be regulated against the master, the way their grandfathers did. They come up that hill because it's habit, and habit is the one thing stronger than fear. Wind it in front of them. Let it strike twelve. Let them count."

"They'll hear midnight strike at two minutes to."

"They'll hear the truth strike," she said, "on the record, in front of forty witnesses and three Merricks who can't burn forty pairs of ears." She lay back, exhausted by the effort, but her eyes stayed open and fixed on the ceiling, on nothing, on 1847 and 1821 laid over each other like tracing paper. "Do you know what my father's crime was? Not the signing. Everyone signs. His crime was surviving comfortably after. Don't survive comfortably, Emrys. I'm asking you for one uncomfortable hour."

* * *

They began coming up the hill at ten.

I heard them before I saw them—the crunch of boots on frozen gravel, voices carrying strangely in the cold air, the creak of the observatory steps taking more weight than they'd held in years. First-of-month winding day. I had half feared the Merricks' presence would thin the crowd, that fear would beat habit after all. It didn't. If anything the tension drew them, the way crowds gather at a house where something has happened. The mill families came, and the church families, and old Sorrel who remembered the flood of '09, and the schoolteacher with her ledger of watch-regulations, and behind them, drifting at the edges like oil on water, the three heirs.

Aldous watched me from the doorway. He had worked it out—I could see it in the set of his shoulders, the too-still way he stood—but there was nothing he could say in front of forty townsfolk that wouldn't confess everything. That was the beauty of it, and Paloma had known: witnesses cut both ways. He had come to honor the dying keeper publicly. Now he had to stand in public and let the public see what honoring her looked like.

I opened the case. The movement gleamed under the lamplight, brass and steel, the striking train assembled true. I had rebuilt it exactly as she'd designed it, weights and pins and the little cam cut two minutes proud of the hour wheel—her confession written in metal, ticking away all these years where only someone who knew how to look could read it. I put my hand on the winding crank.

"Master clock's due," I called out, loud enough for the hall. "Who's brought watches?"

A murmur, a rustle of coats, hands producing pocket watches held out like communion wafers. The schoolteacher readied her pencil. Old Sorrel squinted at the case. And Aldous Merrick moved—one step into the room, his mouth opening—

I wound.

The ratchet clicked under my hand, weight after weight climbing back up the barrel, the mainspring gathering itself like a held breath. Around me the room went quiet in stages, conversation folding inward toward the clock, forty faces turning. I felt Aldous arrive at my shoulder. I heard him say, very low, "Fairweather. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"Regulating the town," I said, and gave the crank its final turn, and stepped back.

The escapement ticked. The second hand walked. Somewhere above us the hands crawled toward the hour, and the whole room watched them the way people watch weather, and I counted my own heartbeats because there was nothing else to count.

The minute hand touched the mark.

The clock struck.

Once. Twice. On the fifth stroke old Sorrel pulled out his watch and frowned. By the eighth the schoolteacher had stopped writing. By the tenth the murmur had become a sound—not alarm yet, not understanding, just the animal noise a room makes when it senses a wrongness it can't name. Twelve strokes rang down through the floorboards, unhurried, iron-voiced, each one falling into silence like a stone into a well.

Then the clock struck thirteen times.

Thirteen. Two minutes past noon—or two minutes past midnight, if you knew how to read the cam, if you knew that the mechanism had been striking the *true* hour all along, every midnight, for thirty years, while every other clock in the valley obediently lied.

The room did not understand. Not yet. I saw it move through them like cold water—confusion, then unease, then the slow terrible dawning that a machine does not err twice a night for thirty years by accident, that someone had *built* it this way, that someone in this room must know why. Heads turned. Toward the clock. Toward me.

Toward Aldous Merrick, whose face had gone the color of the ice on the river, and whose brother Verrin had taken a step backward, away from him, in front of everyone.

"Two minutes," said the schoolteacher slowly, pencil forgotten. "It strikes two minutes fast. It's *always* struck two minutes fast. My grandfather used to say—" She stopped. Old Sorrel had gone very still, staring at the case as if he could see through the wood, and I watched eighty years of half-heard tavern talk rearrange itself behind his eyes: *the mill bell rung wrong the night of the trouble, the mill bell rung wrong.*

"What's the true time, then?" someone asked from the back. "If that's wrong, what's right?"

And before I could answer—before anyone could—the door at the end of the hall opened, and the housekeeper stood in it, white-faced, holding a telegram still warm from the office, and said:

"The ice is breaking. The bridge is closing. And there's a historian's clerk at the ferry landing asking after the keeper of this observatory by name."
