## 10. The Letter, Signed

The ice broke on a Tuesday, with a sound like a door slamming in the belly of the world.

Emrys heard it from the observatory steps at half past four in the morning—a deep concussion rolling up the valley, then another, then a long grinding roar as the river shrugged off the season and began to move. He had not slept. No one in the house had slept. They had all been listening to Paloma breathe for three days, each breath shallower than the last, each one a coin spent from a purse that was nearly empty.

He went down to the water.

The ferryman's daughter was already there, standing on the bank with her shawl pulled tight, watching the river tear itself apart. Chunks of ice the size of tables rode the current, grinding against one another, hissing as they turned. The bridge—closed since November, buried under its crust of white—was shedding its skin, black timber emerging plank by plank like something surfacing from a dream.

"It's open by noon," Maren said. She didn't look at him. "The historian's man is on the far bank. He's been waiting since the thaw began. I gave him the letter two days ago."

Emrys stared at her. "Two days?"

"He has legs," she said. "And I have a brother who skates." Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone. "It's done, Emrys. It's out of our hands now. It was out of them the moment I took it across the ice."

The river ran brown and furious between the banks, carrying winter away in pieces. Emrys watched a branch—whole, still wearing last summer's bark—go spinning past toward the lowlands, toward the district seat, toward the historians and their careful hands.

* * *

Paloma died at dawn.

They were all of them in her room when it happened—the heirs because they could not bear to leave before the end and confirm what they'd won or lost; Emrys because he would not be driven from the bedside by men who had bought a doctor; the household staff because no one had told them they couldn't come, and for once no one did tell them.

The clock struck six while she was dying.

Emrys had set the mechanism true again—or rather, he had left it true, leaving untouched the two-minute error she had built into the striking train, the error that was not an error at all but a memorial in brass and steel. At six o'clock the master clock struck the hour that the town's clocks struck five fifty-eight, and everyone in that room understood, perhaps for the first time all together, what those two minutes held.

Aldous Vane stood by the window with his back to the bed. His brother Corwin sat with his hat in his lap like a schoolboy. The third heir, Seraphine, had stopped performing grief sometime around midnight and simply watched, her face finally honest, which made it worse than any performance.

"Six o'clock," Paloma said. Her voice was very small, but the room was very quiet, and it carried. "You hear it? Every night. Every morning. For thirty-one years."

No one answered her. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said, or bought, or buried.

"I don't forgive you," she went on, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat clarity of a woman past all ceremony. "I want you to know that. Forgiveness was never the point. The point was that somebody wrote it down."

She looked for Emrys then, and he came to her, and her hand found his wrist with surprising strength—the grip of a woman who had spent her life keeping time and knew precisely how much of it remained.

"The letter," she whispered. "Is it out?"

"It's out. It reached the historian before the ice broke."

Something moved through her face—not quite relief, not quite peace. Something more like a clock finishing its count.

"Then wind it every day," she said. "Promise me. Not for me. For the hour."

He promised. She died nine minutes later, between one breath and the next, so quietly that for a moment nobody was sure, and then the doctor—who had withheld his treatments and knew exactly what his withholding had purchased—checked her pulse and closed her eyes with professional gentleness, and it was done.

* * *

The account was published eleven days later.

Emrys saw the first copy in the district newspaper, which arrived on the Thursday coach along with the spring mud and a quantity of new gossip. It occupied the front page entire: *THE MILL AT RIVERBEND: A CORRECTION TO THE RECORD*, followed by the historian's careful summary and, beneath it, reproduced in full, the text of Paloma's letter—her father's coerced signature, the names of the families who had ordered the mill burned with forty-three people inside it, the payments made to the chronicler whose history had stood unchallenged for sixty years.

At the bottom of the column, below the letter itself, was a line the historian had added:

*The original document was preserved and delivered by the keeper of the Riverbend Observatory, Paloma Ashwood (d. 1847), at the cost of her final months. The transcription and its passage across the closing ice were accomplished by E. Fairweather, apprentice clockmaker of this town.*

His name. In print. Attached forever to the thing that would make him a ghost in his own hometown.

He read it standing in the coach yard, in front of God and the postmaster and half the market crowd, and he felt the town reading over his shoulder—he felt it physically, a pressure, a temperature change, the way a room goes cold when a door opens onto winter. Old Mr. Calloway, who had sold Emrys his first file, looked at him and then deliberately looked away. A woman he had known his whole life gathered her daughter closer and walked on. Nobody spoke to him. Nobody spat either—that would have required acknowledging him—and somehow the silence was worse.

Twelve years of apprenticeship. Twelve years of being the boy who swept the shop floor and fetched the coal and learned patience the way other boys learned to fight. All of it dissolved in a single printed line. He had traded the only inheritance he had ever expected—the trade itself, the town's slow nod of acceptance—for a paragraph in a newspaper.

He found he could not regret it. That surprised him most of all. He had expected regret, had braced for it like a man bracing for cold water, and instead there was only a strange lightness, grief and freedom wearing each other's clothes.

* * *

The funeral was held on a bright, hard morning, and the heirs attended in force, dressed in mourning that cost more than most of the mourners earned in a year. Aldous gave the eulogy himself. He spoke of Paloma's decades of faithful service, her devotion to the stars, her quiet dignity—and he spoke well, because Aldous always spoke well, and Emrys stood at the back of the churchyard among the tradesmen and understood that this too was a kind of repair work: the family firm, restoring its reputation the way you'd restore a damaged movement, replacing the ruined parts with new ones until the mechanism told whatever story you needed.

But the master clock struck during the burial.

It was pure accident of timing—the interment ran long, and noon arrived mid-prayer—and the whole assembled town heard it: twelve strikes sounding two minutes behind every other clock in Riverbend, a discrepancy no one could ignore once it had been named in print. Heads turned toward the observatory hill. Hands stilled on hymnals. An old woman near Emrys whispered, "That's her doing. That's her two minutes," and crossed herself, and did not seem ashamed of it.

Aldous's jaw tightened. He finished the prayer flawlessly. But Emrys saw his eyes go up to the observatory, and he saw the arithmetic happening behind them: the letter was published, yes—but the clock remained, striking the true hour twice a day, every day, forever, and every clockmaker in the valley could read what it said.

Some inheritances cannot be burned.

* * *

He packed that same evening.

There wasn't much. His tools, wrapped in oilcloth. Two shirts. The book of logarithms Paloma had given him at Midwinter, inscribed in her spidery hand: *For the boy who listens to mechanisms.* He wound the master clock one last time—full winding, every barrel, enough to run eight days without him—and stood a long moment with his hand on the case, feeling through the wood the patient tick of the escapement, the heartbeat she had tuned to hold a massacre in its teeth.

"You're the letter now," he told it quietly. "Every strike. Every day. Let them try to stop you."

Maren met him at the bridge at first light.

The river ran open beneath it, high and brown and quick, carrying the last of the ice toward the sea. The planks were wet and streaming, and the whole structure groaned softly in the current like something waking. Beyond it the road ran south toward the district seat, toward work he didn't yet have and a name that preceded him into every room.

"They'll never take you back," Maren said. It wasn't cruel. It was just true, and she was the sort of person who dealt in truths; she had carried one across a frozen river, after all.

"I know."

"My father says the historian wants a written account. From you personally. Everything you witnessed, in your own hand." She paused. "He says it should be signed."

Emrys looked back at the town once—the rooftops steaming in the early sun, the smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the observatory white-capped on its hill with its windows catching the light. Somewhere in that town were children being taught the founding story out of the paid chronicler's book, and somewhere else, in kitchens and back shops, adults were reading the newspaper in silence and not discussing it with their neighbors, and both of those things were true at once, and both would remain true, and he would not be there to see which one won.

He took out his pencil stub and, right there on the bridge rail, on the flyleaf of the logarithm book where he kept his accounts, he signed his full name: *Emrys Fairweather*. Not for the historian—he'd do that properly later, pages of it, everything. This signature was for himself. Proof, in his own hand, that he existed, that he had been present, that he chose it.

Then he crossed the bridge.

Halfway over he stopped—because the master clock, far above on its hill, began to strike the quarter hour, its voice carrying clear across the water, two minutes offset from every other bell in the valley, and he stood on the swaying planks above the running river and let the sound pass through him like a blessing.

Behind him lay the town that would not forgive him. Ahead lay the account he had yet to write.

Emrys Fairweather walked on, and did not look back again—except once, at the far bank, to check the time.
