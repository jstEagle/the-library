## 1. The Last Ferry

The ferry came at low tide, the way it always did on its last run of the season, and it sat in the harbor like a fat grey bird that had decided, after much consideration, not to fly.

I watched it from the cliff path with a crate of lamp oil on my shoulder and the particular expression of a man who has already made up his mind and is hoping nobody asks him to explain it. Below me, the whole island had turned out — or the half of it that mattered, which is to say the half that wanted something from the mainland before the bridge closed and the winter took the crossing away. Farmers with letters. Fishermen with grievances. Children with faces pressed to the railings, watching for fathers coming home on leave, though there hadn't been a leave worth the name since spring.

And standing at the gangway, holding a leather bag and a look of professional patience, was Dr. Ferrand.

He'd been on the island three days, doing his rounds — ears, eyes, chests, the usual indignities of public medicine — and he had found nothing wrong with anyone except, possibly, me. Not in the way doctors usually find things wrong. He'd found something *curious*.

"Keeper Vane," he'd said that morning, pressing his stethoscope against my back in the harbor office while I held my breath like a schoolboy. "You have the lungs of a man twenty years younger."

"Clean living," I said.

"Indeed." He'd taken his time writing in his little book. "It's remarkable, the differences between men. Some smoke themselves to death by forty. Others seem to have been built to a better specification entirely." And he'd looked at me over his spectacles with an interest I did not care for one bit, because interest, in my experience, is how trouble begins. A man can live fifteen years on this rock without anyone being interested in him, and that is precisely the arrangement he wants.

Now Dr. Ferrand stood at the foot of the gangway, and when he saw me on the path above, he raised his hand. Not a wave, exactly. More like the gesture a fisherman makes when he's spotted something in the water he isn't sure he wants to net.

There was no help for it. I went down.

"Last crossing until March," he said, as if I might have forgotten. "You're certain you won't come across? The mainland's got proper coal this year, and I could take a proper look at that shoulder of yours. You favor it when you think no one's watching."

"I'm always watching whether anyone's watching," I said. "Occupational habit."

"Come across anyway. There's a hospital in Saint-Malo with equipment they didn't even have before the war. X-rays. Photographs of your own bones, Vane. It's the modern age."

I set down the oil crate and considered the ferry. She was called the *Petite Adèle*, named after somebody's daughter or somebody's mistress, and she had been making this crossing since before the war, before my war anyway, which is the only one I count. In fair weather she made it in two hours. In foul weather she sometimes made it never, and the island would light candles in the chapel and pretend it wasn't relieved, because a lost ferry meant no tax collector for another month.

The truth was that my shoulder was fine. The truth was that I had not been to the mainland in four years, and the last time had nearly gone badly — a customs officer, a queue, a form asking for particulars I could not supply without committing either fraud or perjury, and a narrow escape achieved only because the officer's supper arrived at the crucial moment. The truth, the whole of it, the part that sat under everything like ballast, was this: every mile of mainland was a mile of questions, and I had spent fifteen years arranging my life so that the questions stayed on the far side of the water.

"I appreciate the offer, Doctor," I said. "But the light doesn't keep itself."

"The light has an assistant mechanism. You told me so yourself. Clockwork, weights — it'll turn itself for six hours unattended."

"It will. And then it will stop, and some trawler full of men who survived the Somme will find out exactly where the rocks are." I picked up the crate again, which ended the conversation about shoulders. "Ask me in spring. If I'm still upright, I'll let you photograph whatever you like."

"You say that every autumn," Ferrand said.

"And yet here we both are, none the worse."

He studied me a moment longer than was comfortable. He had that quality doctors acquire during wars, the ability to look at a person and see straight through to the machinery underneath. Most people see what you show them. Doctors see what you're hiding, and then, mercifully, decide it isn't their department.

"Well," he said at last. "Keep warm. Keep the light. And Vane—" he paused, adjusting his bag on his shoulder, "—if ever there's something you ought to tell a doctor, you know where I am until March. After that, you'll have to write."

"I don't write letters," I said.

"Everyone writes letters eventually." He smiled, climbed the gangplank, and became one more grey figure among the grey figures, and I stood there feeling the peculiar sensation of having dodged a bullet I couldn't quite see.

* * *

The *Petite Adèle* left at half past two, and I watched her go from the end of the pier, which is where I watch most things, being conveniently positioned for observation and poorly positioned for participation.

She backed out of the harbor with a great fussing of steam, and the gulls rose off the water to insult her, and the children on the quay waved until their arms must have ached, and then she rounded the mole and pointed her bow at the mainland, and that was that. Four months. No post but what the navy carried on a whim, no doctor, no market, no news except what the wireless at the garrison deigned to share, filtered through a corporal who believed the war was going splendidly right up until the moment it stopped going anywhere at all.

The island settled around me like a house settling at night. I felt it happen — felt the tension go out of the place, out of myself, the way a rope eases when the load comes off it. All winter, the bridges and ferries would be shut, and the island would be ours alone: three hundred souls, one lighthouse, one inn, one garrison of bored soldiers, and one shipping office flying Corvel's flag over the harbor like a gentleman's handkerchief marking his chair.

Corvel. Even thinking the name put a taste in my mouth, tin and salt. Auguste Corvel, whose barges carried coal and grain and, rumor insisted, considerably less lawful cargo, and whose new concrete warehouse squatted at the harbor mouth like a monument to somebody else's hunger. He'd grown fat on the war the way flies grow fat on a wound, and everyone knew it and nobody said it, because Corvel owned the warehouse, the barges, and — as of this year — the debt against half the fishing fleet.

But that was mainland business, mostly. Island business today was simpler: get the oil home before dark, trim the wicks, log the weather, light the light.

I hoisted the crate and started up the cliff path.

* * *

The lighthouse stands on the north point, ninety-one steps up a spiral stair that I climb twice a day and have climbed for fifteen years, and which has done more for my legs than any doctor could. From the gallery you can see the whole shape of the island: the huddle of the village in its fold of hill, the green fields stitched with stone walls, the long arm of the breakwater, and beyond all of it the open water, going grey and silver and finally black as the afternoon died.

Winter was coming in earnest. You could smell it under the salt — a cold iron smell, the smell of weather gathering itself somewhere out past the horizon and deciding how it meant to spend itself on us. Old Marta at the inn had looked at the sky that morning and said, "Three days, maybe four," in the tone of a woman pronouncing a sentence of imprisonment, and Marta's forecasts were law on this island. She'd once predicted a storm to the hour, and the harbor master still tells the story at every opportunity, adding embellishments, so that in the current version Marta also predicted what the storm would cost and whose roof would suffer, and was right on both counts.

Three days, maybe four. Then the sea would close like a book, and we'd be a story the mainland wouldn't read until spring.

I went through the ritual. Ritual is most of what keeps a lighthouse, and I suspect most of what keeps a man. Oil the mechanism. Check the weights. Trim the wicks — five of them, seated in their carriage like small patient soldiers. Wind the clockwork that turns the lens, that great beehive of crystal and brass, so that all night it will revolve with the patience of a planet and throw its beam out over the water, saying the same thing over and over into the dark: *here is the rock, here is the rock, here is the rock.* Fifteen years of saying it. Sometimes I think the light and I understand each other. Neither of us says anything true, and both of us are always burning.

At dusk I lit the wicks, and the lens caught the flame and multiplied it, and the beam went sweeping out across the water like a slow white arm.

Then I went below to the keeper's room, boiled tea on the little stove, and opened the logbook.

*Wind SW, freshening. Barometer falling steadily since noon. Sea moderate, swell increasing from the west.*

I sat with the pen hovering. There was more to record, really. That the ferry had gone. That the season was closed. That a man had declined a free passage to civilization in favor of ninety-one stairs and a kettle. But the logbook is an official document, read by officials, and officials ask questions, and so the logbook gets the weather and nothing else. All the rest goes in the other book — the one with no official standing whatsoever, the one locked in the drawer beneath the typewriter.

Ah yes. The typewriter.

My wife bought it in Rouen, the year before she died. A Remington, secondhand, with a temperamental capital T and a ribbon she kept re-inking herself with a brush and a bottle, because she said buying new ribbons was a racket. She'd wanted to be a writer, in the way that clever women of that generation wanted things they were permitted to want quietly. She wrote essays nobody published and letters everybody answered, and in the last year, when her hands began to fail her — though we pretended otherwise, both of us, right up until pretending was no longer possible — I typed for her, taking dictation, my fingers clumsy on her keys.

After she died I found I couldn't stop. Every Sunday evening, I sit down at the machine and type her a letter. To her old address, the house in Rouen that belongs to strangers now. I report the weather, the state of the wicks, the gossip of the island — Marta's niece growing like a weed, the garrison corporal's hopeless courtship of the telegraph girl, the price of coal. I sign them *your Odile*, which is a joke of sorts, or a prayer of sorts; the name was hers to give and hers to take back, and she never took it back, so I wear it the way you wear a coat someone left you: gratefully, and slightly too large.

Fifteen years of letters. None sent. A complete correspondence with a dead woman, filed in a locked drawer, and I defy any doctor in France to make sense of that prescription.

That night, after the first watch, I typed to her about the ferry.

*The last boat went today,* I wrote. *The island is shut now, like a fist. You'd laugh — you always said I'd end up on an island, you said I had the temperament of a tidal pool. You were right, as usual, and I resent it, as usual. A doctor offered to photograph my bones. I declined. Some architecture is private.*

The T stuck. I thumped it, the way she used to, and it surrendered.

*I know what you'd say. You'd say isolation isn't the same as safety, Odile. You'd say a man can hide so thoroughly that he disappears even from himself. You'd say it kindly, because you were kind, and it would land like a hammer, because you were also right.*

Outside, the wind had begun to work at the tower, testing the seams the way a burglar tests a window. The barometer was still falling. I logged it, wound the clockwork for the midnight turn, and stood a while at the lantern room glass, watching the beam go out and out and come round again.

Far to the southwest, past the line of the horizon, there was weather. You couldn't see it, but you could feel it, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. Marta's three days would prove optimistic. I'd seen that sky before — in '09, the year the *Sainte Anne* went down with eleven men, the year I learned what the sea does with things it takes, and what it occasionally, carelessly, gives back.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of the swell changing.

A man who keeps a light learns the sea's voices the way a mother learns her child's crying — which cry means hunger, which means anger, which means *come quickly*. This was the third kind. The deep, dragging roar of a heavy sea finding the rocks below the point, a sound like furniture being moved in a giant's house, and it had no business sounding like that tonight, because the storm was still a day out at least.

I went up the stairs two at a time, shoulder protesting, and stood in the lantern room with the wind pulling at my coat.

The swell was running high and wrong, out of the southwest, stacked up ahead of the front. And in the white churn below the rocks, just at the edge of the beam's reach, something pale was turning over and over — not driftwood. Driftwood doesn't flash white in that rhythm. Cloth does. A shirt, a jacket, an arm.

For a long moment I did nothing. That's the honest account, and I've never had the habit of lying in my own head. I stood there and thought: *not my business. Report it to the garrison at daylight. Let soldiers do soldiering.* Because anything that washed ashore brought questions, and questions were the one cargo I had spent fifteen years refusing delivery of.

Then the beam swung round, and the thing in the water turned its face up, and even at that distance, even in that light, I saw that it was young. Younger than the war had any right to leave anyone.

I was on the rocks within ten minutes, rope around my waist, soaked to the ribs, telling myself all the while that this was simple decency and meant nothing more.

The courier's satchel was strapped to him so tightly he must have been ordered never to let it go. He had obeyed the order all the way to the bottom of the sea.

I knelt there in the spray with the bag in my hands, canvas stiff with salt, sealed with wax, stamped with an eagle I recognized and wished I didn't — and above me the light went on turning, saying *here is the rock, here is the rock*, as though warning me, as though it had been trying to warn me for fifteen years and I had finally, finally heard it.

The storm was a day out. Whatever this was, I had three days, maybe four, to decide what to do with it.

I should have burned it on the beach. God knows I considered it, standing there with the waves coming for my boots.

Instead I carried it up all ninety-one steps.
