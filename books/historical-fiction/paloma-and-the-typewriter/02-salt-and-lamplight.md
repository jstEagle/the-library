## 2. Salt and Lamplight

There is a particular hour — I have never managed to name it, though I have lived inside it for fifteen years — when the sea stops being water and becomes a kind of weather of its own. Not the wind's weather, not the sky's. The sea's own private temperament, which it keeps the way a widow keeps her husband's coat: out of habit, out of love, and because throwing it away would require admitting something.

I was in that hour now, standing at the gallery rail with the last of the daylight going grey behind me, watching the water decide what it intended to be tomorrow.

The ferry had gone that morning. I had watched it from this same rail, small and stubborn against the swell, carrying Dr. Ferrand and eleven other souls back to the mainland and the season's closing storms. Ferrand had stood at the gangway with his hat in his hands and said, "Vane, you could come. One winter on the mainland wouldn't kill you."

"Neither will one winter here," I'd said, "but thank you for the offer all the same."

He'd looked at me the way he always looks at me — that physician's look, the one that takes a man's pulse through his collar — and said, "You take care of yourself, keeper," in a tone suggesting he knew precisely how unlikely that instruction was to be followed, and by whom, and in what specific manner.

The ferry went. The island stayed. This is the arrangement, and I am the arrangement's chief beneficiary and only prisoner, which is either a contradiction or the most honest description of my life ever committed to thought.

* * *

Morning ritual, then, since a life must be built from something, and mine is built from these:

Five o'clock, or near enough. The alarm clock Marta gave me three Christmases ago — she refuses to accept that a lighthouse keeper needs no alarm, that the sea wakes me whether I ask it to or not — goes off in the dark, and I get up because the alternative is lying there listening to it, which is worse.

Down the ninety-one steps. I counted them once during an illness and have regretted knowing ever since; there is no dignity in numbering your own spine.

First: the lamp room. The great lens turns above me like something patient and enormous, which is exactly what it is. I check the mercury bath — the lens floats on it, thirteen hundred pounds of glass riding on liquid metal like a saint on a miracle — and I wipe the dust from the lower prism panels with a chamois kept solely for that purpose. The glass must be clean. A smudged prism throws a dark sector, and a dark sector is a lie told to a ship, and ships die of lies.

Second: the wicks. Yes, wicks — the island's light is still oil, still flame, still the old honest work of fire, because the commission has promised electrification every year since 1909 and the commission has the memory of a gull. I trim them with the little scissors that were Élise's. Not lighthouse scissors originally; they were sewing scissors, small and bright-handled, and when I first took up the post I meant to buy proper ones and somehow never did, and now fifteen years have passed and the sewing scissors have trimmed more flame than any cloth they ever cut, and I find I cannot imagine the work without them. They fit my hand. That is the whole of the eulogy.

Third: the log. Weather, wind, visibility, state of the lamp, hours burned, oil remaining. The log does not care about my feelings, which is why I trust it absolutely. A page of the log has never once asked me who I am. It asks only what the wind did, and the wind and I are in full agreement about the value of that question.

Fourth: tea, if the oil stores are checked and the stairs swept and the fog signal tested. Tea last, as a reward. A man should be paid for his day, even if he pays himself.

This is the liturgy. Trim, wipe, log, sweep, light. Fifteen years of it. Some men pray on their knees; I pray on my feet, with a chamois in one hand and Élise's scissors in the other, and if God notices the substitution He has been gracious enough not to mention it.

* * *

The afternoon brought the season's first real cold, the kind that comes off the water sideways and finds the gap between a man's collar and his neck no matter how well he believes he has dressed. I spent it below, in the room I call the study though it is nothing but the old coal store with a desk in it, because a man who lives alone is permitted his fictions and indeed requires several to stay sane.

On the desk: the typewriter.

It was Élise's. A Bar-Lock, black as a beetle, bought secondhand in Rouen the year we married because she declared that a woman who intended to write letters worth reading required a machine worth looking at. She wrote on it for six years — letters to her sister, letters to the newspaper under a man's name because the newspaper would not print a woman's opinion, letters to me left on the kitchen table when we were quarreling, which was her way of winning arguments I hadn't known we were having. She typed the way she did everything: fast, certain, with both elbows out, as though the keys might organize a rebellion if she gave them a moment's slack.

She left it to me. She left everything to me, including, though neither of us planned it, the name I wear now and the silence I keep it in.

Every Sunday I write her a letter on it. Never sent. There is nowhere to send it. This is not a metaphor; I want to be clear about that, because I am a precise man and precision is the last virtue I have retained intact. There is simply no address. She is dead nine years, and the dead do not take delivery, and yet the letters pile up in the drawer the way snow piles against a door — quietly, drift by drift, until opening it becomes a decision.

Today being Sunday, I sat down and rolled in a fresh sheet.

*My dear Élise,*

*The gulls are fighting over the midden again, which means the herring boats came in late and dirty. Old Perrin lost a net and blamed the tide, the government, and the Germans, in ascending order of conviction.*

*I have decided not to tell you about the doctor. You would only laugh at me, and say that a man who chooses a rock over a warm room has no business complaining of the cold, and you would be right, and I would sulk, and the letter would go on too long. So instead I will tell you that the light turned four thousand and some nights this week without a stutter, and that I have begun talking to the lens, and that the lens has begun, I fear, to answer.*

*The village asked after me at market. Marta sent up bread I did not order and cannot pay for, which is her way of adopting people without the paperwork. Her niece — Paloma, the little one with the fierce eyes — waved at me from the harbor wall and shouted that my tower looks like a chess piece. I said it was the king. She said kings get captured. I have been thinking about that for two days, which tells you the caliber of conversation available to me.*

*I miss you. I know I write that every week and I know you would tell me that missing someone is not an occupation. But it is the only trade I was left with, and I have become, if I may flatter myself, quite skilled.*

*Your Odile*

I stopped there, hands on the keys, because the name always stops me. *Your Odile.* Three syllables I chose out of a list of names the way a man chooses a coat — does it fit, does it hang right, can I move in it? — and fifteen years later it fits so well I sometimes forget there is skin underneath.

Élise chose it. We had sat up all night with a bottle of cheap brandy and a gazetteer, striking names off like generals striking targets. Too common. Too rare. Sounds wrong in a fisherman's mouth. And then she said *Odile* — her grandmother's name, a woman who had run a boatyard single-handed after her husband drowned, whom the whole coast had called *Monsieur Odile* to her face for forty years and nobody had ever once meant it as an insult — and we looked at each other across the table and it was done before the candle burned down.

"You'll be a plain man," she said, "with a plain name and a plain job, and you'll be so happy it will be disgusting."

She was nearly right on all three counts.

* * *

Evening. I climbed the tower for the lighting — the small ceremony of it, match to wick, the flame taking hold with that soft intake of breath fire makes, the lens gathering it up and turning it into something a sailor forty miles out will read as a promise. Every night the same. Every night a vow renewed with no witness but the dark.

From the gallery, the village showed its few lights below: the inn's windows gold and crowded-looking, the harbor master's lamp, the telegraph office shuttered and blind. Smoke stood straight up from Marta's chimney, which meant the wind had dropped, which meant tomorrow's weather was lying in wait somewhere over the horizon, gathering its argument.

I stood there longer than the work required. This is also part of the ritual, though it appears in no manual: the standing. Looking at the water until the water looks back.

Nine years ago I scattered her here. Not her ashes — she wanted the mainland churchyard, near her mother, and got them — but something of her, some handful of the person, given to this sea because she loved it and because I could not bear to keep all of her on land where I might trip over the grief daily. The sea took the offering and gives back, nightly, the sound of her name in the waves. Or I hear it that way. A solitary man's ear is an unreliable instrument, and I have long since stopped calibrating it.

The light swept out over the water, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere out there — beyond the farthest reach of my beam, past the shoals, past the closing storms — the world was ending a war. They said it would be signed soon, this armistice; the papers that reached us were weeks old and read like dispatches from the moon. Men were coming home. Bridges were being rebuilt. Contracts were being drawn, fortunes made and unmade, history written in rooms I would never see.

And here was I, trimming wicks with sewing scissors, writing letters to a dead woman, keeping a light burning for ships that mostly weren't coming, on an island that the season was about to seal shut like a jar.

Peace, I thought, is for other people. My war ended nine years ago, and I lost, and the terms of surrender were these: the tower, the log, the scissors, the unsent letters, the name that fits like a coat over skin. It is not a bad peace, as surrenders go. I have made my accommodation with it. I have even, on good days, mistaken it for happiness.

Below me, the sea changed its mind about something.

I felt it before I heard it — a new weight in the water, a shift in the sound against the rocks, the way a house shifts when a stranger enters it. The barometer in the lamp room had begun to fall that afternoon, slowly, then less slowly, and I had logged it without alarm, because falling glass in November is merely November doing its work.

But now the wind arrived out of the southeast with intent behind it, and the first spits of rain came flat and hard against the gallery glass, and the water below the tower — the black water, the water I know the way other men know their own handwriting — began to lift itself in long white-maned shoulders and hurl them at the rocks.

A storm. A proper one, by the feel of it, the kind that closes the season and seals the strait and makes the island a fist around whatever it holds.

I went down the ninety-one steps two at a time, checked the moorings on the oil store, shuttered the study window over the typewriter — *goodnight, Élise; tomorrow, I promise* — and climbed back up to keep the watch, because that is the arrangement, and the arrangement does not pause for weather.

All that night the storm built itself, and I fed the light and logged the wind and told myself the familiar lie that a keeper's duties end at the edge of his beam.

Somewhere out in that black rising water, the sea was already deciding what it intended to give back.

I would not learn until morning how much.
