## 3. What the Sea Gave Back

The storm did not end. It merely changed its mind about which direction it meant to kill me from.

By dawn the wind had swung to the northwest, and the rain came in flat and sideways, like something thrown rather than fallen. I had been on my feet since two o'clock, feeding the lamp its oil, wiping salt spray from the lantern glass with a rag gone stiff as sailcloth, logging the barometer's slow collapse into a number so low I wrote it twice to be sure I hadn't imagined it. The glass had been falling for three days. The sea knew before the instruments did. The sea always knows first.

At half past six, with the light still burning against a sky the color of wet slate, I saw the gulls.

You learn to read gulls the way a doctor reads a face. Gulls that circle and scream are gulls at work; gulls that gather low over one fixed point of water or rock and will not leave are gulls at a feast. These were the second kind. Forty of them, perhaps more, hunched and quarrelsome above the black teeth of the rocks below the tower, rising and settling, rising and settling, patient as creditors.

I stood at the gallery rail with the wind trying to peel my coat off my back and told myself every reasonable lie available to a man in my position. A seal, dead of the storm. Driftwood. A cargo bollard off some unfortunate deck. Gulls are not particular, and neither is the sea, and neither, if he was wise, was I.

But I had already begun counting the steps.

Ninety-one down, as always, though this time I took them slowly, because the rocks were running with water and the wind made a joke of balance. At the bottom I crouched behind the shoulder of granite that shelters the landing stage from the worst of the swell, and there the sea gave back what it had taken somewhere out in the dark — though taken from whom, and where, and why, I would spend the rest of the day learning.

He lay wedged in a crevice between two slabs of rock, on his back, one arm flung up over his face as though shielding his eyes from a sun that had never come. He was young. That was the first thing, and it was the thing that stayed. Twenty years old, perhaps twenty-two. His coat had been good once — courier grey, double-breasted, brass buttons gone green at the seams — and the sea had worried it half off him, along with most of whatever else a man carries when he is carrying something important enough to drown for.

His boots were gone. His left hand was swollen white as candle wax. There was a cut above his eyebrow that the gulls had found, and I drove them off with a length of driftwood and language I had not used aloud in fifteen years, and they lifted, circled, and forgave me instantly, because gulls hold no grudges and no opinions, which is more than can be said for men.

I knelt there in the wash with the cold coming up through my knees and looked at his face, and I thought: someone taught you to write. Someone fed you breakfast once, in a kitchen somewhere, and told you to mind the roads and keep the papers dry.

Keep the papers dry.

It arrived in my head with the simplicity of all terrible thoughts, and I looked at him properly then, at the strap across his chest where a satchel should have been, and found the strap broken — snapped clean, not worn through — and looked beyond him to where the surge kept throwing itself against the inner pool between the rocks, and saw, tumbling there in the foam like something playful, a shape of dark leather turning over and over.

* * *

Getting it was a fool's errand, and I am by profession a careful man. The pool filled and emptied with each swell, and the satchel rose and sank in it, and twice I reached and missed and was nearly taken myself, hauled to the hips in water that gripped like a drowning man's hand. On the third try I got a wrist through the strap and dragged it out onto the higher ledge, and sat there afterward, gasping, an old man playing tug-of-war with the Atlantic, while the boy watched me from his crevice with his arm still flung across his eyes.

The satchel was heavier than it looked. Military pattern, oilskin flap, a brass clasp crusted already with salt. And stamped into the leather beneath the flap, half worn but legible even through the grime of the sea:

C.C. & SONS — SHIPPING & FORWARDING — CORVEL LINE

I remember thinking, absurdly: well, that's convenient. Even the sea files its paperwork.

I did not open it on the rocks. Some instinct older than caution made me wait, made me wrap the strap twice around my fist and climb back up the ninety-one steps with the thing knocking against my hip like a second heart. I paused once at the top and looked down. The gulls were settling again. From the height of the gallery he was very small, the boy, a pale comma caught in black stone, and I understood that I could not bury him — the ground was too hard and the tide owned those rocks twice a day — but I could log him. It was the only rite I had. So I went inside, dried my hands, opened the logbook, and wrote, in a keeper's hand that has never once trembled in fifteen years of storms:

*0640. Body of a man recovered below light. Courier's dress. Age approx. 20. No papers on person.*

No papers on person. I sat looking at that sentence for a long time, because it was true, and because it was also the first lie the satchel and I had told together, and we would tell a great many more before the week was out.

* * *

I set it on the table in the study, next to the typewriter, and hung my coat to drip, and built up the stove fire, and then I did what any sensible man does with a thing he does not want: I made tea first.

There is a discipline to dread. You do not rush toward it. You warm the pot, you measure the leaves, you let the water come properly to the boil, and you tell yourself that the packet has waited in the sea for God knows how long and will keep another ten minutes for the kettle. Élise used to laugh at me for this — *you'd make tea at your own execution, Odile, and apologize to the headsman for the mess* — and she was right, and the memory of her laughing arrived unasked, as it does, and I let it sit with me at the table like a third presence while the storm leaned on the windows.

Then I undid the clasp.

Inside: oilcloth, folded twice and tied with tarred string, and beneath that a leather wallet, and inside the wallet, protected from the Atlantic by layers a drowned man must have trusted with his life, papers. Three documents. I spread them on the table with hands that had finally stopped pretending to be steady.

The first was a shipping manifest, Corvel Line letterhead, listing relief cargo bound for the mainland — grain, coal, medical stores — dated the week after the bridge went down. The tonnage figures had been altered. I am a lighthouse keeper, not an accountant, but even I could see that the same cargo appeared three times under three dates, as though a shipload of flour could be delivered, delivered, and delivered again, and billed each time. War feeds men like Corvel whether or not any food reaches the war.

The second was a letter, unsigned, on garrison stationery, thanking an unnamed correspondent for "the delay achieved" and confirming that "the northern span repairs cannot proceed until spring, per arrangement." Per arrangement. The bridge had not failed. The bridge had been *arranged*. The storm season closing the island off from the mainland, the relief ships delayed, the contracts inflated fourfold — none of it weather. All of it arithmetic, done in other people's blood.

And the third —

The third was sealed orders, courier's orders, in a fine clerk's hand, directing the bearer to proceed from the island to the mainland commission at Verrac with all haste, bearing evidence gathered by persons unknown, and warning him — in a line underlined twice — that the Corvel interest had agents on the island, that the harbor master's loyalty had been purchased, and that the bearer should trust no official of any rank until the papers were in the commission's own hands.

Trust no official. I read it three times. Outside, the wind found some new register of fury and the whole tower hummed with it, a held note, like a struck glass.

The boy had been carrying proof. Proof against Corvel, gathered by someone brave or desperate enough to collect it, entrusted to someone young enough to believe that a fast boat and a straight back would be sufficient. And the sea had taken the boat, and the boy, and then — with the blind generosity of the sea — had carried both the messenger and the message to the one door on this coast where a man lived who owed nothing to anyone and was known by no one who mattered.

Or so I told myself. But the orders said otherwise. Agents on the island. The harbor master bought. And a keeper alone on a rock, whose light the whole channel depended on, whose comings and goings were logged and known — I was not invisible. I had only ever been overlooked.

I sat back. The tea had gone cold. The papers lay on my table like something spilled that could not be swept up.

Here is what I decided in the next quarter hour, and I record it here because shame insists on being recorded honestly: I decided to burn them.

It was not cowardice. I want that written plainly, even if no one reads it. It was arithmetic, the same cold arithmetic Corvel himself no doubt used. I was one man, old enough to be that boy's father, living under a name that was itself a forgery fifteen years deep. If the papers surfaced from my keeping, the first question anyone asked would be *who gave them to the keeper*, and the answer would unravel me down to the bare thread of the person I had been before Odile Vane existed. Everything Élise and I had built — the silence, the distance, the beautiful safe arrangement of my small unwitnessed life — would burn with them. For a dead boy who could not be helped. For strangers on the mainland I would never meet. For a war that had already eaten everything it wanted.

I took the papers to the stove. I opened the iron door. The fire was good and hot, and it reached for the oilcloth almost eagerly, and I stood there holding the documents above the flames, and I would have done it. I believe that. I would have done it, and slept that night, and kept the light, and told myself forever after that the sea decides these things, not men.

But when I turned from the stove, he was in the room with me.

Not his ghost — I don't hold with ghosts, and in any case he hadn't earned one yet; ghosts require unfinished business, and his business was sitting in my hands. It was simpler and worse than a ghost. It was his face. The arm flung over the eyes. The good coat ruined. Twenty years old, someone's breakfast, someone's *mind the roads*, sent out with a strap across his chest and an underline twice: trust no one. He had drowned within sight, probably, of the light I keep. My light had burned all night over the water that killed him, steady and faithful and perfectly useless.

A light is not a witness. I have said that to myself for fifteen years, and it is the other familiar lie, and standing there with the fire breathing at my knuckles I finally heard it for what it was.

I shut the stove door. I carried the papers back to the table. And because a man must do something with his hands when he has just declined to burn a piece of the truth, I sat down at Élise's typewriter and rolled in a fresh sheet and wrote, without deciding to:

*Received from the sea, 9 November: one courier, name unknown, and his errand. Errand intact. Bearer willing.*

I looked at that last line a long time. Bearer willing. As though willingness were a fact one could simply type into existence. The wind hammered the shutter, and somewhere below, in the black churn past the rocks, the sea went on sorting through its holdings, patient as a creditor, and I understood that I had just become part of somebody's cargo — and that the route from this rock to Verrac ran through three checkpoints, three pairs of eyes, three people who each knew a different version of the man called Odile Vane.

The harbor master's office opens at nine.

It was then twenty past eight.
