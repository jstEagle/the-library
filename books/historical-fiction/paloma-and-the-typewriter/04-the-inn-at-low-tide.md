## 4. The Inn at Low Tide

The squall came off the water like something with a grudge. I had watched it build all afternoon from the gallery — a bruise-colored wall dragging itself up the strait — and I had told myself, as I told myself every year, that the walk to the village and back could be made ahead of it. I needed lamp oil, a new file for the wick trimmers, and salt, because the sea takes salt from me and I have never understood why a man surrounded by the stuff should run short of it.

I was halfway down the cliff path when it caught me.

There is a particular honesty in weather like that. It does not negotiate. It took my hat first, then my dignity, then any pretense that I might make the return trip before dark with dry clothes and a settled temper. By the time I reached the harbor road I was soaked through to the skin and the wind was pushing me sideways along the stones like a drunk being shown out of a church.

The village of Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer consists of one street, a church, a fish shed, and the inn. The inn is called the Marée Basse — Low Tide — which is either a joke or a prayer, depending on how the fishing has gone. Its windows were lit amber against all that gray, and smoke stood straight up from its chimney for about four feet before the wind shredded it, and I will confess here what I would not confess to anyone on that island: I did not decide to go in. My feet decided. The rest of me simply followed them, the way a drowning man follows a rope without asking who tied it.

I told myself I would stand in the doorway just long enough to stop shaking, then go back out into it. Fifteen years I had kept myself apart from that room. Fifteen years of buying what I needed at market with my collar up and my answers short. The lighthouse gives you a perfect excuse for solitude — people assume keepers are strange, half-salted, married to the work — and I had built a whole life inside that assumption the way a hermit crab builds inside a borrowed shell.

I pushed open the door and the warmth hit me like a wave of its own.

* * *

The inn's common room is low-ceilinged and smells of woodsmoke, tallow, and the ghost of a thousand stews. There were perhaps eight people in it, which on a night like this constituted a crowd: two fishermen playing cards with the grim concentration of men losing money, the priest drinking something he'd deny at confession, and behind the bar a woman built like a ship's keel, broad and necessary, polishing a glass with the ferocity of someone punishing it for existing.

Marta Guilbert. Everyone on the island knew Marta. Widow of the *Pélican*, lost with three others in '16, mother of a son who hadn't come home from the war and didn't write anymore — though she never said that last part aloud; the island said it for her, quietly, in kitchens. She ran the inn the way she presumably ran everything: as if disorder were a personal insult.

She looked up when I came in, and I watched recognition move across her face like weather.

"The keeper," she said. Not a question. On this island I am only ever the keeper, and I have spent fifteen years making sure that is all I am.

"Marta." I dripped on her floor. "I'll trouble you for a bottle of oil and be out of your way."

"You'll trouble me for nothing of the kind." She set down the glass. "Look at yourself. You're dripping on my floor like a landed cod."

"I'm fine."

"You are not fine. Nobody who says they're fine while standing in a puddle of their own making is fine." She was already moving toward the kitchen door. "Tomas! Firewood. And put the screen up, the wind's coming through the door like a bill collector."

From somewhere behind the bar, a chair scraped. A man rose — tall, hollow-cheeked, with the particular stillness of someone who has learned that sudden movement attracts attention. He wore no uniform; the army had taken that back, or he had left it folded somewhere. But he stood the way soldiers stand even after the soldiering stops, feet slightly apart, weight forward, ready for nothing in particular and everything in general.

That was Tomas. I knew him by sight the way everyone did — the veteran who swept the garrison floor in exchange for his bread, who flinched at doors slamming and sat always facing the window. People said he'd been buried alive at Verdun for two days. People said a lot of things. What I noticed, that first night, was that he looked at me not with curiosity but with recognition — the look of one quiet creature acknowledging another across a crowded field.

He brought the wood without a word, fed the fire until it roared, and went back to his corner. He never asked my name. I understood later that this wasn't rudeness. Names cost him something. He hoarded his own the same way I hoarded mine.

"Now sit," Marta said, reappearing with a towel she thrust at my chest like a summons. "And don't argue, keeper, because I've fed stubborn men before and I've buried some of them too, and the difference between the two outcomes was usually whether they ate."

"I couldn't—"

"You can and you will." She pointed at a table near the fire. "Sit."

And here is the thing about Marta Guilbert: she does not ask. Asking gives a person the chance to refuse, and refusal is a door she apparently refuses to recognize. I sat. I dried my hair. And within four minutes a bowl of stew was in front of me — mutton, turnips, barley, bread still steaming — and a cup of something hot and bitter that she called coffee the way the war called a trench a defensive position.

I want to say I resisted longer than I did. The truth is the smell undid me. A man who cooks for himself eats to stay alive; a man who eats alone does it standing up, over the sink, the way I'd done it for fifteen years. This was food prepared by hands that expected someone else to taste it, and my body surrendered to that fact before my mind could lodge an objection.

"Slowly," Marta said, passing behind me. "You eat like the bowl owes you money."

"It's very good," I managed.

"I know it's very good. Flattery gets you more bread." And there was more bread.

* * *

It was while I was on the second bowl that I became aware of being watched.

Not by Tomas, who watched everything. By a girl. She was sitting on the stairs, twelve years old maybe, with dark hair cut bluntly at the jaw — cut by someone in a hurry, probably herself — and eyes so steadily gray they seemed less like a child's eyes than like small harbors where ships waited for permission. She had a book upside-down in her lap, which I suspect was camouflage rather than reading.

She stared at me with the unembarrassed total attention that only children and cats can manage, and I found, to my alarm, that I minded less than I should have.

"Paloma," Marta said, without turning around, "if you're going to stare at our guest, come and stare at him where I can see you doing it."

"He's the lighthouse man," Paloma said, sliding down the banister and landing with the soundless efficiency of someone who has done it many times against explicit instruction. She planted herself at the end of my bench. "You light the big lamp. Do you live all alone up there?"

"I do."

"With nobody?"

"With the lamp."

"That's not a person." She considered this with visible seriousness. "Do you talk to it anyway?"

I opened my mouth to give the answer I gave everyone — the flat, deflecting answer, *the work keeps me busy* — and what came out instead, God knows why, was: "Sometimes it talks back."

Her whole face changed. Adults lie to children politely; children know the difference in their bones, and she knew I hadn't lied. "What does it say?"

"Mostly that I've missed a spot of rust."

She laughed — a real laugh, sudden and loud enough that the card players glanced over — and something in my chest did a thing I did not authorize it to do.

"Paloma is my sister's daughter," Marta said from the bar, in the tone of a woman laying down facts like fence posts. "My sister and her husband drowned when the ferry went down in '13. Paloma's been mine since." A pause, the length of a held breath. "The sea takes what it likes on this island. We just try to raise what's left."

Paloma, entirely unbothered by this accounting of her history, leaned toward my stew. "Can I have the crust?"

"You may have anything on this island except my wick trimmers," I said, and slid the plate toward her, and she took the crust with the gravity of a treaty signing.

* * *

The storm worsened as the evening wore on. The shutters boomed at intervals like slow artillery, and each boom moved Tomas slightly, a twitch at the shoulder he'd smooth away before anyone was meant to see it. I saw it. Of course I saw it. Recognizing the architecture of damage in another person is a specialty of the damaged.

Around nine, the lights guttered — the wind finding some flaw in the chimney — and in the recovered darkness I heard Tomas speak for the first time. His voice was rusty, a hinge long unoiled.

"The light's still burning," he said. "Through the window. You can see it turning."

Everyone looked. Through the diamond panes, far up the black headland, the beam swung its patient arc — once, twice — the only fixed thing on a coast gone to chaos. My light. My work. And absurdly, seeing it from the outside, from a warm room full of people, I felt something adjacent to pride, and something adjacent to grief, because the light was the only thing on this island that knew me completely and it could tell no one.

"Every night," Paloma said softly. "Even in storms like this. Especially in storms."

"Especially in storms," I agreed.

"That must be lonely."

"It's a job."

"That's not an answer," she said, with the terrible precision of the young, and Marta barked a laugh from the bar and said, "Leave the man his mysteries, chick, he's barely eaten two bowls," and the moment passed, but it did not pass cleanly. It snagged. It left thread hanging.

* * *

When the storm eased toward midnight into something merely rude rather than murderous, I rose to go.

Marta appeared in front of the door as if she had been waiting behind it for an hour, arms crossed. "You'll not walk the cliff path tonight. Half of it will be in the sea by now."

"I've walked it in worse."

"I doubt that very much. And even if it's true, you'll do it tomorrow, in daylight, when the path hasn't been trying to kill fishermen all evening." She jerked her chin upward. "There's the loft room. It's dry. It smells of apples, because it's over the storeroom, and if that offends you I'll apologize to the apples."

"Marta, truly, I can't put you to—"

"You're not putting me to anything. You're putting a body in an empty bed that's been empty since August, and the bed doesn't complain, and neither will I." Her voice gentled by a degree, which from Marta was roughly equivalent to other people weeping. "Keeper. You look like a man who hasn't slept under a roof that isn't his own in a very long time. Take the roof. That's all it costs. Nothing else."

I stood there with wet boots and a full stomach and fifteen years of careful distance, and I searched for the argument that would let me refuse gracefully, and I could not find it, because there wasn't one. That was her genius, I began to understand. She didn't defeat refusals. She arranged the world so that refusals had nowhere to stand.

"The loft, then," I said. "One night."

"One night," she agreed, in the tone of a woman agreeing to nothing of the kind.

Tomas carried a candle up for me without being asked. At the loft door he paused, and looked at me, and said — quietly, as if reporting to a superior — "You kept the light through all of it. Every hour." A pause. "Good."

Then he was gone down the stairs, and I lay in the apple-smelling dark listening to the wind worry the eaves, and I conducted my usual audit.

Nothing given away, I told myself. A meal, a bed, a child's laughter. I'd pay for the oil and the board in the morning and be gone, back to the rock and the routine, back behind the glass where the world resolves into weather and intervals and the clean arithmetic of a lamp. They were kind because kindness is Marta's native language, not because they'd seen anything. I was still a stranger. Strangers leave. That is the entire value of strangers.

But underneath the audit, quieter, the truth filed its report: the stew had tasted like a memory of a house I'd had once, with a woman who fed people exactly the way Marta did — by ambush, without receipts. And the girl with the harbor-gray eyes had asked me what the lamp says, and I had answered her honestly, honestly, for the first time in years, and the sky above the headland had not fallen.

I slept better than I had intended to. That frightened me more than the storm had.

* * *

I came down at dawn intending to settle accounts and vanish. Instead I found the common room already awake, Marta hammering dough as though it owed her money, Paloma setting cups with ceremonial precision, and on the table, beside my untouched coffee, a folded copy of the island's thin news sheet — the one that came over on the ferry when the ferry came.

Marta nodded at it without stopping her work. "Before you go. Page two."

I opened it. The item was short. A courier lost overboard from the mail packet in Tuesday's blow, it said. Body not recovered. And beneath it, a notice from Corvel Shipping, printed in bold type: a reward, a considerable reward, for recovery of "official documents believed lost in the incident." All information to be directed to the harbor master.

I read it twice. The paper felt heavier than paper should.

"Odd," Marta said, punching the dough. "A shipping firm paying rewards for government papers. That's not charity. Charity doesn't print notices." She glanced up, flour to her elbows, and her eyes — the same harbor-gray as the girl's — rested on me a moment too long. "You were out on the rocks Tuesday night, weren't you, keeper? The whole point was blown clear across the island."

The wind rattled the shutters. Paloma stopped setting cups.

"Yes," I said carefully. "I heard the sea working the rocks all night."

"Mm," said Marta, and turned back to her dough, and let the silence sit there between us like a fourth person at the table — patient, unhurried, absolutely certain that whatever I was carrying, sooner or later I would set it down.
