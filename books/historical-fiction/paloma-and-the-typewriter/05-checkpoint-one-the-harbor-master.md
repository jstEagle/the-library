## 5. Checkpoint One: The Harbor Master

The harbor master's office smelled of tar, cold coffee, and the particular staleness of a man who had been arguing with a ledger since dawn. I knew the smell. I'd been smelling it for fifteen years, every time the weather forced me down from the light and into the village for lamp oil, flour, or the occasional act of cowardice disguised as errands.

Bram Voss sat behind his desk like a man who had grown into it the way a tree grows around a fence post. He was broad once; now he was mostly desk. His beard had gone the color of old rope, and his hands — hands that had once hauled my wife out of a squall off the point when her dinghy swamped, hands she'd laughed about for weeks — were currently occupied with a pencil he was chewing like it owed him money.

"Keeper," he said, without looking up. "Storm took your rail again."

"Took the rail, left the rest. I call that restraint."

That got a grunt out of him, which from Bram was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation. He set down the pencil and looked at me, and I saw immediately — the way you see weather coming over water before you can name it — that something in his face had been rearranged since the last time we'd spoken. Something careful had moved in behind his eyes.

"You'll want kerosene," he said.

"I'll want kerosene."

"And you'll sign for it, same as always." He pulled the book toward himself, licked his thumb, found the page. "Sign there."

I signed. *E. Vane*, keeper of the light, in the same hand I'd been signing for fifteen years — a hand I had built myself, letter by letter, until it stopped being a disguise and started being mine. There is a strange freedom in forging yourself. Nobody checks the signature of a man they've watched sign a thousand times.

Bram didn't look at the signature. That was the first wrong thing. Bram always looked at signatures; he was a harbor master the way some men are priests, with a genuine faith in paperwork.

* * *

I want to be fair to Bram here, because what came next was not his fault, not entirely, and because a man who lies well has an obligation to be honest about the people he lies to.

He filled the kerosene can himself rather than ringing for the boy. Another wrong thing. The boy fetched; Bram chewed pencils. He carried the can to the counter, set it down, and then stood there with both hands flat on the wood like a man bracing against a swell.

"Coffee?" he said.

"Bram. You've never offered me coffee in fifteen years. Your coffee is a war crime even by island standards."

"It's worse than usual today." And there it was — the ghost of the old grin, the one Marguerite used to fish out of him on the long winter evenings when the three of us played cards and she cheated so flagrantly that losing to her felt like a compliment. "Sit down anyway."

I sat down anyway. This was my second mistake of the morning, though I wouldn't know it until later, and even then only partially. The first mistake was walking through his door at all. But storms close roads, and kerosene doesn't fetch itself, and a lighthouse keeper who runs out of light is just a tall man in a tower shouting at ships.

He poured two cups. The coffee was, as promised, worse than usual — it had achieved a kind of bitterness usually reserved for sermons — and I drank it because refusing would have told him more than any question could.

"Marta fed you yet?" he asked.

"The inn feeds everyone. It's how Marta wages war on loneliness."

"She's winning, too." He turned his cup a slow half-circle on the desk. Outside, the harbor slapped at its pilings, gray and restless, the boats hauled up and wrapped down for the closing of the season. "You heard about the courier."

I kept my face where I keep everything: nowhere. "Courier?"

"Ferry brought mail Tuesday, last run before the bridge closes. One of the bags went overboard crossing the bar — or that's the story. Man came ashore after it in a skiff against advice. Found him Wednesday morning on the rocks under your light."

"Under my light," I said. "I'd have seen him."

"You'd have seen him if he came ashore alive. He didn't." Bram said it flatly, but his eyes weren't flat at all. They were doing arithmetic. "Corvel's office posted a notice yesterday. Reward for anything washed up belonging to the shipping line. Papers, documents, correspondence. 'Lost in transit.' Their words."

"How much?"

"Enough to buy a boat. Enough to buy several boats." He leaned back, and the chair complained about it, and neither of us acknowledged the complaint, which is the foundation of all friendship. "Odd thing, Keeper. Corvel's never cared about this island except as a line item. Now his men are walking the tideline like gulls."

"Maybe he's sentimental."

"Maybe." He looked at me over the rim of his cup. "Anything wash up on your stretch?"

And here is the thing I will say plainly, because the whole business deserves plainness: I lied to Bram Voss without a single hitch in my breath, and I have thought about that every day since.

"No," I said. "Nothing but the usual. Driftwood, a crate of oranges gone bad, one boot. The sea's been generous with boots this year."

It came out smooth. It came out easy. It came out with the small dry humor he expected from me, delivered at the right tempo, with the right shrug, and I watched it land and do its work, and Bram nodded and believed me — believed me maybe ninety percent, which is as much as anyone believes anyone — and the lie cost me nothing at all in the moment.

That was what frightened me. Not the lying. I'd been lying for fifteen years; lying was load-bearing in my life, part of the architecture. What frightened me was the *ease*. The way the false words arrived already dressed, already shod, ready to walk. Fifteen years ago, lying had been a desperate craft I learned at night, alone, practicing in a mirror. Now it was reflex. Somewhere along the way I had become so good at being no one that the skill had spread into everything else I did.

A man should feel resistance when he betrays an old friend. I felt none. I drank his terrible coffee and let him believe me, and I catalogued the fact of my own smoothness the way I'd catalogue a crack in the lens housing: noted, measured, to be dealt with.

* * *

"Marguerite would've known what you're hiding," Bram said suddenly.

The name went through me the way cold water goes through canvas. Slowly at first, then all at once.

"That's a strange thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say." He wasn't accusing. That was the worst of it — he said it with something close to tenderness, the way you'd tell a man his fly was undone. "She could look at a person for three seconds and know their whole ledger. You've got her look about you this morning, Keeper. The one she got when she was keeping something from me and enjoying it."

"Marguerite's been dead eleven years, Bram."

"I know exactly how long she's been dead. I count it the way you count tides." He stood, joints announcing themselves one by one, and carried our cups to the basin. His back was to me when he said the rest. "There's a man at the garrison asking after the courier too. Not army. Civilian clothes, soft hands, mainland accent. Been to see me twice. Second time he asked whether the keeper of the light had reported finding anything on his beach."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him you report everything, same as the regulations say. Told him if you'd found papers, they'd be logged." He turned around. "Then I came home and couldn't sleep, because I remembered that you log what you choose to log, Emeric, and you've chosen to log very little for a great many years."

He used the name. My name — the real one, the buried one — and he said it quietly, almost gently, the way you'd set something fragile down on a table, and for one long moment the office was nothing but the sound of the harbor working at its pilings.

I had not heard that name spoken aloud since the day we put it in the ground. Hearing it was like hearing a door open in a house you'd sealed shut. Every draft in my body came through it at once.

"Bram—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "I don't want to know. Whatever it is you are besides a keeper, it's kept this island's light burning for fifteen years without a missed night, and that's more character than most men manage with less to hide. I'm not asking. I'm telling you what I told the soft-handed gentleman, and why." He came back around the desk and sat, heavily, an old man again. "Because Marguerite loved you. However she loved you — and I never needed the details, she was her own country and I was lucky to trade with her — she chose you. So whatever you're carrying, you carry it carefully. Because that man from Corvel's office isn't looking for papers, Keeper. He's looking for a person. And he's very good at looking."

* * *

I walked home along the cliff path with the kerosene can knocking against my leg and the wind pushing at my shoulders like a crowd with opinions, and I made myself think it through properly, because fear is useless until you've turned it into a list.

One: the courier had come ashore dead below my light, and I had taken his satchel, and the satchel held papers that named Corvel as the architect of the bridge sabotage. Two: Corvel's people knew the satchel existed — otherwise why flood the tideline with searchers and rewards? Three: they did not know where it was, or I would already have had visitors at the light, and soldiers don't knock. Four: Bram knew I was hiding something. Not what — he'd refused the what, bless his rope-colored beard — but the shape of it. Which meant the island's first checkpoint had quietly decided to become a blind spot instead of a gate, and had done it out of loyalty to a woman eleven years dead.

I stopped at the top of the path and looked back at the harbor. Gray water, gray sky, the boats sleeping under their wraps. A small island at the edge of a large war, about to be closed off by storm and bridge both, sealed like a jar.

Corvel had reached here already. His soft hands were in the garrison, his reward notices were on the harbor wall, his questions had reached the desk of a man who'd known my wife's laugh. The reach of a rich man is faster than any ferry. Money crosses water without getting wet.

And I understood, standing there with the wind trying to take my hat and my balance both, that the choice I'd been carrying since the night the sea gave the courier back had quietly finished making itself. I could burn the papers. I had wanted to burn them; I had stood at the stove with them in my hand and the flame licking up, and only the drowned man's face — young, ordinary, salt-pale, someone's son — had stayed my hand. Burning them would be safe. Burning them would also make me complicit, a keeper who watched a ship break and logged clear weather.

Or I could move them. Through the harbor master, who might already be bought or might be pretending to be bought — I genuinely couldn't tell anymore, and that uncertainty was itself a kind of wound. Through the garrison, where soft-handed civilians asked questions. Through the telegraph office, where every wire out of this island passed under eyes I hadn't counted yet. Three checkpoints, three doors, and behind each door someone who knew me by a different weight of truth.

Fifteen years I had spent learning how to be invisible. It occurred to me, with something between horror and laughter, that the papers in my floorboard cache required precisely the opposite skill. They required a man who could be *seen* — seen passing, seen signing, seen drinking terrible coffee — without ever being caught. Visibility as camouflage. The one trick I'd never rehearsed.

The wind shoved me again, impatient, and I let it turn me toward the light. The tower stood above the point with its gallery wet and gleaming, faithful as ever, asking nothing. Tonight I'd trim the wicks, log the weather, write the letter I never send. Tomorrow I'd start figuring out how a ghost carries cargo.

Behind me, down in the village, I could just make out a figure walking the tideline with a stick, turning over whatever the sea surrendered. Soft hands, probably. Patient as gulls.

Let him turn every stone on the beach. The thing he wanted was already dry, already hidden, already awake inside me like a second heartbeat — and I had just discovered that the price of keeping it was going to be paid in the only coin I had left: the truth about who I was, spent piece by piece, at each door, until one of them opened wide enough to let the light through.

I picked up the kerosene and climbed.
