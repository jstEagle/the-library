## 6. Paloma

The trouble with being twelve is that everyone assumes you are either a fool or furniture, and usually both at once. I had spent my whole life — all twelve years of it, which I grant you is not an empire's worth — learning to be invisible in the useful way. Aunt Marta thought I was asleep. Tomas thought I was reading. The garrison boys thought I was nobody's business. And the lighthouse keeper — Mr. Vane, who was the only person on this island I had ever wanted to *be* rather than merely know — thought I was a child who fetched bread and asked too many questions about lamps.

I was not asleep. I was not reading. And I had decided, somewhere between the second helping of barley soup and the moment he pulled his coat collar up against the squall, that Mr. Vane was carrying something heavier than bread home across the headland.

You can tell when a man is carrying a secret. It changes how he walks. Secrets make people walk like the ground owes them money. Mr. Vane had come into the inn that evening with salt spray frozen in his eyebrows and a look on his face like a man who has found a body — which, as it turned out, was exactly what he had found, though I didn't know it yet, and if you're going to be fair about it, neither did he want me to.

He left before the storm properly arrived. Aunt Marta pressed a wrapped loaf into his hands and told him to eat it warm, and he said thank you in that voice of his, low and careful, like every word cost him a coin he'd have to account for later. Then he went out into the rain and the dark, and I did what any sensible girl with a hero would do.

I followed him.

* * *

Now, before you judge me — and everyone judges me eventually, it's one of my talents — you should understand what Mr. Vane was to me. He was the only grown man on this island who had never once lied to me for my own comfort. When I asked him why the light turned, he told me about clockwork and weights and the patience of brass. When I asked him if he was ever afraid out there alone, he considered the question seriously, the way no one else did, and said, "Yes. But fear is just attention pointed the wrong way." When I asked him where he'd come from, before the island, he looked at me for a long moment and said, "From a place that needed me to be someone else," and then gave me half his biscuit, which I understood even then was the closest thing to a confession anyone on this rock would ever get from him.

I loved him the way you love a lighthouse: from below, at a distance, entirely.

So when he walked out into the storm with his shoulders set like a man going to a funeral, I put on my boots and went after him, because funerals are exactly the sort of thing children aren't supposed to attend, which means they're exactly the sort of thing we most need to see.

* * *

The path to the light goes up along the cliff in switchbacks, and by the time I reached the third bend the wind had made up its mind about me. It shoved. It hollered. It filled my hood with rain and my mouth with the taste of the whole gray Atlantic. I could see his lantern bobbing ahead, steady as anything, and I kept my eyes on it the way he'd taught me to keep eyes on the light itself — never on the rocks, only on the point you mean to reach.

I lost him at the top. The fog came down like a dropped curtain, and suddenly there was nothing but white roaring and the great slow heartbeat of the lamp above me, sweeping around and around, saying *here, here, here* to ships that couldn't hear it over the weather.

But I knew the way to his door better than I knew the way to church, and church I actually attended.

His cottage sits tucked under the tower's shoulder like a barnacle. The door doesn't lock — he told me once that a keeper's door should always open for anyone the sea sends him, and I had filed that sentence away among my treasures. So when I got there, soaked through and grinning with the pure criminal joy of it, I let myself into the little entryway to wait for the rain to slacken, planning to knock properly once I'd stopped dripping, so as to seem respectable.

That was the plan. I want that on the record. My plan was respectability.

Then I saw the satchel.

It sat on his table inside the doorway of the main room, which I could see because the inner door stood open and the oil lamp was lit. A courier's satchel — I knew the type, because everything that comes to this island comes by courier and everything that comes by courier comes in leather like that, stamped with the government mark. Except this one was swollen with seawater. Its buckle was furred green. Water had pooled beneath it on the table in a ring, like the satchel was still dripping from somewhere deeper than the sea.

And Mr. Vane stood over it with a poker in his hand.

Not holding it. Standing over it, the way you stand over a snake, with the poker held out in front of him like a divining rod, and his face — I will remember his face until I die — his face was doing two things at once, which I hadn't known faces could do. Half of it wanted to burn the thing. You could see the fire in it, the wanting, the whole arithmetic of *if I destroy this, I go back to being safe.* And the other half of his face was looking at something I couldn't see, some picture behind the satchel, and it was grieving.

I had seen that look before. On Aunt Marta, on the anniversary of her son's ship going down. Grief and fury sharing one small room and neither willing to leave.

Mr. Vane set the poker down. Picked it up again. Set it down. And then he took off his spectacles, which he almost never does, and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, and stood there in the lamplight looking — there's no other word for it — looking *alone*. Alone the way the tower is alone. Alone the way the last star before dawn is alone.

Well.

I am many things, but I have never in my life been able to watch a person be alone without doing something about it. Aunt Marta says it's my fatal condition. She says I collect strays: cats, soldiers, keepers. She says it like a complaint. I say somebody has to.

So I knocked on the open door.

* * *

He moved faster than I'd have credited. By the time the knock finished echoing he had the satchel off the table and under his coat and his face rebuilt into its usual careful calm, and when he opened the door fully he looked down at me with an expression I can only describe as a man watching his own house catch fire in slow motion.

"Paloma."

"Evening, Mr. Vane."

"It is nearly nine o'clock. In November. During a storm."

"Yes," I agreed. "Terrible weather for a walk. Lucky I'm not walking. I'm visiting."

"Paloma—"

"I knocked," I said, "which is more than the sea does, and you told me yourself your door opens for anyone the sea sends. Technically the sea sent me. I followed the coast path and the wind did the rest, and if you want to argue jurisdiction with the Atlantic, you go right ahead, but I'd want witnesses."

For a moment — one whole moment — I thought he was going to carry me back down the hill through the storm himself. His jaw did a thing. His hand tightened on the door. And then, behind him, the wind found some gap in the tower and moaned through it, long and low, like the island clearing its throat, and something in him simply... gave. Not collapsed. Gave, the way a knot gives. He stepped aside.

"You'll take soup," he said. "And then you'll tell me honestly how much Marta knows, and then I'm walking you home."

"Two out of three," I said, hanging my wet coat on his peg, next to his, which felt like a promotion. "Marta thinks I'm asleep. And you're not walking anywhere in this. Even you're not stupid enough to walk the cliff path in this, and you're the least stupid person I know, which is saying something, because I also know Tomas."

He made a sound that in a lesser man would have been a laugh.

* * *

Here is what I learned about Mr. Vane's cottage that night, sitting at his table with soup I pretended I hadn't already eaten at the inn (a girl must keep her sources fed): it is the tidiest room on the island and the loneliest. Everything squared away. Everything in its place. A room kept by a man who has organized solitude into a discipline. There was one chair. One. He dragged a crate over for me and apologized for it, and I said crates were fashionable on the mainland, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and let it stand anyway, which I think is called hospitality.

And there was the typewriter.

It sat on the shelf by the window under a cloth, and I had seen it before — I'd heard it, really, on my other visits, that quick dry clatter like rain on a tin roof — but tonight the cloth was folded back and there was paper in it, a letter begun and abandoned, and I am ashamed to say I read three words upside down before he crossed the room and turned the page face-down with two fingers, gently, the way you'd close a stranger's eyes.

"Private correspondence," he said.

"To who?"

"A person who doesn't receive letters anymore."

I knew then. Don't ask me how. Twelve-year-olds know things the way dogs know weather; we get no credit for it and no one believes our forecasts, but we're rarely wrong. Somebody dead. Somebody the letters were for anyway, because grief needs somewhere to go and a typewriter will hold still for it.

"The sea sent me," I said quietly, "and I think maybe it sent you something too."

He went very still. Stillness, with Mr. Vane, is not calm. I learned that night that stillness is his version of shouting.

"Paloma—"

"There's a satchel under your coat on the peg," I said. "It's been dripping since before I knocked. There's a puddle forming under your coat like your coat is crying, and you've been standing where you can see the peg this entire conversation, and you set the table for one but you took down two cups earlier — I saw them drying by the basin, and you live alone, Mr. Vane, so either you've taken up entertaining or you were expecting someone you didn't want to expect."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the storm leaned on the cottage and the tower answered it, that great slow sweep overhead, *here, here, here.*

"You," he said finally, "are wasted on this island."

"So are you," I said. "That's rather the point of islands."

And then — because I could see him building the speech, the grown-up speech, the *this is dangerous and you're a child and you'll forget all this by morning* speech, I've heard it in four different voices including the garrison captain's — I said the thing I had actually climbed a cliff in a hurricane to say.

"I don't want to know what's in it."

He stopped.

"That's the truth. I don't. Whatever it is, it washed up wearing a dead man's strap, and I watched your face when you thought no one was watching, and I know it isn't treasure. I don't need to read it. Papers are for people who need convincing, and I'm already convinced — convinced that you're frightened, and you don't frighten, and that whatever Corvel's men are offering reward money for at the harbor, you're holding it under your coat right now." I shrugged. "So don't tell me. Just tell me what to do."

The silence went on so long I started counting the lamp's hisses. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

"You understand," he said at last, and his voice had changed — lower, rougher, like a channel dredged clear after years of silt, "that if you're wrong about what this is, it isn't a game you can put down. People who touch this — they stop being invisible. That's the price. Visibility." He said the word like it was a wound he owned personally. "I have spent fifteen years being no one, Paloma. It is the safest thing I have ever been. If you help me, you spend some of that safety too. A child's share of it. I won't spend it for you."

I looked at him across the little table, at this man I had decided years ago to grow up and become, and I understood — really understood, for the first time — that the loneliness in that room wasn't an accident of geography. It was load-bearing. He had built his whole life on it, the way the tower was built on rock, and here I was proposing renovations.

Good, I thought. The tower needs company too.

"I'm already visible," I said. "I'm an orphan who lives above a tavern and asks questions. Nobody forgets me, Mr. Vane; they just underestimate me, which is better. Underestimated people can go anywhere." I held out my hand across the table, the way the mainland merchants do, solemn and ridiculous. "I don't want to know what's inside. I want to know where it needs to go, and what stands in the way, and which of those obstacles likes me. Because Captain Brech at the garrison pats my head and tells me I remind him of his daughter, and the telegraph clerk lets me lick the stamps, and old Sorrel at the harbor slips me peppermints and calls me *petite étoile*, and every single one of them would walk past me carrying a stolen cannon. That's not nothing. That's a road."

He looked at my hand. Then at me. And I watched something happen in his face that I had never seen there in all my years of watching: the loneliness cracked. Not broke — cracked, the way ice cracks on the harbor in spring, all at once and irreversibly, with a sound you feel in your teeth. Fifteen years of one chair and one cup and letters to a person who couldn't answer, and a twelve-year-old in a damp coat had just put her hand flat on the fault line.

He shook it. His grip was careful, like mine might break, which was funny, considering.

"You'll do exactly as I say," he said. "When I say. And if I say run, you run, and you don't look back for me."

"Deal," I said, "with one amendment. If I say hide, you hide. Fair's fair. I've got more experience being overlooked than you have — you're rather conspicuous, all six feet of you and your tragic eyebrows."

"*Tragic*—"

"They point downward, Mr. Vane. Like they're mourning. It's very effective but it's not subtle."

He turned away fast, toward the stove, but not fast enough. I saw it. The laugh got out. Small and rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years, but it got out, and it changed the whole room, the way the light changes the water when it sweeps past.

He ladled me more soup I didn't need. I ate it anyway. Outside the storm threw everything it had at the tower and the tower said *here, here, here*, and under the coat on the peg, the sea's dead messenger dripped its slow count onto the floorboards, keeping time with us.

"All right," Mr. Vane said at last, quietly, mostly to himself, the way men say things when they've stopped arguing with God. "All right. Here's what I know."

And that was how, at nine o'clock on a November night, with the wind trying to take the roof off the nicest lonely room on the island, I became the second member of a conspiracy of two — and the first thing I learned was that tomorrow morning, the harbor master was going to ask him a question he'd have to lie about, and I intended to be there, licking stamps, when he did.
