## 9. Checkpoint Two: The Garrison

There is a particular sound a broom makes on a flagstone floor when the man holding it has stopped listening to the floor and started listening to the room. I know this sound intimately, because for eleven months I have been its sole author. *Scritch. Scritch.* The rhythm of a man who has made himself furniture.

The garrison at Pointe-aux-Morts — the soldiers call it "the Pointe," and the islanders call it nothing at all, which tells you what they think of it — has forty men, one lieutenant with a voice like a slammed drawer, and floors that require sweeping twice a day if a man wants to keep his mind occupied and his hands from shaking. I sweep them both times. Sometimes three. Nobody questions an eager sweeper. Eagerness is the best disguise there is; it is even better than competence, because nobody envies it and nobody looks too closely at it.

I was on my third pass over the mess hall that Tuesday morning — the boards were clean enough to eat off, though God knows no one would, the food being what it was — when Lieutenant Fauvel came in with Captain Rennard's adjutant, and the two of them stood by the window talking in the low voices men use when they think the furniture isn't listening.

*Scritch. Scritch.* The broom went one way. My ears went the other.

"End of the week," the adjutant said. "Rennard wants it done before Corvel arrives, so there's something to show him."

"Corvel again." Fauvel sniffed. "The man buys more of this island every month. What are we searching for?"

"Papers. A courier drowned in the storm two weeks back. Harbor master swears nothing came ashore, but Paris doesn't believe him, and frankly neither do I. A courier carrying sealed orders doesn't drown alone on a calm crossing. And the light keeper found the body, didn't he? Pulled it out of the rocks himself. Where there's a corpse, there's often a satchel."

"And you think the lighthouse keeper has it? Brightwater?" Fauvel laughed, a short bark. "That man is half timber himself. He comes down for flour and lamp oil once a month and speaks perhaps nine words on each visit. I counted once."

"I'm sure you did," said the adjutant, who had the tone of a man who counted other people's counting. "Nevertheless. Friday, before first light. Six men, the lighthouse, outbuildings, and the ground between. If he's got anything buried, we'll find where the earth's been turned. And Fauvel—" the adjutant lowered his voice further, and I let the broom slow, just slightly, just enough, "*be civil about it. He's the only light between here and the mainland narrows. We can't have him sulking.*"

*Scritch. Scritch.*

They left. The floor gleamed. My hands, wrapped around the broom handle, were doing the thing they do — the fine tremor, the ghost of Artois still living in the nerves — and I stood very still in the empty mess hall and thought: *Friday. Before first light.*

Then I thought about Odile Vane's face, and about Marta's kitchen, and about a twelve-year-old girl who had once saluted me with a wooden spoon as though I were a general worth saluting, and I understood that the week had just acquired a shape, and the shape was a problem, and the problem was mine.

* * *

You should understand something about me and uniforms, because it explains most of what follows, and possibly everything that came before.

I wore one for three years. I marched in it across half of France, and I lay in it in a hole outside Arras for eleven days while the man next to me talked to his mother who wasn't there, and I was wearing it when the shell came that didn't kill me, which was the crueler trick, because after that I was a uniform walking around with nobody much inside it. When they discharged me — *unfit for further service*, four words, no ceremony — I took the thing off and I have not worn so much as a button of army cloth since. Marta found me sleeping in her woodshed in the spring of '17. She didn't ask what was wrong with me. She put a plate of stew inside the door and left it, and the next night another plate, and by the third night I had come inside to return the dish, and she said, "You'll be wanting work, then, since you're here," and that was that. Tomas, the sweeper. Tomas, who carries crates. Tomas, who sits in the corner at supper and doesn't startle anymore, or not much, or less than he did.

The army broke me. I want to be fair about it — it broke me the way a cart breaks a road, by using me for what it was built for. I don't hate the men in it. Most of them are boys like I was, hungry and far from home, saluting lieutenants whose voices sound like slammed drawers. But I learned in the garrison what I'd already learned at the front: the uniform asks for your loyalty first and gives you nothing back but the asking.

Marta never asked. That is the whole difference, and it took me a year to see it.

So when I walked up through the rain toward the inn that evening, with *Friday, before first light* folded up small inside my chest like a note I couldn't unread, I was not torn, exactly. People say *torn*, and they imagine two equal halves. It wasn't equal. There was no part of me that wanted six soldiers going through Odile Vane's drawers while he stood there being civil in his quiet way, nine words at a time, hiding whatever it was he was hiding behind those steady gray eyes. But wanting and choosing are different animals, and between them lies the swamp where cowards like me live.

I stood outside the inn longer than I should have. Rain coming down sideways off the harbor. Warm light in the windows. Paloma's silhouette crossing behind the glass, quick as a bird.

Inside, the smell hit me the way it always does — bread and woodsmoke and the faint brine that lives in everything on this island — and Marta looked up from the ledger and said, "You're dripping on my floor, Tomas," which is how she says good evening, and I hung up my coat, and supper was on the table within the hour.

Odile Vane was there.

That was unusual in itself. The lighthouse keeper came to the village maybe once a fortnight, and here he was for the second time in a week, sitting at the corner table with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, looking like a man performing the role of a man drinking tea. He'd been at the inn the night of the squall, too. Twice in eight days. A man could draw conclusions from that, if a man were in the habit of drawing conclusions, and I was, because conclusions had been my trade once — reading terrain, reading officers' faces, reading which way a bombardment would fall. You get good at noticing things right before the things notice you.

Paloma was at his elbow, chattering about something — she chatters the way sparrows sing, constantly, without shame, and it is one of the great restorations of my life that her noise no longer sounds like shellfire — and Odile was enduring it with an expression I recognized. Not annoyance. Endurance. The endurance of a man who finds a thing unbearable and cannot bring himself to end it.

"Tomas!" Paloma had spotted me. "Tell Odile that gulls can sleep on the water. He says they must land. Tell him."

"They can sleep on the water," I said. My voice came out rusted; it always takes a sentence or two to oil it. "Half their brain sleeps at a time. The other half keeps watch."

"There," said Paloma, triumphant. "Keeping watch. Like a lighthouse."

Something passed over Odile Vane's face then — quickly, quickly, gone before you could name it — and I sat down with my stew and ate and kept my eyes on my bowl and thought: *he knows about the courier. Or he knows something. And Friday, six men are coming up that hill before first light, and either I tell these people or I don't, and either way I am about to find out what I am.*

* * *

Here is what I decided, and how, and why it took until nearly midnight.

After supper, Marta sent Paloma to bed — Paloma protested, negotiated, lost, and went, pausing at the stairs to inform the room that she would solve the gull dispute conclusively tomorrow — and Odile rose to leave, and Marta said, "It's blowing hard, keeper. Take the lantern," and pressed one into his hands, and he took it, and there was something in the exchange that was older than either of them, some debt or understanding I wasn't party to.

He paused at the door. Looked back at the room — at Marta, at me — and for a moment he seemed about to say something. Nine words, perhaps. Then he nodded and went out into the dark, and the door shut, and I heard his boots fade up the lane toward the light.

"Tomas." Marta was stacking plates. "You've been carrying something around all evening heavier than the crockery. Out with it or take it upstairs, but don't sit there leaking."

Marta has this way. She reads people the way Odile reads weather — not from instruments, but from the look of the sky. I opened my mouth. Closed it. Opened it.

"There's a search coming," I said. "Friday. Before dawn. Six men, the lighthouse and the grounds. They think the keeper has papers from a drowned courier."

The plate in Marta's hand did not drop, and did not pause. That's Marta. She set it down, wiped her hands on her apron, and said, "Papers. What kind of papers?"

"I don't know. Sealed orders, the adjutant said. Something about the bridge sabotage. And there's a man named Corvel coming to the island — the shipping man — and the captain wants the search finished before he arrives. So there's something to show him." I heard my own voice saying it and marveled slightly, because I was giving away army business, real business, the kind of thing that in another life would have meant a court-martial, and my hands were steady. Steadier than they'd been in a year. "Marta, I shouldn't be telling you this. I swore—"

"You swore nothing to anyone," Marta said. "You swept floors. The army took your oath; it gave you back a woodshed and a tremor. Sit down."

I sat down.

She poured two glasses of the rough cider she keeps for occasions and pushed one across, and for a while neither of us drank, and the fire ticked, and the wind worried the shutters, and I thought about the hole outside Arras, and the man who talked to his mother, and how I had lain there for eleven days knowing exactly what was going to happen to us and unable to make my mouth form the words *we have to move*. Eleven days. Some knowledge sits in a man like swallowed glass.

"Marta," I said. "If they find whatever he's hiding, what happens to him?"

She looked at me across the table with those flat gray eyes that miss nothing. "What happens to any man who stands between Corvel and his money? Nothing good, and nothing legal, and nothing quick." She drank. "But that's not the question you came in with, Tomas. The question you came in with is whether you're a soldier or one of us."

"I'm not a soldier," I said. "I told you. They took the uniform back."

"No," she said. "They took the cloth back. The question stays with a man. It's the only thing they give you that you keep." She gathered the last plates. "You told me. That's the answer. Whatever else you decide this week, remember you already decided the first part."

She went into the kitchen. I sat by the fire with the cider going warm in my hand and felt something loosen in my chest that had been clenched since the mess hall — not relief exactly, but the specific easing of a man who has finally said the true thing out loud and discovered the ceiling didn't fall.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around three I gave up pretending, dressed, and went out into the wet dark, and I walked up the cliff path to the lighthouse, because I am a fool, and because there was a thing I had to say to Odile Vane's face and couldn't say across Marta's supper table with Paloma's ghost listening at the stair rail.

The light turned overhead, that great slow wheel of it, sweeping the sea and the rain and turning again. I'd forgotten how loud it is up close — a sound like breathing, if breathing were the job of a building. I hammered softly on the door, and after a long moment it opened, and Odile Vane stood there in shirt and suspenders with the lamp behind him, and his eyes went from my face to the empty dark lane behind me and back, cataloguing, the way a man catalogs when he's expecting trouble.

"Tomas." Nine words or fewer, presumably. "Is something wrong?"

"The garrison's coming," I said. All the oiled sentences I'd rehearsed on the walk up fell apart at once, and what came out was plain and fast. "Friday. Before first light. Six men. They'll turn out the tower and the sheds and probe the ground. They think you've got papers off a drowned courier. The captain wants it done before Corvel lands, so there's something to show him. I heard the adjutant myself. I came to tell you because—" and here my voice did the thing it does, cracked down the middle like old ice, "—because you people fed me. That's the whole of it. That's the whole reason. You fed me, and the army never once did anything half so decent, and I will not stand in their line and watch them go through your things."

The wind shoved past us into the room. Above, the lens wheeled, and the light swept the sea, and swept us, and moved on.

For a long moment Odile Vane said nothing. He studied me the way he studies weather, and I stood there feeling every crack in myself lit up like the harbor charts, certain he could read the whole broken coastline of me — the shaking hands, the woodshed year, the eleven days in the mud.

"You understand what you've done," he said quietly. "Telling me. If it's discovered you warned us."

"I know what I've done."

"Do you?" He stepped back from the door — an invitation, grudging and unmistakable. "Come in, then, Tomas. Because a man who chooses a side at three in the morning in the rain ought at least to be allowed to warm his hands by the stove of the people he chose."

I came in. The room was spare as a cell but clean as a chapel, and on the desk under the little window sat a typewriter, and beside the typewriter a stack of paper that he shifted, casually, too casually, out of my sightline as I passed.

He poured tea neither of us wanted. We drank it. And then, staring into the middle distance with the light wheeling above us, he said — to me, or to the storm, or perhaps to whoever the letters on that desk were addressed to —

"Friday, you said. Before first light." A pause. The wheel turned. "Then we have four days to decide where a thing hides when six soldiers and the truth itself come looking for it."

Outside, the rain came harder, and somewhere below in the black water, the sea was keeping whatever else it had taken.
