## 10. Hiding the Light

The storm broke on a Tuesday, and the soldiers came on a Wednesday. That is the sort of arithmetic I had come to live by — trouble never travels alone; it sends its luggage ahead.

I heard them before I saw them: boots on the causeway stones, more boots than one man makes, arranged in the particular rhythm of men who have been told they are important. Three of them, by the sound, and one walking slightly apart from the others, which meant an officer, which meant paperwork, which meant that someone in Corvel's orbit had finally done the sums.

I had perhaps four minutes. Perhaps three.

The satchel lay where it always lay now, wrapped in oilcloth inside the bread tin behind the third shelf of my pantry, beneath the jar of pickled herring I kept for no reason except that Marta refused to let me leave her kitchen without food and I refused to eat her food fast enough to suit her. Fourteen years I had lived at the light without anyone caring what was behind my shelves. It took one drowned courier to make my pantry interesting.

I will not pretend I was calm. My hands were steady — hands can be trained like anything else — but there was a bird beating itself against the inside of my ribs, and it wanted very badly out through my mouth.

Three minutes. The lens housing was the only place. Every other hiding spot in a lighthouse is a hiding spot because nobody thinks of it; the lens housing is a hiding spot because nobody dares touch it. The mechanism was the pride of the station, French glass and German brass, older than the war, older than me, and every soldier knows that breaking a light is a hanging matter even in peacetime — especially now, when the whole coast depended on it and half the fleet's charts bore this station's number.

I took up the satchel, climbed the ninety-one steps two at a time the way I did not do anymore, and set to work.

The housing has a false panel — not false, exactly, but serviceable, a plate that lifts for cleaning the lower prism ring. I had discovered it my first winter, when salt crusted the brass and I spent a miserable week learning the machine's anatomy. A man who lives alone learns everything about his house the way a priest learns his parish: intimately, and without witnesses.

The panel came away. The satchel went in flat against the frame, oilcloth side down, and I seated the plate and wiped my fingerprints from the brass with my sleeve, and then I stood there breathing while the great lens turned overhead, throwing its beam out across the grey water as though nothing in the world weighed anything at all.

Below, the knock came. Polite. That was worse than pounding. Pounding is for criminals; knocking is for neighbors.

* * *

"Keeper Vane." The captain stood in my doorway with rain still beaded on his shoulders, a tall man with the kind of face that has been shaved so often it has forgotten any other expression. Behind him waited two privates, young enough that their uniforms seemed to be wearing them rather than the reverse. "Captain Aubry. Garrison command. Forgive the hour."

"It's nearly noon," I said.

"Forgive the hour regardless." He stepped in without being asked, which told me most of what I needed to know about how the conversation would go. His eyes went around my rooms with the methodical patience of a man counting spoons. "There's been a theft. Official documents, off a government courier. We're searching the island, house by house. You'll understand that the lighthouse couldn't be exempted."

"Could it not?" I poured tea without asking whether he wanted it, because offering a man a cup he must refuse or accept tells you more than any question you might put to him directly. He accepted. Good. Men with hot cups sit down. Sitting men are slower to search. "What sort of documents?"

"That's not your concern."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "It wouldn't be."

He drank. I watched him decide whether my agreeableness was stupidity or something else, and I did my best to look like both. Fifteen years of practice had made me a modest expert in the craft of being underestimated. Be dull. Be old. Be a little stiff in the joints and a little slow with the jokes. Let them finish your sentences in their heads before you've spoken them.

"You're the keeper here long?" he asked.

"Fifteen years."

"Before the bridge, even."

"Long before. There was no bridge worth the name when I came. A ferry and a prayer."

"And before that?" He said it lightly, sipping. Too lightly. "Where does a man learn a trade like yours?"

"On ships," I said. "And in ports. And in weather." I smiled the smile of a man whose biography bores even himself. "You'll find the whole of it in the station registry, Captain, if the Admiralty hasn't lost it. They lose most things."

One of the privates shifted by the door. The other had drifted, without seeming to drift, toward the pantry.

I felt it the way you feel a change in the wind before the anemometer records it — some small wrongness in the room's pressure. I turned to the stove and fed it a knot of driftwood, giving my back a reason to be turned.

"Your papers, Keeper," Aubry said. "If you'd oblige."

I fetched the tin box from under my bed — the true documents, the ones that had survived fifteen years of inspections because they were perfectly, boringly genuine. Service appointment. Lighthouse certification signed by a harbormaster long dead. A letter of reference written, God help me, in my wife's careful hand, imitating a clergyman neither of us had ever met. Odile Vane, born in Brest, widowed, no children, no living kin, no history that could be checked by anyone who mattered.

Aubry read. He read slowly.

Here is the thing about a forged life: it holds up to any glance, and it fails only under a stare. A glance says, yes, stamps, signatures, dates, all in order. A stare begins to ask questions the paper cannot answer. Why does a man from Brest have no accent? Why does a certified keeper have hands calloused along the palms in a pattern more rope-and-oar than wick-and-brass? Why has no one from his hometown ever written?

Aubry stared. He held my life in his two hands and tilted it toward the window light, the way a jeweler tilts a stone, looking for the flaw.

"The signature here," he said. "This clergyman. Father—"

"Anselme. Saint-Pierre parish, Brest. Dead these twelve years, I'm sorry to say. He baptized half the harbor and forgave the other half."

"Mm." He did not write it down. That frightened me more than if he had. A man writing things down is collecting; a man not writing them down already owns them.

By the pantry, the second private had stopped pretending to admire my shelves.

* * *

And then the door opened without a knock, which on this island meant exactly one person.

"I found a crab!" announced Paloma, bursting into my kitchen with the confidence of a duchess entering her own ballroom, "a whole entire crab, it pinched Tomas, well, it tried, mostly it just fell over sideways, but it *meant* to pinch him—" She stopped. She saw the uniforms. Any other child would have gone quiet.

Paloma went louder.

"Oh! Are you arresting him?" she asked the captain, with frank delight. "Because if you're arresting him you'll have to wait, he has to light the lamp at sundown, it's the law, Tomas says they fine you a hundred francs if the light goes dark, and he hasn't got a hundred francs, he hasn't got ten, I know because Marta gives him his money in a tin and I've seen the tin—"

"Paloma." I said it gently, which for me was a shout.

"—and anyway he can't be a spy, he's the most boring man alive, he trims wicks, that's his whole life, trimming wicks and writing down the weather, once I watched him log 'rain' eleven days running and he was *thrilled*, he said it was consistent—" She had planted herself between the captain and the pantry with the unerring instinct of small animals finding the one gap in the hedge. "Is it about the drowned man? Everyone says a courier drowned. Marta says drowned couriers wash up in pairs, like boots. Is that true? Have you found the boot yet?"

Captain Aubry looked at her the way a man looks at weather he has decided not to go out in.

"Who are you, then?" he asked.

"Paloma Fenn. I live at the inn. Marta's my aunt. Not really my aunt, she's my aunt by way of being everything." She said it breathlessly, magnificently, a waterfall of a girl, and I understood in that moment — with a clarity that arrived inconveniently late, as such things do — that she knew precisely what she was doing. Somewhere under the chatter, the child had seen the private's hand drifting toward the pantry shelf, and she had thrown herself across the gap like a sandbag.

"Your aunt runs the inn," Aubry said. "The one where the relief officers dine."

"When they can get a table," said Paloma, "because Marta won't turn anyone away, which everyone says is bad business, but we're full every night, so."

The corner of the captain's mouth moved — not quite a smile; the reconnaissance version of one. He looked at Paloma, at me, at the tea cooling in his cup, and I watched him perform the arithmetic that officers perform: an old keeper, a loud child, a tidy room, nothing screaming. Suspicion is expensive. It costs attention, and attention is a currency, and somewhere across the island there were forty more houses and a harbor master and a telegraph office all demanding to be suspected too.

"Your papers are in order, Keeper," he said at last, handing them back. Our fingers did not touch; he was careful about that, I noticed. Careful the way careful men are. "For now."

"For now," I repeated, and gave him back the word like correct change.

At the door he paused. "The drowned courier," he said, not turning around. "When they pulled him from the water, his satchel was missing. Straps cut clean. You'd know, I suppose — you found the body."

The bird against my ribs went very still.

"I reported it to the harbor master," I said. "Satchel gone, straps cut, pockets turned out. Robbery, I'd have said. Though why anyone would rob a corpse of papers and leave him his watch, I couldn't tell you."

"No," said Aubry. "Neither could I." He settled his cap. "Good day, Keeper. Keep the light burning."

"It's what I'm for," I said.

They went down the causeway in single file, and I stood in the doorway until the wind took their boot-sounds, and then a while longer, because my knees had opinions about holding me up and I preferred they voice them privately.

Paloma tugged my sleeve. "You're white as the gull mess on the north rail," she observed.

"Eloquent as ever."

"Was he going to look behind the herring?"

I looked down at her. Twelve years old, wind-scoured, absolutely unafraid, and sharp past all decency. "What do you know about my herring?"

"Nothing," she said, with the transparent honesty of a child who knows everything about the herring. "Can I stay for supper? Marta sent a pie."

She held up a cloth-wrapped dish like a shield, like a flag, like a small round promise that I was not, in fact, alone in this. I took the pie. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Marta had timed it, I realized — sent the girl the moment the soldiers were sighted on the causeway road, the way you send a bucket ahead of the fire.

The whole inn had known. The whole island, probably. And I had believed myself invisible behind my walls.

* * *

After dark, when the lamp was lit and the lens turning its patient circles, I climbed up and lifted the panel and put my hand flat against the oilcloth just to feel it there, real, solid, damning. Ninety-one steps below, Paloma was asleep in my chair with her mouth open, having declared she would keep watch and lasted nine minutes.

Through the glass I could see the garrison lights, and beyond them, out on the black water, a ship's lantern where no scheduled ship should be — riding slow, waiting on the swell, like a dog that has scented something and sits watching the door without barking.

Corvel's ship. It had to be. Papers don't vanish off a drowned man without the owner sending someone to see about it, and owners like Corvel do not send clerks; they come themselves, sooner or later, to stand on the dock and shake the right hands and buy whatever can't be charmed.

The beam swept out, touched the distant lantern, swept on.

"Soon, then," I said to the empty lamp room, and began planning how a man hides a light from the one man who owns the dark.
