## 12. The Typewriter Speaks

I want it on the record — my record, the one I'm keeping in the back of Marta's ledger where she'll never look until it's too late to scold me — that I did not go snooping.

Snooping implies a certain sneaky quality, a hunched-shoulders, tiptoeing-through-someone-else's-things quality that I flatly reject. What I was doing was *chores*. Odile had asked me — well, he hadn't asked me, but I had volunteered in a way so loud and so repeated that refusal would have been rude — to dust the lighthouse cottage while he went down to check the moorings before the storm came back. The storms this winter come back the way bad news comes back: always worse than you remembered, always at the worst hour.

So there I was, being helpful, with a rag and a bucket of sand for scouring, standing in the middle of the keeper's cottage, which is the smallest house on the island and the tidiest and the saddest, though until that afternoon I couldn't have told you why.

It's not sad the way Tomas is sad, with the sadness leaking out of him at night when he thinks no one hears. It's sad the way an empty chair at a table is sad. Everything in the cottage faces one direction. The one mug by the stove sits turned handle-out as if someone might still reach for it. And on the little table under the window, under a cloth folded with military corners, sat the typewriter.

I'd seen it before, of course. Everyone has. It's famous in the way that only island things are famous: the lighthouse keeper owns a typewriter, imagine it, a machine for writing letters when everyone knows the keeper writes none. The mail boat brings him nothing but supply invoices and once a circular about rat poison. He sends nothing back. Auntie Marta says he's a man who keeps his own counsel, which is what people say about men who are keeping something else entirely.

The cloth came off easier than it should have. That's all I'll say in my defense. A covered thing practically asks to be uncovered; it's practically begging.

The typewriter was black and beautiful and heavier than sin, with round keys like little eyes and a ribbon gone pale on one side from overuse and dark on the other from none. There were papers beside it too, in the drawer, stacked neat as hymnals. I want to say now, clearly, that I did *not* mean to read them.

I read them.

*My dearest heart,* said the first one. *The fog came in at four and left at nine and in between I thought of nothing but your hands.*

That's how it started, and already my chest had gone strange, because everyone knows Odile Vane is a widower, everyone knows his wife died before he ever came here, fifteen years ago, and everyone knows you don't mention her because he goes quiet in a way that makes the whole room go quiet with him. But nobody knows — or I didn't — that he writes to her. Every week. Maybe every day. Letters with no address, sealed in envelopes that have never seen a stamp, stacked in a drawer like a second heartbeat.

I should have put them back. I know exactly what Auntie Marta would say. She'd say, Paloma, other people's drawers are other people's hearts, and you don't go rummaging in hearts. She'd be right. She's usually right, which is the most annoying thing about her, after the way she can tell from across a room if I've skipped the soaking water for the dishes.

But I read them.

*The storm took two shingles off the north side. I've mended them. You always said I patched things rather than replacing them, and you were right, as usual. I am still patching.*

*I saw a gull today with a broken wing that healed crooked. It flies anyway. I thought you'd appreciate that.*

*I dreamed you were alive and I woke up and for one moment — one whole moment — I was neither one thing nor the other. Those are the best moments I have. I am sorry that they are. I know you'd want better for me than moments.*

By the fourth letter I was sitting on his bed with my shoes off, because it felt wrong to sit on a man's bed with shoes on while reading his soul, and by the seventh letter I had learned things that rearranged me.

Here is the thing. Here is the thing I will carry until I'm old and gray and telling this story to children who won't believe it.

The letters aren't signed *Odile.*

They're signed with another name. A woman's name. Not at the bottom, either — that's the part that made my hands go cold, colder than the harbor in January. At the bottom of each letter, in the same careful hand, it says *your Odile.* As in: *all my love, always, your Odile.*

And higher up, in the body of the letters, in phrases like *when I wore your dresses that last summer, before we chose this* and *you promised me the sea doesn't ask questions* and *fifteen years, and I still keep the light the way you taught me to keep it, as myself, whoever that is now* — higher up, in the body of the letters, was the truth.

Odile Vane was not born Odile Vane.

Odile Vane was born somebody else. Somebody who loved a woman named — the letters call her *my heart* and once, devastatingly, *my wife*, plain as bread — and who, when that woman died, could not go back to being the person she had married, because that person had only ever been half-alive, and the woman had known it, and had helped him become this instead. Had helped him bury the old name. Had walked with him to the mainland registry herself, the letters said, and stood witness while he became a widower named Odile, because grief gives a man cover that joy never could. Nobody questions a mourning husband who wants to disappear onto a rock with a lamp.

Nobody except me, apparently, sitting cross-legged on his bed with a fistful of his unsent heart.

I sat there for a long time. Long enough that the light through the window moved from one wall to the other. Long enough that I heard the front door below and his boots on the stairs and had exactly enough time to do the arithmetic of panic.

Here's what I understood in those thirty seconds, and I want to be fair to myself, because I've replayed it a hundred times since: I understood that I was holding the most dangerous thing on this island. More dangerous than the satchel hidden inside the lens housing — yes, I knew about the satchel, I'd helped hide it, that's a different chapter. Corvel's men would hang Odile for the satchel. They'd do worse than hang him for the letters. Because the satchel proved Corvel a criminal, and criminals get tried, but a man who used to be a woman, in the winter of 1918, in a garrison full of soldiers with too much rum and too little sense — they wouldn't wait for a trial. The letters were the more lethal paper. I knew it the way you know a stair is missing in the dark: instantly, in the stomach.

And here's the second thing I understood, just as fast, and it surprised me so much I nearly laughed: it changed nothing.

Not *almost* nothing. Nothing. Nothing whatsoever. The man who had taught me to read weather off the water, who had let a twelve-year-old girl hold the lamp key against regulations because she was careful and he trusted her, who had fed me hardtack and honey the day my parents' boat went out and didn't come back and never once told me to stop crying, just sat next to me and cried a little himself, quietly, like rain through a roof — that man was still that man. The name was a coat. I'd known him without it all along.

His boots reached the top of the stairs.

Now, I had maybe four seconds, and a girl in my position has three options: hide the letters (impossible, I'd have to eat them), confess (Auntie Marta's preference in all matters, the coward's road), or act.

I acted.

* * *

He came through the door shaking rain off his oilskin, saying something about the mooring line chafing, and stopped dead, because I was sitting at the typewriter. His typewriter. With his letters squared neatly on the desk beside me, in plain view, because hiding them would have been a lie, and I had decided in the four seconds that lying to Odile was off the menu forever.

And on the platen of the typewriter, fresh off my fingers, hunt-and-pecked with two fingers at tremendous speed, was a letter.

"Paloma." His voice did the thing it does when he's frightened, which is go very quiet and very level, like water finding its level. "Those are private."

"I know," I said. "I'm sorry. I read them."

He set down the mooring line he'd brought up to coil. He looked at the stack of letters. He looked at the page in the machine. I watched him decide whether to be angry, and I watched the anger lose, because he was too tired and too frightened and too decent, and also because I think some part of him has been waiting fifteen years for someone to find out, the way a held breath waits to be let go.

"You understand," he said slowly, "that if anyone else —"

"No one else. Just me. And I'm going to type badly at you now, so brace yourself."

And I rolled the page around so he could read it.

I had written it in the four seconds, or rather my hands had written it while my brain stood aside amazed at itself, and this is what it said, and I reproduce it here exactly because it is the finest thing I have ever composed and I was twelve:

*DEAR ODILE. I READ YOUR LETTERS AND I AM SORRY BUT ALSO NOT VERY SORRY BECAUSE NOW I KNOW YOU AND THE KNOWING IS BETTER. I KNOW ABOUT THE OTHER NAME. I KNOW YOU WERE HER HUSBAND AND ARE NOT ANYMORE AND THAT SHE HELPED YOU BE YOU. IT CHANGES NOTHING. IT EXPLAINS EVERYTHING. YOU KEPT ME COMPANY WHEN MY PARENTS DIED AND YOU WERE REAL THEN AND YOU ARE REAL NOW. ALSO YOUR RIBBON NEEDS CHANGING. LOVE PALOMA P.S. I CAN KEEP A SECRET BETTER THAN TOMAS AND THAT IS SAYING SOMETHING BECAUSE TOMAS KEEPS SECRETS LIKE A GRAVE.*

He read it twice. I know because his lips moved the first time.

Then he did something I had never seen him do, and have only seen him do twice since. He sat down on the bench beside me — the bench built for one, the widower's bench — and he laughed. Not the dry huff he uses for jokes at the inn, but a real laugh, startled out of him, wet-eyed, the laugh of a man whose trap has sprung open instead of shut.

"It changes nothing," he repeated. He touched the words on the page like they might smudge. "You typed that in four seconds?"

"Three. I'm faster when it matters."

"Paloma." He took off his cap, which he does indoors only for serious occasions, funerals and the like. "Do you understand what you know? Do you understand what it would cost me if —"

"I understand better than you think. The garrison captain studied your papers too long. I watch everything, it's my one skill. And I understand that the letters in that drawer could hurt you worse than the satchel in the lens." I said it plainly, because he hates being handled gently almost as much as he hates being pitied. "Which means we move them. Tonight. Before the search. Before Corvel gets brave."

He stared at me. Fifteen years of solitude, and staring at a mud-booted child laying logistics on his kitchen table.

"When did you become the strategist in this family?" he said.

"Someone had to. Tomas can't plan past lunch and Auntie Marta plans with her heart, which is lovely and useless. You plan alone, and alone isn't working anymore." I stood up and put my shoes back on, because the moment for barefoot sincerity was passing. "You're not alone. That's the whole point of what I typed. Read it again if you lost the thread."

"I haven't lost the thread." He folded my letter along its crease, very carefully, the way he handles anything true. Then he opened the drawer, gathered the letters — his wife's letters, his own letters, fifteen years of talking to the dead — and wrapped them in oilcloth with hands that shook only a little. "There's room in the lens housing beside the satchel," he said. "Salt air won't touch them. And if the worst happens, if they take me —"

"They won't."

"— if they take me," he went on, in the tone of a man who has done this arithmetic many lonely times, "then those letters belong to you. All of them. And the name at the bottom of the last one is mine, my real one, and I'd like it read out loud someday, somewhere safe, by someone who loved me. Can you do that?"

I could not speak for a second, which for me is roughly a medical event. So I nodded, and then, because nodding wasn't enough, I said: "I'll type it out first. In fair copy. Your handwriting's terrible."

That got the second laugh, quieter.

We hid the letters together in the great lens housing while the storm built itself out beyond the point, and he showed me how the brass clips worked, and our breath made ghosts on the glass, and it felt less like hiding evidence than like planting seeds.

But walking home along the cliff path, collar up, wind shoving me toward the inn like it had opinions, I looked back at the tower and saw what I should have seen earlier and hadn't, because I'd been busy being profound.

Down at the landing stage, sheltering under the oilcloth awning of Corvel's relief shed, stood one of Corvel's men. Not doing anything. Just watching the lighthouse. Watching us, or near enough.

And behind him, at the inn, a lamp was burning in the window that shouldn't be — the signal lamp, the one Auntie Marta puts in the window only when someone must come quick, and come the back way, and come now.

I ran.
