## 13. Checkpoint Three: The Telegraph Office

I have run an inn for thirty years, and in that time I have learned that every lie has a texture. The smooth ones are the most dangerous — a man who lies smoothly has practiced, and a man who has practiced is lying about something worth practicing for. The rough lies, the stammered ones, the ones with sweat on them — those you can forgive, or at least predict.

So when I told Odile Vane I would carry our business to the telegraph office myself, he looked at me the way men look at a woman who has just volunteered to walk into a fire, and I understood that he thought I was being brave. I wasn't. I was being practical. Nobody watches an innkeeper. An innkeeper is furniture — necessary, invisible, always in the way of nothing. If Corvel's people were going to be watching the telegraph office, they would watch it the way they watched everything on this island: lazily, certain that the important things would announce themselves by wearing fine coats.

The ledger had come to me three days earlier, folded inside one of Tomas's shirts when he came back from wherever he'd been with his knuckles split and his eyes gone somewhere I couldn't follow. He didn't tell me where he'd found it. I didn't ask. There is a whole grammar of not-asking that holds this island together, and I am fluent in it.

It was a shipping ledger, Corvel & Sons stamped on the spine in gold that was already flaking. And it was false — beautifully false, the kind of false that takes real craft. Two sets of figures kept side by side like lovers: the tonnage declared to the commission, and the tonnage actually moved, the difference skimmed into contracts that existed only as ink. Whoever had cooked these books had done it for years, comfortably, the way a cook salts soup without tasting. That comfort was what made it valuable. You can't fake that kind of boredom.

I wrapped it in oilcloth, then in a second layer of plain brown paper tied with butcher's string, because a package that looks hidden is announcing itself, and I addressed it to my cousin in Saint-Aubin, which does exist, though she hasn't spoken to me since 1911 over a matter involving a rooster and a debt she invented. Let her be surprised. She could stand to be surprised more often; it would improve her disposition.

* * *

The telegraph office sits between the chandlery and the shuttered photographer's shop, in a building that used to be a chapel before someone decided God could be better served by copper wire. It's a small room, always too warm, smelling of ozone and machine oil and the particular staleness of rooms where men wait. There is a counter, a window with frosted glass, and behind the counter, Émile Fournier.

Émile had been a boy in my mother's boarding house when I was young enough to still believe my mother was patient. He'd stolen sugar from her kitchen for two years before either of us caught him, and when we did catch him he'd cried so thoroughly that she gave him the sugar anyway and told him that if he was going to be a criminal, he ought to be a competent one. He took the advice seriously. He became a clerk.

He was forty-one now, thin as a hat pin, with a mustache he'd been growing since the war started and never quite committed to, and hands that moved over the telegraph key the way a priest's move over a chalice. He was, I knew with the certainty of thirty years of feeding people, a coward. Not the loud kind — the quiet kind, the kind that survives wars and occupations and marriages by agreeing quickly and regretting privately. A coward of exactly Émile's dimensions could be relied upon to do anything except be the first to do it.

"Madame Marta." He said my name the way he said all names, as though confirming a transmission. "You've come to send something?"

"A package," I said, and set it on the counter. "For my cousin in Saint-Aubin. But I understand the boats aren't running."

"The boats are not running," Émile agreed, with the satisfaction of a man reciting scripture. "Nothing leaves until the armistice traffic resumes."

"Then I'll wire her instead. Tell her the money's coming and she should stop writing me letters about it." I let that sit. "Though I hear the wires go through Corvel's office now."

Something moved behind Émile's face — not guilt, exactly. Guilt implies the possibility of innocence having been expected. This was more like the look of a man hearing his own obituary read aloud and finding it accurate but unflattering.

"All wires are read," he said quietly. "Every outgoing transmission. Mr. Corvel's man — Delaunay — sits in the back room Tuesdays and Fridays, and reads them aloud. For security, he says." His mustache twitched. "He reads them aloud, Madame Marta. Even the ones to sweethearts. Especially the ones to sweethearts. He finds them funny."

"And you transcribe them for him first."

"I copy them fair, and then I read them to him, and he decides which ones matter." Émile's hands were flat on the counter now, very still. "There is no wire off this island that Mr. Corvel has not heard. You should know that before you ask me whatever you came here to ask me."

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You will." He glanced at the package. "You're holding it like it matters."

I had been holding it like a parcel of laundry. I set it down flat and put both my hands on top of it, which was, I admit, worse.

"Émile," I said. "When your mother sent you to my mother's house, do you remember what she said?"

"She said—" He stopped. He remembered. Everyone remembers what their mothers said; it's only the mothers who forget.

"She said, mind Madame Marta, she'll know if you lie." I slid the package across the counter toward him, and untied the string, and folded back the brown paper, and let him see the gold stamp. "She was right."

Émile went the color of candle wax. He looked at the ledger the way a man looks at a snake that has gotten into his boot while he was wearing it.

"That's—"

"Corvel's books. The real ones, or rather the false ones, which are the same thing. The ones that show what he's been skimming off every relief contract since 1915. The ones that, if they reached the commission at Saint-Aubin, would hang him — well. Ruin him. Ruin is slower than hanging but it lasts longer." I folded the paper back down, retied the string, and left it on the counter under my hand. "And here is what I know about you, Émile Fournier. You have spent twenty years copying other people's words and sending them places, and not once — not once in all that time — have your own words gone anywhere at all. You are a man whose entire life is passing messages, and you have never had one of your own."

His throat worked.

"If you help us," I said, "you will have one. One message that matters. The kind a man tells his grandchildren about, if he ever manages to have any grandchildren, which at your rate of courtship seems unlikely, but hope springs eternal."

"That isn't—"

"Bribery works better with flattery. Ask anyone."

He laughed. It came out of him sideways, startled out, the laugh of a man who has not been surprised in years and finds he misses it. Then he looked at the package again, and the laugh died, and I watched him do the arithmetic that cowards do — the weighing of dangers, the counting of exits — and I saw him arrive, as cowards always arrive, at the conclusion that there are no safe choices, only chosen ones.

"What would you want me to do?" he asked. "Send the ledger by wire? You can't wire a ledger."

"No. I want to know the channels. When the armistice is signed, there will be official traffic — commission dispatches, priority wires, the whole apparatus moving at once. In that flood, one more wire, one more coded message among dozens, might slip through unnoticed."

"Delaunay reads everything."

"He reads everything slowly, and alone, and bored. Boredom is the great ally of smugglers, Émile. Has he ever stopped a wire? Truly stopped one?"

Émile hesitated. "Once. A fish merchant tried to report catches to a buyer on the mainland without paying the harbor tax. Delaunay held it three days and fined him double."

"A fish merchant." I let my voice carry all the contempt the subject deserved. "And in the meantime, how many wires have gone through that he never even opened? How many routine ones — weather reports, supply requisitions, the endless paperwork of an army that has stopped fighting but not stopped writing?"

"Some. Most, maybe. He can't read everything closely. There's too much." Émile's eyes flicked toward the back room, where Delaunay's desk sat empty this morning, its chair pushed at an angle that suggested haste or indifference. "But anything unusual — anything coded, anything from an unknown sender — he flags it. Brings it straight to Corvel. And Corvel—" He paused, choosing the word carefully. "Corvel notices."

"Then we won't be unusual. We'll be boring. We'll be the single most boring wire ever transmitted off this island." I leaned closer. "What's the most tedious thing that goes through here regularly?"

"Lighthouse reports." He said it instantly, and then blinked, as if surprised to hear himself say it. "Daily. Weather, visibility, lamp status. From the lighthouse keeper. Signed Vane. They go through every morning at six, and Delaunay has never once read past the header. He calls them 'the lamp man's poetry.'" A pause. Something shifted in his face — the look of a man realizing mid-sentence that he has just handed someone a key. "Nobody reads them. Nobody has ever read them."

I stood there a moment, letting that settle. Every morning at six, Odile Vane filed his report, and every morning it passed through the hands of the enemy unread, a small boat slipping past a sleeping guard every day for fifteen years.

"Well then," I said. "It seems the lighthouse keeper has been running contraband longer than any of us knew. He just didn't know what he was carrying."

* * *

I walked back up through the village with the ledger back inside my coat, heavier than it had been on the way down, though it hadn't changed weight. Émile had given me three things before I left: the schedule of Delaunay's days (Tuesdays and Fridays, mornings, unless Corvel was ashore, in which case daily); the fact that the wire itself ran along the seabed to the mainland relay, untouched by human hands between here and there; and one more thing, which he had said looking at his own shoes.

"There's a new order posted. All outgoing wires require garrison countersignature now. As of yesterday." He'd finally met my eyes. "Even the lighthouse reports. Even the boring ones. Captain Roche signed it himself."

A closed fist. That was the phrase that came to me walking home, and it wouldn't leave. The island had become a closed fist — every road a finger curling inward, every checkpoint a knuckle, and at the center of the grip, whatever it was we were trying to get out, held tighter and tighter until either we crushed it or the fist broke open.

At the inn, Paloma was behind the bar standing on a crate, drying glasses with the fierce competence of a girl who had decided that childhood was a rumor spread by mainlanders. Tomas sat in his corner with tea he wasn't drinking, and when I came in he looked up, and I saw the new thing in his face — the thing he'd brought back from wherever he'd been, some knowledge with a face on it now, a price with a name.

"Well?" Odile was there too, at the end of the bar, hat in his hands like a man at a funeral. He'd aged ten years in the week since the storm brought the sea's terrible gift ashore. "Marta. Did he take it?"

"He took it. Or rather, he'll hold it, and when the time comes, he'll look the other way at precisely the right moment, because Émile Fournier has spent forty-one years looking the other way and he's very good at it." I set the ledger on the bar between the glasses. "But listen, because the news is bad and getting worse. Every wire off this island passes through Corvel's man. Every one. Read aloud, like a bedtime story, to a man who laughs at love letters."

Paloma stopped drying.

"But—" I tapped the ledger. "The lighthouse reports. Daily. Unread. Fifteen years of them. That's our channel — a wire so dull that corruption itself yawns at it. Whatever we send goes out dressed as weather."

Odile had gone very still. Stillness, in that man, was never emptiness; it was a full cup held level. "Countersigned," he said quietly. "Roche's order. My reports need the garrison's signature now."

"Yes."

"Which means Roche sees them."

"Or whoever Roche delegates to. Which is where our friend comes in." I nodded at Tomas, who had begun, very slowly, to sit up straighter, as though his body were remembering a shape it used to hold. "Tomas sweeps that garrison floor every morning. He knows which clerk countersigns, when he takes his coffee, whether he reads what he signs or signs what he doesn't read."

Tomas's voice, when it came, was rough as a rusted hinge. "Lieutenant Aubry. Countersigns at eight, after coffee, never reads past the second line. I've watched him. He signs the same way he does everything — like he wishes he were somewhere else." He looked at his ruined hands on the table. "I can make sure the papers are on top of his pile. I can make sure the pen is out of ink so he uses the one that smudges. Small things. Things a sweeper does without being seen doing them."

"Small things win wars," I said. "Ask anyone who's survived one."

Paloma had climbed down off her crate. She was looking at the ledger with her chin up and her eyes bright, and I recognized the expression because I'd seen it on her uncle's face in photographs — the family stubbornness, arriving a generation late but arriving all the same.

"So the plan is the light," she said. "We hide the truth inside the lighthouse reports, and the reports ride out on the wire under everyone's nose, and nobody ever looks up." She frowned suddenly, twelve years old again in a single crease between her brows. "But a wire can't carry papers. Papers are proof. Wires are just words. Who believes words?"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the clock to tick four times, and in that silence I watched Odile understand what the child had understood in a breath — that a channel is not a delivery, that telling the truth is not the same as proving it, that we had found a door and discovered it was a window. Words could go out. Words could warn, could prime, could prepare the ground across the water. But the ledger itself, the courier's satchel, the physical weight of proof that would make a commission act — that still needed a boat.

That still needed the sea.

"The words go first," Odile said finally, slowly, feeling each one for load-bearing strength. "Coded into the reports. Dates, contract numbers, names — enough that someone at the other end knows what to look for and where. Enough that when the papers arrive, someone is already waiting for them. Already believing." He looked at me. "But the papers still cross by water."

"And the water is watched," said Tomas.

"And the water is watched," I agreed. The fire crackled. Outside, the wind had come around again from the northeast, worrying the shutters the way it had all week, as if the storm season itself had taken Corvel's coin and joined his side.

Hope, I thought, rinsing a glass that didn't need rinsing, hope is a narrow thing by the end. It starts as a river and narrows to a channel and narrows to a pipe and at last it's a single thread of lamplight through frosted glass, and you either thread yourself through it or you spend the rest of your life on the wrong side of the glass.

One wire, disguised as weather. One sweeper, disguised as harmless. One boat, not yet found, in a cove not yet scouted, against a fist that was closing.

I was reaching for another glass when the door banged open and the cold came in ahead of the visitor — and there in the doorway, shaking the rain off a fine gray coat, stood one of Corvel's clerks, smiling the smile of a man delivering good news.

"Madame Marta," he said pleasantly. "Mr. Corvel sends his compliments. He'll be calling on the inn tomorrow evening. He asks that you put on a proper pot of tea."

Behind me, very quietly, Paloma set down the glass she was drying.
