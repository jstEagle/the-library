## 14. The Storm That Wouldn't End

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday it had fallen so far I half expected to find it lying in the yard.

I have kept this light for fifteen years. I know its moods the way a man knows a wife's breathing in the dark — the ordinary grumbles, the small complaints of wind and rain that mean nothing. This was not that. This was the sea drawing back its fist to strike, and everything on the island — gulls, dogs, the inn's crooked chimney — went quiet in that particular way that means every living thing is counting the seconds.

Marta stood at my door with a basket before the first gust arrived, because Marta does not accept weather as an excuse any more than she accepts refusal.

"You'll eat," she said. It was not a question. "Bread, cheese, the ham you pretend not to like. And Paloma's mended your scarf, so don't argue about the stitching."

"The stitching is fine."

"It's terrible. She's twelve. Take it anyway." She set the basket down and looked past me, up at the tower, then out at the water where the horizon had gone the color of an old bruise. For once she didn't scold or fuss. She just said, quietly: "Whatever you're keeping out there, keep it dry. And keep yourself. We've grown accustomed to you."

Then she was gone down the path, small and straight-backed against a wind that would have taken a lesser woman off her feet entirely, and I stood there holding a ham I had never admitted to liking, feeling something in my chest that had no business being there on the eve of a hurricane.

* * *

The storm made landfall at dusk, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

By midnight the wind was a solid thing. You could lean on it. The tower hummed like a struck bell, and the sea came over the rocks below in sheets that hit the gallery glass hard enough to make me flinch the first few times, until flinching became a luxury I could no longer afford.

Because here is what nobody tells you about a lighthouse in a hurricane: the light itself is the easy part. Oil, wick, clockwork — a child could manage the mechanism, and for two years one nearly did, when I first took the post and my hands shook too badly to be trusted with anything but routine. No. The work is the other things. The pump that must be bailed every hour when the spray finds its way into the lower gallery. The lamp room panes that want wiping every ninety minutes or the salt crusts them blind. The rotation gear that complains in cold and must be coaxed with oil and, if necessary, lies.

And through all of it, the knowledge — sitting in the lens housing, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged among the spare prisms where no soldier would think to look and no storm would trouble — that what I guarded was not only a light.

The papers stayed above the tide line of Corvel's reach. But the storm did not care whose papers they were. On the second night, working the wick at three in the morning with the whole tower swaying like a drunk leaning on my shoulder, I thought suddenly: *the boat.* The cove where Tomas had scouted, where we meant to launch whatever fishing boat could be spared — that cove would be full of storm surf now, and the boats tied there were being tested against their moorings, and some of them would lose.

Everything we had planned hung on a hull. And the same sea I served was doing its best to take it apart.

There is a particular loneliness in realizing that the duty you cannot abandon is also the only thing standing between the plan and nothing at all. If the light went dark for even one night, some coaster hugging the coast in this filth would find the shoals instead of the channel, and the wreck would bring the navy, and the navy would bring inquiries, and inquiries are exactly what a man with contraband truth hidden in his lamp room can least afford. So I kept the watch. What else was there? Sleep was a rumor. Food happened between log entries, standing, bread in one hand and the pump handle in the other.

I wrote in the logbook in my careful keeper's hand:

*Night of the 4th. Wind SW by S, force 11, rising. Sea over the low rocks entire. Lamp trimmed at 2 and again at 6. Rotation sound. No shipping sighted — God help anything that tries.*

And beneath it, smaller, the entry no inspector would ever read:

*Still burning.*

* * *

On the third night the storm stopped pretending to be weather and became a siege.

I remember very little of it as time, only as tasks. Wick. Glass. Pump. Wick. The stairs, climbed so many times my knees filed a formal complaint. Somewhere in the small hours I caught myself speaking aloud — to the lamp, mostly, encouragement and abuse in roughly equal measure — and once, without deciding to, to Élise.

"You always said I'd die of boredom," I told the flame. "You owe me an apology."

The flame bent and straightened and said nothing, which was her way in arguments anyway. She'd have won it, too. She'd have sat in the corner of the lamp room with her shawl and her terrible opinions about my handwriting and made me laugh while the world ended outside the glass. Fifteen years, and I still set out two cups some mornings before remembering. Fifteen years, and the remembering doesn't get lighter; it just gets more familiar, like an old coat.

That was the night the gallery door gave its worst argument yet. A wave found the seam — I will never understand how a sea can climb forty feet to find a seam — and drove spray through it in a white gasp that put out one of the standby lamps and left a puddle freezing on the iron floor. I spent an hour with rags and tallow, swearing with an eloquence that would have gotten me excommunicated in three parishes, and all the while the main light turned above me, steady as conscience, sweeping its long arm across a world that had been reduced to black water and white fury.

Somewhere out in it, I knew, was our boat. Or had been. I allowed myself, once, thirty seconds of imagining the cove — the masts splintering, the mooring lines parting one by one like the days of the week running out — and then I shut the thought away and went back to the wick, because a man can carry only one dread at a time up those stairs, and mine was already spoken for.

Duty, I have come to believe, is just love with the sentiment boiled off. You keep the light because people you will never meet might otherwise die, and you keep it whether your heart is broken or whole, whether your name is true or borrowed, whether the papers hidden above your head could hang a rich man or ruin you. The lamp does not ask who you are. It asks only: *are you burning?*

Three nights. By the last dawn I had stopped being a man and become a function — a pump, a wick, a pair of hands — and I found, in a way that would alarm anyone who has never loved solitude too much, that the function was a relief. Odile Vane, with his secrets and his satchel and his dead wife and his impossible plan, could rest somewhere below decks. The keeper remained. The keeper is simple. The keeper trims, logs, endures.

* * *

The storm broke on the fourth morning the way a fever breaks — all at once, leaving wreckage and an unnatural quiet.

I went out onto the gallery into air so clean it felt new-minted, and looked down at the rocks, and understood how narrowly the island had held. Boats in the harbor sat low or didn't sit at all. Half the sea wall was gone. And along the strand below the light, tangled in weed and rope like offerings the sea had reconsidered, lay debris from every corner of the coast — spars, crates, a ship's door, and one long white plank with painted letters I did not let myself read closely.

Paloma came up the path before I'd finished the morning log — of course she did; the storm had barely finished exhaling and there she was, scarf flying, boots ruined, carrying a basket nearly as large as herself.

"Marta says you're alive," she announced, "and if you're alive you're hungry, and if you're hungry you'll lie about it, so don't."

"I'm not—"

"See?"

She set the basket down, looked up at the tower with her chin lifted in that way she had, appraising, proprietary, as though she were already the keeper and I merely the current tenant, and then her eyes went sharp and businesslike.

"Tomas sent me," she said, and the brightness dropped away, and underneath it I saw something older than twelve. "He walked the cove at first light. He says—" She stopped, steadied herself the way he'd taught her, hands flat. "He says the boats took a beating. Two gone entirely. But the *Marie-Jeanne* held. Battered, shipping a bit of water, but held. He's already bailing her out."

The relief went through me like warmth through frozen hands — painful, almost, in its intensity. One boat. One battered, stubborn hull, which was apparently the island's prevailing condition this winter.

"She held," I repeated.

"She held." Paloma grinned, gap-toothed and fierce. "Tomas says anything that holds through a storm like that deserves to carry something important. He says it's a good omen." Then, climbing onto the rail to look out at the wreckage-strewn sea, she added, lighter, almost careless, the way children deliver the things that stop your heart: "Oh — and Marta says Corvel's men came to the inn this morning asking why the lighthouse burned so steady all week. They wanted to know if anyone helped you keep the watch."

The wind moved the grass below the gallery. Somewhere down the coast, the *Marie-Jeanne* waited, bailing and battered and ready.

"They asked," Paloma said, turning back to me with her eyes gone serious again, "who else knows about the papers."
