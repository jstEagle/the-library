## 15. What Tomas Carried Back

They tell you in the army that a man is made of three things: his boots, his orders, and what he can carry. They don't tell you what happens when the orders rot and the boots wear through and all that's left is the carrying.

I had been carrying since 1914. Mostly I carried other men's names — the ones I remembered, and the ones I was too ashamed to have forgotten. I carried the sound of the guns, which never quite stopped; it just went somewhere low in me and waited. And now, it seemed, I carried the plans of a twelve-year-old girl who had drawn them out on the back of Marta's flour list with a pencil she'd licked to a point like a duelist sharpening a blade.

"The narrows," Paloma had said, tapping her map with one finger, "and before that we need to know if the cove will take the boat at half tide."

"And how do we learn that?" Marta asked, though I could see by her face she already knew the answer, and already hated it.

I raised my hand. It felt absurd, raising your hand like a schoolboy to volunteer for treason. But old habits are the deepest ones, and volunteering was the habit they'd beaten into me first and longest.

"You know the garrison patrol routes," Odile said quietly. He didn't say *you could walk past them without being stopped*. He didn't need to. That was the thing about Odile — he never said anything he could make you say yourself, which I suppose is why people trusted him without ever quite being able to explain why.

"I know them," I said. "Better than I'd like to."

Marta put down the bread knife she'd been holding the way you hold a bread knife when you'd rather be holding something else. "Tomas."

"Marta."

"If they catch you—"

"They won't." A lie, delivered smoothly. I was getting better at lying. The inn was teaching me things the army never had.

She looked at me a long moment, and then she did the worst thing she could possibly have done. She reached over and straightened my collar, the way my mother used to, the way nobody had done since, and said, "Come back for supper."

So there it was. Not glory, not duty, not even the papers in their oilcloth bundle under Odile's floorboards. Supper. That's what I was fighting for now. Stew and brown bread and Paloma arguing about whether lighthouses counted as houses or machines, and the lamplight on Odile's face when he almost smiled, which was rarer than rain in August and worth more than everything Corvel's money could buy.

I would come back for supper or not at all.

* * *

The cove sat two miles south of the light, past the black rocks the fishermen called the Teeth because biting was apparently all anyone thought about anymore. I went at dusk on Thursday, wearing my old greatcoat with its sergeant's stripes unpicked — I'd unpicked them myself two years ago, thread by thread, sitting in the loft of the inn like a man performing surgery on his own ghost — and carrying nothing but a length of rope and a lantern with the shutter closed.

The army teaches you to move at dusk. The war taught you to move afraid. The combination makes for a particular kind of walking: fast and silent and hating every step.

The patrol route along the south road ran on the half hour. I'd swept that floor for eight months. I knew Corporal Vasseur took his smoke break at the marker post and stood there exactly four minutes, long enough for any man who needed to cross the road behind him. I knew Lieutenant Aubry's boots squeaked on the left foot only. I knew which sentries watched the water out of habit and which watched the road out of boredom, and that the dangerous ones were neither — they were the ones who watched nothing at all and saw everything anyway. There were two of those. I avoided both.

The cove opened below me like a secret someone had whispered into the cliff and forgotten to keep. Low tide had pulled the water back forty yards, leaving a beach of slate and bladderwrack, and there — God bless it — there was the boat.

It was a fishing lugger, small, tarred black, beached above the tideline with its hull turned up like something dead or sleeping. The Fauvette, according to the letters peeling off her stern. Old Perrin's boat, I thought. Old Perrin who hadn't fished since his sons drowned, who kept the boat anyway because a man needs somewhere to put his grief where the weather can get at it.

I went down to her. I put my hand on the hull and it was cold and wet and solid, and I'll tell you honestly: it was the most comforting thing I had touched since before Verdun.

Half tide, Paloma had written. Will she float?

I looked at the weed line on the rocks, measured against the water, measured against the hull. She'd float with eighteen inches to spare, if the sea behaved, and the sea never behaves, but she'd float. I checked the keel for rot, the rudder pintles for rust, and found the drain plug missing, which meant she'd been sitting open to the sky and had an inch of rainwater sloshing in her bilge. All fixable. All fixable tonight, except tonight wasn't mine.

That was the mistake. Not the boat. The arithmetic.

I was so busy doing sums — plug, tide, patrol, supper — that I stopped listening. Eight months of sweeping a garrison floor and I'd forgotten the first rule of scouting, the rule they teach you before they teach you to shoot: *the ground itself talks*. Gulls lift. Dogs go quiet. Slate shifts under a boot that isn't yours.

I heard the boot too late.

"Don't turn around," said a voice behind me, pleasant as a landlord discussing rent. "Or do. Makes no difference to us."

There were three of them. I knew that without looking, from the way the footsteps spread — one ahead-left, one right, one holding still behind. Civilians walk in clumps. Soldiers and soldiers' hired men fan out. These fanned.

I turned around. No sense dying stooped over a bilge plug.

They were big men in plain coats, the kind of plain that costs more than decoration, and the one in front had a face like a closed ledger. He held no weapon visibly, which meant he was either unarmed or confident, and no man is confident in a cove at nightfall without reason.

"Evening," I said.

"Is it." Ledger-Face looked at the boat, then at me, then at the boat again, doing his own arithmetic. "Old Perrin's lugger. Funny place for a walk."

"I like the sea."

"At low tide. In November. After dark." He smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, the kind of smile that gets oiled like a machine. "You're the sweeper. From the garrison. Tomas — what is it? You've got a surname floating around somewhere."

"Delacour," I said, because refusing to give it would tell him more than giving it.

"Delacour." He tried it out like a coin between his teeth. "Sergeant Delacour, isn't it? Before. Verdun, yes? We know all sorts of things, Sergeant." He crouched, touched the hull of the Fauvette the way I'd touched it five minutes earlier, and I understood that this man was very good at his work and that his work was me. "Checking her over?"

"She belongs to no one now. I was thinking of buying her."

"With what?" One of the others laughed, quick and mean.

"Soldier's savings," I said. "We're famous for them."

Ledger-Face stood. For a moment — just a moment — the professional smile dropped and something underneath looked out at me, something tired and transactional, and I realized he wasn't enjoying this either. That frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruelty has heat in it. This was cold all the way down.

"Here's how evenings go," he said. "Some evenings, a man finds a boat and goes home and says nothing to anyone, and nothing happens to anyone. Other evenings, we search him, find whatever he's carrying, and then the evening becomes complicated. Complicated evenings cost extra. Mr. Corvel pays extra, but he doesn't like to." He held out his hand. "Arms out, Sergeant."

And here is the part I'm not proud of, and the part I am, and they're the same part: I let him search me. I stood with my arms out in the cold like Christ on a bad day and let a hired man pat down a deserter's coat, because the rope and the shuttered lantern were innocent, and the truth — the plan, the tide tables in my head, Paloma's pencil-drawn map folded into my boot lining beneath the arch where a boot always rubs and no searcher thinks to look twice — the truth stayed where I'd put it.

He found the rope. He found the lantern. He turned the lantern over in his hands for a long time.

"Dark lantern," he said.

"For the walk home. The moon's useless."

"The moon's always useless." He handed it back. And then, softly, almost kindly, which was somehow the cruelest available option: "Tell whoever sent you that the cove is watched now. Tell them the price of boats went up this week." He stepped close. Close enough that I could smell bay rum and tobacco on a man dressed like poverty. "Actually — don't tell them anything. Just come back Friday. Walk down here Friday evening like a man buying a boat, and we'll see what you do when the boat isn't there."

"Why would I come back?"

"Because," he said, turning away, "you're a soldier, and soldiers come back to ground they've been warned off. It's in the manual. Page one."

They walked away up the shingle, unhurried, and the sound of their boots faded, and I stood alone in the dark with a boat that was now bait and a heart going like a drumline.

* * *

I didn't go back Friday. Obviously. But I also couldn't go back to the inn yet, because they might be watching the road between the cove and the village, and if they followed me home, everything ended at the inn door with Marta's bread still warm on the table.

So I did what the army taught me. I walked the long way. Three miles through the gorse and the gull-cries, doubling back twice, sitting once for twenty minutes behind a drystone wall feeling my own pulse count out seconds I couldn't afford. The stars came out hard and cold. Somewhere north, the lighthouse turned its beam across the dark, steady as a heartbeat, and I thought: *that's what he does. Every night. Alone. That's the whole job.* And I thought: *and I've been calling myself broken because I can't sleep through the night.*

The beam swung past. Swung past again. I got up and walked home.

They were waiting. Of course they were waiting — Marta at the table with her arms crossed, Odile by the window with the lamp turned low, Paloma pretending to do sums and fooling no one, least of all herself.

"Well?" Marta said.

"Boat's sound," I said. "Keel's true, rudder's sound, needs a plug and a bail-out. Half tide, she floats with room to spare." I hung the dark lantern on its nail by the door, and my hand was steady, and I want credit for that, because inside the hand was screaming. "But the cove's watched now. Three men. Corvel's. They know the boat matters, they know someone's coming for it, and they're using it as a hook. They told me to come back Friday."

Silence. Then Paloma said, with terrible twelve-year-old clarity: "Then we can't use the Fauvette."

"We may not have a choice," Odile said. His voice was calm. His hands, folded on the table, were not entirely. "A boat is a boat. The question is whether they'll let any boat reach the water."

"They'll let one reach the water," I said. "The one they want to follow."

Marta sat down heavily. "Then it's not a boat. It's a trap with a hull."

"It's a trap with a hull," I agreed, "unless the person they're watching walks down to that cove Friday evening, stands around looking guilty, and then goes home — while the boat they should be watching is somewhere else entirely."

Everyone looked at me.

"Somewhere else," Odile repeated carefully, "being what?"

"Being," I said, and here I laid it out, and I'll admit the wargaming felt good, felt like the one piece of my old life that had ever worked properly, "launched from the slipway under the harbor wall at slack water, which is ten minutes' walk from the garrison gate and the last place anyone would launch from, because everyone knows the harbor master watches the slips." I looked at Odile. "Except the harbor master owes Corvel, and Corvel's men are watching a cove two miles south. Nobody watches the slips because nobody's stupid enough to use them."

Paloma was vibrating. "That's brilliant. That's — Tomas, that's *brilliant*—"

"It's also suicide," Marta said flatly, "because the moment the boat hits the harbor water, the whole island sees it."

"Not the moment it hits the water," Odile said slowly, and I watched the idea arrive behind his eyes like weather coming over the headland. "The moment it's *noticed*. Those aren't the same moment. Not if the island is looking elsewhere."

And then he looked at me, and I understood that he'd seen the rest of it before I'd finished saying it, and that he hated it, and that he wouldn't ask.

I asked for him. "Decoy," I said. "Something loud at the garrison. Fire works. Fires are traditional."

"No," Marta said.

"Marta—"

"*No.*" She stood, and for a moment she was not a landlady, she was a force of nature with flour on her apron. "You walked into three of them today and came back because you were lucky and because you gave them nothing. Now you want to stand in the middle of their garrison and set it alight? They'll shoot first and identify the body after, and I will not—" Her voice caught. Her son's name lived in that catch; none of us said it; all of us heard it. "I have buried enough of this family already."

The word hung there. *Family.* She'd said it without noticing, the way you set down something heavy without noticing you've set it down, and I watched it land on each of us in turn. On Paloma, who went pink. On Odile, who looked at the table. On me, who had joined a family for the first time in my life at the age of thirty-one and discovered that families, unlike armies, actually expect you to survive.

"Nobody sets any fires yet," Odile said finally. "Friday comes first. Tomas goes to the cove, as invited, empty-handed, bored, a man buying a boat that won't be there — because it will have moved. Let them waste their night watching an empty beach. Meanwhile—" he glanced at Paloma "—we study the harbor slips. All of us. Slowly. Innocently."

"Innocently," Paloma repeated, radiant with insincerity.

"And Tomas." Odile met my eyes. "If they take you again—"

"They won't take anything," I said. "They didn't today. They think I'm a lonely ex-sergeant with romantic notions about boats. Let them keep thinking it. It's the first time anyone's underestimated me since 1914, and I confess I enjoyed it."

But walking up to the loft afterward, candle in hand, I stopped on the stairs and admitted the truth to the dark, because the dark was the only listener who wouldn't repeat it:

They hadn't underestimated me. Ledger-Face had known exactly what I was. He'd searched me, found the rope and the lantern, and let me go — not because he believed the lonely-soldier story, but because a released fish swims home to the school. I was not the catch. I was the line. And every step I took back toward the inn, I was reeling something behind me: the route, the faces, the weight of what the inn was planning, paid out nice and slow toward a hook that hadn't been set yet.

Corvel didn't need me to talk. He just needed me to keep coming back.

I lay down on my cot and did not sleep, which was usual, and listened to the house breathe around me — Marta banking the stove, Paloma's light going out, Odile's door opening and closing as he left for the light, always for the light — and I understood that the price of failure had stopped being an abstraction years ago at Verdun and had finally grown a face: a face like a closed ledger, a voice pleasant as rent day, and a smile that got oiled regularly.

Friday was coming. So was the armistice. And somewhere between them, one of us was going to be wrong about everything.

I just prayed it wouldn't be the man who swept the floors.
