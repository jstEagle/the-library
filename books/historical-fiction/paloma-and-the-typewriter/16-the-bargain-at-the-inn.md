## 16. The Bargain at the Inn

I had been pouring tea for the dead for fifteen years — my wife, in her way, every night at the lamp; the drowned courier, in my conscience, since the storm gave him back to me. I suppose it was only a matter of time before I was asked to pour for the living as well, and that the living one would be the worst of the three.

Marta came down to the light on the Tuesday after Corvel's ship anchored, walking against the wind with her shawl pinned like armor, and told me the news the way she told me everything: flatly, and with an undertone that meant *prepare yourself*.

"He's taken rooms at the garrison," she said, "but he'll dine at the inn. He asked for the inn by name. Said he'd heard the tea was good."

"Who told him that?"

"You did," Marta said. "Two years ago, at the harvest supper, when you were trying not to talk to anyone. You said the inn's tea was good so you could stop talking." She looked at me over her spectacles. "He remembers things, that man. That's what makes him dangerous. Not the money. The memory."

I said I would not come to dinner.

"I didn't ask you to come to dinner," she said. "He asked for you. By name. By your name — the name on the logbook, the name on the garrison's register. He said, 'Send for the keeper. I should like to thank the man who keeps us from drowning.'" She folded her arms. "You can refuse. He'll take it for guilt. You can come. He'll take it for something else. Choose whichever lie you can hold longest."

That is Marta all over. She never tells you what to do. She simply dismantles every option until you can see the bones of them, and then leaves you alone with the skeleton.

I chose the tea.

* * *

The inn smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, the salt that gets into everything on this island the way grief gets into a house — permanently, and without anyone agreeing to it. Paloma had polished the good table twice. Tomas stood near the door doing his best impression of a man who sweeps floors and has never heard gunfire, which was, given Tomas, a considerable performance. Marta moved through the room straightening things that did not need straightening.

Corvel arrived at seven o'clock exactly, because men like him are punctual the way storms are punctual: they come when they say they will, and they ruin what they find.

He was smaller than his reputation. I had imagined a bulk of a man, some monument to shipping tonnage, but he came through the door narrow and neat, gray at the temples, with the soft handshake of someone who has never held anything heavier than a pen. His coat was mainland wool. His boots were island mud, worn deliberately, the way some men wear humility.

"Monsieur Brightwater," he said, and my false name sounded in his mouth like a coin being tested between the teeth. "The keeper himself. They tell me you've held this light fifteen years without missing a watch. That's devotion."

"That's arithmetic," I said. "A dark light costs lives. I do the sum every night and it always comes out the same."

He smiled as though I had pleased him enormously, which I understood at once to be worse than displeasing him. Marta seated him with the good chair facing the window — away from the kitchen, away from Tomas, away from the stairs where the rooms were. She had thought about that chair. We had all thought about that chair.

Paloma brought the tea.

I want it recorded, wherever such things are recorded, that a twelve-year-old girl carried the pot across that room with steadier hands than mine. She set it down before Corvel and curtsied the small precise curtsy Marta had drilled into her, and Corvel looked at her — really looked, the way he had looked at nothing else since entering — and said, "And who is this?"

"Paloma," she said. "My aunt keeps the inn."

"And what do you keep?"

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "The spoons," she said, and left.

Corvel laughed. It was a fine laugh, warm, well-oiled, the laugh of a man who has paid to have rooms full of people laugh at slightly less. I poured the tea myself rather than let him touch the pot, though I could not have told you then whether I was protecting her or protecting the performance.

We spoke of weather. We spoke of the bridge, and how long the repairs might take, and he shook his head over the sabotage with genuine-seeming sorrow — *a criminal act*, he called it, *an act against the whole island* — and I agreed with him, and we were two honest men mourning the same crime, and only one of us had arranged it. There is a particular nausea in that kind of agreement. It sits under the tongue like a coin.

Then he set down his cup, aligned it with the table's edge so precisely that I knew the gesture was rehearsed, and said:

"They found a courier in the rocks below your light."

"The sea gives things back," I said. "It took me thirty years to learn it doesn't ask permission first."

"It also takes things," Corvel said pleasantly. "Pockets empty. Satchels vanish. Papers dissolve. The sea is a careless clerk." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "There were documents in that satchel. Shipping orders. Contracts. Nothing of value to anyone but lawyers and widows — and yet the commission wants them. Wants them very much." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of harbor water, pleasant and depthless. "There is a reward. And there is more than a reward, Keeper, for the right man. There is a boat. Passage off this island whenever he likes, for the rest of his life, first class, any port. All he has to do is bring me whatever the sea gave him."

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan lid settled. I became aware that I had been holding my breath long enough that my body had begun to object, and I made myself breathe the way I breathe at the lamp: slow, counted, mechanical.

"You believe I have it," I said.

"I believe the sea gave you something," Corvel said. "Everyone believes it. The harbor master believes it. The garrison captain believes it. Half the village believes it, because half the village saw you walking the rocks for three hours in a gale, and no man walks three hours in a gale to look at nothing." He spread his soft hands. "But belief is not proof. Proof is my trade, Keeper. I deal in it the way your innkeeper deals in bread. So here is my offer, made plainly, man to man: bring me what you found, and leave this rock a rich man. Or don't—" and here he smiled again, gently, terribly, "—and stay. Keepers who stay are so very useful. A light that burns all night attracts such interesting wreckage."

There it was. Not a threat — he would never stoop to a threat; threats leave fingerprints. An observation about lights and wreckage. A weather report.

I refilled his cup. My hand did not shake, and I noted this with the distant surprise of a man watching someone else perform.

"Monsieur Corvel," I said, "I have lived alone above the sea for fifteen years. Do you know what the sea gives me? Rope. Timbers that were once someone's hull. Boots, sometimes, with feet still considering them. Bottles with letters in six languages, most of them love letters, all of them regrettable. Last spring it gave me a piano. A whole piano. I have no idea whose." I drank my own tea. "If the sea gave me papers, they are at the bottom of my scrap basket under fish heads, because that is where the sea's gifts go. What I have, Monsieur Corvel, is a light, a logbook, and a bad knee. You may have any of the three."

He watched me for a long moment. I let him watch. The trick, I have learned, is not to avoid a hunter's eyes but to give them something true to land on — the bad knee is real, the loneliness is real, the contempt, God help me, is real. A lie built entirely of lies collapses. A lie with a spine of truth will bear weight.

"You're a strange man, Brightwater," he said at last, almost fondly.

"I'm a lighthouse keeper. Strangeness comes with the post."

He rose, thanked Marta loudly for the excellence of an inn he would now recommend to no one, pressed coins into Paloma's hand — too many coins; I watched her understand, with a flicker only, that the coins were a message and the message was addressed to me — and paused at the door.

"One more thing," he said. "The courier. Did you see his face?"

I had. Every night since, behind my eyelids, young and waterlogged and asking me for something I could not give him while he breathed and something I could not refuse him now that he couldn't.

"No," I said. "The gulls had been at him."

"A pity," Corvel said softly. "Faces are how we identify things." And he tipped his hat to the room, to Marta, to the spoons-keeper, and went out into the dark.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the sound of his motorcar had died against the wind. Then Marta took the coins out of Paloma's fist, set them on the table, and looked at them as if they might hatch.

"He knows," Tomas said from the doorway. His voice had gone flat and quiet, the voice he used when the army was talking inside his head. "Not sure. Knowing isn't the same as sure. But he'll be sure soon. Men like him buy certainty the way they buy everything."

"Then we're finished waiting," Marta said.

I sat looking at my cold tea and understood that the bargain had not been the one spoken aloud. Corvel had not come for the papers. He had come to price me — to stand in a warm room and measure how badly I wanted passage off this island, how much a rich man's exit might weigh against a poor man's silence. And I had answered him, and my answer had been true in every particular except the one that mattered, which is the only kind of answer that survives a man like Corvel.

"He offered me a boat," I said.

"He offers everyone a boat," Paloma said. She was stacking the cups, not looking at any of us, twelve years old and already fluent in the grammar of men like that. "Grandmother used to say a man who offers you a boat is standing in your way of one."

Marta looked at her sharply. Then at me. Then at the window, where beyond the glass the light turned its long slow arm across the water, sweeping and sweeping, faithful as a heartbeat.

"The narrows," Marta said quietly, as if continuing a conversation we had not yet started. "Someone ought to start thinking about the narrows."

I looked at Paloma, and Paloma looked at the table, wearing the expression of a child who has already thought about the narrows considerably, and drawn a map.
