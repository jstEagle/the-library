## 17. Paloma's Plan

The trouble with grown-ups, I have decided, is that they think in straight lines. A road goes from here to there. A checkpoint stands on the road. Therefore the road is impossible, and everyone must sit at the inn drinking Marta's terrible chicory coffee and looking tragic until the island runs out of bread.

I had been listening to them argue for two days. Two days of Odile pacing the length of the kitchen like a caged gull, of Tomas saying *it cannot be done* in the flat voice he uses when he means *I am afraid it can be done and I will be the one who has to do it*, of Marta wringing her apron into a rope that could have moored the boat itself.

So on the third morning I did what any sensible person would do. I took the chalk down from the shelf, wiped the slate where the supper menu was written — nobody was eating the supper anyway, they were too busy despairing — and drew the island.

"There," I said, and tapped the chalk so hard a piece broke off. "That's your problem. You keep drawing the bridge."

They all looked at me the way adults look at children who have interrupted an important conversation about important things. Odile opened his mouth to send me upstairs. I kept talking before he could.

"The narrows," I said. "Nobody has said the word once. Not once in three days."

* * *

You have to understand about the narrows. Every child on this island knows them the way mainland children know their multiplication tables, because every child on this island has been warned about them since before they could walk. The water between the old fort rock and the Gull Teeth runs fast even on calm days — the tide squeezes through like water through a funnel, and it doesn't forgive mistakes. Three boats have gone down there in my lifetime that I know of, and Papa used to say there were more below than above, if you counted properly.

But here is the other thing about the narrows, the thing the warnings leave out: they are narrow because the rocks are tall. The channel runs between cliffs, and the current through them is strongest at slack water going to flood, and — this was the part I'd worked out lying awake with my arithmetic book over my face — the tide table for the armistice celebration put high water at the narrows at half past four in the afternoon. Which meant slack water, or near enough, at the exact hour the whole island would be standing on the quay watching the bridge being strung with lanterns.

"Corvel wants his celebration," I said. "He's paid for the bunting himself — Tomas heard the garrison quartermaster say so. Every soldier will be on the bridge road. The harbor master will be making speeches. The telegraph clerk will be sending congratulations to the mainland, because Corvel will want those wires read aloud." I tapped the slate again. "Every eye on land will be pointed at the bridge. Nobody looks at water during a party. Nobody ever has."

Silence. The kind that has weight, like wet wool.

It was Odile who spoke first, and he didn't send me upstairs. He came and stood at the slate, very close, studying my crooked little island as though it were a chart he'd never seen, though he'd been keeping the light above those same waters for fifteen years.

"How do you know the tide tables?" he asked. Not *why*, not *you're a child*. He asked *how*.

"I copy them," I said. "For practice. Aunt Marta says my figures are better than the harbor master's." This was true. The harbor master adds numbers with his fingers and gets sentimental answers.

"And you've thought about the current."

"Slack water lasts maybe twenty minutes at the narrows in November," I said, "if the wind's westing. Longer if it backs around. You come out of Smuggler's Cove at quarter past four, run with the ebb starting, and you're through the Teeth before it turns against you." I had rehearsed this. I had lain in bed doing it in my head until the numbers wore grooves. "After that it's open water and the dark coming up, and the lighthouse—" I stopped, because I hadn't let myself think past that part yet.

Odile looked at me for a long moment. His face did something complicated — the thing it always does when he's feeling several things and has spent fifteen years practicing showing none of them. Then he turned to the others and said, in the tone of a man reporting weather: "She's right."

* * *

Well. That opened the floodgates.

Marta was first over the top, which surprised no one who had ever seen her argue with a fishmonger. "Absolutely not. She is twelve years old. She proposed it, fine, clever girl, gold star, and now she will sit in the cellar with her aunt while the men—"

"There are no men," Tomas said quietly. "There's me, and I shake so badly some mornings I can't lace my own boots. And there's Odile, who hasn't sailed since—" He stopped, because he didn't actually know when, none of us knew much of anything about before, that was rather the point of Odile. "If she can read water the way she reads tide tables, she's the best navigator on this island under seventy, and everyone under seventy has already been arrested or bought."

"That is not the argument you think it is," Marta said, but her voice cracked in the middle of it, and I understood then that she wasn't really arguing about boats. She'd buried a son on one. She'd buried a sister and gotten me instead, and I think in her head those two facts were permanently braided together: the sea takes, and the sea gives back something smaller that you love more fiercely precisely because you know the price.

"Aunt Marta." I went and stood next to her. I don't like touching people much — I get enough of it — but I took her hand anyway, because this was arithmetic of a different kind and the answer required it. "You can't row it yourself and watch the tiller. Someone has to hold the heading while the other pulls. That's just mechanics. And Odile's hands are steady but he'll need both of them on the oars in that current, and Tomas—" I looked at Tomas. "Tomas, can you steer?"

He was quiet a while. His hands were flat on the table, and I watched them — watched whether they trembled. They trembled a little, and then, when he made them, they didn't. "In the army I drove a supply wagon down the Vosges road at night with the lamps shuttered," he said. "Artillery on both sides. I can steer a boat, Paloma. Whether I can stand to be shot at again is a different question, and I won't know the answer to that until someone is shooting."

"Then we make sure nobody shoots," I said. "That's the whole design. That's why the timing matters. At half past four, Corvel will be on the bridge platform giving his speech — he'll want to be photographed for the papers. The garrison band plays at half past. Even if someone sees us, they'll assume we're late for the party. A fishing boat leaving the cove on armistice day isn't suspicious. It's festive."

"Festive," Marta repeated, in a voice that suggested the word had personally insulted her cooking.

"Wave a flag," I suggested. "Aunt Marta, you have forty flags left over from the Jubilee. Put one on the boat. Nobody in the history of smuggling has ever flown a flag. It's the perfect disguise, being obvious."

And that — that ridiculous, obvious thing — was what broke the argument open. Because Odile started laughing. It was a strange sound, rusty, like a door in a house nobody visits, and it changed the whole temperature of the room. Marta stared at him. Tomas's mouth twitched despite everything.

"She's right," Odile said again, still laughing a little, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand. "God help us all, she's right twice in one morning. Flags. We'll fly the ugliest flag on the island."

* * *

We argued for another hour after that, but it was a different kind of arguing — the kind that builds instead of tears down, each objection becoming a plank. Marta insisted on conditions: I would wear a cork vest, which I hated, and if the weather turned I would turn the boat back without argument, which I promised to hate slightly less. Tomas laid out the decoy — a fire, a real one, lit in the garrison's wood store on the far side of the compound, timed for quarter past four, big enough to pull the duty patrol off the seawall but small enough not to burn the town down. "I set fires professionally for three years," he said. "It's the only part of the army I was good at."

"And the papers?" Marta asked Odile directly, because by now everyone talked to Odile directly, which was new, and which I think frightened him more than Corvel did.

"Oilcloth. Double-wrapped. Tied to me, not to the boat." He said it simply. Boats can sink. Boat-keepers can swim, or fail to. I saw him decide, right there at the table, that he would drown before the satchel did, and I decided not to say out loud that I'd noticed, because some things you file away for later.

"One more condition," Marta said, and now she looked at me, and her eyes were doing the thing where they shine and scold at the same time. "Paloma proposes it. Paloma navigates it. But Paloma does not die doing it, because if she dies I will follow her out of sheer annoyance, and then who runs the inn? So you come home. Do you hear me? Everyone comes home. That's the plan. That's the whole plan, underneath the tides and the flags. Everyone comes home."

Everyone comes home. I wrote it later on the slate, under my map of the island, and didn't wipe it off.

* * *

That evening I climbed the cliff path to the lighthouse, because Odile had asked me to come and look at charts, and also because I wanted to see the light working from outside for once, the great beam swinging its slow white arm across the water like a hand counting sheep.

Inside, the lamp room smelled of oil and brass polish, and Odile had spread three charts across the log desk, weighted at the corners with the typewriter, a spanner, and half a loaf of bread. He didn't talk down to me about any of it. He asked me where I'd enter the cove, and I told him, and he nodded and marked it. He asked me what I'd do if the ebb turned early, and I told him, and he frowned and showed me a trick with the tiller — feathering, he called it — that I practiced with a broom handle until my wrist ached.

"You've been thinking about this a long time," he said at one point. Not a question.

"Since the day the courier washed up," I admitted. "Since I saw what it did to your face." He went very still. "You carry everything alone," I said. "You've been carrying it alone for fifteen years, probably longer, and it's stupid, because carrying is what families are *for*."

The lamp turned overhead. Its shadow went around the walls of the lamp room like a slow wheel, and in the swinging light his face was bright, then dark, then bright again, and I watched him try three different sentences and discard all of them.

"Half past four," he said finally. "Armistice Day. Slack water at the Teeth." He held out his hand, palm down, formal as a treaty. "Navigator."

I shook it. His hand was warm and rough as rope, and it did not shake at all.

Walking home along the cliff path afterward, with the beam sweeping past me every eight seconds — I counted — I thought about how everyone thinks children don't understand war, sitting in our warm kitchens while the grown-ups read the casualty lists aloud. But I understood it fine. War was the thing that took my parents' ferry down in '15, though the official report called it weather. War was the reason Tomas couldn't sleep near windows. War was whatever had happened to Odile before Odile, the name-shaped hole in every story he told.

War was a magnate buying up a bridge and a celebration and a whole island's silence, and calling it relief.

And peace — real peace, the kind with ink on it — was going to arrive on this island at half past four on Armistice Day, in a leaky fishing boat, steered by a twelve-year-old girl flying a Jubilee flag, with a drowned man's satchel lashed to a lighthouse keeper's chest.

It was, I reflected, the most sensible plan anyone had proposed in three days. Which said something rather damning about grown-ups.

Behind me the light swung on, patient, counting. Ahead, two days off, the narrows waited, black water between black rocks, and somewhere beyond them the open sea, and beyond *that* the commission, and beyond *that* — if the tide ran true and the flags flew foolish and the fire caught on schedule — a signature that would finally mean what it said.

Two days. I had two days to become the best navigator this island had ever produced, or die trying, and Aunt Marta had been extremely clear about which of those outcomes was acceptable.

I picked up my pace. There were tide tables to memorize.
