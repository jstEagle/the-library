## 18. Armistice Eve

The morning came in grey and quiet, the way a stage comes quiet before the curtain rises, and every soul on this island was an actor who hadn't been given their lines.

I woke before dawn, as I have for fifteen years, to the arithmetic of the light: oil, wicks, glass, wind. But today the ledger had extra columns. Today I counted rope, tar, tide, and the distance between our little cove and freedom, which is not a distance you can measure with any instrument sold by chandlers.

Down at the inn they were hanging bunting.

I saw it from the cliff path — red and white streamers going up between the inn's eaves like the island had decided, overnight, that it had something to celebrate. And perhaps it did. The wireless at the garrison had crackled all week with rumors: the armistice would be signed any day now. Tomorrow. Tonight. Peace, peace, peace — shouted from rooftops by men whose pockets grew fat on every day it failed to arrive.

Corvel's terms. That was the joke nobody but five of us was in on. The whole island would dance in the square for a peace that Corvel had purchased like a side of beef, delayed and doctored until it suited his shipping schedules. They'd sing for him. They'd toast him. Marta's son was dead because of the weeks he'd bought, and the village would hang bunting over the fact of it because grief is heavy and hope is light, and people carry what they can.

Paloma had proposed the route. A twelve-year-old girl with salt-stiff braids and the strategic instincts of an admiral. Run the narrows during the celebrations, when every checkpoint would be watching the bridge and the square instead of the water. Tomas had gone silent when she said it — the particular silence of a man doing sums about guns — and then he'd nodded once, and that nod had settled the matter more thoroughly than any vote.

Today we prepared. Tonight, if the weather held — and the weather, for once, seemed inclined toward mercy — we would run it.

But first there was work, and work is a kind of prayer, so I went down to the cove where the fishing boat lay under her tarpaulin like something sleeping, and I began to make her ready.

* * *

You learn things about boats after fifteen years of keeping a light. You learn that a boat is honest in a way people are not. She will tell you exactly what she is worth, exactly what she can bear, exactly when she means to kill you — and none of it is personal. I have always found this restful.

The boat was old Maren's, or had been, back when Maren fished the north shoals before her hands gave out and her sons moved to the mainland. She was clinker-built, twenty-two feet of oak and stubbornness, named *Petite Vérité* — Little Truth — by some earlier owner with a sense of humor I appreciated. We'd borrowed her with Maren's blessing and a bottle of brandy, and Marta had sworn on her mother's grave that no harm would come to her, which is the sort of oath that makes a boat either safer or more dangerous, depending on your theology.

I worked through the morning while the gulls argued overhead. Checked every plank seam and paid fresh oakum into the two that wept. Oiled the thole pins until they slid like whispers. Scrubbed the bilge, tested the bailer, coiled the lines so they'd pay out clean even in a shaking hand. I found a soft spot near the stem where rot had begun its patient work and drove in a wooden patch with tar and prayer.

The whole time, my hands knew what they were doing and let the rest of me think.

And what the rest of me thought was this: tonight I would put to sea with a child at the tiller and the truth in the bottom of the boat, and somewhere behind us, men with rifles would be deciding whether a drowned courier's satchel was worth our lives. And ahead of us, across forty miles of winter water, a commission sat waiting for documents that might unmake a profiteer and save a peace — or might arrive too late, or sink, or never be believed at all.

Odds. I ran them the way I run the lamp: methodically, without flinching, and without much expectation of being thanked.

By noon the boat was as ready as oak and tar can be made. I stood back and looked at her, and she looked back at me with the flat honesty of all boats, and I said aloud, to nobody, "Well, old girl. One more crossing."

She didn't answer. Boats never do. That's why I trust them.

* * *

Marta came down the cliff path in the early afternoon with a bundle under her arm and her chin set at the angle that meant argument was available but not recommended.

"Oilcloth," she said, holding it out. "Boiled and waxed. Best I could manage." Then, looking at the boat: "Is she sound?"

"She's honest."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer that matters at sea." I took the oilcloth. It was good work — she'd waxed it double at the folds, thinking of spray, thinking of the narrows, thinking of everything I was trying not to think of. "Thank you, Marta."

"Don't thank me yet." She watched me fold the papers into the cloth — the sealed orders, the counterfeit ledger pages, the courier's manifest, everything the sea had given back and we had spent three weeks turning into a weapon. My fingers were steadier than I expected them to be. Fifteen years of trimming wicks teaches a man what his hands are for.

"You've thought about what happens," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"If we're caught? Yes."

"And?"

"And Corvel can't afford a public trial any more than we can afford capture. If they take us, they'll take the boat out past the point and there'll be an accident, and by spring there'll be a new lighthouse keeper who asks fewer questions." I tied off the last knot. "It's cleaner for him that way. Profiteers hate mess."

Marta was quiet a moment. The wind came up the cove and moved her hair off her face, and I saw how tired she was — the deep tiredness of a woman who had buried a son and then dug up his death and made it march.

"My Étienne waited eleven days on that relief ship," she said. "Eleven days. Corvel bought those days the way he buys everything. When Paloma told me what was in those papers, I wanted—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Marta recalibrates faster than anyone I've ever known. "I wanted him dead. I still want him dead. But dead men don't sign confessions, and a hanged magnate is just a martyr with money. So. Papers. Commission. Dawn." She looked at me. "You'll get them there."

"I'll get them there."

"Swear it properly. Not lighthouse-keeper swearing. Real swearing."

I looked at her. Fifteen years I had kept every oath small enough to keep alone. "On my wife's grave," I said. "And on my own name, whatever that turns out to be worth."

Something moved behind her eyes — she knows, of course; she has known since the night she decoded the papers beside me and saw the letters I write and the name I sign them with, or don't — and she reached out and gripped my forearm, hard, keeper to keeper.

"That'll do," she said.

* * *

Paloma found me at the inn at dusk, as I knew she would. She has a talent for finding me that I stopped questioning around the time she discovered my letters and answered one without asking permission. There are betrayals that turn out to be gifts. I have learned to leave room in my life for them.

"Tomas says the garrison's issued rum rations for tomorrow," she reported, sliding onto the bench across from me with the gravity of a war minister. "Double rations. For the celebration."

"Will you drink yours?"

"I'm twelve."

"A tragedy. Double rations wasted."

That got the half-smile I was fishing for. Then it faded, and she studied her hands, and I recognized the posture of someone carrying a question they'd rehearsed.

"Ask it," I said.

"When we're through the narrows." She kept her eyes on her hands. "After. On the open water. If the soldiers come after us — will you tell me to jump?"

The question landed the way her questions always land: precisely, in the exact center of the thing. I put down my tea.

"No," I said.

"But if the boat's too slow—"

"Paloma. Look at me." She looked. Twelve years old and steady as a bearing light. "I have kept a lamp burning for fifteen years so that strangers' boats wouldn't break on rocks in the dark. You think I'm going to put you out into forty-mile water to lighten the load?" I shook my head. "If the soldiers come, we outrun them, or we beach her, or we go into the water together and swim for the kelp line where their launch can't follow. Nobody jumps. Not while I'm aboard. That's not sentiment — it's seamanship. Dead passengers make poor ballast."

She absorbed this with the seriousness she gives to anything I say, which is both flattering and a responsibility I feel daily.

"And the papers?" she asked. "If it comes to the water — do they float?"

"They're in oilcloth, sealed and weighted to sit low in the bilge. If we swamp, they stay with the hull long enough to be recovered. Probably."

"Probably."

"I'm a lighthouse keeper, not a prophet. Probable is the best currency I deal in." I leaned forward. "But here's what I'll promise you, and I keep my promises the way I keep my light: every hour, without fail, whether anyone's watching or not. We get through the narrows. We get the papers east. And then we come home, and Marta feeds us until we can't move, and Tomas complains about the noise, and you pester me until I let you trim the wicks. That's the plan. Everything else is weather."

"Weather can kill you."

"Yes," I said. "So can staying home. Ask the bridge."

* * *

Night came down clear and cold, with a wind out of the northwest that promised to hold. I climbed the tower and lit the lamp and set it turning, because tomorrow night the light would keep itself company for a few hours and I would not be here to introduce them, and I wanted to say goodbye properly. You may laugh. Everyone does. The lamp and I have an understanding that predates most of my friendships.

Then I went back down to the keeper's room, where the typewriter waited under its dust cloth, and I pulled the cloth away, and I sat for a long time with my hands in my lap.

The letters are in the drawer beneath. Fourteen years of them, one for nearly every Sunday since she died, written on her machine in her room, in a voice I only ever used with her. Never sent. Where would I send them? To what address? Grief doesn't take a forwarding order.

Tonight's letter was different, and I knew it was different before I rolled in the paper.

*My dearest Hélène,*

*Tomorrow I sail. You'd laugh at the crew — a child at the tiller who steers better than I ever did, and the truth in the bilge, and me pretending I know what I'm doing. You always said I was braver than I believed and stupider than I claimed, and tomorrow will likely prove both at once.*

*I am going to tell you the thing I should have told you at the start, except you already knew it, didn't you? You always knew. You married a woman wearing a man's coat and you never once said so, you simply helped me buy a better coat and a lighthouse and a life, and you called me your husband in front of priests and magistrates and never once let your eyes flicker. Fifteen years I have signed these letters with nothing, because a signature is a name and my name died with you.*

*Tonight I sign it.*

I wrote the name. My true name — the one from before, the one buried on the mainland in a grave with no body, the one that exists now only in church registers nobody consults and in the memory of a woman eleven years dead. I wrote it slowly, letter by letter, the way you raise something fragile from a long sleep.

It looked strange on the page. It looked like mine.

*If I drown tomorrow, let this be the record: Odile Vane was a fiction, well-made and well-loved, and I regret nothing of him. He kept the light. He kept faith. He found a family in an inn and a daughter in a girl who reads other people's letters and answers them kindly. He was the truest lie I ever told.*

*Keep the lamp for me tomorrow night. I'll only be gone a day or two.*

*Your husband,*

and beneath it, the name.

I folded the letter and put it atop the others in the drawer, and I closed the drawer, and I sat in the dark a while listening to the tower breathe above me.

The secret didn't weigh less, set down. I'd expected it to. But it weighed differently — no longer a stone carried, but a stone placed, a foundation-stone, something built upon rather than borne. Tomorrow the whole world might see the plans of it. Let them read the architecture. I was done hiding the house I live in.

* * *

I went down to the inn one last time before the watch changed, and found them all awake, which surprised me not at all.

Tomas had cleaned his boots. That was how I knew. Tomas's boots are a barometer of his soul, and tonight they gleamed like a chapel floor. He sat by the fire re-wrapping the strapping on his bad hand and did not look up, and I did not ask him what he was thinking, because I had seen that look on soldiers' faces in another life: it is the look of a man who has finished being afraid and has arrived somewhere past it, somewhere quieter, where the only thing left is the doing.

Marta was in the kitchen laying out bread and cheese and hard-boiled eggs against tomorrow — provisions wrapped in cloth, a flask of coffee, the practical liturgy of departure. She caught my eye across the room and nodded once. No speech. We'd had the speeches.

And Paloma sat at the table with the typewriter — she'd carried it down from my room without asking, which is Paloma's entire method of existence — and she was typing. Slowly, with two fingers, tongue between her teeth.

I came and stood behind her. On the page, in uneven capitals, she had typed:

**TO THE COMMISSION OF THE ARMISTICE: THE ENCLOSED PAPERS ARE TRUE. WE SAW THE MAN DROWN. HIS NAME WAS LUCIEN FERRAND AND HE WAS TWENTY-TWO YEARS OLD AND NOBODY CAME FOR HIM BUT US.**

Below that, after a pause in which I heard her decide something:

**FROM THE PEOPLE WHO KEPT THE LIGHT.**

"Lucien Ferrand," I said quietly. "You remembered his name."

"He shouldn't be anonymous," she said. "Not in the record. Records are how the dead get justice." She looked up at me. "You taught me that. Sort of. By accident."

"Most of what I've taught you was by accident."

"Everything good is." She pulled the page from the platen, folded it with the papers-in-waiting, and squared its edges against the table like a small official. Then she looked around the room — at Tomas by the fire, at Marta in the doorway with flour on her hands, at me — and something passed over her face that was older than twelve, the way weather passes over a harbor and leaves it calmer.

"So," she said. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Marta agreed.

"The narrows at full dark, during the fireworks," said Paloma, reciting it like a catechism. "Decoy fire at the garrison. Every eye on the bridge. We run the channel on the flood tide and we don't stop for anything."

"And if we're hailed?" asked Tomas, to the fire.

"We aren't," said Paloma.

There was a silence, and in it I felt the strangest sensation, one I hadn't felt in fifteen years of solitary Sundays and unsent letters: that I was standing inside a family on the eve of a dangerous thing, and that every person in the room would walk into that danger for the others without hesitation, and that I was one of the others. That I had been, apparently, for some time. The discovery did not announce itself. It simply settled, the way a lamp settles once the wick is trimmed right.

Marta broke it by putting bread and cheese in front of all of us, because Marta believes no decision should be made hungry, and we ate together in the warm room while outside the wind held steady from the northwest and the first stars came out over water that would, God willing, carry us east by this hour tomorrow.

I walked home late along the cliff path with the light turning above me, and at the door of the tower I stopped and looked back at the village — the inn's windows gold, the bunting stirring on its line, the whole island asleep on top of a secret it didn't know it was keeping.

Tomorrow night, while they danced for a stolen peace, four of us would go out through the dark to steal it back.

I climbed the stairs, checked the lamp, wound the clockwork, and began to count the hours like heartbeats.

They passed quickly. They always do, on the last night.
