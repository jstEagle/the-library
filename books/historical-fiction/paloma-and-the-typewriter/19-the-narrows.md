## 19. The Narrows

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives on schedule. You would think that knowing it was coming would blunt it, the way a man who knows a punch is coming can roll with it. It does not work that way. Knowing only gives the fear time to find a comfortable chair.

I had been sitting with it since sundown.

The boat lay in the cove below the light, half-hidden under netting and kelp-cut branches, and I had oiled her until my hands smelled like a machine shop. She was Marta's late husband's boat — *the* late husband, the one who had died of a fever and not of anything Corvel could be blamed for, which made her the only thing on this island Corvel hadn't yet put his thumbprint on. Sixteen feet of clinker-built stubbornness named *Étoile du Matin*, which Marta said her husband chose because he liked a joke and never got to make this one.

Paloma sat in the stern with the tiller between her knees, wearing Tomas's oilskin coat with the sleeves rolled back four times, looking like a small admiral who had commandeered a fleet by accident.

"Say it again," she said.

"The narrows." I checked the oilcloth bundle one more time, though I had checked it eleven times already and the twelfth would not improve it. "Half a mile of water between the old fort rock and the Widow's Teeth. At slack tide it's ugly. Tonight, with this swell running out of the southwest, it will be a washing machine full of knives."

"And the garrison?"

"Will be watching the bridge." I hoped. "Every lantern, every rifle, every eye on the celebrations. Nobody watches water they believe is empty."

"Except us."

"Except us."

She nodded, satisfied, as though we had agreed on a recipe rather than a suicide. Twelve years old. In another life she'd have commanded something larger than a fishing boat. In this one, she commanded me, which was more than enough.

We waited for the signal.

* * *

You could hear the armistice celebration from the cove — or what passed for celebration on an island that hadn't seen its men home in four years and wouldn't see most of them ever. The garrison band murdering a march. Fireworks hoarded from God knew where going up over the harbor in bursts of green and red, their light sliding across the black water like spilled paint. Somewhere a gramophone playing the same song twice because nobody had the heart to change the record.

And then, at nine o'clock precisely, the sky above the garrison turned orange.

Not firework-orange. Not the tidy bloom of a Roman candle. This was the deep, greedy orange of a building eating itself, and beneath the band music came a sound I knew from fifteen years of keeping watch against disaster: the crackle-roar of dry timber going up fast.

Marta's work. The hay barn behind the stables, packed with straw and lamp oil and, if Tomas had done his part, every scrap of kindling the inn had quietly gone without all winter. A fire big enough to pull every soldier off the bridge parapet and onto the bucket line. A fire lit by a woman whose son had drowned waiting on a relief ship that Corvel delayed for profit, and who had stood in my kitchen three nights ago and said, in a voice so level it frightened me more than shouting would have: *I have spent five years being sad. I would like to try being useful.*

Bells began to ring at the garrison. Shouting carried across the water, thin and urgent. The searchlight on the parapet swung away from the sea and swept inland, hunting the blaze.

"Go," Paloma whispered.

I pushed us off the shingle and took the first strokes myself, because the mouth of the cove was mine to handle — rocks on both sides, the swell shoving us toward the northern one like a drunk toward a doorway. Three pulls, four, and then we were through into open black water and I shipped the oars and moved forward to the mast while Paloma slid aft to the tiller.

The sail went up with a crack. The wind took us.

* * *

I want to say I was calm. Novelists are permitted certain flattering lies about themselves, and calm-under-fire is the classic one. But the truth is that my hands shook so badly on the mainsheet that Paloma, without turning around, said, "You're making the sail flutter, Odile," in exactly the tone I used with her when she gripped the tiller too hard, and I laughed — actually laughed, a short ugly bark of a sound — and the shaking stopped. Fear shared is fear halved. Fear shared with a child who trusts you absolutely is something else entirely: it becomes fuel.

The fireworks kept bursting over the harbor, and I used them. Every green flare gave me the Teeth — those two fangs of rock at the narrows' throat, black against black until the light touched them. Every red one showed me the fort rock on the far side, low and slick and hungry. Between flashes I steered by memory and by the sound of the water, which is a language I have spoken longer than any other: the deep mutter of open swell, and underneath it, growing, the hiss-and-boom of water hitting stone.

"Hold her a point to starboard," I called. "Then steady as she goes until I say otherwise."

"A point to starboard. Steady." Her voice didn't waver. Her hands, I saw when lightning-less firework-light let me look, were white-knuckled on the tiller and absolutely still.

The narrows opened ahead of us like a wound.

Even in daylight, at slack water, in summer, fishermen crossed themselves before running it. Tonight it was a quarter mile of chaos between two walls of spray. The tide was ebbing against the southwest swell, and where they fought, the sea stood up in pyramids — not waves, pyramids, great heaving cones of water that collapsed without warning and left holes behind them that a boat could fall into and not come out of.

"We can still turn back," I said, and hated myself for saying it, because I didn't mean it and she'd know.

"No, we can't." She said it simply, the way children say true things. "The papers have to reach the mainland before morning. You told me. The commission signs at dawn."

"I did tell you."

"So." And she set her jaw the way Marta set hers, and I understood in one lurching moment that this family had made her out of themselves, piece by piece, and that whatever happened in the next ten minutes, they had built something the war and Corvel and the whole rotten world couldn't take apart.

We went in.

* * *

The first pyramid hit us broadside and threw the boat sideways like a dog shaking off rain. I went down on one knee, grabbed the mast, felt the whole hull groan — that long wooden complaint of a boat being asked to be more than a boat. Water came aboard in a cold slap, knee-deep across the floorboards, and the oilcloth bundle, lashed under my coat, pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Bail!" I shouted, and Paloma tore one hand from the tiller, found the bailer by feel — we had practiced this, God help us, we had practiced in the harbor like children playing — and threw water over the side in steady, unhurried scoops while holding our heading with the other hand and both feet braced against the thwart.

A rifle cracked somewhere behind us.

I turned. On the fort rock — a lantern. Two lanterns. Figures moving, and the flat orange flicker of a signal lamp trying to read our silhouette against the fireworks. Someone down there had not gone to fight the fire. Someone had stayed at his post, and that someone was now very interested in a sixteen-foot boat running the narrows at night with no lights.

Another shot. The bullet went wide — I heard it pass, that small tearing whisper — and then a third, closer, and a splinter jumped off the gunwale six inches from Paloma's elbow.

"They're shooting at us!" she said, and here is the thing I will carry to my grave: she sounded *delighted*. Outraged, thrilled, twelve years old and alive in every nerve.

"Keep your head down and your tiller true!"

"You keep your head down!"

I hauled the sheet in and we hardened up, driving closer to the wind than any sane sailor would, trading speed for angle, putting the fort rock's own bulk between us and the rifles. The boat leaned until the water lipped the leeward rail. Paloma leaned with her, counterweighting, reading the tiller the way she read everything — completely, with her whole self.

And then the sea decided to test us properly.

It came out of the dark on the starboard bow — I heard it before I saw it, a rising roar like a train — the biggest pyramid of the night, standing up where the ebb met the swell directly over the shallow spine between the Teeth. There was no avoiding it. There was only the choice every helmsman dreads: take it head-on and risk pitchpoling, or take it on the quarter and risk broaching.

"Paloma! When I say — bear away hard! HARD!"

"Now?"

"On my word—"

It broke.

Not beside us. *On* us. The world became white noise and white water, and the boat rose under me like something alive, stern lifting, bow plunging into the trough beyond, and I heard myself screaming "*NOW*" and felt more than saw her throw the tiller over. We surfed. That's the only honest word. Sixteen feet of fishing boat rode the broken face of that wave like a toboggan, spray tearing past us horizontally, the fort rock's lantern whipping past to port close enough that I could have thrown a boot at it, and at the bottom of the wave the sea dropped away and we slammed down into the trough with a crash I felt in my teeth, and the mast bent, and something aloft cracked —

— and held.

Everything held. The mast held. The sail, patched three times by Marta's hands, held. The tiller, gripped by two small hands, held true.

We were through.

Behind us the narrows roared on, indifferent, and the lanterns on the fort rock swung wildly, signaling to a garrison that was busy fighting its own fire, and the last rifle shot of the night fell a hundred yards short and sank without ceremony.

* * *

Open water. Real open water, the long rolling southwest swell, and the mainland lights a gray smudge on the horizon where the storm had finally spent itself and left the sky scrubbed clean.

I bailed while Paloma held course. My arms were lead; I bailed anyway, throwing the water over the side in the same steady rhythm she'd used, until the floorboards showed. Then I sat down amidships, in the wet, next to the oilcloth bundle, and discovered that I was laughing. Quietly, helplessly, the way a man laughs when the alternative is lying down and not getting up.

Paloma looked back at me over her shoulder. Her hood had blown back. Her hair was plastered to her face and she had salt crusted in her eyebrows and she was grinning like the sun coming up.

"I steered," she said.

"You steered."

"Through the narrows. Through the *wave*. Did you see the wave?"

"I saw the wave."

"Tomas is going to be so sorry he missed it."

"He'll be sorry about a great many things, mostly the state of his coat." I crawled aft and settled beside her, taking the tiller from her hands only after she'd surrendered it voluntarily, which took some doing. "Paloma."

"Yes?"

"When this is done — when the papers are delivered — there are things I need to tell you. About me. All of it. You deserve to hear it from me, properly, instead of—" 

"I know," she said.

I stopped. The boat ran on. The mainland smudge grew by inches.

"Marta knows too," she went on, matter-of-fact, eyes on the horizon. "She's known since the winter you had the fever and she nursed you. She says a person is who they are, and the rest is just paperwork." A pause. "Tomas figured it out last month. He says nothing, obviously. He barely says anything anyway." Another pause, and here her voice softened, and she reached over and put her small salt-stiff hand on top of mine on the tiller. "I typed you a letter. It's in your typewriter. You can read it when we get back."

"Paloma—"

"It changes nothing," she said. "That's what the letter says. I practiced it."

I am not, as a rule, a man who weeps. Fifteen years of practice at being unreadable will do that to a person. But out there, in the middle of the black channel, with the evidence of a great crime lashed to my chest and a child's hand over mine and the dawn coming up behind us like a promise being kept, I put my head down on my arms against the tiller and let the wind have the sound I made, because the wind keeps secrets better than I ever did.

* * *

We raised the mainland headland at half past two. By three we were in the lee of the point, running quiet under jib alone past the sleeping customs house, and by half past three I was carrying the oilcloth bundle up a wet lane toward the hotel where the armistice commission slept — or pretended to sleep — with a twelve-year-old girl walking beside me because she refused, absolutely refused, to wait with the boat.

"There'll be soldiers at the door," I warned her.

"There were soldiers at the narrows," she said. "Look how that went."

The sentry at the hotel steps straightened as we approached, and I saw him take in the pair of us — a soaked lighthouse keeper and a child in a man's oilskin, dripping on the marble at three in the morning — and decide, visibly, that we were either mad or important, and that mad people were safer to turn away.

I put my hand inside my coat, on the bundle, on the truth that a dead courier had carried and a living family had saved, and I looked the sentry in the eye and said:

"Wake whoever signs things. Tell them the keeper of the Pointe-aux-Roches light is here about the bridge."

Somewhere behind us, far out across the water, the first gray edge of dawn touched the sea — and back on the island, I knew, the ink was already drying on a different signature, in a different hand, and whether the world woke to Corvel's peace or the true one depended entirely on what happened in the next hour.

The sentry looked at us a moment longer.

Then he turned and ran up the stairs.
