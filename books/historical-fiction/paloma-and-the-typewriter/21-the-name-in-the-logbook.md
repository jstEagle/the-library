## 21. The Name in the Logbook

The boat came back to the harbor on the morning tide, and I came back with it, which was more than I had any right to expect.

I had made the crossing twice now — once out through the narrows with a child's hands on the tiller and my heart somewhere behind us in the gunfire, and once back again with an escort of two naval cutters that had no business being so courteous to a fishing boat with a patched sail and a lighthouse keeper aboard. The naval lieutenant had shaken my hand at the quay. He had used a rank I hadn't held in twenty years, and then he'd caught himself, gone pink, and said, "Mister Vane," very carefully, as though setting down something fragile.

I let him set it down. It seemed kinder than picking it up again.

The island looked different from the water. That is a thing you learn only after you've left a place: that it has a shape, a silhouette, a way of holding itself against the sky, and that you never see it while you're inside it. The lighthouse stood where it always stood, white against the gray morning, and below it the village climbed its hill like a congregation rising from prayer — chimney smoke, gulls, the inn's crooked sign swinging on its bracket. The bridge, what remained of it, reached toward the mainland and stopped, the way it had stopped for months, the way it had stopped because a man named Corvel had paid to make it stop.

They had taken Corvel off the island by launch the evening before, under guard, before the ink on the armistice was properly dry. Marta told me later that he had not struggled. He had simply stood on the quay adjusting his gloves, looking at the crowd as if they were weather — something inconvenient, something that would pass. I have thought about that since. Evil, when you finally get close enough to see its face, is almost always wearing gloves.

But I am getting ahead of myself. The boat touched the harbor wall. Paloma jumped ashore with the rope before I could stop her, because Paloma did not wait for permission from anyone or anything, including gravity, and I followed more slowly, the way old men follow children onto dry land — grateful, and pretending not to be.

* * *

I should say something about what I expected, because what I expected says more about me than about them.

I expected questions. Fifteen years is a long time to keep a secret, and secrets rot from the inside; you begin to assume everyone can smell them. I had spent the whole return crossing composing answers. *Yes, I was born a woman. Yes, my name was not always Vane. No, I never meant to deceive you — I meant only to survive, and survival is a habit that looks like deception from the outside.* I had rehearsed it in the voice I use for the logbook, flat and factual, the voice that keeps the sea from meaning anything personal.

And I expected, underneath the questions, the withdrawal. The polite distance. The way a village closes around a thing it doesn't understand — not cruelty, nothing so dramatic, just doors that take a half-second longer to open, conversations that pause when you enter the shop. I knew that distance intimately. I had built my whole life inside it and called it peace.

So when I walked up from the harbor with the satchel — empty now, its contents in the hands of the commission, but I carried it anyway, the way you carry a thing that has become part of your skeleton — I braced myself the way you brace against a wave you know is coming.

Tomas met me at the top of the quay.

He stood there in his coat that didn't fit him anymore, the one Marta kept letting out at the seams because grief, she said, either shrinks a man or broadens him, and Tomas had chosen broad. His face was still marked where Corvel's men had worked him over in the cove. He'd given up nothing, they said afterward. He'd given up nothing and come back walking upright, which is more than most men manage.

He looked at me for a long moment. I waited for the question. Any question. *Is it true? Why? Who are you?*

What he said was: "Your lamp went dark three nights running while you were away. Marta had me up the tower oiling the mechanism, and I'll tell you plainly, Mr. Vane, I don't know how you do it alone. My hands shook the whole time."

That was all. That was the whole of it. He fell into step beside me and we walked up the hill together, two men who had each been broken by an institution and mended by an inn, and neither of us said another word about it, because some mercies are too large to speak aloud and must be delivered sideways, in talk of wicks and oil.

* * *

The village did not stare. That was the strangest part. Word travels fast on an island — faster than boats, faster than telegraph wires, faster than truth, usually — and by the time I reached the square, every soul in Sainte-Marie-sous-la-Falaise must have known what I was and what I had been and what I had carried out through the narrows while their own celebration drowned the sound of the engine. And yet they nodded. They tipped their caps. Madame Fouché, who sold bread and opinions in equal measure and had never once given me the larger loaf, pressed a loaf into my hands without being asked and said, "For the tower. You'll be wanting your strength back," and moved along before I could thank her, which I suspect was deliberate, because Madame Fouché dispensed charity the way she dispensed everything: quickly, and with plausible deniability.

A boy ran past shouting the news from the mainland — the armistice signed, the terms read aloud, the shipping contracts voided pending inquiry, a certain magnate's name appearing in the dispatches beside words like *sabotage* and *conspiracy* and *in custody*. The boy pronounced *conspiracy* with tremendous relish, having clearly practiced. Nobody cheered much. It was too late for cheering; the cheering had been spent on the armistice itself, two nights ago, when the whole island had lit lamps and sung in the square while Paloma and I were being shot at in the narrows. What was left over was quieter. Satisfaction, maybe. The feeling of a debt collected rather than a gift received.

I understood that feeling. I had been living on it for fifteen years.

* * *

The inn door was open, as it always was, as if Marta considered closed doors a form of ingratitude toward the cold. Inside, the smell of onions and woodsmoke and the particular warmth of a room that has been argued in and laughed in and wept in, all within recent memory.

Paloma saw me first. She came across the floor at a speed that endangered furniture and hit me at a height that endangered ribs, and I put my arms around her and stood there like a post in a harbor, taking the impact, glad of it.

"You're late," she said into my coat. "You said dawn."

"The tide said otherwise."

"The tide doesn't keep appointments." She pulled back and looked at me with her chin up, the way she'd looked at me in the boat when the waves were breaking over the bow and I had asked her, God forgive me, whether she was frightened, and she had said, *Of course, keep steering.* "Did they read the letter?"

"They read it." The final letter, the one I'd written on the last night, sealed with the papers, addressed to the commission — the one in which I had set down, in my wife's typewriter's uneven capitals, the whole of it. Not just Corvel and the bridge and the delayed relief ships, but the other thing too. The name I was born with. The reason a stranger had come to this island fifteen years ago and become a man called Vane. I had written it because a lie wrapped around a truth is still mostly lie, and if the truth was going to save the armistice, it deserved to arrive undressed. "All of it. There was a moment — a clerk looked up at me, a long look — and then he went on reading."

"Good," said Paloma, with the terrible simplicity of twelve years old. "It changes nothing."

She had typed those words to me once, on my own machine, in my own room, having found the letters I wrote to a dead woman and never sent. *I know, and it changes nothing.* I had thought, at the time, that it was the kindest sentence anyone would ever compose about me. I had been wrong only in scope. It turned out the whole island felt the same way, and simply hadn't gotten around to saying it.

Marta came out of the kitchen wiping her hands, took one look at me, and said, "Sit down before you fall down," which is the closest she comes to tenderness, and I sat, and there was soup in front of me before the chair had finished creaking.

* * *

It was Tomas who showed me the registry. He did it obliquely, the way he does everything now — he'd learned subtlety the hard way, in places where directness got men shot. He simply left the book open on the bar while he swept, and let it catch my eye.

The inn keeps a registry. Has kept one for forty years; Marta's mother started it, back when the inn took summer visitors instead of smugglers and soldiers. Every guest who ever slept under Marta's roof was written there in her round, unyielding hand. I had stayed at the inn perhaps six times in fifteen years — storm-bound nights, the night of my wife's funeral anniversary when the tower felt too much like a bell with me inside it — and each time my name was entered: *Vane, O., lighthouse keeper.*

Except now the page had been ruled fresh, a new month begun, and at the top, in Marta's hand, dated three days past:

*Vane, O. — resident. Room 2. Long term.*

Not a guest. A resident. Written before I had agreed to anything, before I had even landed, while I was still out on the water being escorted home by the navy. She had simply decided, the way she decided things — the way she had decided to feed me the first night I ever sheltered from a squall at her door, before I could refuse, before I had even understood I was refusing — that I belonged on her books.

"Tomas," I said. My voice did something embarrassing. I cleared my throat and tried again. "I have a house. A tower. I'm not—"

"It's not for sleeping," Marta said from the kitchen doorway, not turning around, her hands busy with something that did not need doing. "It's for belonging. Different thing entirely. Sleep in your tower if you like, you always have. But you'll take your meals here, and you'll sit in that chair by the fire on Sundays, and when winter comes and the light needs two pairs of hands, you'll knock on my door and ask, and I'll send whoever's free." She turned then, and her face was stern and wet at the same time, which is a trick I have never learned. "Fifteen years you've kept that light alone. Alone is a habit, Odile. Habits can be broken."

Nobody had said my name like that before. Not the clerk at the commission, reading it flatly into the record. Not the naval lieutenant, catching himself. Marta said it the way she said *soup's on* — as a fact of the household, requiring no comment, admitting no argument.

"I—" I began.

"Eat your soup," said Marta.

I ate my soup.

* * *

I went back up to the tower in the late afternoon, because the light does not care about armistices, and neither, in the end, do I. The walk was familiar and strange at once — the same path, the same gulls arguing over the same rocks, but I walked it as a man whose secret had been unpacked in public and found, to his astonishment, to weigh less than he feared. There is a physics to shame that nobody teaches you. You carry a thing for years and it grows heavier with every mile, and then one day you set it down in front of witnesses and discover it weighed almost nothing at all, and what was heavy was the carrying.

Inside, the tower smelled of oil and cold stone and, faintly, of other people. Tomas's boot prints in the dust of the stair. The smudge of Paloma's small hand on the lens housing, right where I'd hidden the satchel from the garrison search — she'd touched it afterward, deliberately, I think, the way you touch a place where something important happened. The mechanism oiled by hands other than mine, competently, lovingly, badly in one place where Tomas had over-greased a bearing, and I found I could not bring myself to correct it.

On the desk, the typewriter waited. My wife's machine, her one extravagance, bought secondhand in a year when we had no business buying anything, because she said a keeper's wife needed a witness and paper was cheaper than people. I had written her letters on it for fifteen years. Unsent. Unsendable. A correspondence with the dead conducted in carbon ribbon.

I sat down and rolled in a fresh page.

*My love,* I wrote. *You will want to know how it ended, so: it ended well, which will surprise you, because it usually doesn't, and you always said I planned for the wrong outcome out of superstition. The papers went through. The man who broke the bridge is in custody. The relief ships will run on schedule now, and somewhere a family that would have starved in February will eat instead, and they will never know our names — yours or mine, either one. I find I mind less than I expected.*

*I have been given a room at the inn. Not for sleeping — Marta was very clear — for belonging. I did not know such a distinction existed. You would have liked her. You would have liked all of them, and they would have liked you, and between the four of us we might have managed to be honest a decade sooner.*

*Paloma has appointed herself my apprentice. I did not agree to this. She informed me of it. She says the light will need keeping long after I'm done, and that she intends to learn it properly, wicks and weather and all, and that when she keeps it, she will keep it as herself, whatever that turns out to mean, because I taught her — her words — that a person is whoever shows up when the sea asks something of them.* 

*I am signing this with my true name. Both of them. They are both true now. That is the discovery of my old age, and I got it from a child and a landlady and a broken soldier and a loaf of bread I didn't earn: that a name is not a hiding place or a flag. It's a door. It only matters who's willing to knock.*

Below, in the gathering dusk, the village lit its windows one by one. And at the foot of the tower path, small and quick against the last of the light, someone was climbing the stairs — two sets of footsteps, actually, one heavy and careful, one light and taking them three at a time — and I heard Paloma's voice ahead of the rest of her, calling up the stairwell:

"Odile! Marta says come to supper, and Tomas says the tide table's wrong for Thursday, and *I* say—" she arrived in the doorway, breathless, triumphant, holding a folded page torn from a school notebook, "—I've drawn the narrows from memory. Every rock. You'll want it for the logbook. In case."

In case of what, she didn't say. In case the sea ever asked something of us again.

I took the page from her hands. Behind her, Tomas filled the doorway, patient as ballast. Below, the inn's windows glowed on the hill like a ship at anchor.

"Well then," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised us all. "Show me these rocks."
