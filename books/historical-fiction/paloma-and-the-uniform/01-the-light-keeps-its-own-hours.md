## 1. The Light Keeps Its Own Hours

The lamp at Paloma Point had never once cared what year it was.

Astrid Quintana liked that about it more than she could reasonably say. She had kept it now for eleven winters—through the last years of her husband's life, through the winter after his death, through the first hard seasons of Sef's grief when the girl had come down from the interior with a single trunk and a face like a locked door—and in all that time the light had asked nothing of her but oil, wick, glass, and hours. It did not ask whose flag flew over the customs house at Ferrow Bend. It did not ask which side of the river was winning. It burned for every hull equally: the fishing boats, the mail sloop, the gray patrol craft with their muffled engines, and once—she did not log this—a long black barge running dark and low in the channel at three in the morning, carrying whatever it carried to wherever such things went.

A light is not neutral because it is indifferent, her father had told her once, standing on this same gallery with his hands behind his back as though he were reviewing troops rather than water. A light is neutral because it refuses to choose whom to save. She had been nineteen and had thought this wisdom. Now she suspected it was something else—an apology rehearsed until it sounded like philosophy—but the lamp did not care what she suspected either. At dusk she climbed the ninety-one steps, trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turned the lens, and Paloma Point spoke its slow sentence out over the water: one flash every seven seconds, all night, every night, whether the world made sense or not.

*Paloma* meant dove. Someone far back had named the point hopefully. Astrid had looked up the surveyor's records once, in her early years here, and found the name entered by a man who had drowned two miles downstream the following spring. Hope was a thing you wrote into charts so the water would have something to live up to.

She came down the tower stairs at half past nine, counting them out of habit though she no longer needed to, and found Sef in the kitchen standing at the open window in the cold.

"You'll let the heat out," Astrid said.

Sef didn't move. She was twenty-two, her uncle Tomás's niece before she'd been anything of Astrid's, and she had her mother's dark hair cut short and raggedly, as if by her own hand with sewing scissors, which was exactly how it had happened. "I'm listening."

"To what?"

"The ice." The girl tilted her head. "It's talking different tonight. Looser."

Astrid listened too. Out past the point the river ice groaned and settled in its long winter voice, the sound it made every night, but tonight there was something underneath it—a wetter note, a suggestion of movement where there should be none. Early thaw was still six weeks away if the season behaved itself. Seasons on this river rarely behaved themselves anymore; even the weather seemed to have taken sides.

"It'll refreeze by morning," Astrid said, and closed the window herself, gently, so it wouldn't be a correction. Then she sat at the table, opened the logbook to the day's page, and wrote, in the small upright hand she'd used since she was a girl copying her father's ledgers:

*Feb. 3. Wind NW, freshening toward midnight. Ice groaning, possible pressure ridge forming off the north shoal. Lamp lit 5:41, burning well. No traffic.*

No traffic. Two words that could mean *nothing passed*, or could mean *I saw nothing worth recording*, or could mean *the world may be ending elsewhere but not here*. In eleven years Astrid had written *no traffic* four hundred times, give or take, and each time it had felt less like an observation and more like a small act of faith—the keeper's version of a prayer, addressed to no one, asking only that the emptiness hold.

She closed the book. Sef watched her do it with an expression Astrid had learned to read but not yet learned to answer.

"You're going to say history happens elsewhere," Sef said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to think it very loudly."

Astrid almost smiled. This was their evening liturgy now, worn smooth as the brass fittings on the lens: Sef needling, Astrid deflecting, both of them pretending the ritual wasn't holding up the roof. "History happens elsewhere," she said anyway, because denying it had become its own kind of agreement. "It's happening right now in the capital, in the provincial offices, in every room where men are arguing over maps. It is not happening in my kitchen, and it is not happening in your bedroom, and tomorrow it will still not be happening on these stairs."

"And when it comes here?"

"Then I'll log it," Astrid said, "and move on."

Sef turned away and began, unnecessarily, to rearrange the dishes in the drying rack. Her grief had settled into her body as motion—she was always moving, always fixing, walking the gallery at odd hours, repainting woodwork that didn't need it, taking the dinghy out alone in weathers Astrid pretended not to see. For the first month after Tomás's telegram had reached them—*lost, presumed, regretted*, three words doing the work of a whole war—Sef hadn't spoken above a whisper. Then one day she'd started talking again and simply never stopped moving, as if silence and stillness were the same danger and she meant to starve them both.

Astrid understood. She had buried a husband and, before him, in a way, a father—the man who died in his uniform in a boarding house in Ferrow Bend while the army he'd served dissolved around him like sugar in rain. She knew what the body did with grief it couldn't spend. Hers had gone into the light. Every night, ninety-one steps, one flash every seven seconds: a grief converted to arithmetic, which was the only conversion she trusted.

* * *

The supply boat came on Thursday, late, riding low and listing slightly, which meant the pilot had overloaded her again against the possibility of the channel closing early. His name was Brannoc, and he was sixty if he was a day, with a beard gone to iron-gray and hands that had handled rope so long they'd taken on its shape. He came up the path with two crates of lamp oil, a sack of flour, salt pork, a bolt of the coarse blue flannel Sef used for everything, and the particular careful blankness of a man carrying news.

"Roads past Kessel are closed," he said, once the crates were down and Astrid had paid him from the tin under the floorboard. "Army took the bridge at Vell. Both sides want it, apparently, which means neither side has it."

"That's upriver," Astrid said.

"That's upriver," Brannoc agreed. He wiped his boots longer than the mud required. "They say the fighting's coming down the valley. Come spring, maybe sooner if the frost holds and lets the guns travel." He glanced toward the tower, then away, the way people had learned to glance at lighthouses during wars—as at useful things that might become targets, or witnesses that might become problems. "You've got your duplicates laid in? Oil?"

"Six months' worth."

"Good." He hesitated on the step, and for a moment Astrid saw him decide not to say something, and then say it anyway. "There's talk of new papers. Registration. Keepers having to declare who they served under, kin, that kind of thing. Nothing signed yet." He shrugged elaborately, a man disclaiming his own words. "Talk."

"Talk travels faster than armies," Astrid said. "Thank you, Brannoc."

He touched his cap and went back down through the pines to his boat, and Astrid stood on the step watching the gray water take him, feeling the cold work its way through her shawl, thinking about registration papers and about the trunk in the attic and about the folded wool inside it that she had not opened in eleven years.

That night she logged it. That was the discipline; that was the creed made flesh in ink:

*Feb. 6. Supply boat in, 2:40 PM. Oil, provisions. News of fighting at Vell Bridge, upriver. Roads past Kessel closed. Talk of registration orders for keepers. Unconfirmed.*

She read the entry twice, then added, after a moment, in smaller letters, as though the margin might keep a secret better than the line:

*Elsewhere.*

* * *

But the word *elsewhere* had begun to feel less like a wall and more like a window left unlatched, and Astrid noticed things now that she would have sailed past a year ago.

She noticed how Sef read the newspaper scraps Brannoc brought wrapped around the flour sack—read them standing up, all the way through, twice, her thumb pressing white against the cheap print—and then folded them into the woodbox instead of the fire, which was either thrift or archiving, and Astrid couldn't tell which worried her more.

She noticed that Sef had stopped asking questions about Tomás. All through the first year she'd asked—what was he like as a boy, did he write to you often, why did he go north that last time—and Astrid had answered in the shallow registers available to her: *he was stubborn, yes, monthly, I don't know.* Lately the questions had stopped, and stopping was worse. Stopping meant the questions had gone underground, where they grew sideways and put down roots and eventually broke through the foundation of whatever you'd built over them.

And she noticed, on the third night after Brannoc's visit, that Sef had begun sleeping with her lamp lit. Not the big storm lantern from the boathouse, which would have been practical. The little reading lamp, the one with the chipped green base, burning on the nightstand behind a door that no longer quite shut since the frame swelled in the damp. Astrid passed it going up to the tower at dusk and passed it again coming down at dawn, and each time she thought: *she's afraid of the dark,* and each time the thought arrived accompanied by its twin, quieter and colder: *and I taught her that the answer to fear is a schedule.*

Because that was the truth under the creed, wasn't it. History happens elsewhere—so long as you keep the hours, so long as you log the wind, so long as you convert everything unbearable into oil and wicks and seven-second intervals, the world will agree to leave you alone. It was a bargain. She had struck it with the war herself, years ago, and the war had honored it, more or less, the way rivers honor banks: patiently, and only until the season changed.

On Friday morning the frost held hard and blue, and Astrid walked the gallery rail checking the lens carriage while below her, on the rocks, Sef stood looking downriver—not at the water but along it, toward Kessel, toward Vell, toward the capital she'd never seen—with her collar up and her breath smoking and her whole body aimed like a compass needle at somewhere else.

Astrid wound the clockwork. One flash every seven seconds. The mechanism took its weight and began, as always, to speak.

Below, without turning around, Sef said: "Brannoc forgot something. There was a letter in the oil crate. It's been sitting in my room three days."

Astrid's hands went still on the crank. The river ice groaned, long and low, the way it does just before something gives.

"A letter," she said.

"For the Quintana family," Sef said, and turned, and her face had the look of someone standing on ice they no longer trust. "Sealed twice."
