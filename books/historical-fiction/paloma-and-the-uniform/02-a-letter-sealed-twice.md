## 2. A Letter Sealed Twice

The supply boat came on the last Tuesday of the month, as it always did, riding low in the water with flour and lamp oil and the particular smell of men who had been too long in a cabin together. Sef watched it round the point from the gallery of the tower, one hand on the cold iron rail, and felt the old quickening she always felt—the hope that this time someone would look up at her, that this time the boat would bring something more than goods.

It brought a letter.

She saw it first as a small pale rectangle in the bosun's mittened hand, held out across the gap between gunwale and dock while Astrid signed for the crates below. The envelope was heavy stock, the kind that cost money, and it was addressed not to *Astrid Quintana, Keeper, Paloma Point*—the way official correspondence always came—but to *the Quintana family*, in a hand Sef knew better than her own.

Her uncle's hand.

Tomas Quintana had been dead eleven months. She had watched them lower him into ground so frozen the gravedigger had needed a fire all night just to open the earth, and she had stood beside Astrid with her hands folded like a good girl while the minister said words about service and rest. His handwriting had filled the margins of every book in the keeper's house, had labeled every jar in the pantry, had written her name on birthday cards that arrived late because the river was unreliable. She would know it anywhere. She knew it now, looking down from thirty feet up, through sleet, at four words and a surname.

"Package for you too," the bosun called up cheerfully, misreading her stillness. "Heavy one. Books, by the feel of it."

Sef made herself climb down. Made herself take the crate inventory Astrid handed her without comment. Made herself carry the letter inside pressed flat between two ledgers, where no one could see it, least of all the woman who had taught her that a lightkeeper's business was oil and glass and weather, and nothing else.

She told herself she would give it to Astrid that evening. That was the honest plan, and Sef Quintero—no, *Quintana*, she corrected herself, the way she'd had to correct it since she was nine years old and came to live here; Tomas had insisted, had filed the papers himself, had said a girl should belong to the people who kept her—Sef Quintana was an honest girl. Everyone at Paloma Point said so.

But evening came, and Astrid was tired from the unloading, and the letter sat in the drawer of Sef's desk like a coal that hadn't cooled. And then it was morning, and there were wicks to trim and the log to copy fair, and the letter sat in the drawer. And then it was the second night, and Sef lay awake listening to the wind work at the shutters, and she thought: *if I give it to her, I stop being the girl who found it. If I read it first, I get to know who I am before she tells me.*

That was not an honest thought. She recognized it as such, distantly, the way you recognize weather coming over the water. She got up anyway.

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in winter because the stove never went fully cold, and it was there, at two in the morning, with a single lamp turned low and her coat over her nightdress, that Sef finally broke the second seal.

The letter had been sealed twice. She had noticed this on the boat day and hadn't understood it until now: there was wax beneath the wax, an older seal broken carefully and then covered over with fresh, as if the sender wanted the letter to look untouched—to look like it had come straight from him—when in fact it had been opened somewhere along its journey, read by someone, and resealed. Someone had wanted whoever received this to know it had passed through other hands. Or someone had wanted the passing-through hidden. She couldn't decide which frightened her more.

Inside, folded twice around a smaller enclosure, was a single page in her uncle's hand. Dated eight months ago. Six weeks before he died.

*Sef—if Astrid opens this instead, tell her I am sorry, and that none of what follows is her burden unless she chooses it.*

*I have carried something for thirty years and I find, now that the doctors have given me their estimate, that I cannot carry it into the ground. There is a place under the boathouse floor, third plank from the north wall, beneath the tarred canvas. In it are papers. Affidavits taken at Vellmar's Landing in the winter of the retreat. A muster list. My own statement, signed and witnessed.*

*You will have heard of Vellmar's Landing the way everyone has heard of it—as a tragedy of the crossing, ice taking the barges, God's will or the river's. It was not the ice. Read the affidavits and you will know it was not the ice.*

*I did not fire. I want that written somewhere, even if only here: I did not fire. But I stood on the bank and I heard it, and afterward I helped write the report that made it weather. I helped make it weather, Sef. Thirty years the river has been blamed for what men did on its banks, and I held the pen.*

*Astrid's father knew. He knew and he kept his silence longer than I did, and his silence bought him a uniform and a pension and a daughter who never knew why he drank. Do not hate him. He was afraid the way we were all afraid, and fear is not an excuse but it is the truth of us.*

*The victors are writing their histories now. Whatever they write, it will be cleaner than the truth. These papers are the dirt under the fingernails of that clean page. Burn them if keeping them endangers you both—I release you from them, do you hear me? You are released. But if you can carry them, carry them upriver before the ice breaks. After the break, after the archives close, they will be worth nothing but danger.*

*I loved you both. Forgive me the timing of this love, which arrives, as everything I ever did honestly does, too late.*

*T.Q.*

The smaller enclosure was a key, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire. Old brass, heavy, the teeth worn nearly smooth.

Sef sat in the dark kitchen for a long time. The stove ticked as it cooled. Out in the channel the ice spoke in its long groaning language, plates shifting against each other miles away, a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

She had known her uncle as a gentle man. A fussy man, particular about his tools, fond of bad puns and worse tea, patient with a frightened nine-year-old who arrived with one suitcase and a name she wasn't allowed to keep. He had taught her to read the water. He had never once, in fourteen years, raised his voice.

And he had stood on a bank in the winter of the retreat and listened to people die, and then he had picked up a pen.

The vertigo came slowly, the way seasickness comes—not all at once but in swells, each one worse. Because it wasn't only his guilt settling onto her shoulders. It was the whole architecture of her life shifting underneath it. Every kindness he had shown her was still real—that didn't change—but it was real the way a room is real when you learn the floor above it is rotten. She thought of him labeling the jam jars. *Blackcurrant, August.* His careful block letters. A man who had helped erase three hundred names writing *blackcurrant, august* in his neat hand, year after year, as if order were the same thing as innocence.

And Astrid's father. The uniform in the chest upstairs that Astrid touched once a year and never spoke of. Sef had always assumed it was grief, plain grief for a dead parent. Now she understood it might be inheritance—the kind you don't choose, the kind that fits whether or not you ever put it on.

She should wake Astrid. It was the middle of the night and she should go up the stairs and knock on the door and say, *there's a letter, there's a boathouse, there's a massacre with our name on it,* and let the weight split between them the way they split the wood and the watch-keeping and everything else.

She looked at the letter. She looked at the key.

*Third plank from the north wall.* The boathouse was forty feet away. The tarred canvas would be stiff with cold but it would move. She could know, by dawn, exactly what they had inherited—and then she could decide how to say it, which parts to soften, whether to soften anything at all. Wasn't that also a kind of care? Wasn't there mercy in choosing the hour?

She heard the lie in it even as she dressed warmly, even as she took the lamp and the key and stepped into the blue-black cold. Mercy was what Astrid would call it, kindly, later. What it was now, tonight, alone, was the same instinct that had made a man seal a confession inside a confession: the wish to control what the truth would cost, and to whom.

The boathouse door stuck and gave. Inside, the skiff hung in its slings like a sleeping animal, and the river breathed under the floorboards, and Sef knelt on planking hard as iron and counted. One. Two. Three.

The third plank rose with a shriek of frozen nails.

Beneath it, wrapped in more oilcloth, flat and dense as a paving stone: papers. Bundles of them, string-tied, the topmost cover marked in her uncle's hand—*Vellmar's Landing — TRUE ACCOUNT — DO NOT LET THIS BE WEATHER*—and beneath the bundles, a leather folio stamped with the crest of a government that no longer existed.

She did not open them. Not yet. Her hands were shaking too badly to untie string, and some animal part of her understood that once she read those pages she would never again be the person who hadn't.

Above her, through the boathouse roof, she could see the tower against the stars, and the lamp turning, steady as a heartbeat, sweeping its long arm of gold across the ice. Astrid's light. Keeping its own hours, minding its own business, asking nothing of the world except to be seen and to see.

Sef put the plank back. She buried the papers under it again, nail-shriek and all, and stood in the dark with the key burning a cold shape through her pocket.

Three days, she had kept the letter. Tomorrow it would be four, and tomorrow Astrid would sign the log and trim the wicks and ask her, as she asked every morning, *did you sleep, little bird?*—and Sef would have to answer her with this in her chest.

Or she wouldn't answer. Or she would stand in the kitchen in the grey morning light and set the letter down on the table between the bread and the oil ledger, and watch Astrid Quintana read her father's name in her uncle's hand, and see what became of her family in the space of one page.

The lamp swept overhead. The ice groaned. Sef went back inside, and did not sleep, and waited for morning the way you wait for a wave you have decided not to dodge.
