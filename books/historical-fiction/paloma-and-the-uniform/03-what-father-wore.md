## 3. What Father Wore

The letter sat on the table for the better part of an hour before Astrid touched it.

She had come down at first light, as she always did, and found Sef already awake—Sef, who had to be pried from sleep like a mussel from a rock on any ordinary morning—and understood before she reached the bottom stair that something in the house had changed its weight. The girl stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the ice, and would not look at her. The bread was made. The bread was never made before Astrid came down. It sat there under its cloth like an apology.

"What's this?" Astrid asked, and then she saw it, and did not need to ask again.

The paper was good stock, river-bleached at the edges, sealed twice: once in plain wax, once beneath it in red, the way official things were sealed in the years when officials still believed their work would outlast them. Her uncle Tomás's hand on the front—*To the Quintana family*—in letters that trembled only slightly, the way his letters had trembled ever since the fever that took half his hearing. She knew the hand. She had known it since she was nine years old, copying out her sums beside him while he drafted reports he never let her read.

Astrid set down the lamp. Outside, the light swept its long arm across the grey water and came back empty, the way it always did, the way she had taught herself to believe it always would.

"You've read it," she said.

"Yes." Sef's voice was flat, which meant she had been crying earlier and had spent the night making sure no one would know it. "Three days ago."

"Three days." Astrid pulled out the chair. The wood complained. Everything in this house complained if you listened to it—the stairs, the doorframes, the old keeper's clock that ran four minutes slow because its pendulum had been shortened during the blockade years and no one had ever fixed it. "You sat across from me at this table for three days with that in your pocket."

"I didn't know what to do with it."

"So you decided I wouldn't either." She said it without heat. Heat was a luxury; she had learned that young. But she saw Sef flinch anyway, and hated herself a little for how easily the girl still flinched, three winters after the fever took her uncle, after the war took everything else, after Astrid had promised—out loud, once, drunk on plum brandy at the solstice—that no one in this house would ever be left holding something alone again.

"I was going to tell you," Sef said. "I was waiting until—"

"There is no *until*, Sef. There is just the thing and the telling of it." Astrid picked up the letter. It was heavier than paper should be. "Go on, then. Tell it now. Since you've had your three days of carrying it alone, let me have mine."

Sef told it badly and honestly, which was the best anyone could do with a thing like that. The archive. The boathouse at the old station upriver, or maybe the one below the falls—she'd read it three times and still couldn't say for certain. A muster list. Affidavits. And the word Sef could not say smoothly no matter how many times she said it, so that it came out each time like a stone turned over in the mouth: *the massacre*. At Paloma Point itself, or near enough—the shingle beach below the north headland, in the last autumn of the war, when Astrid was nineteen and keeping this same light for a father who wore a uniform she had never once described to her niece.

"The letter names your father," Sef finished. "It says he was there. It says—" She stopped. Started again. "It says the order came down and he carried it out, or he watched it carried out, and that afterward he wrote everything down and hid it, because he couldn't burn it and couldn't show it."

Astrid looked at the letter. The light swept past the window, and for one rotation the whole kitchen went gold, and then grey again.

"That's not what it says," she said quietly.

Sef stared at her. "I read it four times."

"You read what your uncle wrote. Your uncle wasn't there." Astrid laid the letter down, smoothing it flat with both hands, an old habit from her ledger work, as if truth were a page that could be pressed into legibility. "Sit down. Pour the coffee. This is going to take longer than the walk to the tower, and my legs aren't what they were."

---

But first, the uniform.

It hung in the chest at the foot of her bed—not hung, folded, folded the way garrison men folded kit, corners squared, buttons wrapped separately in a scrap of wool so they wouldn't scratch the cloth. Astrid knelt and lifted the lid, and the smell rose up out of the cedar the way it always did: camphor and cold iron and underneath it something older, something like the inside of the tower at midnight, like the air in the lantern room before the mantle catches.

Grey wool, double-breasted, the collar tabs faded almost to nothing. Her father had been a major of signals in the army of the Republic—which was to say, by the time Sef was born, a man who had served the losing side, and by the time Sef could talk, a word nobody used in front of children, and by the time Sef was fourteen and asking questions, a silence so complete that Astrid had built her whole life on top of it the way you build a house over a well.

She lifted the coat out. It was heavier than wool should be.

Behind her, Sef stood in the doorway. Astrid hadn't heard the stairs. The girl moved quiet these days, the way the grieving move, apologetic even in their own footsteps.

"Is that it?" Sef asked. "The uniform?"

"This is it." Astrid held it up against herself, absurdly, the sleeves hanging past her wrists. "He was bigger than me. Louder, too, though you'd never guess it now, everyone remembering him however it suits them. He used to say a signals officer's whole job was to make sure the right words got to the right place at the right time." Her throat closed briefly. She waited it out. "And then the war ended and the wrong words won, or the right ones lost, depending who you ask, and he spent eleven years here saying nothing at all."

"Why didn't you ever tell me?"

"Because telling you made it yours." Astrid folded the coat back along its old creases, careful, careful, the way he had shown her when she was small enough to be trusted with buttons but not with truths. "That's not kindness, Sef. Don't mistake it. Silence isn't protection. It's inheritance. I was handed his silence the way you're handed his coat, and I put it on every morning of my life, and it fit worse every year, and I kept wearing it because taking it off meant deciding what I was underneath."

"And what are you underneath?"

Astrid laughed—a short, surprised sound, real. "Cold. Usually."

But Sef didn't laugh, and after a moment neither did Astrid, and she sat down on the edge of the bed with the coat across her knees like a sleeping animal.

"He was there," Astrid said. "At the shingle. I don't know what he did—I don't know if the letter tells the truth of it or a version of it—but he was there, and he came home, and he lit this light every night for eleven years, and he never once said the name of that beach in our house. And when he died he left me the light and the coat and the silence, all three, and I have kept all three faithfully, and I told myself that was a kind of virtue. Keeping the light. Keeping quiet. History happens elsewhere, I always said. It happens in capitals, to other people." She looked up. "Your uncle has just informed me that history happened on our beach."

Sef came into the room slowly and sat beside her, the way you sit beside someone at a wake. Below them the sea kept its own counsel. Somewhere out on the point the ice shifted and groaned, a long low note like a struck bell heard through a wall.

"What will you do?" Sef asked.

"I don't know yet." Astrid said it plainly, because lying to this girl had gotten hard lately, and she suspected that was the beginning of family rather than the end of it. "There's a part of me that wants to feed that letter to the stove and keep the light and keep us, exactly as we are, invisible and safe and complicit. And there's a part of me—" she pressed her palm flat on the wool, feeling the ghost of the buttons through the fabric, "—that thinks about my father folding this coat every morning for eleven years, squaring the corners, wrapping the buttons so they wouldn't scratch anything. All that care. All that tending. For a thing he couldn't wear and wouldn't destroy."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It's a coat."

They sat a while longer. Then Astrid rose, and folded the uniform back into its chest along its old lines, and wrapped the buttons in their wool, and closed the lid, and did not lock it, though her hand rested on it a moment as if considering.

Downstairs, the letter lay where she had left it. She did not burn it. She did not answer it. She set the kettle on instead, because decisions keep better with hot coffee, and because outside, far down the grey reach of the river, she had just seen something that made the cold go through her like wire: a boat, running without lights, coming upriver against the current, toward Paloma Point.

* * *

By the time it rounded the headland the sky had begun to snow, fine and dry, the kind of snow that doesn't melt on wool. Astrid stood at the window with the field glasses her father had also left her, and watched the boat come on—small, engine knocking, flying no flag at all, which in this winter was its own kind of message.

"Sef," she said, without turning. "Put the kettle on again. We have a visitor."

"How can you tell?"

"Because nobody crosses the ice-line in weather like this by accident." She lowered the glasses slowly. In the bow, a figure in a long dark coat stood with the particular stillness of a man who has spent his life being saluted. "And because I think I recognize how he stands. My father's regiment stood exactly like that."

The boat came on. The light swept overhead, gold and grey, gold and grey, keeping its faithful hours over water that had not yet learned what was coming.
