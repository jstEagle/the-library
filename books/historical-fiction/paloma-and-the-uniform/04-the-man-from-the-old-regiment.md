## 4. The Man from the Old Regiment

Astrid saw the boat first as a flaw in the water—a dark line where no current ran. She was on the gallery with the logbook under her arm, checking the day mark, when the flaw resolved itself into a hull: a river skiff, painted the gray of old ice, moving against the current with the unhurried patience of something that had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to get there.

She had learned to read boats the way other people read weather. The supply boat came quarterly, heavy in the water, trailing its wake like a skirt. Fishing boats came at dawn and left by noon, their crews shouting to each other across the water because silence frightened them. This one made no sound at all. No engine cough, no hail, no bell. It simply arrived, the way bad news arrives—already there before you understood it was coming.

The man at the tiller wore a civilian coat, wool, good quality, cut for a colder city than this coast. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that is easy to forget a minute after looking away. He brought the skiff alongside the jetty without asking permission, which told her he had decided already that permission would be granted.

"Quintana Point?" he called up, and his voice was pleasant, modulated, the voice of a man who had spent a lifetime being believed.

"This is it," Astrid said.

"I've come about a light." He smiled. "I'm told yours is the only one still burning on the lower river."

"Then you were told true."

He tied off with quick, competent knots—knots she recognized, the knots her father's hands had taught hers, the knots of a navy that no longer existed except in men's fingers. Her stomach went cold and quiet, the way it did before storms.

"My name is Voss," he said. "Emeric Voss. I knew your father. We served together."

There it was, then. Spoken plainly, out over the open water where any gull could carry it. Astrid kept her face still, the way she'd learned to keep the lamp still in high wind—not by force but by giving the wind nothing to push against.

"We don't get many visitors," she said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point of a lighthouse."

* * *

Sef heard the stranger's voice through the floorboards and stopped mid-chore, a dish towel going damp in her grip.

She had been raised on the sound of visitors. The supply boat's captain bellowed; the fish buyers complained; once, two years ago, a lost kayaker had wept gratefully in this kitchen over soup. Voices came up through the old house like water through gravel, arriving softened but legible. This voice did not soften. It stayed exactly itself, every word placed down carefully, like coins on a counter.

"—kept everything, then. Your father was thorough that way. A man who logs the weather every day for thirty years doesn't stop logging when the weather turns historical."

Her aunt's voice, low: "The house keeps what the house keeps."

"And the house would be... where, precisely? Forgive me. Old habit. I spent the war asking after supplies, and it ruined me for small talk."

Sef moved to the window. Through the frost-ferned glass she could see the jetty, the gray skiff, the man in the good coat standing with his hands clasped behind him in the posture of inspection. Not a fisherman's slouch. Not a traveler's fatigue. She had seen that posture only in photographs—in the stiff rows of officers her uncle had never explained, the ones Sef had found as a girl and studied until Astrid took the album away.

She should go downstairs. That was what family did; family stood together in kitchens when strangers arrived. But her feet would not carry her toward that voice, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like shame, that some part of her already knew what the man wanted, and wanted to hear him ask for it from a distance first.

"The papers," the voice said again, patient as tide. "Muster records. Correspondence. Anything with his hand on it, or near it. The provisional commission is compiling regimental histories, madam. They're collecting everything, and they are paying—modestly, but paying—for primary material. Your father's records could be worth a season's oil, a new boat. A future."

Through the floor Sef heard the pause stretch, long enough that she could count it. Then Astrid, and Sef heard something in her aunt's voice she had never heard in four years beneath this roof: fear, folded flat and hidden, like a letter sewn into a coat lining.

"We keep the light," Astrid said. "We don't keep the war."

"What a lovely sentence," Voss said. "You must forgive me for not believing it."

* * *

They gave him tea because refusing would have been louder than offering. Astrid set out the good cups—the ones with the blue rim that had belonged to her mother—and hated herself for the courtesy even as her hands performed it. Hospitality was armor too, her mother had taught her. Feed the wolf at your door and he may leave believing himself your friend.

Voss sat with his spine straight against the chair back, cup balanced perfectly, steam rising past a face that smiled without ever quite arriving anywhere. He praised the tea. He praised the house, the tower, "the remarkable steadiness of the light—I watched it burn all night from the point." He asked nothing directly. He arranged his questions like furniture around an empty space where the real question sat.

"Your niece lives with you now?" he said. "Sefronia. A fine name. Old-fashioned."

"She's my late husband's brother's daughter," Astrid said. "She keeps the house."

"And you keep the books." His eyes went, briefly and precisely, to the shelf where the logbooks lived—forty years of her father's hand, then twenty of her own, spines cracked, ink faded to the color of dried blood. "All of them?"

"All of them."

"A remarkable archive." He turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "Madam Quintana, I'll be plain, since plainness is the courtesy of old soldiers. There are people downstream—people with commissions and letterheads—who very much want to know what your father left behind. Some of them want to preserve it. Some want to control it. And a few—" he smiled again, and this time the smile reached his eyes, which was worse, "—a few simply want it gone. I have taken it upon myself to find out which category applies to this house before someone less gentle does."

"And which category do you apply to yourself?"

"Ah." He set down the cup. "I am a friend of your father's memory. That is a category, though perhaps not one you'll credit."

"He never mentioned you."

"He wouldn't have." Something moved behind the pleasantness—brief, genuine, quickly shuttered. "Your father was careful with names, especially mine. We did things together during the retreat that a man doesn't put into letters. Things that were written down afterward anyway, by other hands, in other ledgers. Ledgers, madam, that may or may not be sitting on that shelf."

Outside, the wind shifted, and the foghorn spoke its low note across the water. Voss glanced toward the sound with what looked, absurdly, like affection.

"You know what I remember most about your father?" he said. "Not the war. The winter after. He came back here, took up the light, and he never once spoke of it. Thirty years of silence. I used to think it was peace. Now I think it was a debt he was servicing quietly, month by month, the way one services a loan one can never repay. And debts, madam, pass to heirs. That's the cruelty of them. You didn't borrow anything, and yet here you are, holding the paper."

Astrid rose and gathered his cup before her hands could shake visibly. "It's a long row back to the channel before dark," she said. "And the ice moves at night."

"Yes," Voss said, standing, smoothing his coat. "It does."

At the door he paused, and for one moment the courteous man was gone entirely, and what remained was older, wearier, and infinitely more frightening for how ordinary it looked.

"Mrs. Quintana. When I was leaving the jetty I noticed your boathouse door has a new hasp. Brass. Recent." He said it gently, the way one mentions a spot of paint peeling. "Whatever you decide to do—or not do—decide soon. Ice breaks early this year. And when the river opens, everything on it moves. Papers. Boats. Men like me. Nothing stays where it was." He buttoned his coat to the throat. "Burn it, if you must. Give it away, if you're foolish. But whatever you do, do it while the water is still hard. After the break, no one will be asking politely anymore."

Then the smile returned, warm as lamplight, and he tipped his head to her. "Thank you for the tea. Your father would be proud of the light. Truly."

She watched from the gallery until the gray skiff became a flaw in the water again, and then became nothing.

Inside, Sef was waiting in the kitchen, the dish towel still twisted in her fists, her face pale in the stove light.

"Aunt Astrid," she said. "What did he mean—the boathouse?"

And Astrid, who had logged wars and buried husbands and worn her father's silences like a second skin for thirty years, heard herself answer with the one sentence she had sworn she would never say:

"Sef. Go down to the boathouse. Now. Before anyone else comes asking."
