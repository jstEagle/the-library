## 5. Ice on the River, Ink on the Page

The boathouse smelled of tar and cold iron, a smell that had been there so long it seemed less like a smell than a fact of the building itself, like the nails or the tide-line stains on the planks. Sef went down to it on the fourth morning after the stranger's visit, because Astrid had told her to fetch the spare lamp glass from the loft, and because Sef had not slept well and wanted to be anywhere but inside her own skull.

The ice on the river made its sounds at night now—a low groaning, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and she'd lain awake listening, thinking about the letter hidden under her mattress, thinking about how many days she had left before she either told Astrid or was caught out. Three nights. She'd counted them like coins.

She found the lamp glass where it always was, wrapped in burlap on the shelf above the rowboat. But as she reached for it her sleeve snagged on something—a nail head, or a hook—and when she turned to free herself, she saw that the loose board behind the oar rack was not merely loose. It had been moved recently. The dust along its edge was broken, swept clean by a hand.

Sef stood very still. The river breathed beneath the ice. Somewhere overhead a gull complained and gave up.

Her uncle Tomás had lived at Paloma Point for eleven months before he died—the winter she was nine, the winter her whole family thinned down to nothing but her and grief. She remembered him as a tall shadow who smelled of pipe smoke and who had taught her to gut fish without flinching. She did not remember him going into the boathouse much. But then, she had been nine. Children see what they are shown.

She worked the board free with both hands. It came away easier than it should have, as if the hinges of it—hinges, she realized; it was a door, small and cunning, set flush into the wall—had been oiled within living memory. Behind it, a cavity lined with oilcloth. And in the cavity, a tin box.

It was heavier than it looked. That was the first thing she noticed, the way weight can be a kind of language. Whatever was in this box mattered enough to be protected against damp, against rust, against time itself. Her fingers were shaking. She sat down on an upturned dory to open it, because her legs had opinions of their own.

Inside: paper. Bundles of it, tied with string gone brittle, wrapped in waxed linen that crackled when she peeled it back. The topmost page was written in a hand she knew—not her uncle's careful schoolroom script but a rougher, hurried version of it, the letters leaning like people walking into wind.

*Affidavit of Marta Iriarte, witnessed 14 October,* it began. *Concerning events at the quarry road above Vellmar.*

Sef read the first line twice. Then she read the date. Then she read the word *Vellmar*, which she had heard only once before in her life—in the sealed letter under her mattress, in her dead uncle's handwriting, in a sentence that had used the word *massacre* the way other sentences used the word *storm*: as something that happens, that cannot be argued with, that one survives or does not.

She read until the light through the boathouse window went from white to amber, and then she stopped reading and simply held the papers, all of them, against her chest, the way you hold something that might bolt.

There were affidavits—seven, eight, more, some signed, some marked only with a cross because the witness could not write. There was a muster list, columns of names and dates, and beside certain names a small notation she didn't understand until she understood it all at once: a dash. Just a dash, where a discharge date should have been. Men who had gone onto a list and off the world between one column and the next.

And at the bottom of the tin, folded separately, sealed in its own envelope, a confession. Not unsigned, not deniable. *I, Tomás Quintana,* it said, *being of sound mind and unsound conscience—*

Sef laughed. It came out wrong, a cracked little sound that echoed in the empty boathouse and frightened her. *Unsound conscience.* He had been funny even in damnation. She had loved him. She was holding his guilt in her lap like a sleeping cat, warm and heavy and purring, and she could not put it down.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing she had felt first in lamplight three nights ago and felt now with the papers actually in her hands: these were not her crimes. She had not been born when Vellmar happened. She had never worn a uniform, never given an order, never stood at the edge of anything worse than the tower stairs. And yet the papers were addressed to her—to *the Quintana family*, which meant her, which meant Astrid, which meant the two of them alone at the end of a peninsula keeping a lamp lit over water where no ships came anymore.

Inheriting someone else's guilt was not like inheriting money or land or even grief. Grief you could carry openly; people made room for it on trains. Guilt you carried like contraband. You felt every eye. You imagined every knock was for you.

And yet—and this was the thought that arrived unbidden and would not leave—if she burned it, she would not be putting the guilt down. She would only be making sure no one ever learned whose it truly was. The men with dashes beside their names would stay dashed forever. The women who had signed with crosses would have crossed nothing.

She repacked the tin. She did not hide the board again quite as carefully as she'd found it. Some part of her, she realized as she climbed the path to the house with the lamp glass swinging forgotten in her fist, had already decided.

* * *

Astrid was at the kitchen table mending a net, and when Sef came through the door without speaking, just stood there dripping river-cold air and holding the tin out like an offering, Astrid set down her needle very slowly.

"Where," Astrid said.

"The boathouse. Behind the oar rack."

Astrid looked at the tin for a long moment. Then she got up, locked the front door, and drew the curtain over the kitchen window facing the lane—an ordinary gesture, done a hundred times for privacy, that suddenly looked like what it was: preparation.

They opened it together at the table, and Sef watched Astrid's face do the thing faces do when they meet a truth they've been outrunning for years. No shock. Recognition, which is worse. Astrid read the muster list with one finger moving down the columns, and when she reached the dashes her finger stopped, and stayed stopped, and her mouth went thin as a wire.

"I knew some of these names," she said quietly. "From Father's letters. From things he said in his sleep, near the end." She looked up. "You found this today?"

"Today. Read the letter three nights ago."

Something moved behind Astrid's eyes—hurt, maybe, at the concealment—but it passed, and what replaced it was almost relief, and that frightened Sef more than anger would have. "Three nights," Astrid said. "Carrying that alone." She reached across the table and took Sef's wrist, briefly, hard. "Don't do that again. Whatever we're carrying now, we carry it split between us. That's the arithmetic of it."

Then Astrid did a strange thing. She went to the dresser, took down the good paper—the cream stock she saved for correspondence with the Lighthouse Board, the paper that said *we are respectable, we are precise, we are not to be dismissed*—and laid a sheet flat. She sharpened a pencil, tested it, sharpened it again.

"What are you doing?"

"Copying." Astrid pulled the first affidavit toward her. "All of it. Twice."

"It'll take days."

"Yes." Astrid's voice had changed. Sef had heard it steady and heard it gentle and heard it, rarely, sharp, but she had never heard it like this—level and hot at once, like iron that has been in the fire long enough to lose all color and is therefore at its most dangerous. "That man who came asking questions will come back. When he comes back, I want it to matter whether he finds this or doesn't. If there's one copy, he burns it and it's finished. If there are two—" She wrote the heading, her letters neat as stitching. "Two copies mean someone has to find us both. It doubles the cost of silence. That's the whole strategy, Sef. Make the truth expensive to kill."

So they worked. The afternoon went down toward dusk and the lamp hour approached and Astrid refused to stop, so Sef climbed the tower alone and lit the light and came back down to find the kitchen table transformed—papers sorted into ranks, the good candle burning alongside the cheap one, Astrid's copying hand having acquired speed without losing its care. Sef sat down across from her, took up the second pencil and the second stack of paper, and began.

They copied in near-silence. Outside, the ice groaned and settled, and once—far off, upriver—something boomed like a door slamming in a large house, and they both looked up, and neither asked what it was. The ice talking. Or the war, advancing downriver toward them at the pace of freezing water.

It was near midnight when Sef copied the last line of the confession—*unsound conscience*, in her own hand now, so that the words were partly hers too—and set down her pencil. Across the table Astrid was blurring the duplicates together: one set sewn into oilcloth, one set being measured against the lining of their coats with a length of thread and a look on her face that Sef had seen her wear only while calculating tides.

"Astrid," Sef said. "Before the ice breaks. We're taking this upriver, aren't we."

Astrid didn't answer immediately. She bit her thread, tied it off, and looked at Sef across the candlelight—at the girl who had hidden a letter for three days and found a massacre in a boathouse and copied a dead man's confession in a fair round hand until midnight without complaint.

"Yes," she said. "We are."

Outside, the ice boomed again, closer this time, and under the boom, faint but unmistakable, came another sound neither of them had heard since autumn: runners on frozen gravel. A sledge, coming down the lane toward the lighthouse, long past any honest hour.
