## 7. Upriver Before the Break

The sledge smelled of fish and tar and the particular cold of things that had been left outside for a long time. Sef sat wedged between two crates of salted pike with the documents sewn into her coat pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat—flat packets of oilcloth, affidavits and muster lists and her uncle's confession, stitched into the lining by Astrid's steady hands over three nights of lamplight.

Astrid sat opposite, wrapped in wool, her face turned toward the river. She had not worn the uniform. It lay folded in the bottom of her pack, and when Sef had asked why bring it at all, Astrid had said only, "Because it's mine to carry now," in a voice that closed the subject like a door.

The driver was a man named Oskar Vey who owed Astrid a debt from some winter neither of them named aloud. He did not ask what they carried. He asked only once, as they left Paloma Point behind and the tower shrank to a white splinter on its rock: "How far?"

"The provisional capital."

Oskar whistled through his teeth, low, the way men do around fires when they mean *that far* without saying it. "Ice breaks in nine days, maybe twelve. After that there's no crossing, no road, nothing but water and patrols on the water." He flicked the reins. "We'll make it if the weather holds and if nobody looks too hard at us."

"Nobody ever does," Astrid said quietly. "That's been our whole life."

Sef looked down at her own hands, chapped from the copying. Nobody looks at us. For nineteen years that had been true, and she had thought of it as safety. Now she understood it was also the thing they were traveling three hundred versts upriver to break.

* * *

They traveled by night and hid by day, which inverted everything Sef knew about time. The nights became long bright corridors of snowlight; the days became dark burrows under spruce boughs or in the root cellars of farms where Astrid's name—or rather names, for she seemed to collect them, Quintana here, Lindqvist there, a woman called Marta in one village who wept briefly at the sight of her—opened doors that would have stayed shut for strangers.

On the fourth night the ice began to talk.

Sef had heard the river groan before, back at the point, but never like this—a deep shifting language underneath them, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep. Oskar drove wide of the main channel, hugging the eastern bank where the ice was older and thicker, and still the sound followed them, rising through the runners into the crates, into Sef's teeth.

"It knows we're on it," she said, and immediately felt foolish.

"Rivers don't know anything," Oskar said. "That's their virtue."

But Astrid's hand found Sef's knee in the dark and stayed there, and Sef understood that the touch meant more than any argument about rivers. Her aunt—she was not her aunt, exactly, she was her uncle's widow, which was a word with no warmth in it, except that somewhere in the last year the wordlessness had filled itself in, quietly, the way snow fills a footprint—her family, then, was afraid too, and was choosing to be afraid beside her.

They slept that day in a chapel with a broken stove, and Sef dreamed of the boathouse at Paloma Point, of lifting the floorboard and finding not paper beneath it but her uncle Tomas himself, sitting in the dark, saying *I'm sorry it was you.* She woke with frost on her eyelashes and Astrid already awake, watching the door, and for a moment Sef almost told her about the dream. Almost. There was so much they had only recently begun telling each other that the telling still required effort, like rowing against current.

* * *

The skiff came at the narrows, where the river pinched between cliffs and the ice went rotten early because of the current underneath. A fisherman named Brandt handed them over the gunwale along with a sack of boiled potatoes and a warning: "Two checkpoints past the bend. They're stopping everyone going up. Looking for couriers, they say." He spat over the side. "Everyone's looking for couriers this month."

The skiff was small and leaky at one seam and moved through open water between walls of broken ice that groaned and shifted as the current shouldered them apart. Astrid took the oars first. She rowed the way she did everything—without flourish, without complaint, with an endurance that seemed less like strength than like refusal to admit weakness existed.

Sef kept watch astern, counting patrol boats.

There were four in sight by midday, gray hulls with gray smoke, moving up and down the channel with the patient menace of dogs working a field. Once, one of them swung toward them and Sef's whole body went cold and certain—they were finished, the papers would go into the river, the massacre would stay an accident forever—and then the boat turned away after a drifting cake of ice, and Sef discovered she had been holding her breath long enough to see spots.

"They can't stop every boat," Astrid said between strokes. "Not yet. Not before the break. That's our whole advantage—that they're stretched thin and the season is against them."

"You sound like you've done this before."

"I haven't done anything before." A grim half-smile. "I've kept a light for twenty years. Everything else is new."

Sewage-smell of fear, potatoes gone tasteless in her mouth, the papers against her ribs: Sef catalogued all of it, the way she'd learned to catalogue the lamp room, wick and glass and brass, each thing in its place. Fear was just another mechanism. It ran whether you watched it or not.

* * *

The checkpoint appeared at dusk on the sixth day, where the river bent west and a timber boom stretched across the narrows, planks laid end to end with gaps just wide enough for a boat—if the boat submitted first to inspection.

A lantern hung from a pole driven into the ice shelf. Two soldiers stood behind sandbags, rifles sloped, bored in the way that frightened Sef most—not cruelty, not suspicion, just procedure, a machine that processed boats the way a mill processes grain. An officer sat behind them on an upturned crate, smoking, a ledger open on his knees.

"Pull to the boom," one soldier called. "One at a time. Names and business."

Sef felt Astrid shift beside her, and understood in a flash of terror that her aunt was about to give them both away—that Astrid, who had spent twenty years being no one, could not lie under pressure the way she could keep silence, that her voice would do what honest voices do, which is tremble.

"I'll speak," Sef heard herself say. "You row."

Astrid looked at her. Something passed across her face—surprise, then pride, then a surrender to both. She shipped the oars and let the current carry them in.

The boat bumped the boom. The lantern light fell yellow across the gunwales, across the potato sack, across Sef's chapped hands.

"Name," said the officer without looking up.

"Sef Lindqvist." Her mother's name, dug up from a grave in her memory. It came out steady, and hearing it steady made her steadier. "This is my mother, Greta. We're going up to Karelstad to my sister's lying-in. She's due any day and my mother won't be talked out of it."

The officer glanced up. His eyes went to Astrid—older woman, plain wool coat, hands blistered from oars—and Sef saw him assemble them into exactly the picture she had given him: mother and daughter, dull errand, no consequence. The papers burned against her ribs. She thought of the affidavit in the third packet, the one signed by eleven fishermen describing what the soldiers did at Molnaya Bend, and made her face into the face of someone who had never heard of Molnaya Bend.

"Lying-in," the officer repeated. "Which sister?"

"Marta. Married name Verhoeven." A name invented whole, delivered with the mild impatience of a woman repeating something obvious. Somewhere inside, Sef marveled at herself. Lying, she discovered, was mostly confidence; the words were the easy part. What mattered was refusing to look like a person with anything hidden, and she had spent her whole life practicing invisibility at Astrid's side without knowing it was training.

"Open the sack."

The soldier pawed through the potatoes. Sef watched him with polite boredom, and the boredom was armor. Under her coat lining, oilcloth and ink waited, and she thought: *you will not find us, because I have decided you will not.*

"Papers?"

She produced their travel passes—the ones forged by the schoolteacher in the third village, whose hand shook so badly Astrid had redone the seals by candlelight. The officer compared them against his ledger, licked his thumb, turned a page.

"Verhoeven," he said again. "Karelstad's full of Verhovens."

"Then we'll fit right in."

It was the wrong thing. She knew it was the wrong thing the instant it left her mouth—one degree too quick, one note too dry—and she watched his eyes come back up off the page and fix on her, really fix, and the machine of procedure stopped and the man behind it started looking instead.

For three seconds the world narrowed to the width of a rifle barrel.

Then he grunted, half a laugh. "Cheeky. Like my own girl." He stamped the pass against his knee, handed it back. "Through on the east gap. And tell your mother"—a nod at Astrid—"the ice won't hold a week. Whatever family emergency this is, it better be worth drowning for."

"It is," Sef said, and was surprised to find she meant it completely.

Astrid pushed off from the boom. They slid through the gap into the darkening channel, and Sef kept her back straight until the lantern was a star behind them, and only then did her hands begin to shake, great useless shaking that made her grip the gunwale and breathe.

"You lied," Astrid said. It wasn't accusation. It was wonder.

"I lied fluently."

"Yes." Astrid pulled the oars through the black water. "I heard. Where did that come from?"

Sef thought about it honestly, watching the ice walls slide past. "From you. From twenty years of learning how to be unseen. Turns out it's the same skill, just pointed the other way."

Astrid didn't answer, but in the dark Sef heard her breathing change—the small catch that meant she was either laughing or crying, and with Astrid it had always been impossible to say which, and lately it had begun not to matter.

* * *

They made the last stretch through the night, trading places at the oars, sleeping in shifts wedged among the ice cakes. Toward dawn the river widened and the current slackened and the air changed—it carried woodsmoke and iron and the many-voiced hum of a city waking, the provisional capital announcing itself before it appeared.

Sef stood up in the rocking skiff to look. Chimneys against the pale sky. Bells. On the far bank, a long building flying the victors' flag, and she knew without being told that its windows held the historians already writing the war the way they wanted it remembered.

"We made it," she whispered.

Behind her, Astrid had gone very still, oars shipped, head cocked toward the near bank as though listening for something under the bells. Then Sef heard it too: footsteps on the ice shelf, unhurried, deliberate. A figure stepped out of the tree shadow at the water's edge, gray-coated, hands visible, and called across the open channel in a voice roughened by years and river wind:

"Astrid Quintana. I know what you're carrying. Let me help you bring it in."
