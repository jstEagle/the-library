## 9. On the Record

**Astrid**

The provisional capital did not look like a capital. It looked like a river town that had woken up wearing someone else's coat—barricades half-dismantled, their timbers already repurposed as benches outside a print shop; flags of the victors hanging from balconies whose owners had not yet decided whether to be proud or afraid. Astrid walked through it with the affidavits sewn into her coat, the thread Sef had used still faintly visible if you knew where to look, and she knew where to look because she'd watched her sew it by candlelight, stitch by stitch, the way you'd close a wound.

The Hall of Records stood at the top of the hill above the river, a grey stone building with columns that had survived three governments and intended to survive a fourth. Ice still held the river below it—a whole winter's worth, groaning in the thaw like an animal turning over in its sleep. She could hear it from the steps. All the way up the stairs, she could hear it.

Sef walked beside her. They had agreed on this much: they would go in together or not at all.

Inside smelled of ink and wet wool and the particular staleness of rooms where paper was older than the people handling it. A queue had formed at the intake desk—petitioners, widows, men with bundles of deeds and claims—and above the desk hung a new sign, painted so recently the letters still gleamed: *COMMISSION FOR THE HISTORICAL RECORD OF THE LIBERATION.*

Liberation. Astrid turned the word over. It fit some people. It had never once been offered to Paloma Point.

"Name," said the clerk at the desk without looking up.

"Astrid Quintana."

That got his attention. He looked up then—young, maybe Sef's age, with ink-stained fingers and the wary eyes of someone who had learned to read faces for danger. "Keeper Quintana. Of Paloma Point."

"You know my name."

"There's a list." He said it quietly, as though the list were something he was ashamed of. There were lists now, Astrid understood. Lists of names to be entered into histories, and other lists of names to be left out of them. She had spent forty years being invisible on purpose. She had not expected to discover how it felt when the invisibility was assigned.

"I've come to file testimony," she said. "Affidavits. Original documents. Concerning the events at Marisol Bend."

The clerk's pen stopped moving. Around them the queue murmured on—deeds, claims, grievances—but between Astrid and the young man there opened a silence with weight to it, like the pause before ice lets go.

"Marisol Bend," he repeated. "That matter has been adjudicated."

"It's been *narrated*," Astrid said. "There's a difference. Narration is what happens when only one side keeps its papers."

She began unpicking the stitches.

* * *

**Sef**

Sef had imagined this moment a hundred times on the river—the hall, the desk, the handing over—and in none of those imaginings did the man behind the desk turn them away. In her imaginings he always took the papers with both hands, reverent, the way you'd take a child.

In the real hall he read the first affidavit with a face like a closed door, and when he finished he set it down flat on the desk and smoothed it with his palm, twice, as if trying to press the words back into hiding.

"These are serious accusations," he said. "Against officers of the former regime."

"They're against *men*," Sef said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "One of them signed a confession. His name is in your muster records. You can verify every line against your own archives—that's why we copied everything in duplicate. That's why we carried it upriver before the ice broke. Because we knew that once the record was written without these papers in it, no paper would ever be allowed to contradict it again."

The clerk looked at her then, really looked, and Sef saw him do the arithmetic of her: too young for the war, too calm for a liar, too tired for anything but truth.

"The commission," he said slowly, "is compiling a history of liberation. Accounts of atrocities committed *by* the old government have standing priority."

"And who ordered Marisol Bend?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The answer was in the room, in the new paint on the sign, in the whole arrangement of the world since the ice had begun to groan: the victors' dead were massacres, and the defeated's dead were regrettable incidents, and the difference between the two words was going to be written by men in warm rooms who had not been there.

Astrid laid the second copy of the affidavits beside the first. Two sets, identical, stitched into two coats, carried up two hundred miles of freezing river. "You don't have to decide today," Astrid told him. "But I'm leaving both copies with you, and I'm testifying aloud, and after I do, you'll find it harder to say you never heard."

Behind the clerk, a door opened onto an inner office. A man leaned out—silver-haired, comfortable, the kind of face that commissioned portraits—and said, "Is there a problem at intake?" and the clerk said, "No, Director. A filing question," and the door closed again.

Sef understood, in that moment, exactly what a filing question was. And she saw the young clerk understand that she understood. Something passed between them, quick and wordless, the way things pass between people who are both pretending.

"Take a seat," the clerk said. "I'll call you when the commission is ready to hear testimony."

They sat on a bench beneath the new sign, and waited, and the river went on groaning below the windows like something being asked, all winter long, to hold its breath.

* * *

**Astrid**

They called her in the last hour of the afternoon, into a committee room with a long table and five men behind it and one chair facing them, placed like a defendant's. Astrid did not sit in it. She stood. She had stood watches for thirty years; she could stand this.

The chairman was the silver-haired man from the door. "Madam Keeper," he said, with courtesy laid on thick as frost. "The commission appreciates civic diligence. However, the events at Marisol Bend fall under the category of wartime misfortune, already addressed in the official narrative. Testimony to the contrary would be—divisive."

"Divisive," Astrid repeated.

"History requires consensus."

"History requires *witness*," Astrid said. "Consensus is just what's left when the witnesses stop talking." She unbuttoned her coat—not the one with the papers; those were upstairs in the clerk's hands now, but the gesture mattered, the baring of it—and she told them.

She told them about her father, who had worn the losing uniform and come home carrying a silence he never set down until they buried him in it. She told them about the letter sealed twice, in her husband's hand, naming the boathouse. She told them about the affidavits, the muster list, the confession, the comrade who came to burn it and instead handed her his own account, dying of what he'd done. She told them about Marisol Bend—how it was ordered, how it was covered, how the order bore a signature that appeared nowhere in the official narrative because the narrative had been built, brick by careful brick, to leave it out.

Her voice did not rise. She had learned from the lamp that a steady light carries farther than a flare.

When she finished, the room was quiet except for the river, audible even here through the tall windows, a long tectonic complaint, the sound of a thing too big to be ignored forever.

The chairman folded his hands. "The commission will take your statement under advisement."

"No," Astrid said.

"Excuse me?"

"You'll enter it. Or you'll refuse it to my face, on the record, with a witness present." She nodded toward the corner of the room, where the young clerk from intake stood with his notebook, having followed the summons without being asked. "He heard every word. Ask him."

The chairman's gaze flicked to the clerk—dismissal forming on his lips, *you may go*—and the clerk, ink-stained, twenty-some years old, born into the losing side or the winning one, Sef had never asked which, said:

"I have it all, sir. Verbatim."

And the ice on the river chose that moment to let go—or to begin letting go—a crack running under the windows like a rifle shot, long and splitting and unmistakable, and every man at the table flinched except Astrid, who had been listening to that sound her whole life, and who knew it for what it was: not destruction. Release.

* * *

**Sef**

They walked out at dusk with no promise in their hands. The chairman had recovered himself by the end—"under advisement" repeated like a charm against witchcraft—and the silver door had closed, and Sef had wanted, walking down the great stairs, to put her fist through something.

It was the young clerk who caught them, hurrying after, in the colonnade where the lamps were just being lit.

"Madam Quintana." He was slightly out of breath. "Your filings—they're logged. Intake log, number 4471. Dated, timed, witnessed. Both copies."

Astrid turned. "Under advisement isn't entry."

"Intake is entry," he said. "It's small. It's the bottom of a ledger nobody reads. But it's *in the record*, madam—it exists in ink under this roof, and ledgers get consulted. Ledgers get *audited*. Someday, someone tracing the official narrative back to its sources will find 4471 and follow it, and when they do—" He hesitated, and then said the rest quickly, like a man stepping off a ledge: "When they do, there will be a trail. I made sure of it. Cross-referenced to the muster rolls. Your testimony, the affidavits, the confession. It's filed against the current, but water—" he glanced toward the river, where the ice was breaking in earnest now, plates grinding and tilting in the blue dusk, the whole winter coming apart at the seams—"water finds its level eventually."

Sef felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the letter arrived sealed twice. Not victory. Nothing so clean. But a foothold. Truth wedged into the official stone like a seed into a wall crack, patient, alive, waiting for weather.

"What's your name?" Astrid asked him.

"Tomas Vey, madam."

"Keeper Astrid Quintana, of Paloma Point. If anyone asks where you learned that a record is a living thing, tell them you learned it from the light."

Tomas Vey almost smiled. Then his face went careful again—he glanced past them, up the steps, where two men in commission coats were descending together, talking low, and one of them, Sef noticed, was holding a telegram slip, and the other was reading it with an expression she had seen once before, on the face of the courteous stranger at their own kitchen table.

Tomas Vey followed her glance, and whatever he saw made him step closer and lower his voice.

"You should leave the city tonight," he said. "And madam—when you get home—keep the lamp lit. Keep it lit every night, whether anyone needs it or not."

"Why?" Sef asked.

"Because lights are the easiest thing in the world to see," Tomas Vey said, "from either direction."
