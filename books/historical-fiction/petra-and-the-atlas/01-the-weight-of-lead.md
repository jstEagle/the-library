## 1. The Weight of Lead

The lamplight in the Delacroix print shop was the color of weak tea, and Paloma had long ago stopped noticing. That was the trick of it—she had learned, without anyone teaching her, that a life could be made invisible if you repeated it enough times. The same lamp at the same hour. The same cases of type along the same wall. The same father bending over the same cutting stone, his back curved like a question mark that no one had ever answered.

She sat at the composing stick with a galley of funeral notices for the parish printer two streets over, who paid late and complained about kerning he couldn't have identified with both hands and a magnifying glass. The letters came to her fingers without thought: *died peacefully, survived by*, the small gray vocabulary of endings. Her hands knew where every character lived. The e's crowded together in their compartment like sheep; the m's and w's took up space and gave nothing back; the ampersands were kept in a drawer by themselves because her father said they were vain creatures.

Outside, February pressed its face against the glass. The Rue des Fondeurs had gone quiet an hour ago—not the honest quiet of a street asleep but the held breath of a street listening. For three weeks now there had been gatherings at the Place Saint-Michel, men standing in circles in the rain, passing newspapers hand to hand like contraband, which some of them were. Banquets had been banned, so the citizens threw dinners instead, and the dinners turned into speeches, and the speeches were printed on presses exactly like theirs, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

Paloma set another line. *In the twenty-third year of his age.* Twenty-three. She was nineteen and sometimes felt the whole century pressing down on her like a hand on the back of her neck, gentle but insistent: *hurry, hurry, something is coming.*

"Your spacing is drifting," her father said, without looking up from his stone.

"It is not."

"It drifts when you think about other things. Your word gaps go wide as doorways." He blew a curl of metal dust off the burin's edge and squinted at whatever he was cutting—a new sort, lowercase g, for a client who wanted a face "modern but not French-modern," which meant he wanted to pretend the type hadn't been made during any particular government, in case governments changed. They changed often enough. Type endured. That was her father's whole philosophy in a single sentence, though he'd never say it aloud. He believed in lead the way priests believed in God: quietly, totally, and with the understanding that belief would outlast every institution that claimed it.

"I'm not thinking about anything," Paloma said.

"You're always thinking about something. You get it from your mother."

He said it the way he always did—flatly, already reaching for the next tool—and she let it pass, because letting it pass was also part of the routine, part of the invisibility. Ten years since her mother died, and the house still arranged itself around the absence like water around a stone. There was her mother's chair by the window, never sat in. There was her mother's side of the type cabinet, kept in order by a dead woman's logic that no living person dared disturb. And somewhere in the shop, in drawers and boxes and corners, were hundreds of pieces cut in her mother's hand, mixed in with her father's until even he could no longer tell them apart. That was the closest thing they had to keeping her.

The burin whispered against steel. *Shk. Shk. Shk.* It was, Paloma thought, the most honest sound in Paris. A press told stories, some of them lies; ink could be made to say anything. But a graver removing metal one thread at a time admitted only what was true: this much removed, this much remaining. No rhetoric in it.

"Father."

"Mm."

"Monsieur Aubry at the corner—the one who sells the oil—says there were soldiers on the boulevard today. Real ones. With the new muskets."

Her father's hand didn't stop, but it slowed, the way a river slows around a rock. "Monsieur Aubry says many things. He said last autumn that we'd all be speaking German by Christmas."

"He wasn't joking tonight. He closed early. He never closes early—he stays open past midnight for the coach drivers."

*Shk,* went the burin. Then it stopped altogether, and the silence that replaced it was louder than the work had been. Her father looked up at last. He was not a handsome man anymore, if he ever had been—too many years bent over stones had reshaped him, shoulders forward, neck craned, eyes ruined at middle distance and sharp only up close, as if the world further than arm's length had become someone else's business. His hands were stained gray to the wrist. Lead got into you, he'd told her once, when she was small enough to sit on his bench and watch. Lead got into your blood, your bones, your dreams. Printers dreamed in mirror-writing. She'd waited years for that to happen to her and was almost disappointed when it did, because it felt less like magic than like catching a cold from the family.

"If soldiers come here," he said, "they will find a print shop that prints wedding announcements, funeral notices, price lists, and the occasional christening card. They will find nothing worth carrying away and nothing worth reading. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That we're boring."

"That we are *unremarkable*. Boring is an opinion. Unremarkable is armor." He went back to the g. "The shops that get searched are the ones somebody remembers. Nobody remembers us."

Nobody remembers us. She turned the phrase over as she picked up the stick again, and found, to her surprise, that it fit her the way her mother's old shawl fit—slightly too large, smelling of someone else, warm anyway. She had built herself out of unremarkable things. Plain brown dress, hair pinned without ceremony, a face she kept pleasant and empty behind the counter when customers came. She could stand in a room and be forgotten before she left it. She used to think this was a flaw she'd learn to fix. Lately she suspected it was a skill she'd learned too well.

* * *

They worked until the church of Saint-Leu struck eleven, and then her father did what he did every night: he walked the shop. It was a ritual she'd watched her whole life and never remarked upon, because remarking on things was against the family religion. He checked the bank of the press, wiped the ink plate, latched the type cabinets—one, two, three, four, five—and paused at the sixth, as he always did, with his hand flat on the wood for a moment longer than latching required. The sixth drawer was at the bottom, near the floor, and it was locked, and Paloma had assumed since childhood that it held debt papers or her mother's letters or simply nothing at all, the way locked things often did.

Tonight his hand stayed a moment longer than usual. Tonight he glanced—at the window first, then at her—and something moved through his face like a fish under ice.

"Bed," he said. "Early start tomorrow. The Aubry woman wants five hundred playbills by Thursday."

"The theater on the boulevard? Father, those plays are—"

"Playbills," he said, "list the names of actors. Actors are not politics. Five hundred copies, large type, and if anyone asks, that is all they will ever have been." He blew out the lamp over the cutting stone himself, leaving her in the smaller light of her own bench lamp, and stood a moment in the dark with his hand still resting on the sixth drawer, a man saying goodnight to something.

Paloma finished her line. *Deeply mourned by all who knew him.*

She put the stick down and looked at the window. Below, the Rue des Fondeurs lay empty under a thin wash of gaslight, and at the far corner, where the street bent toward the boulevard, two figures stood talking with their collars up. Not lovers. Lovers shifted weight; these men stood like furniture. One of them turned his head slowly, deliberately, reading the street the way she read a galley—line by line, looking for errors. The shuttered fronts of the print shops. The names painted above the doors: *Vasseur & Fils. Imprimerie Morel. Delacroix.*

Delacroix. Her own name, four meters tall, in gold leaf gone green with weather. She watched the man look at it, and watched him say something to the other, and watched neither of them write anything down—which should have been reassuring and somehow was not.

When she looked again a minute later they were gone, dissolved into the ordinary dark, and the street was just a street again, and the gas hissed, and somewhere a dog was making its argument with the night. Everything as it had been. Everything, she would remember afterward, precisely as it had been on the last ordinary night of her life.

Her father touched her shoulder on his way to the stairs—two pats, the family benediction—and paused.

"Paloma."

"Yes?"

A pause. She could hear him deciding not to say whatever it was, the little click of a thought being set back in its case. "Lock the bolt behind me. And tomorrow—if a courier comes, or anyone asking after parcels, deliveries, anything of that nature—you send them to me. Not 'we're closed,' not 'come back later.' You come fetch me. Whatever I'm doing. However it looks."

"We never take parcels."

"No," her father said. "We don't." And he went up the stairs, and the floorboards spoke his weight across the bedroom overhead, familiar as her own pulse.

Paloma stood alone in the shop with the cooling smell of ink and metal, and did the thing she had done every night of her working life: banked the lamps, covered the press, squared the loose sorts into their houses. At the bottom of the cabinet, the sixth drawer sat shut and silent in the dark, holding whatever it held.

She latched it anyway—out of habit, out of tenderness, the way you latch a door on a sleeping child—and followed her father up into the dark.

Below her, as she climbed, the city murmured. Rain beginning again, or footsteps, or the sound of a century turning over in its sleep.
