## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The courier came on a Tuesday, in the gray hour when the lamps in the shop had burned down to their last finger of oil and the morning had not yet decided whether to be worth the trouble of arriving.

Paloma heard him before she saw him: the knock of a boot heel on cobbles with a rhythm too deliberate for an ordinary caller. Ordinary callers hurried. They came in from the rain already talking, shaking off water like dogs, wanting their pamphlets or their bills of sale or their wedding notices set before noon. This man did not hurry. His steps had the measured patience of someone paid by the delivery and not by the hour, and they stopped precisely at the door of Delacroix & Fils, which was a name that contained one son who had never existed and one daughter everyone pretended not to notice.

She was standing at the composing stone with a stick of type in her hand—quarto pages for a wine merchant's inventory, forty lines of the dullest prose in the quarter—and her first thought, absurdly, was that she should put it down. As though holding type made her look like what she was. As though anyone looking through that door would see anything but a shop girl at work.

The bell above the door gave its small cracked note. Cold air came in with the man, carrying the smell of wet wool and horse, and behind it, faintly, the smell of the city itself—that low smoke-and-river smell that had thickened over the winter until even the bread tasted faintly of it.

"Delivery." He said it the way other men said *move along*. "For this address."

He held out no letter yet. He stood instead with his hand inside his coat, looking around the shop with the unhurried attention of a man counting things. And there was plenty to count. The press itself, iron-framed and black with years of oil, squatting in the middle of the floor like a patient animal. The racks of galleys along the wall, locked and labeled. The bank of type cases rising to the ceiling, each drawer a small city of lead streets. The paper stock wrapped in oilcloth against damp. The stove, the stool, the stone. Two lamps burning yellow. One girl.

"My father isn't in," Paloma said.

That was true in the way most useful things are true only sideways. Augustin Delacroix was upstairs, three steps from where she stood, sharpening his gravers at the window because the light was better there and because he could hear everything that happened below without appearing to be part of it. She knew he was listening now. She could feel his silence pressing down through the floorboards like weather.

"Not in," the courier repeated. He smiled without warmth. "Then I'll leave it with whoever keeps the place."

From inside his coat he drew the letter.

Paloma had handled thousands of letters in her life—not read, mostly, but handled—and she knew them the way a farrier knows horses. There were letters that arrived soft and swollen from rain, letters gone thin and sharp-cornered from crossing many hands, letters sealed with a dab of cheap gum that any child could pop open. This was none of those. This letter had been built, deliberately, to be difficult. It was heavy paper, cream-colored, and it had been folded once around something rigid inside—a card, perhaps, or a thin sheet of metal—and closed with wax the color of dried blood. The seal had been pressed with a signet she did not recognize: not a crest, not initials, but a circle crossed by lines, a wheel or a compass or a star seen through fog. The address was written across the front in a hand so fine and upright it might have been engraved rather than written.

It was not addressed to Augustin Delacroix.

It was not addressed to Delacroix & Fils.

It read:

*To the keeper of the Atlas — these premises.*

Paloma read it twice before the words agreed to mean anything, and even then they kept slipping, the way a word repeats itself when you stare too long until it becomes a shape and then a sound and then nothing at all. *Atlas.* She turned the word over. There was an atlas of the world on the shelf above the accounts ledger, bought secondhand when she was twelve, its maps colored in the confident pinks and greens of empires. Surely that was all it meant. Surely some clerk somewhere had misdirected a schoolbook order into a print shop on a street that smelled of ink and cabbage water.

But the courier was watching her face, and she understood, with a cold sliding feeling beneath her ribs, that whatever he saw there confirmed something. He had been told what to look for. Perhaps not a name. Perhaps only a flicker.

"The keeper," he said again. "Someone here keeps it?"

"We keep pamphlets," Paloma said. "Bills of lading. Funeral notices." Her voice came out steady, and she marveled at it the way you marvel at ice bearing weight. "I don't know anything about an atlas."

"No?" He held the letter out at last, arm extended, and she saw she would have to take it or let it fall. "No signature needed. Someone's already signed for it, in a manner of speaking." He nodded toward the front of the envelope, and she saw the line beneath the address, smaller:

*By hand of promise. By blood of the house.*

The courier touched his hat brim—the gesture performed rather than felt—and left. The bell rang its cracked note. His footsteps went away up the street at the same unhurried pace, and Paloma stood in the middle of her father's shop holding a letter that weighed almost nothing and seemed to pull downward like a stone.

Above her, the scraping of steel on leather had stopped.

* * *

Her father came down the stairs the way he did everything—without wasted motion, without noise, a narrow man in a leather apron gone shiny at the chest, his sleeves rolled to the elbow over forearms mapped with old nicks from graver slips. He was fifty-one years old and had the hands of a much older man and the eyes of a much younger one, quick and pale gray, always moving, always reading surfaces: the grain of a sheet of paper, the lie in a customer's smile, the mood of the street outside the window. For as long as Paloma could remember, her father had practiced a kind of deliberate dullness, a studied unremarkableness worn like an apron. Be clean, be punctual, be forgettable. Print what is asked; ask nothing about what is printed. In a city where printers had been hanged before and would be again, invisibility was not cowardice. It was craft.

She watched him read the envelope. Watched his eyes travel the line *To the keeper of the Atlas*, and stop there, and go back to the beginning, and stop again.

His face did not change. That was the terrible thing, she thought later. His face did not change at all, and that was how she knew.

"Who brought this?" His voice was level. Almost gentle.

"A courier. Gray coat. Paid to be patient."

"Did he say who sent it?"

"He didn't say anything. He looked at me like he was counting the room."

Her father turned the letter over. The seal caught the lamplight, that dark red circle with its lines crossing. Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise, she realized, but recognition, and beneath the recognition something older and sadder, like water under ice.

"Paloma." He said her name the way he did when he wanted her full attention and could not afford to ask for it loudly. "Go and fetch us coffee from Madame Roux. Take your time. Take the long way."

"It's not yet seven."

"Take the long way," he said again, and there was something in his voice now that she had heard perhaps twice in her life, both times in connection with soldiers, and she went and got her shawl and did not argue, because arguing would have meant admitting she was afraid, and neither of them could afford that either.

* * *

Madame Roux's coffee stall stood two streets over, wedged between a bookbinder's and a shuttered glove shop, and Paloma walked to it slowly, as instructed, and stood in line among the early workers, and listened.

You could read the city like a proof sheet if you knew how. That was the one lesson of the shop she had taken deepest: everything leaves marks. The men ahead of her in line talked low and fast, and the words that surfaced were the words of the season—*wages*, *bread*, *the Chambers*, *banquets banned*, *they've called out the Guard*—and beneath the words ran the tone, which was new since autumn, tighter and hungrier, the tone of people who have stopped complaining and started counting. The printing quarter felt it first, always. Paper was the nervous system of a country. When trouble was coming, it came to the quarter as orders: more pamphlets, faster, cheaper, names spelled correctly, names misspelled on purpose. Her father had learned to feel an uprising in his fingertips months before the newspapers admitted anything was wrong.

This winter his fingertips had been telling him February.

Paloma carried the coffee back through streets still blue with morning shadow, past the guildhall with its flaking lion over the door, past the archway where boys sold newspapers shouting the government's version of events to men who bought them to learn what the government was afraid of. The long way took eleven minutes. She used every one of them, and when she pushed open the shop door she smelled it immediately, before she saw anything: burnt paper. Not lamp oil, not the stove's coal. Paper, and beneath it the sweet resinous ghost of sealing wax.

Her father stood at the stove with a poker in his hand. On the grate, what remained of the letter was curling into black lace, and the wax had run and hardened in little garnet beads among the ash. The envelope's rigid inner thing—whatever card or plate had stiffened it—had been pulled out and lay separate on the stone shelf, half-consumed. Through the charring she could make out engraved lines. Fine ones. Parallel, precise, curving.

Lines like coastlines.

"You burned it," she said.

"I burned a letter addressed to no one we know."

"It was addressed to this house."

"It was addressed to a phrase." He did not turn around. "Phrases can't inherit anything."

Paloma set the coffee down on the composing stone harder than she meant to, and the cups jumped. "Father. *Keeper of the Atlas.* What atlas? We own an atlas. It's on the shelf, it has Napoleon's campaigns in it, it cost four francs—"

"That is a book." Now he turned, and the poker hung loose in his grip, and in the flat gray light from the window his face looked suddenly older than it had at breakfast, older than it had in years. "An atlas is not always a book, little one. Sometimes an atlas is a debt."

"Whose debt?"

He looked at her for a long moment. She knew that look—it was the look he wore when setting a difficult line of type, weighing each character, deciding what fit and what must wait in the case. He was deciding what to tell her, and she watched him decide, and she watched him choose the empty drawer of it, the safe blank space.

"Old business," he said. "Finished business. The kind that stays finished if we're sensible." He tapped the ashes down through the grate with two efficient strokes, then wiped the poker and hung it back on its hook, and just like that the shop was a shop again, the stove was a stove, the morning resumed its ordinary machinery. "Drink your coffee. The wine merchant's pages won't set themselves, and Monsieur Aubry expects his proofs by Thursday."

"And if the courier comes back?"

"He won't come back. Couriers deliver. They don't return unless they're sent." He said it lightly, but his eyes went to the window as he said it, to the street, and stayed there a beat too long.

They worked. That was the liturgy of the house: whatever happened, they worked. Paloma took up her stick and her tweezers and began setting the wine merchant's inventory, *one hundred cases claret, forty of burgundy*, the letters cold and familiar in her fingers, and her father went back upstairs to his engraving bench, and the scrapers and burnishers resumed their small voices, and for an hour the shop sounded exactly as it had sounded every morning of her life.

But she caught herself listening past the sounds. Listening for boots. And twice she rose, ostensibly to check the drying rack, ostensibly to sort misprints, and each time she passed the stove she looked down into the grate, where the ashes of the letter sat in their little black nest, and each time her father's half-burned fragment of metal plate glinted up at her from the stone shelf where he had forgotten—or failed—to destroy it. Coastlines. Fine parallel curves, cut by a steadier hand than his, she somehow knew. A hand she had known once, long ago, and could not remember knowing.

At midday a boy from the Rue Saint-Aubin came with a rush job—five hundred handbills for a political banquet that had just been banned, the irony apparently lost on his employer—and while her father haggled over price at the door, Paloma did a thing she had never done in her life.

She took the metal fragment from the stone shelf and slipped it into her apron pocket.

It was warm still, or she imagined it was. Lighter than it looked. She stood there with it hidden against her hip, heart knocking strangely, listening to her father close the deal in his mild unremarkable voice, and she understood that she had just crossed some line drawn in a substance invisible and permanent, ink that could never be washed from the forme.

Because her father had burned the letter. But her father had missed the plate.

And Paloma Delacroix, who had spent nineteen years learning to be nobody, stood in the falling light of the workshop window with a piece of somebody's secret warming her pocket, and found—for the first time in her careful, careful life—that she did not want to give it back.
