## 4. The Empty Drawer

The letter had been in the house for three days, and it had changed the shape of every room.

Paloma noticed it first in small things: the way her father now stood between the stove and the front door when he worked, as though he might need to choose quickly which one to face; the way he had taken down the framed specimen page above his bench—the one with their family name cut into the header—and put it away in the cupboard beneath the stairs, leaving a pale rectangle of unfaded wallpaper behind like a scar. The shop smelled the same, of ink and oil and the faint metallic sweetness of molten type metal, but underneath it there was a new smell, or rather an absence: the absence of ease.

He had not burned the letter. She knew this because she had watched him try. On that first night, after the courier's footsteps faded on the cobbles, he had held it over the flame of the lamp, and she had seen his hand tremble—not with fear, exactly, though there was fear in it too, but with something older and more stubborn, something that would not let the paper go. In the end he had carried it upstairs instead, and she had heard the floorboard outside her parents' old room—*his* room now, alone—creak twice under his weight, once going in, and much later, once coming out, empty-handed.

He thought she did not know where it was. He thought she had been asleep. But Paloma Delacroix had spent seventeen years learning to read a pressroom the way other people read letters, and she knew that when her father wound his watch before bed instead of after, when he set the bar of the door and then stood looking at it a moment too long, when he left the lamp in the back room burning low instead of pinching it out—he was not sleeping well, and a man who does not sleep well hides things badly.

Tonight he had gone out. That was the strangest part. Her father had not gone out after dark since the letter came; he had invented errands for her instead, sending Paloma to the oil merchant, to the chandler, anywhere the walking might take until full dark, so that no one would mark him abroad while the city seethed. But tonight he had kissed the top of her head at half past eight—an act so unusual that she nearly asked him outright what was happening—and said he had business with Maren at the sign of the Two Compositors, and gone out into the wet street with his collar up and his hat pulled low.

She waited until his footsteps turned the corner by the tannery. Then she counted to two hundred, because caution was a habit like any other, and went to find what he was hiding.

* * *

The letter was where she guessed it would be: inside the false bottom of the candle box in the back room, beneath a layer of stubs and drippings that had not been disturbed in years. He had chosen the hiding place in haste and it showed; the board sat crooked, its edge proud of the surrounding wood by the width of a fingernail. Anyone who had ever packed that box would have seen it. Anyone who had ever been told, age nine, never to touch the candle box because the wax stains—would have seen it.

Paloma lifted the board with both hands, gently, the way one lifts a stone to see what lives beneath.

The letter lay folded on top of a second object, and it was the second object that stopped her breath.

The letter itself she had glimpsed before, or enough of it: heavy stock, foreign postmark, the seal broken and re-melted clumsily, as if her father had opened it and then, in some fit of guilt or denial, tried to make it whole again. The wax held the impression of a rose—not a garden rose but a compass rose, eight-pointed, its petals the rays themselves. Even half-crushed it looked deliberate, looked *meant*, the way a signature is meant.

But beneath it lay a map.

Not a printed map. An engraved plate—a copperplate, small, perhaps the size of a hymnal cover, wrapped in a sheet of rag paper gone soft with handling. She drew it out into the lamplight and turned it over, and the light caught the incised lines and threw them into shadow, and for a moment the plate seemed less like metal than like water, like a pond at dusk with the world written on it in ripples.

Coastlines. A coastline, rather—one long, confident curve running diagonally across the plate, broken by a harbor mouth, and along the shore, cut so fine they might have been made by a needle, strings of tiny letters. She tilted the plate. The letters were backwards, of course—they all were, everything her father had ever taught her lived backwards—but she could read them anyway; she had been reading backwards since she could read at all. A name ran along the coast in a fine italic: *Costa Serena*. And below it, smaller, in a hand she knew almost better than her own, because it was the hand that had taught her hers:

*Plate VII.*

Her father had engraved maps. Not jobbing work, not the trade plates every print shop kept for bills of lading and estate inventories—these were different. She had grown up thinking she knew everything the shop contained. She could have drawn its contents blindfolded: the cases of type arranged in their wooden ranks, the two presses (the old iron hand-press and her father's beloved Washington), the stone litho nobody used, the drawers of ornaments and rules, the paper store smelling of size and dust. There were no map plates in her mental inventory. There had never been map plates.

So this one had been hidden. Hidden from the inspector's visit last month, certainly—she understood that now, understood why her father had spent that whole fevered day moving crates in the cellar while claiming to be sorting damaged stock—but hidden from *her*, too, for longer than that. For years, maybe. Since before the letter. Perhaps since before she could have asked about it.

The letter lay where the plate had rested. She unfolded it, though her fingers had gone cold, and read it through again slowly, because the first time—three nights ago, over his shoulder while he pretended not to notice her reading—she had been too busy watching her father's face to properly read the words.

*To the keeper of the Atlas,* it began. Not to a name. To a duty.

*You will remember the summer of '36, and the work done under your roof by three pairs of hands. You will remember what was promised when the last plate was pulled and the brandy was poured. The time approaches when borders will close like doors, and what we made must go home before there is no home for it to go to. Send it by safe means. Send it soon. The daughter waits, and grows no younger.*

There was no signature. Only the compass rose again, stamped in ink this time, small and precise at the foot of the page.

Three pairs of hands. Her father's, obviously. Whose else? She turned the phrase over the way she turned type in the composing stick, feeling for the nick, the flaw that would tell her how it was cast. Three pairs of hands, in the summer of '36. She had been five. Her mother had been alive.

Paloma set the thought down carefully, the way one sets down something hot, and told herself she would come back to it.

First: the atlas.

* * *

An atlas of hand-cut maps, engraved years ago in peacetime—that was what the letter demanded be delivered, and if one plate lived in the candle box, the rest must live somewhere, and the somewhere had to be near, because her father would not have risked transporting such a thing through streets that had lately grown accustomed to searches.

She began in the cellar, because cellars are where secrets live in print shops. She moved down the stairs with the lamp held high and stood a moment letting the dark sort itself into shapes: the paper store with its reams stacked to the joists, the bindery bench under its shroud of canvas, the crates of spoiled stock her father had been rearranging the week of the inspection. She went through the crates first. Spoiled sheets, mostly—makereadies gone wrong, posters for puppet shows and patent medicines, a stack of funeral invitations for a man whose name she didn't know, printed in a hurry and never collected. Under the bottom crate, wedged against the foundation wall, a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that made her heart knock once against her ribs before she got it open: not plates, only a set of brass galleys, old ones, corroded green at the corners. She repacked them exactly as they had lain, down to the orientation of the rust streaks.

Upstairs again. The shop proper, then, methodically, the way she would hunt a mis-sorted character in a case: by system, not by hope.

The type cases themselves she dismissed early—too shallow, too public. Whatever the atlas was, it was not loose type to be distributed among B's and Q's. The cupboards under the imposing stones: ledgers, dusters, a tin of gall for stopping ink stains, mouse droppings in the corner of the lowest shelf like a grim little punctuation. The drawer under the stone where her father kept his engraving tools—burins, scrapers, the burnisher worn bright as a spoon from forty years of polishing metal smooth. She lingered here despite herself, touching the tools that had touched the plate in the candle box. The roll of felt they were wrapped in bore a stain she recognized: madder red, the same red that colored the sea on the Costa Serena plate. So the plate had been engraved *here*, in this room, at this stone, within reach of her whole childhood, and she had never once known.

That was the thing she kept circling, the splinter in the mind: not that her father had secrets, but that the secret had been built around her, walls raised on either side of her life without her ever hearing the mason at work.

The stone itself. She knelt and looked underneath, feeling along the underside of the slab where the casting was rough, and found nothing but old wax and a dead spider curled like a comma.

It was nearly midnight when she admitted the shop had given up everything it meant to give. She sat back on her heels in the middle of the floor, surrounded by opened drawers and the particular silence of a room that has been searched by someone who loves it, and let herself think instead of search.

Where would I hide a book I loved? she asked herself. Not a book I feared—fear hides things deep, in cellars, in candle boxes. Love hides things close. Love keeps a thing where it can be visited.

Her father's desk stood in the corner by the window: a plain pine desk with three drawers on the right and a shallow shelf of pigeonholes above, where he kept accounts, the rent receipts, the guild dues card, her school reports in their faded ribbon. The top drawer stuck, as it always had, and you had to lift the handle while pulling, a trick she had learned at seven.

She opened it without expecting anything. Receipts. A spare nib. Half a stick of sealing wax. A dried orange peel, saved for kindling in the manner of all careful households.

The middle drawer was the deep one, the one that should have held the atlas if anything did. It held, instead, almost nothing: a spare lamp chimney, a coil of wire, a folded apron gone gray at the folds.

And at the very back, lying flat against the wood as if it had slid there and been forgotten—or as if it had been placed there to look forgotten—

a key.

Brass, small, the length of her little finger, of a pattern she did not recognize: not a house key, not a cabinet key, not the flat warded keys of the paper-store chests. Its bow—the handle end—was cast in the shape of a rose.

Not a garden rose. Eight points, each point a ray, the center a small circle polished bright by touch. The compass rose. The same device that sealed the letter, stamped now in metal instead of wax, and it fit the two halves of the mystery together with a click she felt in her teeth.

She picked it up and it was warm.

No—it was not warm, it was ordinary brass at cellar temperature, but her hand made it warm in the space of a heartbeat, and she sat there on the floor of her father's shop holding a key that hummed in her palm like a struck tuning fork, except the humming was her own blood and she knew it and did not care.

A key meant a lock. A lock meant a place. And a place meant that the atlas was not a rumor in a stranger's letter but a real object, bound and covered and waiting, sitting somewhere in this city of four hundred thousand restless souls in a room with a lock that this little brass rose could open.

She tried to imagine her father placing it there. When? Last week, after the letter came—moving it from wherever it had truly lived, hiding the body of the secret and keeping only its heart in his desk, close enough to touch? Or years ago, in another summer, closing the drawer on it and turning the key over to someone—to *someones*—with a promise?

Three pairs of hands.

She closed her eyes and saw, unbidden, an image she had no right to have: her mother at the big stone by the window, sleeves rolled, a burin in her fist, pushing a line of coastline out of copper the way a plow pushes a furrow. Her mother had died when Paloma was seven, of the fever that took half the quarter that winter, and what remained of her was a wedding portrait, a christening gown in tissue, a smell of violet pastilles that lived nowhere anymore, and a voice Paloma could no longer entirely trust herself to remember. She had never once imagined her mother's hands at work. Hands at rest, yes—in the portrait they lay folded in her lap like sleeping birds. But working, creating, cutting metal beside her father as a partner and not an ornament—

The image would not leave. It had the authority of memory, which is a strange thing to say of a thing remembered never at all.

Paloma opened her eyes. The shop stood around her in the lamplight, familiar and suddenly estranged, every surface doubled now: the shop she had always known, and the shop that had been performed for her benefit, and somewhere in the overlap between them, the true shop, the one with the atlas in it.

She put the key in her pocket.

She did not decide to do it; her hand simply closed around it and then it was against her thigh, through the cloth, a small hard fact beating time with her pulse. Later she would tell herself she had only borrowed it, only meant to return it by morning before her father woke. But even as she told herself that, she knew the shape of a lie when she set it, and this was one: she had set that key in her pocket the way a compositor sets a word that changes the meaning of the whole sentence.

She restored the desk, replaced the candle-box board, banked the lamp. Outside, the rain had started again, soft on the cobbles, and the city beyond the window lay in its uneasy half-sleep, chimneys smoking, patrols passing somewhere to the east with the unhurried rhythm of men who had not yet been given anything to fear.

In the morning her father would come down and open his desk and find the drawer empty, and she would have to meet his eyes across the width of the shop and decide, in that instant, whether to give the key back or tell him she had earned it.

But tonight—tonight she climbed the stairs with her secret riding warm against her leg, and for the first time since the letter came, since the courier's knock cracked the shell of their quiet life, Paloma Delacroix was not afraid.

She was awake. Fully, dangerously awake, the way she felt only when a difficult page came together under her hands and she saw the whole form of it at once. Somewhere in this city a locked door waited for a brass rose, and behind it waited maps her father had cut and a name her mother had signed, and no inspector, no list, no sealed letter from strangers would stand between her and the finding out.

She lay in the dark and listened to the rain and turned the key over and over in her mind, tracing its eight points, until at last she slept—and dreamed, for the first time in ten years, of her mother's hands.

They were cutting letters. The letters spelled a word Paloma could not yet read, but she knew, even dreaming, that it was a name, and that it was hers.
