## 5. Soldiers at the Door

The knock came at half past three on a grey Thursday, when the light through the workshop windows had gone the color of dirty pewter and Paloma was deep in a jobbing order for the notary on Rue des Tanneurs—two hundred calling cards, black ink on cream stock, names that meant nothing to anyone.

She knew before her father moved that it was not a customer. Customers knocked twice, lightly, apologetically, as though the door were doing them a favor by existing. This knock was three blows, evenly spaced, struck with the flat of a fist and the confidence of a man who had never in his life been made to wait.

Her father set down his graver. He did it slowly, laying the tool parallel to the edge of the workbench with the same care he gave to a finished letterform, and Paloma understood that she was watching him arrange himself—that everything which followed, from the set of his shoulders to the pace of his steps across the floor, would be chosen rather than felt.

"Paloma," he said quietly. "Wipe your hands. Stand by the press. If anyone speaks to you, you know nothing about anything except type."

"I don't—"

"You do." He almost smiled. It did not reach his eyes. "You've always known."

Then he opened the door, and the afternoon came in wearing boots.

* * *

There were two of them, though only one mattered. The soldier who entered first was young, red-faced from the cold, a musket slung with the awkwardness of a farm boy handed a weapon too heavy for his opinions. He posted himself by the door and looked at nothing, which Paloma recognized as a mercy or a discipline; either way, she stopped watching him.

The second man was not a soldier. He wore no uniform at all, only a long coat of good dark wool and a hat brushed clean, and this absence of costume frightened her more than any epaulette could have. He carried a leather folio under one arm and a pair of thin gloves folded over it, and when he stepped into the shop he paused just inside the threshold and let his gaze travel the room the way a man runs his tongue along his teeth after biting something.

"Maître Delacroix." He said the name as if tasting it. "Augustin Delacroix. Proprietor."

"My shop," her father said. "How can the Delacroix press serve the government today?"

"Not serve." The man smiled without warmth. "Assist. There is a difference, and I suspect you appreciate differences, being a man of letters." He produced a folded paper from the folio—not a warrant, Paloma saw, not exactly, though it bore a seal and a signature and the particular dense typography of officialdom, all small caps and justified lines. "I am Inspector Roche, Ministry of the Interior. There is an inquiry underway concerning the printing establishments of this quarter. Seditious material. Pamphlets, broadsides, handbills inciting disorder." He let the words sit in the air like smoke. "You will answer some questions. Your daughter may remain. She is your daughter?"

"She is."

"Name?"

"Paloma."

Roche wrote it down. Paloma watched the nib move across the page and felt, absurdly, that he was setting her in lead—that by evening there would be a line of type somewhere bearing her name, ready to be printed a thousand times over.

* * *

The questions began gently, the way a surgeon's knife begins beneath the skin where there are no nerves.

How many presses. How many apprentices—none, since Baptiste left for Lyon in the autumn. What work had they taken in February, March, April. Her father answered each one in the level voice he used for quoting prices, unhurried, precise, occasionally pausing as though to consult memory when Paloma knew perfectly well he had every job ticket of the past year arranged in his head like type in a case, each item in its compartment.

"The Girardin funeral notices," Roche read from his list. "March the eleventh. Five hundred copies."

"Four hundred. The family reduced the order."

"And the tract on the reform of the salt tax?"

"A fair copy of a petition, signed by ratepayers of the third arrondissement, submitted through proper channels. We printed what the law permits: petitions, addresses, matters of commerce." Her father spread his hands, palms up, ink-stained, honest-looking. "We are tradesmen, Inspector. Borders and barricades are bad for business. A republic, a monarchy, an empire—paper is paper. Someone must print the marriage contracts."

Something flickered across Roche's face—amusement, perhaps, or its professional imitation. "A philosopher of commerce."

"A widower with a roof to keep over one child."

It was beautifully done. Paloma stood by the iron frame of the hand press with her hands clasped before her apron and watched her father lie, and marveled, and was afraid. Not because he lied—but because he lied so well, because there was not one seam showing, because the man who had fumbled and gone white as unbleached stock when the courier's letter arrived ten days ago now stood in his own doorway radiating the mild, boring truthfulness of a man who had never had a secret larger than a mispriced invoice.

*When did you learn to do that?* she thought. *And what else have I been watching all my life without seeing?*

Roche turned a page in his folio. "Your establishment appears on a list."

The words dropped into the room like a sort into the wrong box. Paloma heard her own heartbeat. Beside the door, the young soldier shifted his weight and looked harder at nothing.

"A list," her father repeated.

"Of printers whose output merits review. It is a long list, Maître Delacroix. Nearly every house in the quarter is on it. Yours is not near the top." Roche's eyes lifted from the page and settled on her father's face with the patient attention of a man who has learned that people tell you everything with the half-second before they speak. "You will understand that we ask everyone the same questions. And that those who have nothing to hide lose nothing by answering."

"And those who have something to hide?"

"Lose considerably more."

The silence stretched. Rain had begun against the windows, fine and persistent, ticking on the glass like someone patiently sorting type. Paloma became aware of details with terrible clarity: the smell of ink and lamp oil; the brass balance weights gleaming dully on their shelf; a fly walking the windowpane; her father's left thumb pressing against his forefinger, once, a tiny motion she had seen him make ten thousand times at the workbench and had never once thought about until this moment, when she understood it was not a habit but a tell—the motion of a man rolling an invisible pellet of something between his fingers, checking that it was still there.

*He does that when he's afraid,* she thought. *He's been doing it my whole life. I thought it was how he thought. It's how he hides.*

"No seditious material has passed through this shop," her father said. "Not in February, not in March, not ever. You may search the premises. You will find type, paper, ink, and a girl who sets a better line than I do. That is the whole inventory of our lives."

Roche regarded him for a long moment. Then he closed the folio, and the soft clap of leather on leather sounded, to Paloma's strung-tight nerves, like a pistol shot.

"We will take you at your word, Maître Delacroix. For now." He put on his gloves, finger by finger, unhurried. "The quarter is restless. Men are saying things in cafés that they should keep behind their teeth. When the weather turns—and it will turn—houses like yours will matter. Presses are worth more than muskets, in the end. Muskets kill men; presses make them angry first." He tipped his hat, a small ironic courtesy. "Keep it that way. Print weddings. Print invoices. Print almanacs."

At the door he paused, and turned back, and looked—not at her father, but at her. Directly, fully, the way no official had ever looked at her in her life. She had spent seventeen years perfecting the art of being furniture: the quiet girl in the corner of the shop, head bent over the composing stick, invisible as dust in a sunbeam. She had thought herself good at it.

Roche's gaze went through the performance as if it were wet paper.

"Mademoiselle Delacroix." A slight bow. "They say the quiet ones see everything. Do be careful what you see."

Then he was gone, and the soldier with him, and the door shut, and the rain filled the silence they left behind.

* * *

For a full minute neither of them moved.

Then her father crossed to the window and stood with his hand pressed flat against the frame, watching the street below, until the footsteps faded and the inspector's hat appeared briefly between the awnings and was swallowed by the grey afternoon.

"They'll come back," Paloma said.

"Yes."

"With a search warrant next time. Or soldiers instead of one bored boy."

"Yes." He turned from the window. In the dimness his face was hard to read, and she realized she was studying it the way Roche had studied it—looking for the seam, the tell, the truth underneath the trade. "Paloma—"

"On the list," she said. "Our name was on the list before he walked in. He wasn't here to find out if we'd done anything. He was here to see how we'd act when asked." She heard her own voice, low and quick, and did not recognize it. "You knew that. You answered him like a man reciting a lesson he'd prepared."

Her father was silent. Rain ticked at the glass. Somewhere down the street a drum was beating—not a military drum, something looser, a festival rhythm or a warning, impossible to say which, the kind of sound a city makes when it cannot decide what it is becoming.

"There are things you don't know about me," he said at last.

"The letter." She took a step toward him. "The courier. The drawer you emptied before I could look inside. Father, I'm not a child, and I'm not blind, and I am apparently not invisible either, whatever I've spent my life believing. A man from the Ministry just looked straight through me as if I were a window. Our name is on a government list. Whatever it is you're carrying—it's not carrying itself anymore. It's carrying us."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw something in his face that frightened her more than Roche had: not guilt exactly, and not fear, but grief—the particular grief of a man watching a shelter he built get torn open by weather.

"You have your mother's way of asking questions," he said quietly. "She never asked one she didn't already know the answer to. She just wanted to see whether you'd tell her the truth."

"Would you?" Paloma asked. "If I asked you plainly? Would you tell me the truth?"

His hand went to his thumb again. Rolled the invisible pellet. Stopped himself. Behind him, on the shelf above the type cases, the late light caught the spines of the reference volumes, the ledgers, the tin box where the shop's papers lived—and below them, the row of shallow drawers where the atlas had slept for years, one of which she had opened a week ago and found empty except for a key stamped with a compass rose, which now lay against her breastbone under her collar, warm as a second heart.

"Tomorrow," her father said. "I promise you. Tomorrow I'll tell you everything, and you'll wish I hadn't, and then you'll be glad, and then—" He broke off, shook his head. "Tonight, help me pull the formes from the press and break up the type. All of it. Everything set and unfinished goes back in the cases. If they return with a warrant, I want them to find a shop that has printed nothing but calling cards for a month."

"That's destruction of evidence, not innocence."

"It's survival," he said, "dressed as tidiness. Learn the difference, Paloma. It's the most useful thing I can teach you."

* * *

They worked through the falling dusk without lighting the lamps. Her father distributed the type back into its compartments with the economy of long habit, each letter finding its cell by touch, and Paloma cleaned the press and washed the rollers and tried not to think of the key against her skin, of the locker in the old guildhall library, of the thing waiting there with her mother's name inside it.

But the thoughts came anyway, and they came changed. All day—her whole life—she had believed herself to be the still point of the shop, the quiet one, the one things happened around. Now she understood that stillness had been a role assigned to her, rehearsed daily, performed so well that even she had believed it. Her father's smooth lies. Her mother's absence explained in four words and never four more. The drawer locked, the letter burned—or nearly burned—the whole household built like a composed page: orderly on the face of it, and held together, underneath, by pieces of type set in secret.

*I am the daughter of forgers of calm,* she thought, sliding a quoins tight. *Well. Two can keep a secret. One of us just learned how.*

Outside, the drumbeat had multiplied. Other drums now, out of step with the first, and voices in the street, and once a whistle, sharp and brief. The quarter was talking to itself in the language of gathering crowds. Her father paused at the window, listening, his silhouette black against the last grey light.

"Paloma," he said, without turning. "Whatever happens in the coming days—if the streets fill, if the shops close, if men come with warrants or without them—there is a key you found. I know you found it. I have known since the morning the drawer hung crooked."

Her breath stopped.

"Do not go to the guildhall," he said. "Not yet. Not while the inspectors are walking the quarter with lists in their pockets. Promise me a few days' patience, and tomorrow I will give you the whole story—your mother's part in it, and mine, and yours."

He turned. His face was open now, finally, all the seams showing, and it was worse than the smooth mask and better than it too.

"Promise me," he said again.

And Paloma, with the compass rose burning cold against her chest and the drums growing louder in the dark outside, heard herself say: "I promise."

It was the first lie she had ever told him well enough that he believed it.
