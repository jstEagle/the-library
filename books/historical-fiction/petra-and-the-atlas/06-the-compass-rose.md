## 6. The Compass Rose

The key was small enough to lose in a seam of her apron and heavy enough that she felt it there all morning, like a swallowed stone.

Paloma had turned it over perhaps two hundred times since the night she found the drawer empty. She knew its every flaw now: the worn teeth, the faint green bloom of age along one edge, and on its bow—the flat round head—a stamp so shallow she had needed lamplight angled just so to read it. A compass rose. Eight points, the north one longest, and beneath it a letter she had first taken for a *G* until she understood it was a *C*, curled like a sleeping animal.

*Guildhall.* Her father had said nothing when she asked—had gone very still, the way he went still whenever the atlas came up, which was not often but always too often—and that stillness had told her more than any answer would have. He had not told her *no*. In their house, silence was a country with borders of its own, and Paloma had spent nineteen years learning to read its maps.

So on Thursday, when her father went down the hill to deliver the quarterly ledgers to the tax office—a trip that would swallow three hours at least—she tied her shawl, tucked the key into her glove where her palm could warm it, and walked into the city as though she were going nowhere at all.

That was the trick of it. She had learned from watching him: never walk as if you have a destination. Walk as if you are bored. Carry something dull. She carried a wrapped parcel of blank paper, the kind an errand girl might be hauling to some clerk, and she let her shoulders slump under its imaginary weight, and she moved through the print quarter's crooked streets with her eyes down and her thoughts roaring.

The city was louder than it had been a month ago. That was the thing no one said plainly, but everyone adjusted to, the way you adjust to a rising river. There were more men standing in doorways talking low and fast. More chalked symbols on walls—she did not know what they meant, only that someone scrubbed them off by morning and they returned by evening like mold. The cafés near the Rue des Fondeurs had stopped putting chairs outside. And everywhere, everywhere, the presses kept running, because paper did not care what was coming; paper only wanted ink.

She passed the window of Marchand & Fils, where the great iron press stood idle behind glass like a caged animal, and old Marchand himself was out front arguing with a boy about a delivery that had not come. She passed the corner where the lamp had been broken for a year. She passed the wall where, last week, someone had pasted a broadsheet in letters tall enough to read from across the street: *THE PEOPLE ARE THE SOVEREIGN.* By the time she reached the end of the street the broadsheet had been torn down, but the paste marks remained, a ghost of a sentence.

The guildhall library sat behind the Hôtel de Ville like a shy relative, a long gray building with arched windows and a door that most citizens passed without seeing, because it was the kind of door that looked permanently shut. Inside, she knew from her father's occasional errands, was the archive of the printers' guild: contracts, registers, specimen books, the sediment of two centuries of type. It was also, less officially, a place where certain members kept certain things in certain lockers, because a guildhall library was the one room in the city where privacy was considered a professional courtesy.

The door was not shut. A porter dozed inside on a stool, and Paloma's heart performed a small acrobatics routine before she saw the wooden box beside him labeled *KEYS OF MEMBERS — LEAVE HERE*, and understood that the porter was furniture, paid to be present rather than attentive. She signed the visitor book with a name that was almost hers—*P. Delacroix* became *P. Delaunay*—and her hand barely shook at all, and she noted with a strange cold pride that lying had gotten easier, and hated herself a little for the pride.

Inside, the reading room stretched away under a barrel-vaulted ceiling, lit by high windows that poured down columns of dusty gold light. Shelves rose to the vault. The smell was one she wanted to live inside forever: leather, glue, iron gall ink, and beneath it all the sweet dry breath of old paper. Somewhere a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlasted empires. Two scholars bent over a folio at the far table, murmuring. No one looked up.

Along the left wall ran the lockers: a bank of tall wooden cabinets, each door pierced with a small brass grille, each marked with a number in white paint. Numbers in the hundreds. She had no number. She had only a key stamped with a compass rose.

She began at the far end, casually, as though browsing, sliding her finger along the doors. The key was in her right glove now, pressed against her palm. She tried to think how it would work—perhaps the numbers meant nothing, perhaps the lockers were assigned by some older scheme, perhaps—

The third door she tried opened before she even turned the key.

It was unlatched. Empty. But the fourth door, directly below the empty one, resisted—and when she drew the compass-rose key from her glove and slid it in, the mechanism took it with a soft, oiled click, the sound of a thing recognizing its purpose after years of waiting, and the door swung open onto darkness.

Paloma stood very still. Behind her, the clock ticked. The scholars murmured. Dust drifted through the window-light like slow snow.

Then she reached in.

Her fingers found cloth first—oilcloth, folded and refolded, stiff with age. She lifted it out in both arms; it was heavier than she expected, dense as a sleeping child. She set it on the nearest table, in the pool of light from the window, and unwrapped it with hands that had suddenly become clumsy, that had set ten thousand pieces of type without trembling and now could not manage a knot.

Inside the oilcloth was a second wrapping: canvas, tied with cord. Inside the canvas, boards—oak, edge-worn, corners bumped from years of handling. Stamped into the front cover in gold that had dulled to the color of old honey:

*ATLAS DES PAYS OUBLIÉS*

An Atlas of Forgotten Countries.

And beneath it, smaller, in the same gold: *MDCCCXXXIX.*

Nine years ago. She had been ten. Her mother had been alive.

Paloma looked over her shoulder. The scholars had not moved. The porter's snore reached her faintly through the archway, rhythmic as surf. She opened the cover.

The endpaper was marbled—blue and green swirled together like water seen from above, like the earth itself reduced to pattern—and pasted inside the front board was a bookplate she did not recognize: a cartouche bearing a name in a language she could not read, and beneath the name, a motto in Latin. She copied none of it down. She simply looked, the way you look at a stranger's face trying to find your own features in it.

Then she turned the first leaf, and forgot entirely how to breathe.

The map filled the page edge to edge, and it was not printed. It was *painted*. Hand-colored, wash upon wash, the blues laid in with a brush so fine the rivers seemed to shimmer, the mountains shaded in sepia hatching that rose off the page like relief made visible. And the country it depicted—she searched the map's margins for the title, found it in a delicate italic hand: *VALDRESSE*—was a country she had never heard of, in a shape she had never seen, sitting where she knew—knew absolutely, from every schoolroom chart, from every newspaper map of the current war—there should be something else. A province, a borderland, a name in another language. Here it was not a borderland. Here it was whole, entire, sovereign, its towns named in a script like embroidery, its roads picked out in red, its forests stippled in a green so tender it looked freshly grown.

She turned the page. Another country. Another impossible shape. *MIRAVELLE.* Then *OSTROVETZ*. Then a double-page spread of a coastline that matched no sea she knew, its ports named in three languages stacked like verses.

Country after country. Nine, then twelve, then—she counted twice, disbelieving—seventeen maps, seventeen nations, each drawn with the loving precision of a portraitist who has studied the sitter for years. Each colored by hand. Each, she understood with a vertigo that started in her stomach and climbed into her skull, belonging to a world that no longer existed. Not destroyed—*erased*. Redrawn. Absorbed. Their names scraped off the charts the way the broadsheet had been scraped off the wall, leaving only paste-marks, only ghosts.

These were countries that existed now only here, in paint and ink, in a book hidden in a locker in a guildhall library, guarded by a snoring porter and a key shaped like a promise.

She sat down without meaning to. Her knees simply decided the matter for her.

*Who made this?* The question arrived late, absurdly late, because some part of her had known since the drawer, since the letter, since the way her father had gone pale as wet paper. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different climates altogether. She turned to the title page.

There it was, in formal engraved capitals:

*ATLAS DES PAYS OUBLIÉS*
*engraved and coloured by*
*A. DELACROIX*

Her father. His name, in type he must have cut himself—she recognized the letterforms, the particular curl of his *x*, the deep sturdy bowl of his *D*. She touched the letters with one fingertip, the way you touch a face in a photograph.

But the title page did not stop there. Below his name, in smaller capitals, in the same engraving, the same hand:

*and M. DELACROIX*

Her mother.

Paloma's own name was Marguerite—no. Her mother's name had been Marguerite. *Marguerite Delacroix, née Vasseur.* Dead ten years this autumn, buried on a day of cold rain that Paloma remembered mostly as the sound of rain on the coffin lid, a sound she had never been able to describe to anyone without feeling obscene.

*M. Delacroix.*

Co-engraver. Listed. Named. On a title page of a book her father had hidden in a locked drawer and never once mentioned in ten years.

The vertigo came back, worse now, a tilting of the floor of the world. She gripped the table's edge. The dust motes wheeled in the golden light. Somewhere in the stacks a page turned with a sound like a wingbeat.

She had thought the secret was the atlas. She had thought the secret was the letter, the exile, the promise—whatever promise it was that had made her father burn papers at midnight with his hands shaking. Those secrets were large, but they were *his*, and she had grown up inside the weather of his silences; she knew how to stand in them.

This was different. This was a secret with her mother's name on it.

She forced herself to keep turning pages, because stopping felt dangerous, because her hands needed occupation or they would shake apart. Maps of mountains with passes named for the people who used them. A city plan, exquisite, every street labeled, every public garden shaded in a green dotted with tiny painted trees. A map of languages, of all things—regions tinted by tongue spoken, borders dissolving into gradients, as though speech itself were weather. Whoever had conceived these plates had not been making a commercial atlas. Commercial atlases showed what buyers already believed. These maps showed what had been *lost*, lovingly, deliberately, as though the makers had known even then—in 1839, in peacetime, in the long golden afternoon before everything changed—that the losing was coming.

And then, on the map of Valdresse, in the lower margin, she saw the handwriting.

Not engraved. Written. In pencil, faint, in a quick slanting script she recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall: her father's. She had watched that hand cut punches and correct proofs her whole life; she knew its temper. The note read:

*V. asked that the eastern valleys be shown as they were before '15. Done against my better judgment. Done anyway.*

*V.* Paloma stared at the letters. The letter had come sealed, unsigned except by a law firm's clerk—but the courier had said a name, hadn't he? No. The letter itself had named no one. Only *the keeper of the Atlas*, and the debt, and the deadline that was now fewer than five weeks away.

She turned more pages, hunting the margins now, and found more notes—her father's pencil scattered through the atlas like footprints: corrections, jokes, once a small sketch of a dog in the corner of a map of Ostrovetz, rendered with such affectionate precision that Paloma felt her throat close. Her father had *lived* in this book once. This was not a commission he had grudgingly fulfilled. This was a summer, or several summers, of his life, bound in oak boards.

And threaded among his notes—she found them on the fourth map, then the ninth, then the eleventh—another hand. Rounder. Slower. With little flourishes on the descenders, the *g*s and *y*s looping like the tails of kites.

She did not know whose hand it was. She told herself she did not know whose hand it was.

But her mother had been a printer's daughter before she was a printer's wife; Paloma had seen her writing exactly once, on the flyleaf of a psalter that had survived the general burning of papers after the funeral, and she had been nine years old, and she had not known to memorize it, and she had hated herself for ten years for not memorizing it.

The round hand, on the map of Miravelle, in the margin, in faded brown ink:

*Coloured this plate by candlelight while A. slept. The blue is wrong in the northeast bay but I cannot bear to scrape it. Let it stand as proof we worked by imperfect light and loved the work anyway.*

Paloma read it four times. On the fourth reading, tears dropped onto the page—one, two—and she caught the third with her sleeve, horrified, blotting at the first two with the oilcloth until the spots of moisture bloomed and faded in the old paper like small weather passing.

*I cannot bear to scrape it.*

She sat in the golden column of light with the atlas open in front of her and grieved, freshly, stupidly, ten years late, in a public reading room where grief was not on the catalogue. Her mother had painted maps. Her mother had stayed up by candlelight getting the blue wrong and refusing to fix it. Her mother had been a person—not the smoothed-over absence her father's silence had worn into the shape of a person, but a woman with opinions about pigment and a sense of humor and tired eyes at midnight.

And her father had locked her away in a drawer for ten years.

No. Paloma corrected herself, pressing her knuckles to her mouth. He had locked her away to *protect* her—or to protect the book, or the promise, or whatever web connected a dead engraver, an exiled foreigner, and a family that survived by being unremarkable. Protection and erasure wore the same coat. She was only beginning to learn to tell them apart.

The clock struck three. The sound went through her like cold water. Her father would be home by half past, and the atlas could not leave the building—could it? Could it? She looked at the great object on the table, seventeen painted countries, and felt the full absurdity of what she was contemplating. Smuggling it out under her shawl. Carrying a treason-shaped thing through streets full of chalked symbols and torn-down broadsheets.

No. Not today. Today she did something harder: she closed the book, and rewrapped it, and put it back in the dark locker, and turned the key, and felt the click in her chest like a small door closing on her own heart.

But she did not leave empty-handed.

At the far end of the reading room, unattended, stood the copying press and a stack of thin tissue paper—the kind clerks used for taking impressions of documents. Paloma had grown up with impression-paper; she knew its appetite for ink, its patience. While the porter snored and the scholars argued softly over their folio, she opened the atlas one last time—to the map of Valdresse, the first country, the one with her father's note about the eastern valleys—and she laid a sheet of tissue over the margin notes, both hands, both scripts, and rubbed gently with the side of her pencil stub until the graphite ghosted the words up through the paper.

Three notes. Her father's twice. The round hand once.

She folded the tissue into eighths and slid it into her glove, where the key had lived, where it was warm now from her skin.

Walking home through the loudening streets, past the paste-ghosts of sentences and the shuttered café chairs, Paloma held her arm close to her side as though carrying a bird. She had come looking for an atlas and found instead a sentence in her mother's handwriting, and she knew—with the certainty of a compositor who knows a line is justified before the gauge confirms it—that tonight, when the shop was dark and her father slept, she would take the atlas home in her mind, sheet by sheet, note by note, and read every margin in that book until she had her mother's hand by heart.

She did not yet know that the round hand had written more than margins. She did not yet know that somewhere in those seventeen impossible countries waited a dedication, sewn into the binding like a letter into a coat.

She only knew that the blue in the northeast bay of Miravelle was wrong, and that her mother had let it stand, and that Paloma loved her completely for it.

* * *

Her father was in the shop when she returned, and he was not alone.

Through the gap in the shutters—two fingers' width, the gap he always left to watch the street—Paloma saw him standing behind the composing stone with his ledger open, wearing his face for strangers: pleasant, slightly dull, a man with nothing in his head but orders and prices. Across the stone from him stood a man she had never seen, in a gray coat too good for the quarter, holding his hat in both hands like a student.

They were speaking quietly. She could not hear the words.

But she saw her father's hand, resting on the open ledger, and she saw that the fingers had gone white at the tips, pressing into the paper as though to hold the page down against a wind only he could feel.

Paloma slipped in through the back, quietly, and stood in the doorway of the stairwell with her parcel of imaginary paper in her arms, and waited to be noticed, and was not, and understood that for the moment it was better to be a shadow on the stairs—better, and harder, than it had ever been before.
