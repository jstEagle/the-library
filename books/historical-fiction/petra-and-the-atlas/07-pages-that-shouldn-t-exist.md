## 7. Pages That Shouldn't Exist

The atlas lived under her bed for two days before Paloma dared to open it.

She had told herself it was caution. The house was thin-walled; the inspector's visit had taught her how little separated their ordinary life from accusation, and a book of maps for a country that no longer existed was not the kind of thing one read by daylight with the shutters open. But honesty, in the small hours, required more. She was afraid of it. She was afraid the way she had been afraid, as a child, of the locked drawer in her mother's sewing table—afraid that whatever waited inside would be smaller and sadder than the wanting of it.

On the third night she could bear the waiting no longer. Her father slept badly these days; she heard him turning above her, the old beams ticking as he shifted, and she waited until his breathing found its long slow rhythm and the lamp behind the ceiling went dark. Then she knelt, drew the atlas from beneath the bed, and carried it to the kitchen table where the coals still held a dull red memory of fire.

She did not light the lamp. She banked the coals instead, coaxing them with the poker until they glowed, and set the atlas in that low orange light like a thing brought to an altar. It seemed right somehow. It seemed like something that had been waiting to be looked at properly.

The cover was calfskin gone soft as chamois at the corners, dyed a deep bottle-green that had faded along the spine to the color of old glass. No title stamped on it. Nothing at all to say what it was—only, pressed blind into the lower right corner, a compass rose no bigger than a thumbnail, the same device that rode the brass key now hidden in the lining of her apron pocket. She touched the impression with her fingertip. Whoever had made this book had not wanted it named. They had wanted it *found*—but only by someone who already knew how to look.

She opened it.

The first page was a title page, hand-lettered in a script so fine it might have been engraved rather than written: *An Atlas of the Marchlands*, and beneath it, smaller, *Being a True and Faithful Description of Those Territories Bounded by the Rivers Vell and Ostra, as Surveyed in the Year of Peace*. There was no printer's name. No place of publication. Only a date, eleven years old, and below the date three lines that made her breath go strange in her chest:

*Surveyed and drawn by E. Delacroix.*

*Engraved by E. Delacroix & M. Delacroix.*

*Colored by M. Delacroix.*

Her father's initial she knew, of course. Étienne Delacroix had signed every proof that ever came out of the shop, had taught her to cut her initials into scrap lead when she was six years old. But the second name—

She sat back on the stool. The coals ticked and settled.

*M. Delacroix.* Marta. Her mother. Dead ten years this autumn, dead of the fever that had come up the river with the autumn fogs, dead so completely that Paloma sometimes had to work to summon the exact color of her eyes—they were brown, she thought, brown with something gold in them, or perhaps that was only the lamplight of remembered evenings playing tricks. Her mother had been a compositor's daughter who married a type-cutter, who kept the household accounts and sang off-key while she starched aprons and who had, according to everything Paloma had ever been told, never once put her hands to a graver.

And yet here her name stood, twice, in a book of hand-colored maps: engraver, colorist. Not *assistant*. Not *wife of*. Engraver. Colorist. Equal billing with her father, letter for letter, as though whoever set this page had thought the two of them a single craft.

Paloma turned the page because her hands wanted something to do while the rest of her caught up.

The first map filled the whole leaf. It showed the river country—the Vell winding down from the northern hills, the Ostra coming in from the east, and between them a territory shaded in washes of green and rose that had been laid on so delicately the paper still breathed with it, colors sunk into the fiber rather than sitting on top, the work of a brush held by someone who understood that watercolor is mostly patience. Little forests stood in clusters of stippled dots. The hills were hatched like sleeping animals. Along the rivers, towns sat in their loops and bends with names beside them in the same fine script: names Paloma half-knew from her father's other work and half did not know at all, names that sounded like songs sung slightly wrong, or exactly right, depending on which country's tongue you used.

She found the compass rose again in the map's corner, larger here, ornamental, its points worked into tiny fleurs. And beneath it, in ink gone brown with age, a line of handwriting—not the title-page script but a quicker, more personal hand, slanting downhill as if hurrying:

*Here the border is a fiction. Drawn in a drawing room. See note, plate IX.*

Paloma read it three times. *A fiction. Drawn in a drawing room.*

It was her father's hand. She would have known it anywhere, would have known it upside down, backward, in mirror-reverse on a sheet pulled fresh from the press. Ten thousand proofs had passed under her eyes carrying that hand in their margins: *reduce this, recast that, watch your spacing, girl.* She had spent her whole life reading Étienne Delacroix's annotations the way other people read scripture.

But she had never read anything like this in them. Her father's marginal notes were about kerning and leading and the price of Dutch paper. They were not declarations that borders were fictions. They were not instructions to see notes on plates yet to come.

Plate IX. She counted forward through the quires with fingers that had begun, without her permission, to tremble.

* * *

The maps came at her one after another like rooms in a house she'd entered without knocking.

There was a plate of the hill country rendered in blues and umber, and another of the marshes where the two rivers braided and unbraided, each channel traced in silver-gray wash so that the water seemed actually to move when she tilted the page toward the coals. There was a town plan, streets drawn with such loving particularity that she could see where the market square opened and where a church tower threw its shadow, and there were plates of roads, of bridges, of a single extraordinary fold-out showing the whole territory from hill to marsh with the rivers running through it like a sentence through a paragraph.

Every few pages, the margin notes. Always in her father's hand. Always quick, private, unguarded in a way she had never seen him unguarded on paper:

*Measured this ford myself, June, knee-deep and cold enough to swear by.*

*M. insists the orchard road runs north of the mill. M. is right. M. is usually right. Do not tell her I wrote this.*

*They will redraw all of this within our lifetime. Mark it. Paper remembers what treaties forget.*

She read that last one twice, and something cold walked down her spine. Because he had been right. Whatever country these maps described—and she was beginning to understand, with a vertiginous lurch, that it was not any country that appeared in the almanacs or on the customs charts hung in the shipping offices—it had been redone. Redrawn, renamed, portioned out among neighbors the way a dead man's coat is portioned among his sons. These maps were not wrong. They were *orphaned*. They described a body whose soul had been reassigned by men in conference rooms, and that was precisely why the atlas could not exist, why it had hidden in a locker for years, why a letter under seal had turned her father's fingers white against a ledger.

Paper remembers what treaties forget. He had written that himself. Had he known, even then, what remembering would cost?

She reached plate IX near midnight, and her hands stopped.

It was a map of the eastern plateau, done in greens so deep they were nearly black, with the border in question—a line some treaty-drafting pen had drawn across it—rendered not in ink but in a dotted rule, tentative as stitching. In the margin, her father had written: *As promised. Here is the line, and here is what the line ignores.* And then, beneath, in different ink, fresher, added later: *For V., who asked the question first.*

V. Véra. The name from the letter, the exile, the enemy of the state. Her father had written notes in a forbidden atlas addressed to a woman branded a traitor, and he had done it in the same careless hand he used for corrections to jobbing work, as if it were nothing, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Paloma pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. She was tired. That was all. Tiredness made the room swim, made grief arrive uninvited.

She turned to the final plate because she could not stop herself, and that was where the night broke open.

* * *

The last map was unlike the others. It was smaller, set alone on its leaf with wide margins, and it was not a survey at all but something more like a memory: the river country again, but softened, idealized, colored in dawn tones—pale golds and washed pinks—as though the land were being shown not as it was but as someone loved it. There were no roads marked. Instead, along the margins, in the fine title-page script, someone had written small sentences, like captions to pictures only the writer could see:

*Here the ice on the Vell breaks in March with a sound like cannon fire, and the children run down to watch.*

*Here the swallows return to the granary eaves the same week every year, and the miller's wife says they are paying rent.*

*Here my grandmother stood in the doorway during the flood year and refused to leave until the water reached the third stair.*

Paloma read them slowly. Each one was a small lamp lit in the dark of the page. Each one was the kind of knowledge that does not live in surveys—that lives, instead, in families, in kitchens, in the mouths of grandmothers, in the particular stubborn love of people for the ground that raised them. Whoever had written these had not been mapping territory. They had been mapping *belonging*.

And she recognized the script. Of course she did. It was the title-page script, the fine formal hand, and she had seen it a hundred times before she understood what she was seeing: on the spice tins in the kitchen cupboard, labeled in her mother's careful letters. On the flyleaf of the family Bible. On the birthday cards Marta Delacroix had written her daughter in the years before the fever took her, the last of which still lay in Paloma's trunk with its pressed flower gone the color of weak tea.

Her mother had written these. Her mother had colored these maps, had engraved them, had—her mind stumbled over the arithmetic—had spent a summer, or seasons, bent over copper with a graver in her fist, and had come home afterward and said nothing. Had starched aprons and sung off-key and kept accounts and let her daughter believe she had never once touched the trade.

Why? Why hide it? Why hide *this*—not just the skill but the tenderness of it, the flood-year grandmother, the swallows paying rent?

Below the last caption, centered at the foot of the plate, was a signature line. Two signatures. Her father's, firm and familiar as a handshake. And beside it, flowing and sure, the letters *Marta Delacroix*—with, after them, a small flourish that curled back under the name like a hand tucking something safely away.

Paloma laid her palm flat on the page, over the signature, the way you would touch a face.

"Mother," she said aloud, and the word sounded enormous in the dark kitchen.

Ten years. Ten years since the fever, since the closed coffin because of what the sickness did, since her father had packed her mother's sewing things into a chest and not opened it since, since the house had rearranged itself around an absence the way water rearranges itself around a stone. Paloma had grieved. She had grieved thoroughly, dutifully, the way one grieves when there is nothing left to learn. Her mother was a finished story: a good woman, a quiet woman, a compositor's daughter who had married well enough and died too soon.

Now the story had torn open at the seam, and what spilled out of it was a person Paloma had never met—a woman who cut copperplate by lamplight, who argued with her husband about orchard roads and won, who wrote *the ice breaks in March with a sound like cannon fire* in a hand as sure as any master engraver's, who had helped make a beautiful dangerous book and then walked away from it and never said a word.

What else had she not said?

Paloma sat with the question while the coals sank from red to gray. She thought of her mother's hands—she could see them suddenly, clearly, better than her eyes: broad, capable, ink-stained at the edges no matter how she scrubbed, and Paloma had always assumed it was because she helped with the presswork, everyone in the house had ink somewhere on them. Had it been graver-work instead? Had those hands, teaching young Paloma to hold a pencil, been correcting her grip with a craftsman's precision that came from somewhere deeper than a schoolmistress's?

She thought of the way her mother had gone silent, once, years ago, when a traveling merchant spread a chart on the shop counter and complained about the new border tariffs. Just silent. Watching the map with an expression Paloma had been too young to read and now understood, with a pain like a toothache, had been recognition. Longing. The look of a woman looking at ground she had helped to draw.

And she thought of the letter. *Deliver the atlas to its exiled owner.* To V.—to Véra Lindqvist, who had asked the question first, whoever's question that was. A promise made, apparently, to three people at once: her father, the foreign cartographer, and—

Her mother.

The promise had been made to her mother too. Perhaps especially to her mother. Perhaps this whole buried book, this secret summer, this double life of gravers and watercolor brushes, had been Marta Delacroix's as much as anyone's—perhaps more—and her father had kept it locked away not only from the inspectors but from his own daughter, keeping faith with the dead by keeping silence, and had nearly suffocated under the weight of it.

Paloma closed the atlas. Her hands were steady now, steadier than they had been all night. Grief was still there—it would always be there—but grief had changed shape, the way ice changes when the thaw comes: the same substance, suddenly moving.

She had spent ten years mourning a finished woman. Now there was an unfinished one, and unfinished things demanded to be continued.

She slid the atlas back under the bed. She got into her own bed beside it and lay listening to her father turn restlessly overhead, and she made a decision the way she had learned to set a line of type: one character at a time, deliberate, irreversible.

Tomorrow she would ask him. Not gently. She had been gentle for ten years, and gentleness had kept her a stranger in her own history.

Tomorrow she would ask him who her mother really was—and why, of all the secrets in that atlas, the one her mother had left behind felt less like a map and more like an inheritance.

Above her, the floorboards creaked one last time and went still. And Paloma lay awake in the dark, holding her mother's name in her mouth like a coin she had only just discovered was gold.
