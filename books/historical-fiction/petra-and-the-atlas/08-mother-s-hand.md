## 8. Mother's Hand

The summer of 1836 arrived in the print quarter like a guest who intended to stay: slowly at first, then all at once, filling every room with light and dust and the smell of warm pitch from the roofers' kettles across the lane. Éloise Delacroix woke on the first of June with her hands already aching for the burin.

She lay a while in the grey half-light listening to her husband breathe beside her, and beyond him the city breathing too—the milk carts, the first tramplings of pigeons on the gutter-lead, someone two streets over already working a press, the rhythmic sigh-and-stamp of it like a sleeping animal turning over. Ten years she had lived above the shop on the rue de la Fontaine, and still the sound of another man's press made her heart lift, the way a sailor's lifts at any water, even water he does not sail.

She rose without waking Étienne, dressed in the dark, and went down the narrow stair into the workshop where the type cases waited in their long ranks like a congregation at prayer. She did not light the lamp. She knew the room by touch, by the particular cold of the stone floor near the window and the warmth of the stove-wall near the cutting bench, and she sat down at the bench and drew out her tools one by one, laying them on the leather cloth in their old order: gravers fine and coarse, the scorper, the burnisher, the little oilstone worn into a valley by twenty years of edges.

On the bench's far corner, weighted under a flatiron, lay a single sheet of paper. She did not uncover it yet. She only rested her palm against the cool iron, as if greeting a horse before the day's work began, and let herself feel the whole of the summer ahead of her—the way you can feel weather coming in an old scar.

Three weeks ago, in this room, over brandy and the last of the winter oranges, Anders Lindqvist had unrolled his drawings across the cutting bench and asked them for the impossible thing.

* * *

He had arrived on an evening in May, dusty from four days on the road, and Étienne had known him from the doorway—from the hat, from the stride, from the way the man filled a threshold as though borders were things that happened to other people. They had embraced in the street like boys, these two grey men, and Éloise had stood in the door laughing until Anders turned and swept her hand up and kissed it and said, "Madame Delacroix. The finest engraver between here and the sea, and married to that."

"Anders," she said. "You flatter my husband by comparison."

"I wound him," Anders agreed happily. "It is why I come. Flattery I could get anywhere."

They ate late. Little Paloma, six years old and solemn as a magistrate, was permitted to sit up past her bedtime on the condition that she say nothing, which she interpreted as a challenge rather than a rule, and asked Anders three questions about the sea, having never seen it, and one about whether kings could be arrested, which stopped the table quiet for a moment until Anders said, gravely, "In a just world, child, yes," and raised his glass to her, and she raised her cup of watered wine, and that was settled.

Afterward, when the child slept and the candles had burned down to stubs, Anders grew quiet in the way he did when something mattered, and reached into his coat, and brought out the roll of oilcloth, and unrolled it across the cleared bench.

Maps. Éloise leaned over them with her hands behind her back, the way she had learned to look at other people's work—never touching first, seeing first. There were perhaps thirty sheets, drawn in a strong, quick hand, ink over pencil, corrections layered like sediment: coastlines, river systems, mountain passes, and then stranger things. Roads marked not with distances but with the names of inns. A valley rendered almost lovingly, with notes in a language she half recognized about the color of the wheat in August. Borders—and this was what made her breath catch—borders drawn in dotted lines, provisional, apologetic, as if the pen itself doubted them.

"You're asking us to cut these," Étienne said.

"I'm asking you both to make them permanent." Anders turned the top sheet toward them. "Copper. Hand-colored proofs. An atlas of—" he paused, and smiled ruefully, "—of a country that will not exist much longer. Perhaps does not exist now except on these pages. My country, madame. Or the shape it had when I loved it. The new surveyors are redrawing everything to suit the ministries. In ten years no chart will show these roads. I want them cut before they are forgotten, so that somewhere, in copper, the truth survives."

The candle flames stood very still. Somewhere below in the street a drunk was singing, tunelessly, faithfully.

"Why us?" Étienne asked quietly. "There are houses in Paris—"

"There are houses in Paris that will ask me whose authority stands behind the work, and when I cannot tell them, they will decline, politely, and remember my face." Anders looked at Éloise. "But there is one house on one street where a woman once cut a plate for me in three days because I told her the maps were for a schoolhouse in a village that had none. Do you remember, madame?"

She remembered. She remembered the plate—a simple thing, parish boundaries, a gift—and she remembered thinking at the time that the man's eyes had been too sad for schoolhouses. She had not asked. That was the etiquette of their trade: you engraved what was set before you, and the reasons belonged to whoever carried them.

"The work must be secret," Anders said. "Not shameful—secret. There is a difference, and I ask you to trust me on it. No marks of the shop. No names on the plates. And when it is done, it goes away—to me, or to mine—and neither of you ever speaks of it again unless I release you to speak."

Étienne was looking at the maps with the expression he wore over difficult settings, the slight forward lean, the lips parted. But it was Éloise who spoke first.

"Who receives it?" she asked. "When it's done. Who is 'yours'?"

Anders turned to her, and something in his face eased, as though she had passed some examination he had feared she'd fail.

"My daughter," he said. "Véra. She is fourteen. She draws better than I do and pretends not to care about any of this, which is how I know she cares completely." He touched the edge of the nearest map, the valley with the wheat. "I have spent her childhood away from home, drawing borders for men who move them like furniture. This atlas is the only inheritance I have that isn't compromised. I want it to be beautiful. I want it to be true. And I want it to reach her even if—" He stopped. Started again, lighter: "Even if the roads between here and there grow complicated. Which roads do, lately."

Éloise looked at the dotted lines. She thought of her daughter asleep upstairs, six years old, who had asked whether kings could be arrested; thought of the small fierce certainty in the child's voice, and of how few true things she would be able to leave her that the world would not try to tax or confiscate or rewrite.

"We'll cut it," she said.

Étienne looked at her. Not in surprise—she saw that—but in recognition, and something else beneath the recognition, a shadow moving under ice, gone before she could name it. Later, much later, she would understand what it had been. That night she took it only for love, and it was mostly that.

"Then we have a contract," Anders said. "Brandy?"

"Brandy," said Étienne.

They drank to it, three glasses lifted over thirty doomed maps, and outside the drunk had found his key and gone in, and the summer stood open before them like a road.

* * *

They worked through June with the shutters half-closed against prying eyes and open just enough against the heat, and the workshop became a country of its own with its own laws. Étienne took the lettering—all those patient, upright place-names, each one set and proofed twice, because Anders insisted the names were the soul of the thing, that a map which misspelled a village had already begun to erase it. Anders drew and corrected and argued with himself, crossing out his own coastlines in favor of older surveys he kept in a locked valise. And Éloise cut.

God, how she cut. Her hands remembered everything; that was the terror and the glory of the craft, that the body kept faith when the mind faltered. She worked the burin through the polished copper in long confident sweeps and short hesitant ones, learning each coastline the way you learn a face, by its irregularities, its private asymmetries. The eastern mountains took her nine days and gave her blisters. The river delta she cut in a kind of trance, four days, hardly speaking, emerging at the end as if surfacing from deep water, blinking, starving, happy.

Paloma would sit on the stairs in the evenings watching the lamp-light jump on the copper, watching her mother's hands move with a sureness the child had no words for yet. Once she crept close and put one finger, featherlight, on the edge of a plate, and Éloise said, "Oil," meaning the skin's oils ruin a burnished surface, and Paloma snatched the finger back as though scalded, and Éloise laughed and lifted her onto her knee instead.

"What is it?" Paloma whispered, staring down at the map, at the tiny etched hills and the marching names.

"It's a place," Éloise said. "A real place. It has bread in it, and bells, and a river you can swim in if you're brave about the cold."

"Is it ours?"

"No, little one. It belongs to itself. Maps are only promises that places happened."

Paloma considered this with the terrible thoroughness of the six-year-old, then said, "Will anyone read it?"

"Somebody will," Éloise said. "Someday. Someone who needs it." She kissed the top of her daughter's head and smelled the sun in her hair and did not know, could not know, how literally the years ahead would take her at her word.

* * *

July came in hot and the work slowed and sweetened. Anders stayed at the inn by the gate but might as well have lived in the shop; he took his breakfast standing at the bench, critiquing a hatching pattern with buttered bread in hand, and in the afternoons he dragged them both to the river, insisting that engravers who never left their benches began to believe the world was twelve inches wide.

It was on the river, in fact, on a green afternoon with their trousers rolled and their feet in the current, that the promise was finally spoken aloud—not the contract, which was signed in brandy and secrecy, but the other thing, the human thing underneath it.

Véra wrote letters. That was how Éloise came to know her before knowing her: a girl's careful script arriving weekly from the north, full of drawings in the margins—a dog, a boat, a caricature of her father with an enormous compass rose for a head. Anders read them aloud shamelessly, and answered them at the bench, dictating half to Étienne's amusement and drawing little maps in the margins of his replies: *turn left at the crooked church, the baker's wife remembers me, buy the dark bread.*

"She'll have my whole geography in her head by autumn," Anders said, wading, splashing Étienne like a boy of ten. "She'll navigate to this town by bakery alone."

"She sounds like you," Éloise said from the bank, where she sat with the last plate across her knees, finishing the wheat-valley's shading with a needle-fine drypoint in the shade of a willow.

"She is entirely her mother, thank God, and occasionally, in moments of weakness, me." He sobered then, in the way he could, the laughter draining out and leaving the serious man behind it, the one who had unrolled doomed maps on their bench. He came and crouched by Éloise, dripping, and looked at the valley taking shape under her hand.

"When it's finished," he said, "and it goes north—I may not be the one who carries it. Things being what they are." He tapped the copper, gently, beside the little etched church. "So I want your word. Both of yours. That whatever happens, however the roads run—if it cannot come to me, it comes to her. To Véra. Even if it takes years. Even if it takes longer than I do."

The river made its small continuous argument below them. Étienne had stopped smiling.

"That's a strange thing to ask," Étienne said.

"It's the only thing worth asking." Anders held Éloise's eye, and there it was again, the sadness under the humor, older than this summer, older maybe than their friendship. "A map is a promise that places happened, madame. You said so—I heard you say it to your daughter through your open window like a fool who leaves secrets on sills. Well. I am asking you to keep one. If the century turns ugly—and I have watched it turn ugly before, in countries that swore it couldn't happen here—then this atlas becomes contraband. Not because it's weapons. Because it's memory. And the first casualty of every ugliness is the same: they take the maps and redraw them, and then they take the memories and redraw those. Somebody has to keep the originals."

"Someone keeps them," Éloise said, "and gets called a traitor for it."

"Gets called worse," Anders agreed cheerfully. "Welcome to cartography."

But his eyes did not laugh, and she took his hand, ink-stained meeting water-stained, and said, "You have our word. If not to you, then to Véra. On the plates themselves if need be."

"On the plates," he repeated softly, as though filing it somewhere permanent. Then he stood, and shouted at Étienne that the water was fine, which was a lie, and the afternoon righted itself and went on being golden.

That evening she cut the dedication space into the final page—a small reserved panel, blank, waiting—at Anders's instruction, though he refused to say what would fill it. "Something for her," was all he would say. "For Véra. When the time is right, you'll write it, madame. Your letters are truer than mine."

And Éloise, who had lost her own mother young and knew exactly what it was to inherit a question instead of an answer, understood then what the blank space was. It was a door left unlocked in a house about to be emptied. It was a father building a bridge to a daughter across a gap he suspected he would not live—or would not be permitted—to cross himself.

She said nothing. She cut the panel clean and true, and covered it with tissue, and felt it waiting under her palm all that night like a heartbeat.

* * *

August ended the way good summers do, too quickly and with a false suggestion that such things are renewable. The plates were finished—thirty-one of them, wrapped in oiled paper and packed in a cedar box that had once held type—and on the last evening they did not pack it. They opened a second bottle instead, and spread the proof sheets across the floor of the workshop, and walked among their own country, all three of them, in stockinged feet, careful of the wet ink of their triumph.

Little Paloma was allowed down for it, and went solemnly from sheet to sheet with a lamp, holding it high, announcing each map's name like a herald. When she came to the valley with the wheat she stopped.

"This one," she said. "This is the best one."

"Why?" Anders asked, crouching to her height.

"Because my mama made the hills look like they're breathing."

Anders looked up at Éloise across the lamplight and the scattered empire of their summer, and whatever was in his face, she looked away from it first, because she was suddenly certain that if she didn't, she would cry, and she had decided years ago that she would not cry in front of this man until the work was paid for.

"To breathing hills," he said, raising his glass to the child.

"To the keeper of the Atlas," Étienne said—joking, easy, coining the phrase in ignorance, and Éloise heard it land in the future like a coin dropped down a well whose depth no one knew.

Later, when the proofs were gathered and the box corded and Anders gone up the stairs to his bed, she stood alone a moment at the bench with the final page uncovered, the blank dedication panel pale as an unfallen snowfield. She took up her drypoint. She did not write anything—there was nothing yet to write; the words would come when the time came, or they would not, and the panel would wait either way, patient as copper.

But she laid her bare palm flat in the empty space, the way she greeted the flatiron each morning, the way she greeted all her tools, and she made it a vow in the old silent grammar of her hands:

*This reaches her. Whoever carries it, whatever it costs—this reaches her.*

Down in the street a late carriage passed, wheels loud on the stones, heading east. She listened to it fade, and trimmed the lamp, and went up to bed.

She had eleven more years. She used five of them well.
