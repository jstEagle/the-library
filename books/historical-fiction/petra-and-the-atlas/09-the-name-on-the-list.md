## 9. The Name on the List

The name came to her the way bad news always comes in the print quarter: not by proclamation, but in ink.

Paloma found it on a Tuesday morning, pasted to the wall of the Café des Typographes where she had gone to buy a twist of cheap tobacco for her father—a small errand, an ordinary errand, the kind of errand that made a girl invisible in all the right ways. The notice was printed on government stock, that heavy, grayish paper the prefecture used for everything from dog licenses to death warrants, and it had been pasted up crooked, one corner already curling away from the damp stone. She might have walked past it. She almost did.

But her eye was trained now, these last weeks, to catch certain words the way a compositor's eye catches a dropped letter in a galley of type. *Lindqvist* leapt off the page at her before she had decided to read it at all.

*By order of the Ministry of the Interior: the persons hereinafter named are declared agents of foreign powers and enemies of the state...*

There it was, third from the bottom, set in the same tired serif every official notice in Paris used: **VÉRA LINDQVIST, cartographer, of Stockholm — declared enemy agent. All citizens having knowledge of this person or her affairs are bound to report such knowledge to the authorities. Concealment constitutes treason.**

Treason. The word sat there in cold black type, and Paloma stood in front of it with the tobacco twist going soft in her fist, and the café door opened and closed behind her spilling out noise and coffee-smell, and none of it reached her. She read the line again. And again. As though the fourth reading might rearrange the letters into something survivable.

She thought of the atlas sleeping in its locker beneath the guildhall library, behind a brass door stamped with a compass rose. She thought of her father's face when the sealed letter had come, the color draining out of it like water from a cracked forme. She thought of the marginal notes in her mother's hand—her mother's hand!—and the promise threaded through those old pages like a wire through wax.

*Deliver it to Véra Lindqvist.*

And now the government said the name aloud too—but with a different verb attached. Not *deliver*. *Report.*

---

She did not run home. Running drew eyes, and she had learned that much already, though she could not have said when. She walked, fast but evenly, past the paper-sellers shouting about barricades in the faubourgs, past the National Guard drilling in the Place with their new blue coats, past the window of Mère Aubry's shop where the old woman sat shelling peas and watching the street with the patient, all-seeing gaze of a woman who had watched this street for fifty years and missed nothing in any of them.

Paloma felt that gaze between her shoulder blades all the way home.

Her father was at the stone sink when she came in, washing ink from his hands with the yellow lye soap he kept for the purpose, scrubbing at the stains that never fully left him—the map of his trade written into his knuckles. He looked up, saw her face, and set down the soap without being told.

"You've seen it," he said.

"The notice." She laid the tobacco on the bench between them like evidence. "Véra Lindqvist. Father, they've named her an enemy agent. It says—" Her voice caught on the legal flatness of it. "It says concealment is treason."

He dried his hands slowly, each finger separately, the way he did when he was buying time. "I know."

"You *know*?" The word came out sharper than she intended. "Since when?"

"Since before you were born, in a sense." He hung the towel on its nail, straightened it so its edges matched exactly, which was what he did instead of sighing. "A man does not engrave maps of a country that no longer exists without understanding, eventually, that someone will call it treason. I hoped the calling would wait until after I was dead. That was vanity. Hope usually is."

"Then we give it back," Paloma said. "We put it in the locker and we walk away and we let the compass rose keep its own secrets—"

"And the letter? The courier's letter demanding delivery? You think a man who sends sealed letters across borders will simply forget us because we've returned his property unread?" Her father shook his head. "No, little one. We are already inside the story. The only question left is which ending we choose."

She wanted to argue. She wanted it so badly her teeth ached with it. But underneath the argument she could feel the other thing, the thing she had been carrying since she'd first turned those hand-colored pages by candlelight: the sense that the atlas was not merely a debt. That it was hers. That somewhere in its margins her mother had left a trail of ink leading directly to her daughter's feet, and that walking away would be a second, quieter death for a woman who had already died once.

"I'm not giving it up," she said quietly.

Her father looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight—for the day outside was gray enough that the lamps were already lit—she saw something move across his face that was not anger. It looked terribly like fear, and worse, like recognition.

"Your mother said almost exactly that," he said. "Word for word, once, about a smaller thing. I lost that argument too."

Three days later, the neighbor knocked.

Monsieur Ferrand lived in the rooms above the ribbon-maker's, two doors down, a widower with a cough and a lodger who claimed to be a clerk. He had bought almanacs from Papa Delacroix for twenty years and complained about the price of them for nineteen. He was, in the economy of the quarter, a nothing-man—and Paloma had long ago learned that nothing-men were the most dangerous men in any revolution, because they had nothing to lose and everything to prove.

He came to the shop door at dusk, hat in hand, apologetic as a bill collector.

"Delacroix. Forgive the hour." His eyes went past her father's shoulder into the shop, cataloguing it: the cases, the press, the stack of jobbing work, the lamp. "I wouldn't trouble you, but there's a man from the prefecture asking questions on the street. Asking about deliveries. Foreign correspondence." He lowered his voice to a register meant to sound kind. "I thought you'd want to know. Given your... connections."

"We have no connections," Papa said evenly.

"No. Of course not." Ferrand smiled with most of his mouth. "Only, my lodger saw a young woman of this household at the guildhall library last week. Asking after the archive lockers. And the prefecture man mentioned the library specifically. Mentioned it by name." He put his hat on. "I've said nothing, naturally. But a man can't answer for what others say. You understand. These are... fluid times."

He wished them good evening and went, and his footsteps faded down the rue des Écoles with the unhurried tread of a man who had done exactly what he'd come to do.

Paloma stood in the doorway of the composing room, her heart going hard and slow, like a press pulling its first proof of the day.

"He informed," she said. "Just now. He dressed it up as a warning, but he informed."

"Yes."

"Because of me. Because I asked after the locker."

"Because he was always going to find a reason." Papa began putting away the loose type, sliding the sorts into their compartments with small precise clicks, the evening ritual that ordered his world. "Ferrand has been waiting two years for this city to make informing respectable again. You gave him a reason; he would have manufactured one otherwise. Do not carry his sin for him—it weighs enough on its owner."

But she did carry it. She carried it all through supper, through the washing of the dishes, through the long hour after when her father read and she pretended to mend a torn forme and neither of them spoke. Loyalty and survival sat across the table from each other wearing her face and her father's, and between them lay the atlas, invisible and enormous, like a body buried under the floorboards.

---

She went to the locker that night.

She knew she shouldn't. Every reasonable voice in her head—the voice of survival, the voice of her father, the voice of the notice pasted crooked on the café wall—told her to stay home, to be unremarkable, to let the drawer stay shut until the city finished burning or didn't. But there was another voice, older than all of those, in her mother's hand, in the margins of maps of a country that existed nowhere anymore except in ink and memory. And that voice had never once told her to be careful.

The guildhall library at midnight was a country of sleeping giants: shelves rising into shadow, dust hanging in the lamplight like snow that had forgotten how to land. The night porter knew her—or knew her father's name, which amounted to the same thing in the old networks of the trade—and took her coin without counting it, which was its own kind of message about how the world was tilting.

The brass key turned. The little door swung open on its oiled hinge.

The atlas lay where she had left it, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed. She unwrapped it there in the dark of the archive, by the light of a single candle stub she'd brought for the purpose, and turned to the page she had been avoiding for a week: the title page, and beneath the title, the colophon—the engraver's declaration, the names of those who had cut and colored these plates in a peacetime summer that now belonged to another century.

*Engraved by A. Delacroix.*

*And by M. Delacroix, née Vasseur.*

*For our friend V. Lindqvist, who taught us that a border is only a line men agree to stop arguing along.*

Paloma read it four times. Her candle guttered. Somewhere far off in the sleeping city, a church bell counted the hour, and she realized her cheeks were wet and had been for some time.

*Concealment constitutes treason.*

Very well. Then she was a traitor. She had been one since the moment she'd first touched these pages, since before that, since the summer before she was born when three friends had bent over copper plates and sworn a promise over brandy. Some inheritances come as houses or money. Hers had come as a crime, and she found—to her astonishment, to her terror—that she could hold it without shame.

She rewrapped the atlas. And then, because she was her mother's daughter and her father's both, she did something more: she took the final page—the last map, the one whose margins were densest with annotations, the one that had always struck her as different somehow, worked over and over, layered—and she pressed it flat against her breastbone, memorizing its weight, before folding it back into place.

If the shop fell, if the locker was seized, if Ferrand's lodger and the prefecture man and the whole gray machinery of the state closed around them—she wanted to know, absolutely, which page she would save first.

---

She was three streets from home when she understood she was being followed.

Not followed, precisely. Accompanied. A man had fallen into step some distance behind her near the library steps, and he was still there at the corner of the rue aux Ours, still there past the shuttered bakery, matching her pace with the unconscious patience of a man paid by the hour. He wore a worker's jacket and carried no lantern, and he made no attempt whatsoever to hide, which frightened her more than pursuit would have.

They wanted her to know. Or they wanted Ferrand's testimony confirmed by observation. Either way, the net was drawing in, thread by thread, and it had begun with a name pasted crooked on a café wall.

Paloma did not go home. She turned instead toward the river, toward the noise of the late taverns, and lost herself deliberately in a crowd of students coming out of a political meeting, their voices hot with talk of reform and banquets and the end of the July monarchy. The man in the worker's jacket held back at the edge of the crowd, and then, when she slipped down an alley between a bindery and a wine merchant's, she heard him curse softly and give up—or pretend to.

She stood in the dark smelling of glue and sour wine, her heart slamming, the atlas's weight a phantom against her ribs.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would tell her father everything: the follower, the notice, the decision she had already made in the archive by candlelight while her tears fell on another woman's dedication. Tomorrow they would fight about it, properly, the way families fight when love and terror wear the same coat.

Because tonight she had understood something that could not be unsaid, even inside her own head:

The name on the list was not Véra Lindqvist's alone anymore. Three names were engraved in that colophon, and the government needed only to read the atlas to learn the other two—and one of them, dead ten years, could not defend herself, and the other two were living above a print shop on the rue des Écoles with a neighbor who kept his hat brushed and his conscience rented out by the week.

Paloma stepped out of the alley and walked home the long way, by the river, under a sky the color of wet slate.

Behind her, in the east, beyond the faubourgs, the city murmured in its sleep like a press building pressure against the platen. Everyone in the print quarter could feel it—the whole season balanced on the point of impression, one pull of the lever from breaking.

She just hadn't yet guessed how soon.
