## 10. Riot in the Print Quarter

The first stone came through the window of the *Gazette des Faubourgs* at half past eleven on a Thursday morning, and by noon the whole quarter had learned what it meant.

Paloma heard it before she understood it: not the crash itself but the sound after it—the way the street went quiet in that particular held-breath way, the way every press in every shop seemed to stop mid-pull, as though the city had lifted its type from the forme all at once. She was standing at the stone sink washing ink from her hands when the silence arrived, and she stood with her sleeves dripping black water and listened to it spread.

Then the shouting began, and the silence was over forever.

She went to the broken window—careful of the glass still clinging to the frame like frost—and looked down into the Rue des Imprimeurs. The morning's gray light fell on a crowd that had not existed an hour ago. They came out of the faubourgs in knots and streams, workmen with their aprons still tied on, women with market baskets set down and abandoned on the cobbles, boys running messages between groups like shuttles across a loom. Someone had already dragged a barricade cart into the mouth of the street; someone else was prying up paving stones with an iron bar, the stones coming loose with a sound like teeth being pulled.

"Paloma." Her father's voice, low, from the stairs. "Away from the window."

He was already moving through the shop with the economy of a man who had decided something, dousing the lamp in the composing room, pulling shutters. He moved as he always moved when there was work to be done—no wasted gesture—but she could see the difference underneath, the way you can see water moving under ice. His hands were steady. His breathing was not.

"They're heading for the government printing office," someone called from the street, and then another voice corrected him—no, the prefecture, no, the barracks—and the corrections dissolved into a roar that had no single direction at all.

Her father barred the door and stood with his back against it for a moment, listening. Paloma had never seen him listen to a street before. He listened to presses, to customers, to the small honest sounds of lead and paper. Now his head was tilted like a bird's, and his eyes were somewhere ten years away.

"Papa," she said. "What do we do?"

He looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him answer honestly, which frightened her more than the shouting did.

"We do nothing," he said. "We are a print shop among print shops. We are nobody. We keep being nobody until this passes over us." He pushed off the door and began, absurdly, calmly, to cover the press with its canvas sheet, as if the danger were dust. "Help me with the type cases. The steel sorts go in the cellar."

* * *

From the upstairs window, wedged behind the shutter where she could see without being seen, Paloma watched the quarter tear itself open.

The barricades went up with a speed that seemed almost practiced. By two o'clock there were three of them within sight—the cart and the paving stones at the corner, a second of overturned drays and felled trees at the far end of the street, and a third, nearest of all, built from a furniture wagon, iron railings torn from a garden she knew, and door after door after door, whole doors with their hinges still bleeding screws, carried on shoulders like litters. Men worked on them in teams, passing stones hand over hand, and the rhythm of it was so familiar it made her chest ache: it was the rhythm of a shop floor, the wordless cooperation of people doing one thing together. They had all been trained for this, she realized. Not for the fighting—for the making. Every man in this quarter knew how to assemble something heavy and exact under pressure. They were printers, binders, founders, smiths. A barricade was only a very large job of composition.

Then the soldiers came, and the making became breaking.

They came down the Rue Saint-Antoine in column, bayonets catching what light the clouds allowed, and the crowd's roar changed key. Paloma pressed her cheek to the shutter gap. She saw the first volley—or rather she felt it, a flat concussion that rattled the window in its frame—and saw the front rank of the crowd fold inward like a closing book. After that she stopped seeing individuals. It was too fast, and then it was too slow, hours compressed or stretched, she couldn't tell. Smoke rolled up the street in brown ropes. Somewhere a press shop was burning—not theirs, farther east—and the smoke from it smelled like every shop she'd ever loved: hot metal, oil, and now wood going black.

And the ink. That was the thing she would remember longest, the thing she would dream about for years: the ink. When the crowd broke the windows of the encreur's warehouse on the corner—she watched it happen, watched men who minutes before had been building now smashing with the same efficient joy—the great vats went over and the ink ran out across the cobbles in black rivers, thick and slow and shining, finding every gutter and channel the streets offered, pooling around the barricade stones, printing the quarter with itself. People ran through it and left black footprints up the sides of buildings. A woman slipped and rose laughing, both hands black to the elbow, and shouted something Paloma couldn't hear over the noise, and other women took up the shout, and they smeared their palms on the walls and pressed them there, dozens of black hands, a signature the size of a street.

It should have been terrible. It was terrible. But some part of her—the part that had spent twenty-two years setting other people's words in order—looked at those handprints and thought, unbidden: *that is the truest impression I have ever seen.*

She hated herself a little for thinking it. She kept thinking it.

* * *

For Henri Delacroix, the afternoon divided itself into problems, and he was grateful for the division.

Problem one: fire. The wind had turned east-southeast, carrying sparks from the burning shop three streets over, and their roof was old slate over old beams. He put Paloma on the upper landing with buckets and the trapdoor to the roof, and himself climbed out twice to wet the slates with a mop like a madman, and the sparks that landed hissed and died. Problem solved at four o'clock when the rain finally came, a thin cold drizzle that everyone in the street received with open arms and open mouths, as if the sky itself had chosen a side.

Problem two: the mob. Twice it surged past their door, and twice it passed them by—not out of mercy but out of indifference, which was exactly what he had spent a lifetime purchasing. Their window was intact because their shutters were closed because their shop was dark because Delacroix & Fille had never once printed anything worth remembering. He had kept his politics out of his formes for thirty years. Other printers had not. Through the gap in the shutter he watched the crowd break the door of Béranger's shop across the lane—Béranger, who printed pamphlets, who had always printed pamphlets, whose press had sung sedition the way other men's sang hymns—and he felt no triumph and no shame, only the cold arithmetic of survival. Béranger had made his choice years ago. Today the bill came due. Henri had paid his own bills in advance, in invisibility, in all the things he had refused to say.

Problem three was the one he could not solve, and it stood in his cellar wearing his daughter's face.

He found her there at dusk, after the worst had moved east toward the Hôtel de Ville and the quarter settled into its shattered, watchful quiet. She was sitting on the cellar steps with a rushlight, and beside her, wrapped in sacking, sat the atlas.

His stomach dropped through the floor.

"You said the steel sorts," she said, not looking up. "I brought them down. And then I thought about what else shouldn't be found upstairs."

"We agreed—" he began, and stopped, because they had agreed nothing; he had simply assumed obedience the way he assumed the press would hold registration, and here was the proof that assumptions, like type, could be knocked crooked by a single careless pull.

"If they search us," Paloma said, "they'll find it anyway. Better down here than in my room." She looked up at him then, and in the rushlight her face was half shadow and wholly stubborn, and he saw her mother so plainly that for a moment he couldn't speak. "Papa. Listen. Do you hear it?"

He listened. Under the distant, exhausted noise of the city—musketry somewhere far off, a bell ringing wrong, the crackle of the fire dying in the wet—he heard what she meant. Singing. From the barricades, from the rooftops, ragged and rising: the Marseillaise, and verses he didn't know, and laughter between the verses, the laughter of people who have crossed a line and cannot be pushed back across it.

"It's not going to pass over us," Paloma said quietly. "Whatever happens next—this isn't weather, Papa. It's a season. Nothing will be the same after. Which means the old rules won't hold either. The rules that said we stay invisible, we stay safe, we wait." She put her hand flat on the sacking over the atlas, the way you'd quiet a living thing. "There won't be a better time. There may not be any time."

Henri stood on the cellar steps with his hand on the cold stone wall and understood that the afternoon had asked him its real question, and that he had answered all the wrong ones. Fire, mob, roof—he could manage those. But his daughter had grown a will in the dark of his careful house, patient as type waiting in its case, and it had come out tonight, and it was her mother's will entire.

"The letter," he said at last, hoarsely. "You've read it again."

"I've memorized it."

"Paloma—"

"Tomorrow," she said. "Or the day after. While everything is upside down and no one is watching the roads. Papa, everything is upside down. It's the only chance we will ever get."

* * *

The night came down on the print quarter like a lid.

Fires burned themselves out. Patrols of citizens replaced the soldiers at the barricades, and the soldiers' abandoned shakos lay in the gutters filling slowly with black rainwater and blacker ink, until the two could not be told apart. In the Delacroix shop, father and daughter sat up by one shaded lamp among the steel sorts they had carried to safety, neither sleeping, both listening.

Near midnight there came footsteps in the street—deliberate, unhurried, wrong for the hour—and then a soft scraping against their shutters, and Henri was across the room with the poker in his hand before Paloma had risen from her chair.

A folded paper slid under the door.

They let the footsteps fade before Henri picked it up. Inside was a single printed slip, cheap paper, crude type—a street press, done in haste—and the type was familiar to Paloma in a way that turned her cold. She knew that battered long s, that worn e with the chipped counter. It was Béranger's type. Béranger's press, printing from wherever Béranger had fled, and the slip bore a list:

*Printers of the quarter who served the police. Names taken from the Prefecture. DELACROIX & FILLE.*

Below it, in a different, handwritten hand: *We know what you burned. We know what you kept. The quarter has a long memory, citizen. Choose your side while choosing is still possible.*

Henri read it twice. Then he looked up at his daughter, and she watched thirty years of invisibility die in his face—die not easily, but die, the way a fire dies, in stages, giving light all the way down.

"They were watching the shop," he said. "Before all this. During. They know about the letter." His voice was very quiet. "Someone talked."

Outside, the rain thickened toward snow. Somewhere east, beyond the river, the city rang its bells all night without stopping, and the quarter's new masters walked the barricades with torches, and the atlas lay wrapped in sacking on the cellar steps like a sleeping animal that had finally, unmistakably, begun to stir.

"We leave tomorrow," Henri Delacroix said.
