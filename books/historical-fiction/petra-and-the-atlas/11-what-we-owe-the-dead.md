## 11. What We Owe the Dead

The riot had passed them over like a storm that splits around a single house, and in its aftermath the print quarter lay strangely quiet, the way a street lies quiet after a funeral procession has gone by—everyone still indoors, everyone listening to their own heartbeats. Smoke hung low over the rooftops from the burning barricades on Rue des Tanneurs, and through it the morning light came down the color of tarnished brass.

Paloma had not slept. She sat at the worktable with the atlas open before her, both hands flat on the pages as though the thing might rise and walk away if she loosened her grip. The lamp had burned down to a stub. Outside, somewhere below the window, a cart creaked past carrying something heavy—she did not let herself imagine what.

Her father stood at the top of the stairs.

She heard him before she saw him: the particular weight of his tread, slower now than it had been three days ago, as if the letter had aged his bones overnight. He came into the workshop with his coat still buttoned wrong, one lapel folded under the other, and he looked at the atlas, and then at her hands upon it, and Paloma watched him arrive at a decision the way one watches a man step off a curb into traffic.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say we should burn it." She turned the page—the map of a country called Vessengard, its rivers inked blue-green, its mountains stippled in her mother's fine crosshatch—and laid her palm across it. "You've been working up to it since the inspector came. I've watched you do it. You look at the drawer the way you look at the stove."

Henri Delacroix took off his ruined hat and set it on the shelf where the good brushes lived, which was what he always did when he wanted a moment to think and didn't want anyone to know it. The gesture was so familiar, so much *him*, that for one treacherous second she almost softened. Then she remembered the empty drawer. The brass key warm against her breastbone where she'd hidden it inside her bodice all through the inspector's visit. Her mother's name signed in the margin of page forty-one.

"Paloma." He said her name gently, and gentleness was worse than shouting; gentleness meant he believed she could be persuaded. "Sit down properly. Take your hands off the book."

"They're my hands."

"And this is my shop," he said, "and my debt, whatever it is, and I will be the one to pay it. Not you. Not your mother's memory dragged through a tribunal because you've fallen in love with a parcel of paper."

*Parcel of paper.* She felt the words go through her like a bodkin through a signature. She stood up. The chair scraped back and the sound was enormous in the hushed room, and she thought absurdly: *the neighbors will hear, the neighbors will inform,* and then thought: *let them.* Something had come loose in her during the night, some bolt that had held her whole life together, and she found she was no longer afraid of noise.

"The promise wasn't made to you alone."

"It was made to me."

"It was made to *three* people." She opened the atlas to page forty-one before her courage could fail her and turned it toward him, her finger under the signature. *M. Delacroix, engraver. A. Delacroix, co-engraver.* Her mother's hand, small and exact, the letters leaning slightly eastward like a row of birds about to fly. "Read it. Read her name. You've had this book locked away for ten years and I don't believe you ever once read her name."

Her father went very still.

It was a stillness she knew. She had seen it in him twice before: once when the doctor told them there was nothing more to be done, and once, years earlier, half-remembered, when a man had come to the shop asking after engravings and her father had said *we only do type* in a voice like a slammed door. It was the stillness of a man standing at the edge of something deep.

"She asked me to lock it away," he said at last. His voice had gone rough. "That summer. When it was finished. She wrapped it in oilcloth herself and she said, *not until Véra sends for it, Henri, not before, promise me.* And I promised. And then—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "And then she died, and the sending never came, and I thanked God every year that it never came, because every year the borders grew uglier and the men who drew them grew crueler, and I thought: *she would understand. She would want us safe.*"

"Would she?"

"Don't." The word cracked out of him like type snapping under the press. "Don't you dare stand there and tell me what your mother would have wanted. You were eleven years old. You remember her hands and her humming and the smell of the varnish, and that is a treasure, Paloma, but it isn't the whole woman. You didn't sit across from her while she worked. You didn't see what it cost her."

"What what cost her?"

He turned away. That was answer enough, and it was no answer at all, and the fury that had been building in Paloma all night crested and broke.

"I have been invisible my whole life!" She heard her own voice climb and did nothing to lower it. "You taught me to be! Keep the shutters half-closed, don't take commissions from strangers, don't print anything with an edge on it, smile at the inspector, bow to the censor—we are dust, Papa, we are *furniture*, we are so careful to be nothing that I began to believe I *was* nothing! And now there's one true thing in this house, one beautiful, dangerous, honest thing, and it has *her name in it*, and you want to feed it to the stove because you're frightened!"

"Of course I'm frightened!" He rounded on her, and up close she saw that he hadn't slept either, that his eyes were rimmed red and his hands shook where they gripped the edge of the composing stone. "I sat across from Inspector Marchand and lied to his face and my heart went like a trip-hammer, and do you know why I could lie? Because I have practiced. Twenty years of practicing being harmless. And it worked—it worked right up until a courier put a sealed letter in my hand and I understood that everything I built our safety out of was made of paper too. Paper burns, Paloma. Men are burning presses in the streets right now. Men are dying for less than what's sitting under your hands."

"Then give it to me."

The words came out of her before she had decided to say them, and having said them she could not unsay them, and she discovered she did not want to.

Her father stared at her. "What?"

"The atlas. Give it to me. If it must be someone's neck, let it be mine. I'm the one who found the key. I'm the one who—" She faltered, because saying the next part aloud made it real: "I'm the one who has to know what else is in here. What she hid in here. Papa, she planned this. She signed herself co-engraver on a book you were supposed to have made alone. That isn't pride. That's a message. She left me a message sewn into the borders of a dead country, and you want to burn the postman's bag before I've read the letter."

* * *

Henri Delacroix looked at his daughter and saw, with the terrible clarity of exhausted men, that arguing with her was like arguing with a river in spring.

She had her mother's face. He had never told her that—he had spent ten years not telling her that, because every time he looked at Paloma's jaw or the set of her mouth he felt the loss fresh as a laceration—but it was true, and it had never been truer than now, standing in lamplight with her chin up and her hands spread over another woman's life's work. Aurélie had argued exactly like that. Not loudly. Relentlessly. With that terrible patient certainty, as though the rightness of a thing were a fact already settled and everyone else simply needed time to catch up.

He had loved her for it. He had buried her for it, in the end—not in the ground but in silence, in a locked drawer, in twenty years of careful gray harmlessness that he had mistaken for a life.

And now the letter had come, and the world outside was on fire, and his daughter was asking him to choose between the two things he had sworn to protect: the child, and the promise. Except that the child was grown, and the child was choosing, and perhaps that was the part that frightened him most—not the soldiers, not the tribunals, but the discovery that he had raised a person who could stand in front of a fire and refuse to flinch.

"Burning it doesn't bring her back," Paloma said, quieter now. "But keeping it might bring *her* back. To me. Whatever's in those margins. Don't you want—I know you do, Papa, I saw your face when I showed you her name. For one second you weren't frightened. You were *home*."

"That's exactly what terrifies me." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. When he lowered them he looked older than his father must have looked, older than any man of forty-six had a right to look. "A home can be taken. A shop can be seized, a press can be smashed, a family can be scattered to three different prisons. But if I burn it—if we burn it together, tonight, and watch the smoke go up the flue—then it's finished. The debt dies. Marchand finds nothing. We go on."

"We go on *being furniture*."

"Yes! Yes, God help me, yes—being furniture, being dust, being alive!" His voice broke on the last word, and Paloma saw, suddenly, the boyish man her mother must have married, the one who had engraved maps in peacetime and drunk brandy with a foreigner and believed borders were temporary. Grief had calcified him, year by year, into this careful gray creature, and underneath the calcification he was terrified and grieving and hers. "I cannot bury a daughter, Paloma. I did it once already. Do you understand? Every day since she died has been a kind of burial, and I got through it because I still had you, and you are asking me to dig the grave myself."

The room was silent except for the hiss of the lamp and, far off, the tolling of a church bell that nobody had asked to ring.

Paloma came around the table slowly. She did not touch the atlas. She touched her father's sleeve, the coarse wool of it, the way she had when she was small and the thunder scared her.

"Papa. The promise was made to her too. Not just to Véra. To *her*. She wrapped it in oilcloth. She made you swear. If you burn it, you break a promise to a dead woman to keep a promise to a living coward—and I love you, and you're not a coward, you're the bravest frightened man in Paris, but you know I'm right." She swallowed. "And there's a girl—or a woman now, an old woman by now—across the border who has been waiting ten years for a book her father never got to hold. Waiting the way we wait. What do we owe the dead, Papa? You taught me the trade. Tell me. What do we owe them?"

He did not answer for a long time. His eyes had gone to page forty-one, to the small eastward-leaning letters, and Paloma watched him read the name again and again, a man drinking water after years in a desert and unable to stop even though it hurts.

When he finally spoke, his voice was so low she had to lean close to catch it.

"There's a man on the Rue du Fouarre," he said. "Cazal. He does papers. He owed me money from before the censorship laws, and he's never once repaid a debt in his life without turning a profit on it, and he'll want something from this book, and giving it to him will be like handing a knife to a stranger on a dark road." He drew a long breath, and let it out, and something in his shoulders gave way—a decade of careful gray harmlessness collapsing quietly, like snow sliding from a roof. "And it may be the only road east that isn't watched."

Paloma's breath caught. "You'll help me?"

"I'll help you get it out of Paris," he said, and his hand came up and covered hers on his sleeve, and it was trembling, and he did not hide it. "After that, I find I am no longer the man who decides things in this family. Your mother fixed that ten years ago and I'm only now reading the letter." He managed something that was almost a smile, wrecked at the edges. "Go and fetch the oilcloth. She kept it in the bottom of the drawer. She always said a book travels best dressed for bad weather."

Paloma crossed to the drawer and knelt, and her fingers found the folded oilcloth where it had lain untouched for ten years, stiff with age and smelling faintly of linseed and her mother's varnish. As she lifted it out, something slipped from between the folds and rang softly on the floorboards—a second key. Smaller than the first. No compass rose stamped on this one, but a single letter, worn nearly smooth:

*C.*

She held it up, and her father looked at it, and the blood left his face for the second time that week.

"Cazal," he said slowly. "Your mother kept a key to Cazal's door."
