## 12. The Forger's Favor

The rain had turned the Rue des Tanneurs into a river of grey tallow by the time Paloma found the door she was looking for. It was narrower than the others, wedged between a chandler's shop and a house whose shutters had not been opened since the last king sat on the throne, and it bore no sign, no number, nothing but a knocker shaped like a fish that had been polished by so many nervous hands it gleamed like a coin.

She had walked past it twice already, telling herself the third time was coincidence. Her father's ledger lay against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath it, in a satchel disguised as a schoolgirl's book-bag, the atlas slept under a false bottom of cheap primers. She could feel its weight even through the layers, the way one feels a bruise—constantly, and more when touched.

Cazal. The name had surfaced the night before, while her father slept and she sat turning the pages of the old debt-book by candle stub. Her father kept accounts the way he kept everything: meticulously, and with a tenderness he would have denied. Most entries were ink and paper, type and labour. But here and there, in the margins, in a smaller hand, were the other debts—the ones never spoken of at the dinner table. *A. Cazal, rue des Tanneurs. For services rendered, 1841. Forgiven, not forgotten.* And beneath that, in her father's careful script: *He will not forget it. Men like Cazal remember what they owe longer than what they are owed.*

She had not told her father she was coming. That was the trouble with the argument they'd had three nights ago—it had not ended; it had only gone underground, like the river beneath the city, and it moved through her now with the same cold pressure. He wanted the atlas burned. She had said no, and he had looked at her as though a stranger were sitting at his table wearing his daughter's face, and she had looked back and thought: *perhaps one of us is.*

The atlas could not be burned. She had made her peace with that the way one makes peace with a stone in the shoe—by deciding to walk anyway.

She raised the fish knocker and let it fall twice.

A long silence. Then a voice, dry as paper left too long in the sun: "If you're from the inspector's office, you've wasted a climb. I'm a decorator of picture frames. I have been for thirty years."

"I'm not from any office," Paloma said. "I'm from the Delacroix print shop, Rue Saint-Aubin."

Another silence, longer. She heard a chair scrape, a body rising with the complaint of old joints, and then the sound of three bolts and a chain being drawn, each one reluctant, like a man undoing buttons he'd rather keep fastened.

The door opened the width of a forearm. One eye regarded her, brown and quick and surrounded by skin the texture of crumpled newsprint. "Delacroix," the man said. "The old one's dead, then?"

"No. He's well. He doesn't know I'm here."

"Even better." The door opened fully, and Auguste Cazal stepped back to admit her into a hallway that smelled of turpentine, old varnish, and beneath both, faintly, the acid bite of the etcher's bath. "Come in before the street remembers your face. The street forgets nothing."

* * *

The room above the hallway was a museum of false lives. Frames of every style leaned against the walls—gilt baroque, plain oak, painted vernis Martin—each one empty, each one waiting to lend its respectability to whatever would hang inside it. On the workbench, half-finished, lay a marriage certificate so convincing that Paloma's eye snagged on the embossed seal and believed it before her mind caught up. A brazier glowed in the corner, and above it hung sheets of blank paper being aged by smoke, their edges already browning like autumn.

Cazal lowered himself into a chair by the brazier and studied her with the unhurried attention of a man who had spent his life studying things closely enough to reproduce them. He was smaller than she'd imagined, and older—seventy, perhaps, or a well-preserved sixty that had stopped pretending. His hands, though, were extraordinary: long-fingered, steady, the nails trimmed to the quick, the right middle finger callused into a ridge from a lifetime of holding a graver or a pen.

"Paloma," he said, reading her name off her face the way she read type. "The daughter. You used to come with him on Sundays, years ago, when he still came to the quarter. You sat on that stool and inked proofs and got it on your nose." He almost smiled. "You had ink on your nose the way other children have freckles."

"I don't remember you."

"Good. That was the arrangement." He gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit. And tell me what the old man wants, since he's too proud to come himself. A passport? A bill of lading with a date that never happened? A receipt for a sale that didn't occur? I've done all three for better men and worse."

"He doesn't know," Paloma said again, and sat. "I came for myself."

That interested him. She saw it move through his face like a fish under ice. "Go on."

She had rehearsed this on the walk over, three versions, four, each one trimmed of risk. Now, in the amber light of the brazier, with the false marriage certificate lying on the bench like evidence, she found all her versions had evaporated, and what came out was the plain truth, or near enough.

"I need travel papers. For me, and for a crate that will travel with me. I need to be an errand girl for a print shop in Besançon—compositor's assistant, delivering type to a client over the border. And the crate needs papers that make it boring. The kind of papers a customs man reads twice and waves through."

"And where over the border?"

She hesitated. He watched her hesitate with something like professional appreciation.

"You see," Cazal said gently, "that's where it becomes difficult. Anyone can make papers for Besançon. Besançon is nothing. But if the crate is going *through* Besançon to somewhere else—Switzerland, say, or further east—then the papers must survive a second inspection, and a third, and the men at the second and third inspections are not the sleepy ones. So." He folded his hands. "Where over the border?"

"East," Paloma said. "Far east. But the papers only need to say Besançon. What happens after Besançon is my problem."

"Your problem," Cazal repeated. He rose, joints crackling, and went to a cabinet whose lock was disguised as a knot in the wood. From it he took a bottle of something dark and two glasses, and poured with the ceremony of a man who had outlived everyone who might have judged him for it. He set one glass before her. She didn't touch it. He drank his own and settled back.

"Let me tell you what I know, girl, and you tell me where I'm wrong. There's a war coming—not the kind with armies, that's already here, look out any window—but the other kind. The kind where names become crimes overnight. I've lived through three of them. Each time, the papers get harder, the questions get longer, and the men who used to wave you through start reading. And here you come, a printer's daughter with her father's careful hands and her mother's—" he stopped. Blinked. Started again. "With her father's careful hands, asking for papers good enough to cross a continent, in a year when crossing a continent with papers is how people die. So either you're running from something, or you're carrying something. And you don't look like a girl who's running."

Paloma said nothing. Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and somewhere down the street a drum began, far off, the way drums had been beginning all week—first one, then an answer, then a chorus.

"Carrying something," Cazal said, answering himself. "And heavy, from the way you sit. Like a woman who's been carrying it for days and has learned not to shift in her chair." He turned his glass slowly on his knee. "I'll tell you a story, and then we'll talk price.

"Eighteen forty-one. Your father came to me on a Sunday night, wet through, with a debt he couldn't speak aloud and a favour he couldn't ask anyone else. There was a man—a friend of his, from before, from the old days when your father and your mother and a certain cartographer used to sit in that back room of yours drinking bad brandy and drawing countries that no longer existed on any map worth printing." Paloma's breath caught, and Cazal noted it, and continued as though he hadn't. "This friend had gotten himself into trouble with the authorities over some of his—associations. Nothing proven, but proven isn't the standard anymore, is it. Your father wanted a set of papers made for him. Clean ones. The kind that let a man become someone else entirely, in another country, permanently."

"And you made them."

"I made them," Cazal said. "And your father paid me half of what I asked, and swore the rest, and I let him swear it, because I liked him, and because—" he paused, and for the first time the forger looked his age— "because the man he was saving had a daughter. A little girl. And I have a rule, girl, one rule, older than your father's shop: I don't ask what happens to the people on the far side of my papers. But I knew, that night, that a child was going to sleep safer in another country because of what came off my bench. It's the only kind of work I've done that I've never regretted." He looked up. "Your father never paid the rest. I never asked. That was the arrangement. So you see, you've come to the right door. The question is what you've brought to pay with, because I don't work for gratitude anymore. Gratitude doesn't buy the charcoal I need, or the silence I need, or the doctor I'll need soon, the way this cough is going."

Paloma's hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the satchel at her side.

Cazal's eyes followed the movement and came back to her face. "Ah," he said, very softly. "There it is."

* * *

"I can't show it to you," she said. "I can't leave it with you. And I won't cut it."

"Cut it," Cazal repeated, and something flickered across his face—hunger, she thought, the specific hunger of a craftsman confronted with a masterpiece he cannot touch. "So it's engravings. Hand-cut. Hand-coloured?" She didn't answer, and he nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a suspicion becomes a certainty he'd half hoped to avoid. "I heard rumours. Years ago. Three engravers, one summer, an atlas of a country that the treaties had erased—maps so fine that a certain ministry would pay a fortune to have them, and another fortune to make sure no one else ever saw them. I assumed it was a story printers told apprentices to keep them at the bench past midnight." He set down his glass. "It's real. It's here, in that satchel, in my rooms, and the whole quarter is about to come down around us, and you want papers to carry it east."

"To deliver it. To the person it was promised to."

"The cartographer's daughter." Cazal said it flatly, without question, and watched Paloma's face do what her voice refused to do. "Ah," he said again, and this time the *ah* was different—quieter, and sadder. "You didn't know that was who you were carrying it to, until recently. Or perhaps you didn't know who *you* were. With families like yours, girl, those are often the same discovery."

The drum down the street had been joined by others. Through the window, in the wet grey light, she could see people moving faster than people should move on a Tuesday—knots forming, dissolving, reforming. The city was a press being set, she thought. Every element in its place. The impression not yet taken, but coming, coming.

"Name your price," she said.

Cazal was quiet for a long moment. The brazier ticked. On the bench, the false marriage certificate dried in its false seal, promising a union that had never occurred to two people who had never met, and would hold up under any scrutiny in the kingdom.

"One page," he said at last.

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. The answer is no. The atlas leaves this city whole or not at all. That's not negotiable. That's the entire—" she stopped, because her voice had risen, and because the entire point was not something she could say aloud in a forger's rooms: *that's the entire debt, that's my mother's hand on every page, that's the promise, that's the only thing in my life I have ever chosen for myself.*

Cazal held up one of those extraordinary hands. "One page," he said again, "and hear me out before you refuse it a second time, because the second refusal I'll take personally. Not a page cut from the atlas—I'm not a barbarian, and I don't want your mother's hand, whoever she was, sliced out of history on my account. A *copy*. One page, chosen by me. I copy it, plate by plate, colour by colour, and I keep the copy, and you keep the original, whole, and no one on this earth will ever know the difference except the three of us in this room, and one of us is a brazier." He leaned forward. "You think I want it to sell? I'm seventy years old, girl. I want it because in fifty years of making false things, I have never once held a true masterpiece on this bench, and I would like, before my hands go, to make one perfect copy of one perfect thing. That's the price. That's the whole price. One page, copied, and the papers you need—yours and the crate's, good through Besançon and beyond—done by Friday."

Paloma looked at the workbench, at the certificate, at the papers browning over the coals. She thought of her father's ledger, *forgiven, not forgotten*, and understood suddenly that this was what her father had understood at twenty-five: that men like Cazal did not want money, because money was the least of what they could make for themselves. They wanted to have touched something real.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

"Then I make your papers anyway," Cazal said, "because I owe your father, and because of the little girl with ink on her nose, and because a man my age is allowed one act of foolishness a decade. But the papers will be my second-best work. There is a difference, and at the second checkpoint, you will learn what it is." He smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, but it was not a kind one either. It was the smile of a man laying his cards down one at a time. "So. Which page?"

She opened the satchel. Her fingers were steady on the buckles, which surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and they did not, and she understood that this too was something she was becoming: a person whose hands did not shake.

The atlas came out into the amber light, and Cazal went very still.

She turned the pages slowly, watching his face as much as the maps—the vanished country unfolding in its impossible colours, rivers that had been renamed and here ran under their old names still, mountains whose borders were drawn not in the hard black of treaties but in the soft watercolour of someone who had loved the land enough to paint its mist. When she reached the final third of the book, she stopped turning.

"Here," she said, and opened to a map of the eastern valleys—the one whose margins carried the most notes in her mother's hand, the little annotations, *wind from the north by October*, *the ferry runs when the snowmelt allows*, in a script Paloma had begun, without meaning to, to know as well as her own.

Cazal bent over it. For a long time he said nothing at all. When he finally straightened, his eyes were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it, and Paloma understood that she had just seen the only honest thing in the room.

"Friday," he said, his voice rough. "Your papers will be ready Friday. Bring the page Thursday night, and bring bread, because we'll work till morning." He was already clearing the bench, sweeping the marriage certificate aside, pulling down a fresh sheet of the finest rag paper from a drawer that breathed camphor. Then he paused, his back to her.

"One more thing, and then you go, because you shouldn't be in this quarter after dark anymore—not this week, not any week again. The inspector who's been working the print shops—Marchetti, the one with the list." He turned. "He was here Tuesday. Not for me. He was showing a copy of a list, and asking did I know the Delacroix shop, and what did they print, and had I ever done work for them." His brown eyes held hers, steady and old and not unkind. "I told him I'd never heard the name. He wrote something down. And as he left, girl, he said a thing I've been chewing on for two days, and I'll give it to you the way he gave it to me, because it's been sitting in my chest like a stone ever since."

Paloma stood, the atlas already back in its satchel, her heart beating in her ears.

"What did he say?"

Cazal wiped his hands on his apron, once, slowly.

"He said: 'Everyone in this quarter is innocent. That's what makes them so interesting.'" He pulled open the door for her, onto the rain and the drums. "Go home the long way. And Paloma—when you cross that border, and you will, because I'm going to make you papers that would fool God's own clerk—don't look back at the checkpoint. Whatever you hear behind you. Don't look back."
