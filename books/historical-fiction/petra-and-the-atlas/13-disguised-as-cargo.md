## 13. Disguised as Cargo

The crate arrived on the morning of the fourth day, and it was the most beautiful lie Paloma had ever seen.

She had watched Cazal's boy drag it up the alley at first light—rough pine, iron-banded, stenciled in fading paint with the name of a type foundry in Lyon that had burned down two winters ago—and by the time the sun cleared the rooftops of the print quarter it sat in the Delacroix back room looking as though it had been sitting there for years. Inside, beneath three layers of straw, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of lamp-black and tallow, lay the atlas: twenty-two hand-colored maps of a country that no longer existed, sewn into boards her mother had once held.

Around it, packed tight as teeth in a jaw, were the sorts. Thousands of them. Lead and wood, pica and brevier, letters she herself had cast on slow evenings—the *e*s and *t*s and *a*s of ten thousand ordinary sentences, none of which would ever be printed now. Her father had spent two nights sorting them, saying nothing, his hands moving with the terrible fluency of a man who had decided something he could not yet say aloud.

"They're searching freight going east," he had told her on the second night, not looking up from the case. "But they're searching for papers. For pamphlets. For men." He had set a row of capital *N*s into the straw like soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder. "Nobody searches type. It's heavy, it's dull, and half the inspectors can't read backwards."

"And if one of them can?"

Her father had paused then, a sort between his fingers, and looked at her across the lamplight with an expression she had never seen on him before—not fear exactly, and not pride either, but some alloy of the two that frightened her more than either alone.

"Then you'll have to be duller than the type, little one."

* * *

The disguise had been Cazal's idea, or perhaps her father's, delivered through Cazal so that no one could say later that Delacroix had sent his daughter anywhere at all. On paper—if any paper were ever demanded—there was no Paloma Delacroix traveling east. There was only a girl called Marguerite Fossier, sixteen years old, employed as an errand-runner by the house of Vasseur & Fils, type-founders of Lyon, whose crate of defective sorts was being returned to the smelter at Besançon under escort. The papers Cazal made her were good. She had studied them by candlelight until she could have recited them drunk, and then she had made herself study the girl they belonged to: Marguerite Fossier, who slouched. Who chewed the inside of her cheek when she was bored. Who had a chilblain scar on her left wrist from a hard winter she had never actually lived through, and who did not flinch, did not go quiet, did not lower her eyes when men in uniform asked questions—because Marguerite Fossier was nobody, and nobodies had nothing to hide.

"You're too well-spoken," Cazal had told her, watching her rehearse. "Say it again, but stupid this time."

"I'm taking the crate to Besançon. Foundry's orders."

"Stupider."

"I'm takin' the crate to Besançon. Foundry's orders." She had scowled, chewed her cheek, scratched at the imaginary chilblain. Cazal had grunted, which from him was applause.

Her father had said almost nothing at all. He had walked her to the door on the last morning, and there he had done something worse than embrace her: he had straightened her collar, twice, though it needed no straightening, the way he used to when she was small and he was buying time before leaving for work.

"The driver is Vasseur's man. His name is Aubry. He knows what he's carrying, but not who made it, and you will keep it that way." He tucked a fold of her shawl under her arm. "You set type for six hours yesterday without lifting your head. Do that. Be the work, not the girl doing it."

"And when I get there?"

"When you get there—" He stopped. Outside, the drums were going again; they had not stopped for three days, coming nearer and then farther, like a tide deciding. Somewhere down the street a shop grate came down with a bang and someone shouted a word that might have been *liberty* or might have been *fire*. "When you get there, you'll know. Your mother always knew the next step. She said it was the only kind of knowing worth having."

He had kissed her forehead, once, hard, and turned back to his cases before she could see whatever crossed his face.

* * *

The road out of the city was a river of everything the century had broken loose.

They went east in a cart pulled by a horse too old for its work, behind a wagon train that seemed to stretch to the horizon: farmers with their livestock tied to carts like hostages, families walking beside the few things they had chosen to save, a man pushing a wheelbarrow containing nothing but a birdcage, the bird still singing inside it as if the whole world were a normal place. Soldiers stood at the crossroads and counted heads but mostly let them pass—these were not deserters, these were not pamphleteers; these were simply people, and people fleeing were the season's weather, unremarkable, unavoidable.

Paloma rode the tailboard of the cart with her feet among the crates and her hands in her lap, and she practiced being Marguerite.

It was easier than she expected, which unsettled her. All those years of keeping her head down, of setting pages by lamplight while the quarter seethed, of being small and punctual and unremarkable—it had not been hiding after all. It had been rehearsal. She knew how to be invisible the way a compositor knows his letters: not by thinking about each one, but by never needing to.

Aubry drove in silence for the first hour. He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the forearms of a man who had lifted type frames all his life, and he smoked a pipe he never seemed to light. Around noon he spoke without turning his head.

"You're Delacroix's girl."

It was not a question, and lying to it felt pointless. "I'm Vasseur's errand girl."

"Mm." He drew on the unlit pipe. "Vasseur says take care of the cargo. Delacroix says take care of the girl. I've decided both are the same job, so don't make it complicated."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't 'sir' me. I work for a living." But the corner of his mouth moved, just barely, and after that he talked—a little, here and there, in the way of men who drive long roads—about the state of the bridges, about how the ferry at Montbéliard had changed hands twice in a week and neither garrison had thought to change the toll, about his daughter in Dijon who was expecting her first child in spring and whom he intended to see whether the century approved or not.

Paloma listened, and answered when spoken to, and kept one hand resting lightly on the nearest crate the whole way, feeling through the pine boards the faint cold weight of lead, and beneath the lead, deeper than any hand could reach, the atlas riding east toward a woman she had never met who was, apparently, her godmother.

She still could not say the word to herself without her chest tightening. *Godmother.* As though her whole childhood had been a page set in type with one line missing, and here, forty leagues away, was the missing line, waiting in the case.

* * *

They reached the first checkpoint an hour past Montbéliard, where the road narrowed along a riverbank and the Doubs ran high and brown with the week's rain.

Paloma heard it before she saw it: the sound of hooves on planks, voices pitched in the bored register of authority, and then the checkpoint itself—a barrier of rough timber across the road, a brazier burning despite the mild afternoon because braziers were what checkpoints were *for*, four soldiers and a sergeant with a ledger, and beyond the barrier the queue of carts and wagons stretching back the way they'd come, patient as cattle.

"Aubry." A soldier with a corporal's stripes wandered up the line, kicking a loose stone off the roadway with the air of a man performing diligence. "What's this, then?"

"Freight. Defective sorts going back to the smelter at Besançon." Aubry produced the manifest from inside his coat with the weary smoothness of a man who had shown papers at this barrier since before the corporal owned boots. "You've seen my seal a dozen times, Corporal Renaud. You drank my brandy at harvest fair."

"That was before the republic, wasn't it." But there was no heat in it. The corporal took the manifest, glanced at it, glanced at the cart. "Who's this?"

"My escort. Foundry's errand girl."

The corporal looked at Paloma.

Here it was, then. The moment Cazal had warned her about, the moment she had rehearsed in front of a cracked mirror until the mirror fogged: *don't look back at the checkpoint, whatever you hear.* She let her gaze slide past the corporal's shoulder to the river, to the brown water carrying sticks and leaves eastward, and she chewed the inside of her cheek and scratched at her wrist and thought, deliberately, about nothing. About the boredom of long roads. About how many times she had been asked her name in her life and how little it had ever mattered.

"Papers?" the corporal said.

Paloma handed them over with slightly grubby fingers and the expression of a girl who had surrendered papers at every barrier between here and Lyon and expected to surrender them at several more. The corporal read. He was young enough that his mustache was more ambition than achievement, and he read slowly, his lips moving over Cazal's forged names—*Marguerite Fossier, born Dole, 1832*—names that had never existed and were therefore perfectly true.

"Errand girl," he said. "For a type foundry."

"Someone's got to carry things," Paloma said. "I carry things."

"What sort of things?"

"Sorts." She shrugged. "Heavy ones."

One of the soldiers behind the barrier laughed—a short bark, quickly suppressed. The corporal's ears went faintly red. He thrust the papers back at her. "Open the crate."

And there it was. Not the question she had prepared for—she had prepared for *where are you going*, for *who is your father*, even for *take off your coat*—but not this. The atlas lay in the third crate, wrapped in oilcloth beneath straw beneath a stratum of lead type that her father had packed with the density of a man laying a foundation wall. If the corporal dug past the sorts—

"He'll want the straw repacked proper," Aubry said mildly. "Vasseur counts the sorts on arrival. Every one. You want to explain to a founder why his shipment came up short because a corporal got curious?"

The corporal hesitated. Down the queue, another cart driver shouted something about the war being lost while they stood chatting, and the sergeant at the barrier barked at the corporal to move the line, they'd be here till dark at this rate, and the corporal—caught between a sergeant's displeasure and a founder's manifest—did what most men do when authority presses from both sides.

He jabbed a bayonet into the top crate, the nearest one, the one full of nothing but lead *e*s and wooden quads, stirred it around with a scrape and clatter that made Paloma's teeth ache, and pronounced it type.

"Move along."

The barrier swung up. The cart rolled through, onto the plank bridge, over the brown water, eastward. Paloma kept her eyes fixed on the middle distance, on the road, on her own grubby hands folded in her lap—did not look back, did not look back, did not look back—even as the corporal's voice drifted after them, complaining to the sergeant about smart-arsed errand girls, even as the brazier smoke thinned behind them and the sound of the queue resumed its patient murmur.

Only when the bridge was a hundred yards behind them, when the road bent north into beech woods and the checkpoint vanished entirely, did Aubry speak again.

"Well," he said, drawing on the dead pipe. "Either you're the best liar I've ever driven, or you really are that bored."

"Both," Paloma said, and discovered her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her knees.

Aubry glanced sideways at her, and this time he did smile, briefly, like sun through cloud. "First checkpoint's the worst. Everyone says so. After that it's just road." He shook the reins. "Eat something. You've gone the color of your cargo."

* * *

They made camp that night in the yard of a burned-out coaching inn, one of a dozen such along the eastern roads, its roof open to the stars, its walls black to half their height. Other travelers had gathered there too—a mason's family, two old women with a goat, a young man with a bandaged hand who said nothing to anyone—and fires sprang up in the ruins like flowers growing through pavement.

Paloma sat with her back against the cart wheel, sharing bread and hard cheese with Aubry, and listened.

That was the thing she had not expected, the thing no plan had accounted for: how much people talked, once the lamps were out and the borders were close. The mason's wife described the village her husband had been born in, across the line, where his brother still lived and where he had not been able to visit in nine years because the border had moved one autumn and cut the village's churchyard in two, so that half the family's dead now lay in a foreign country. One of the old women spoke of a valley that appeared on no map anymore—not because the mapmakers had missed it, but because the valley had been traded away in some treaty signed by men in a room she could not have found on any map either. "My mother was born there," she said, to no one, to the fire. "There's a country where my mother was born, and it doesn't exist. What am I supposed to do with that? Where do I put it?"

No one answered her. There was no answer. But around the fire, heads nodded, one after another, the way wheat nods in wind, and Paloma sat very still with her bread going cold in her hand and understood, for the first time, not with her mind but somewhere lower, in the body, what she was carrying east.

Not maps. Not lead and straw and oilcloth. Twenty-two proofs that places did not have to vanish—that a valley, a village, a churchyard split by a treaty could be drawn in ink and watercolor by loving hands and kept alive on paper until someone came to claim it. Her mother's hands. Her father's hands. And hers, now, the errand hands, the carrier's hands, the hands that would finish what the peacetime summer began.

She thought of the marginal notes in her mother's writing—*here the light comes earliest*, written beside a river bend; *the bell tower leans, draw it leaning*—and she thought: I am delivering the leaning tower. I am delivering the early light.

Aubry banked the fire and rolled into his blanket. "Sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we take the ferry road. There's a crossing at L'Isle that isn't on the army's schedule, which means it also isn't on anyone's mercy."

Paloma lay back against the cart wheel, the satchel with her papers under her head, the atlas within arm's reach, and looked up at the stars wheeling slowly over the ruined inn. Somewhere ahead, past ferries and barriers and a border drawn by men in rooms, an old woman named Véra Lindqvist was waiting for a book—or didn't know she was waiting, or had waited so long she had stopped. Somewhere behind her, her father was sitting alone among his cases in a darkening shop, cutting letters for a world that might burn them.

She closed her eyes. Sleep came slower than she wished, and lighter than she needed.

She woke once, deep in the night, to the sound of hoofbeats passing on the road—several horses, moving fast, going east, riders not stopping to rest—and lay listening until the sound faded, telling herself it was a courier, a patrol, anything.

But when dawn came gray over the beech woods, Aubry was already awake, standing in the road looking east, and his face, when he turned back to her, had gone carefully, professionally blank.

"Up, Marguerite," he said quietly. "We're not the only ones on this road anymore."
