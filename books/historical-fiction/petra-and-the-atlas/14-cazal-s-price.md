## 14. Cazal's Price

The lamp in Cazal's back room burned the way it always burned—low, hooded, turned toward the wall so that no glimmer showed through the shuttered window. Forty years of forgery had taught him that a man's light was the first thing that betrayed him, and so Auguste Cazal had lived most of his life in a glow no wider than a coin.

He had the page flat on the table now, weighted at its corners with four smooth stones he'd taken from the Seine in another decade, from another life, and he sat looking at it without touching it the way other men sat looking at a woman they had no business wanting.

It was beautiful. That was the trouble with it. Anyone could counterfeit an ugly thing and sleep well after; but this—the coastlines inked in a hand so fine the lines seemed less drawn than breathed, the mountains hatched like the grain of walnut wood, the sea shaded from a pale green near the shore into a blue so deep it looked cold to the touch—this was the sort of work that made a forger feel what he was: a man who made copies of things because he had never quite managed to make anything of his own.

*Carta della Contea di Valmorea*, read the cartouche, in letters shaped like the arches of some small and patient church. And beneath it, smaller, in the same hand: *Engraved in peace, that it might be remembered in war.*

Cazal poured himself two fingers of brandy and did not drink them. The glass went down on the table with a click, and the page trembled at its edges, very slightly, as if it had felt the sound.

He knew the country on it. Not by name—he had never heard the name Valmorea before tonight, and he suspected that half the ministries of Europe hadn't either—but by *kind*. It was one of those places that existed the way a mist existed over a river valley: real enough to walk through, impossible to hold. A duchy once, perhaps. A free territory. Something old that the treaties of 1815 had folded into larger shapes the way a laundress folds a sheet into a larger sheet, until the original seams vanished altogether and only the cloth remembered.

Except the cloth didn't forget. That was the whole secret of his trade. Paper remembered everything ever written on it, even when scraped. Ink bled through skins. Men who redrew borders believed they were drawing on emptiness, but there was no emptiness anywhere in Europe anymore, only older drawings underneath, waiting.

He turned up the lamp a fraction and bent closer.

There were notes in the margins. He'd noticed them the moment Delacroix's daughter laid the page on his table and unwrapped the oilcloth, and he'd kept his face still, because a forger's face is the first tool he owns and must never show wear. But now, alone, he let himself look properly. Tiny annotations, pencilled and then inked over in a careful brown, in two different hands—one upright, methodical, the hand of a man who set type for a living; the other slanted, quicker, almost laughing, the letters leaning east as if they wanted to travel. Beside a village called Serevane someone had written a word in a language Cazal half-recognized and beneath it, in French: *they call it the threshing place — festival in late July — ask for the Ferenc family.*

Ask for the Ferenc family. In a village that had not appeared on an official map since before the girl who'd brought him this page was born.

Cazal picked up his brandy finally and drank, and the drink tasted the way all his drinks had tasted for thirty years, like money earned slightly dirty.

* * *

He had known Théodore Delacroix since before either of them had grey in their beards, which in Cazal's case was a considerable time ago and in Delacroix's case had arrived early, worn into him like weather into a south-facing wall. They had met over a debt—a small one, a printer's debt, paper bought on credit during a bad winter—and Cazal had repaid it not in coin, which he never had when it mattered, but in services. A marriage certificate for a cousin whose bride was already showing. A bill of lading adjusted here and there. Over the years the debt had become a friendship of the peculiar kind that exists between a respectable man and an unrespectable one: warm, infrequent, conducted mostly across doorways, each of them privately certain the other would come to a bad end.

Delacroix had never asked him for anything large. That was the point of the arrangement. The day the daughter came alone to his door, wrapped to the chin, with oilcloth under her arm and her father's hesitation written all over her face, Cazal had understood at once that something large had finally arrived, and he had named his price without letting himself think about it, the way you step onto thin ice quickly so as not to hear it crack.

One page. Any page I choose. That was the price, and she had gone white and then set her jaw in a way that must have been her mother's way, because it certainly wasn't the printer's, and she had said, "You may choose any page except this one," and put the Valmorea map down between them like a card played out of turn.

And Cazal—who had spent his whole life reading what people meant instead of what they said—had understood that the page she protected was the page worth having, and chosen it anyway, gently, the way you take a thing a person cannot afford to give and pretend afterward that they offered it.

Now it lay on his table under four river stones, and he could not make himself begin.

* * *

The copying itself was nothing. He had done finer work drunk. He prepared a sheet of the same rag paper—he kept stocks of papers the way other men kept wines, by age and provenance—and ground his inks from the same pigments, testing each against the original edge with a jeweler's loupe until the greens agreed and the blues agreed and the deep cold blue of the open sea matched so exactly that holding both sheets to the lamp he could not have sworn which was mother and which child.

That should have satisfied him. Copying had always satisfied him, the way counting satisfies a miser: the pure arithmetic of the thing, line answering line, no judgment required, no soul implicated. For forty years the pleasure of his trade had been precisely that it asked nothing of him but skill. A forger, he liked to tell young men who came to him wanting to learn the craft—which they always did, and whom he always turned away, because the craft needs fewer hands than people think—was not an artist. An artist chooses. A forger only obeys. The document tells you what it wants to be and you become its servant, and there is a kind of cleanliness in servitude, a freedom from the terrible weight of deciding.

But tonight the document would not tell him what it wanted to be. Tonight the document just lay there being a country, and every hour he worked at it he became more aware that countries are not documents, they are people—they are villages with festivals in late July and families named Ferenc, they are the particular blue of a particular stretch of cold sea—and that somewhere in the ministries of Paris there were men whose entire profession consisted of erasing such places, and that his copy, perfect as it would be, could serve those men as easily as anyone else.

A border is information. That was the thought that kept stopping his pen. And information about where a border used to be, or ought to be, or was claimed by some exile or some general to still be—that was not a curiosity. That was a weapon with a delay on it, a mine buried in paper, waiting for the right foot. He had forged love letters and death certificates, banknotes and pardons, and told himself each time that his work moved individuals, one life at a time, and that one life at a time was a scale a compromised man could live with. But maps did not move individuals. Maps moved armies. Maps moved the idea in a chancellor's head that a valley was rightfully his.

He set down the pen. His hand had begun, he realized, to shake—not the tremor of age, which he knew intimately and had negotiated with for years, but a different shaking, older, from further down.

* * *

There had been a night, in '14, in Brussels—no. Earlier. Before. There had been a night when he was young and clever and poor and a man had paid him extravagantly to alter a surveyor's chart, shifting a river boundary by the width of two fingers. Two fingers of ink. Cazal had done it in an afternoon and been paid for a year, and he had laughed about it for months, told the story in taverns as his own private joke against the great world, how empires could be nudged by Auguste Cazal and his steady left hand.

He had learned, later, what the alteration had been *for*. He had learned it secondhand, the way one learns such things—a rumor, a newspaper, a name in a list of the dead from a district that had changed hands. A village had ended up on the wrong side of his two fingers. Or rather: it had ended up on whichever side suited the men who held the paper, and the people of the village, who could not read a survey chart and had never been consulted about rivers, had discovered one morning that they were foreigners in their own street. Some of them had walked away from their houses rather than change their allegiance, carrying what they could, and the road they'd walked had been muddy, and winter had been coming, and Cazal had read the account of it sitting in a warm café with the feeling of a man who touches his own pocket and finds a knife there he doesn't remember buying.

He had never altered a border again. That was his one rule, self-imposed, unannounced, absurd in a man with no other principles anyone could detect: *no boundaries.* He would forge anything a human being needed to survive—papers, names, histories, pasts—but he would not move a single line between one people and another. Other forgers thought him superstitious. Perhaps he was. But a man who lives by breaking rules must keep one intact somewhere, or there is nothing left of him to break rules *with.*

And now look at him. Look at the page under the stones. Because this atlas—this beautiful, doomed atlas—was nothing but boundaries. Every page of it was a boundary, lovingly cut, hand-colored, annotated in two devoted hands: the shape of a country that treaties had erased, preserved in ink by three friends in a peacetime summer as if ink were a place to hide a nation. It was the most dangerous object that had ever lain on his table, more dangerous than the banknote plates he'd once copied for a man later hanged for it, and it was dangerous precisely because it was not a weapon. It was a memory. And memories, Cazal had learned in forty years of watching governments work, are the only things those men truly fear, because a weapon can be confiscated and a man can be shot, but a memory, once loose, has to be argued with—and no ministry in Europe has ever won an argument with a memory.

So why did his hands want to shake?

Because the same quality that made the atlas harmless to armies made it priceless to them. A completed record of a vanished country's true borders—its exact, documented, defensible extent—was worth more to a chancellery than a regiment. Somewhere in the archives of the Quai d'Orsay or its rivals there were men who would pay a fortune for these pages, and other men who would burn a printing quarter to keep them from circulating, and Cazal had chosen as his fee the one page that proved the whole enterprise real: the master map, the heart of it, the sheet every other page referred back to. He had told himself it was a fair price for treasonous labor. He told himself still. But a fair price and a clean one are different animals, and he had known, watching the girl's face as she surrendered it, that he was taking something she could not replace and giving her something she could not refuse.

She had needed papers for herself and safe passage for the atlas. She had not told him where. She had not told him for whom. She had the Delacroix gift for silence, that family—ask them the time and they'll tell you the weather, and mean kindly by it. But Cazal had watched her wrap the page again in its oilcloth with the tenderness people reserve for relics, and he had done his sums. A vanished country. A courier's secrecy. A girl traveling east in a year when everyone with sense was traveling west. There was really only one arithmetic it made.

Véra Lindqvist. The name had been in the newspapers all spring, wearing the black clothes of infamy: enemy agent, foreign spy, corrupter of loyal subjects, her property subject to seizure, her correspondents subject to arrest. Cazal had read the notices with the professional detachment he brought to all official language, noting how the accusations grew more florid with each printing, which is the surest sign that a government fears rather than knows its quarry. Enemy agent. Of *what*, exactly? Of a country that no longer existed, apparently. Of a country that existed only in an atlas locked in a guildhall library and in the margins of the page now lying under four stones from the Seine.

They were delivering a country to a woman who had been declared its enemy. And he, Auguste Cazal, was being paid in its capital.

* * *

He worked until the third hour past midnight. That much must be recorded in whatever ledger heaven keeps for men like him: he did the work, and he did it perfectly. The copy grew under his hand line by line, coast answering coast, the little church-spired letters of the title taking shape, the sea cooling from green to that deep, cold, faithful blue. Twice he stopped to compare a pigment against the original, and twice the match was exact, and each time the exactness gave him no satisfaction at all, only the old gray comfort of obedience, of serving the document, of not choosing.

Near the end, working on the marginal notes, he copied the annotation beside Serevane—*festival in late July — ask for the Ferenc family*—and found that his eyes had blurred. He sat back. Wiped them with the heel of his hand, annoyed, and blamed the lamp smoke, which was a lie so old it practically qualified as a colleague.

It was not the note that undid him. It was the second hand. The quick slanted one, leaning east, the one that wrote like laughter. He had seen that hand before—once, decades ago, on the flyleaf of a book in Delacroix's shop, above a woman's name he had not thought of in years, a name the whole quarter spoke softly the way one speaks in a room where someone has died. Co-engraver. The dead wife. The girl's mother. Whoever she had been, she had cut these lines with her own hands, colored this sea, written *ask for the Ferenc family* in a village that no longer officially existed—and then she had died, ten years ago, and her country had stayed dead on every map in Europe except these, and her husband had locked it in a drawer and raised their daughter to set other people's words and want nothing, and now the daughter was carrying the whole resurrection east through a country full of soldiers, and Cazal was being paid one page of it for forging her papers.

One page. He had demanded it casually, the way powerful men demand things, and she had given it the way the powerless give things: completely, with her jaw set, keeping her eyes dry by force of will. He had told himself it was commerce. Everything in his life had been commerce; commerce was how a compromised man slept.

But the copy was finished now, and finished things stop being arguments and start being facts, and the fact lay drying on his table: a perfect twin of a country, made by a forger, for no purpose yet declared, in a season of revolution.

Cazal capped his inks. He cleaned his pens with the slow ritual care of a man delaying a decision, then gave up the pretense and simply sat, elbows on the table, looking from the original to the copy and back, the way he had looked at it hours ago, like a man looking at something he had no business wanting—or, tonight, no business *having.*

Here is what he knew, in the honest hour when the lamp burns low and the brandy bottle stands empty and no client can see you: he knew what such borders were worth, and to whom. He knew that within the week, men from the ministries would come sniffing along the routes out of Paris, following rumors of a printer's crate, because a neighbor had talked—someone always talked; betrayal was the only industry in Paris that never lacked workers. And he knew that when they came asking, the trail would pass through him, because trails always passed through him; he was the narrow gate through which every false paper in the quarter eventually flowed. They would find his copy. They would recognize what it was. And then Auguste Cazal, who had survived forty years by mattering to no one, would discover whether his one rule was worth anything at all.

Unless.

He got up, stiffly, knees cracking like the ice on the millpond of his childhood, and took from the shelf behind the false panel a plain wooden box, and from the box a sheet of good paper, and he sat back down and began—for the first time in thirty years—to write a letter that no one had hired him to write. His pen moved slowly. He was out of practice at meaning what he wrote.

*To the Archivist of the Guildhall Library,* he began. *Concerning a map you may soon be asked about—*

Outside, far off toward the river, a church bell struck four, and somewhere nearer, a shutter opened and closed, and Cazal did not look up, because a forger learns early that in Paris, at four in the morning, someone is always watching—and for the first time in his long, gray, obedient life, Auguste Cazal found that he did not especially care.
