## 15. The Road East

The road east began where the cobblestones ended, and Paloma learned within the first hour that it had a language of its own.

It was not a road so much as a river of people—carts drawn by horses too thin to complain, handcarts stacked with mattresses and birdcages, a man carrying an entire loom on his back like a beetle's shell. Children ran between the wheels. Old women walked with their arms folded across their chests as if holding something in, or keeping something out. Nobody hurried and nobody rested; the whole procession moved at the pace of exhaustion, which is to say steadily, without hope of stopping.

Paloma had meant to travel alone. She had rehearsed it: keep to the ditches, keep her hood up, answer no questions, be nobody, the way she had been nobody all her life in the print quarter. But by noon of the first day she understood that alone was impossible on this road. The road did not permit it. The road collected people the way a gutter collects rain, and once you were in the current you were carried among them whether you wished it or not.

She fell in beside a woman driving a small cart heaped with bedding and a locked chest, pulled by a donkey with a mournful face. The woman was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, wearing a man's coat over a good dress gone gray with dust. She looked at Paloma sidelong for half a mile before she spoke.

"You're not fleeing," she said. It was not an accusation. It was an observation, made the way one might note the weather.

"I'm going east," Paloma said carefully. "To family."

"Nobody goes east to family," the woman said. "Everybody goes west, or north, or anywhere but east. East is what we're running from." She clicked her tongue at the donkey. "So either you're lying, or you're the strangest girl on this road."

"My name is Lise," Paloma said, giving the false name she'd sewn into her papers along with Cazal's careful ink. "I'm a printer's errand girl. I'm delivering type."

"Type," the woman repeated. "Letters."

"Sorts. Little pieces of metal. For printing."

"And there's family east who needs little pieces of metal." The woman snorted, but there was no malice in it. "Well. Lies keep better than bread on a road like this. I'm Marta. From Kesselwald."

Paloma didn't know the name. She nodded anyway.

By evening they had formed something like a caravan—not by agreement, simply by proximity, the way stones in a current gather smaller stones. There was Marta with her cart; a young couple named Anselm and Ida who had walked out of a burning village with nothing but each other and a frying pan; a schoolteacher, Herr Brandt, who wore his spectacles on a string around his neck so he could take them off when he cried, which was often; and two brothers, fourteen and nine, whose mother had paid a smuggler to take them west and had not been able to afford passage for herself. They were going east to an aunt. They did not speak of the mother. The younger brother, Tomas, had attached himself to Paloma by the second morning, walking in her shadow, asking questions.

"What's in your crate?" he asked. Everyone asked about the crate. She had bought it a place in Marta's cart for a share of her coin, and it rode among the bedding like a sleeping animal.

"Type sorts. Metal letters."

"Why?"

"Because someone far away needs them."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My father was a printer," he said. "In Kesselwald. Before."

Before. On this road, everything was *before*—before the soldiers came, before the harvest burned, before the border moved, before. Paloma had lived her whole life inside a single *before* that had never ended: her father's shop, her father's rules, the lamplight and the lead. These people had watched their *before* end in fire.

"What kind of printing?" she asked, and Tomas's whole face changed, the way a shutters-open window changes a dark house.

"He printed hymnals," Tomas said. "And almanacs. And once—" he lowered his voice, though there was nothing to hear but wheels and wind—"once he printed maps. Little ones, folded into the almanac, of our country. Our real country." He glanced at his brother, who was walking ahead and pretending not to listen. "The new maps say Kesselwald belongs to the Empire now. But Papa said maps are just men's opinions drawn in ink. He said the country doesn't stop existing because a line moves. Then he died, and I don't know if that's true anymore."

Paloma's hand went, without her willing it, to the strap of her satchel beneath her coat, where the atlas's final page lay sewn flat against her ribs. She had been carrying it like a coal against her skin for days now, aware always of its weight, its warmth, its terrible singularity. But Tomas's words landed on it like a bell struck.

"The country doesn't stop existing because a line moves," she repeated.

"That's what Papa said." Tomas kicked a stone off the road, viciously, a boy-sized act of war. "Everyone on this road knows it. That's why they're crying, even the old ones. Not because their houses burned. Houses can burn anywhere. Because the map changed under their feet while they were standing on it."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined chapel, and around the fire the stories came out the way stories do at fires—unhurried, passed hand to hand like food.

Herr Brandt told of the school in his town where the new textbooks arrived in autumn and the old ones were collected and burned in the yard, and how he had stood watching the pages rise on the heat and thought: *I am watching children learn to forget.* He had kept one book, hidden inside the lining of his coat, and now he could not decide if he was a thief or an archivist. "Perhaps both," he said, and took off his spectacles.

Anselm told of the night the soldiers came through his village requisitioning grain, and how his grandfather, who could not read, had hidden the deed to the farm inside the frame of a picture of a saint, because paper had saved them once and he trusted it to save them again. The farm was gone now, over the new border. But Anselm knew where the deed was. "Someday," he said to Ida, and she put her hand over his, and neither of them finished the sentence.

Marta talked least. When she finally spoke, it was past midnight, most of the camp asleep, and Paloma was awake because she was always awake now, listening to strangers breathe.

"Marta," Paloma said quietly. "Kesselwald. What happened there?"

The old woman fed a stick to the fire. "You really don't know the name."

"No."

"No reason you should. It was never on the important maps." She said it without bitterness, the way one states the color of a stone. "There was a valley. Vineyards on the south slope, timber on the north. A river that flooded every spring and everyone complained about and everyone loved. My husband's family had been there four hundred years. Four hundred years, girl. Longer than the Empire, longer than the Kingdom on the other side of it. We weren't theirs and we weren't anyone's. We were Kesselwalders." She poked the stick deeper. "Then there was a war nobody in the valley wanted, and afterward there was a table in a city full of gentlemen with rulers, and they drew a line down the middle of the river. Half to one empire, half to the other. Families split. Churches split. Graveyards split—you couldn't bury your dead next to your dead anymore without a passport."

Paloma sat very still. In her mind, unbidden, rose the image of the atlas open on her father's workbench at night: the hand-colored plates, the borders drawn soft and sure, the country rendered in greens and golds that existed nowhere else. She had thought, studying it, that it was beautiful and strange—a country that was only ink. Now she understood that the ink was the truest thing about it. The country existed exactly there, in pigment and line, because everywhere else it had been erased.

"There was a cartographer once," Marta said suddenly. "Years ago. Before the line. A foreigner, came through measuring everything, making friends with my husband over brandy. Odd pair. He said he was going to make a proper map of the valley, a loving map, the kind governments never make. My husband laughed at him. Said no one would ever want such a thing." She looked into the fire. "I wonder sometimes if it got made. If somewhere there's a piece of paper with Kesselwald whole on it, the way it used to be. The way it still is, if paper counts."

Paloma's heart was beating so hard she was afraid it would wake the camp.

"What was his name?" she whispered. "The cartographer."

Marta frowned, dredging decades. "Foreign name. Northern. Lindqvist—no, that was the daughter. Véra. Lovely girl, spoke three languages, drew flowers in the margins while the men argued about elevation. The father was... Anders? Aldous? Something old-fashioned." She shook her head. "Why do you ask?"

"A name from home," Paloma said. It was almost true. "A name I've seen written down."

Marta studied her a long moment in the firelight, and Paloma braced herself to be seen through—the way one braces for cold water. But the old woman only nodded slowly.

"Names travel funny," she said. "Further than people. Further than armies. You write a name down and fifty years later it turns up in a stranger's mouth in the middle of nowhere." She pulled her coat tighter. "Sleep, Lise. The road takes more than it gives unless you sleep."

But Paloma lay awake until the stars wheeled, her palm pressed flat against her ribs, against the page, against the buried weight of everything she was carrying and had not yet understood. Her mother's hands had colored these margins. Her mother had drawn flowers beside a cartographer's daughter while men argued about elevation. Somewhere in the atlas—lost now, seized with the crate at the ferry crossing, God rot the soldiers who took it—somewhere in those stolen pages was a valley called Kesselwald, whole and green and four hundred years unbroken, drawn in peacetime by people who believed paper could hold what armies could not.

Paper counted. Tomas's father had said it. Marta had wondered it. And Paloma, who had set ten thousand lines of other people's words in lead without once believing they mattered, felt the belief arrive in her chest like a struck match: quiet, small, and utterly consuming.

* * *

Two days further east, at a crossroads shrine where refugees had left offerings—a ribbon, a boot, a child's carved horse—they met a priest walking the wrong way.

He was young, dusty, barefoot, carrying his shoes tied around his neck like Herr Brandt's spectacles, and he was going toward the border instead of away from it. The caravan regarded him with suspicion until he explained: he was returning to his parish, which lay on the far side of the new line, because his congregation could not cross to him and he would not ask them to come to faith over a checkpoint.

"They moved the church," he said simply, sitting on the shrine's step and accepting a heel of bread. "Not the building—the border. By eleven kilometers. Eleven kilometers, and now my parishioners are foreigners in their own baptisms. So I walk back. Let them arrest a priest if they must. Christ was not born on one side of a line."

The schoolteacher laughed, wetly. "They'll arrest you for less."

"Then they'll arrest me for the truth," the priest said, "which is the only charge I've ever wanted."

That evening, walking again, Paloma found herself beside him. Something had loosened in her since the fire at the ruined chapel—some door she hadn't known was barred. She heard herself say, "Do you believe paper lasts longer than power?"

The priest looked at her with mild, tired eyes. "I believe," he said, "that power writes on paper too. Every decree, every border treaty, every list of names to arrest. Paper is where power lives." He smiled faintly. "Which is why paper is also where it can be fought. A man with a sword can kill a village. A man with a press can raise a hundred villages against him. That's why they burn presses first, daughter. Did you know that? In every uprising, in every occupation—the presses go first. Power fears paper more than powder."

Paloma walked in silence for a long time. Ahead of her the road climbed a low ridge, and beyond it, hazed in evening gold, lay the country she was walking into: fields, woods, a river catching light—and threaded through all of it, invisible, the new line, the opinion drawn in ink that had split graveyards and churches and families and valleys, that had turned Marta's four hundred years into contraband.

Her crate of type sorts rode behind her on Marta's cart, ordinary, unremarkable, exactly as designed. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false bottom she had built herself with a joiner's borrowed saw, lay the final page of an atlas that did not officially exist, addressed to a woman declared an enemy agent, describing a country that appeared on no current chart.

Once, it had been cargo. Once, it had been a debt. Once, in the lamplight of her father's shop, it had merely been a promise she didn't understand.

Now it was ammunition. Now it was the only map of Kesselwald that might exist anywhere on earth. Now it was her mother's hand and a godmother she'd never known and a valley full of people like these—like Tomas and Marta and the priest with his shoes around his neck—who needed someone, anyone, to prove that the line had not erased them.

"Lise?" Marta called from the cart. "You've stopped walking."

Paloma realized she was standing motionless on the ridge, and that everyone was looking at her, and that tears were running freely down her face in front of forty strangers. She wiped them with the back of her wrist and laughed—a strange, cracked sound.

"Sorry," she said. "I was thinking about maps."

"A dangerous trade," Herr Brandt said gently, settling his spectacles back onto his nose. "Thinking about maps."

They crested the ridge together as the sun went down, and in the last light Paloma saw, far ahead at the bottom of the valley, the ferry crossing where her crate was supposed to meet the river barge—and beside the crossing, unmistakable even at distance, the gray tents and tethered horses of a military post that had not been there when she planned her route.

Marta saw it too. She said nothing. But her hand found Paloma's shoulder, and gripped it, and did not let go.
