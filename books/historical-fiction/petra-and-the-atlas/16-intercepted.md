## 16. Intercepted

The ferry crossing at Viremont was nothing but a scar in the riverbank—a mud shelf, two pilings wrapped in tarred rope, and a flat-bottomed barge that had been groaning back and forth across the water since before Paloma was born. On the map in her head—the map she had memorized from the atlas before sewing it away—it was marked with her mother's small, patient hand: *crossing, seasonal; ice from Nov.*

There was no ice yet. Only fog, lying on the water like something drowned.

Paloma stood among the crates waiting for the ferryman, her hands buried in the sleeves of a coat too big for her, her hair bound up under a printer's cap that smelled of someone else's sweat. She had not slept properly in four days. Sleep came to her now only in fragments, and each fragment ended with the same image: the crate of type sorts going onto the cart, Cazal's fingers drumming once on its lid—*good luck, little Delacroix*—and the road unspooling eastward under a sky the color of dirty tin.

The atlas was forty paces behind her, packed in sawdust between trays of ten-point Caslon, disguised as an ordinary consignment for a provincial bookseller. Forty paces. She had learned to measure distance that way now: not in miles but in how long it would take to reach it if everything went wrong.

Everything went wrong.

* * *

It began with the sound she would hear in dreams for years afterward: the wet slap of boots on mud. Not the ferryman's boots—the ferryman wore wooden shoes and moved like a man who had made peace with slowness. These were soldiers' boots, six pairs of them, coming down the cart track through the fog with the unhurried confidence of men who did not need to hurry.

Paloma turned toward the crates as if checking them, as if she were exactly what she appeared to be—an errand girl minding freight—and let her eyes do what her father had taught her eyes to do in the shop when inspectors came: go flat, go stupid, go nowhere.

The soldiers emerged from the mist one at a time, materializing like figures pulled out of a developing print. A sergeant in front, gray-whiskered, his sword hilt worn bright from handling. Behind him five infantrymen with their muskets slung and their collars up against the damp, and behind *them*, worse than any soldier, a thin man in a civilian coat with a leather document case chained to his wrist.

She knew the shape of that case. She had seen its twin in her father's shop three weeks ago, held by an inspector with soft hands and soft questions.

"Whose freight?" the sergeant said, not to anyone in particular, which meant to everyone.

A drover further down the bank shrugged elaborately. The ferryman spat into the water. Nobody wanted to own anything this morning.

The thin man walked the line of crates without touching them, reading stenciled labels upside down, his lips moving. He stopped at the third crate. The third crate. The one with the false bottom and the bookseller's mark and Cazal's forged consignment papers sewn into oilcloth beneath the lid.

"Delivered by whom?"

"A carter," Paloma heard herself say, and was astonished at how steady her voice came out. "Paid in advance."

He looked at her then. She felt the look travel over her like a hand—cap, coat, boots, the smudge of ink she had deliberately rubbed along her right forefinger so that her hands would tell the truth even if her mouth lied. Ink-stained girls were invisible. She had counted on it.

"You're from the printing quarter," he said.

"Everyone's from somewhere."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement, more like the memory of it—and he turned back to the crate. "Open it."

The sergeant produced a hatchet. Paloma watched the blade rise and thought, with a strange floating clarity: *I could run.* The bank dropped away to the reeds on the left; the fog was thick enough to swallow a person whole; the ferryman would ask no questions because asking questions got ferrymen hanged these days. Three steps into the reeds and she would be nothing again, nobody, the invisible girl, safe.

But the crate would be opened anyway. And whatever they found in it would be read, copied, catalogued, carried back to the men whose whole trade was borders—who drew lines across countries the way her father drew rules across a page, except that where her father's lines held words, theirs held armies.

Her mother had spent two summers engraving a country that no longer existed, so that someone might remember it had ever lived at all.

The hatchet fell.

* * *

They took the lid off in pieces. Sawdust spilled out pale as bone meal. The sergeant reached in past the trays of type and lifted them out one by one, setting each down with unexpected care—he was a man who respected other men's tools, or perhaps simply other men's property when there was paperwork attached—and then his arm went deep into the crate, elbow-deep, shoulder-deep, and stopped.

When he withdrew it, the oilcloth bundle was in his hands.

Paloma watched him turn it over. Watched the thin man step forward and cut the twine with a little knife from his waistcoat pocket, the way one opens a gift. Watched the first corner of the wrapping fold back to show the marbled endpaper, green and gold, the pattern her mother had chosen because it reminded her of river water in summer—

—and Paloma moved.

Not toward the reeds. Toward the crate.

"No," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like a bad casting, and that was better, that was perfect, because no smuggler's accomplice sounds like that, only a frightened girl does. "That's my father's. That's—I'm supposed to see it delivered. If it's broken he'll beat me. Please." She let her chin tremble. It did not take much acting; the trembling was real, only misdirected. "Please, monsieur, it's just maps."

"Maps." The thin man weighed the word. He had the atlas open now, and Paloma saw his face change as he looked at the plates—the fine engraved coastlines, the hand-colored hills, the marginal notes in two different hands, one firm, one flowing—and she understood, with the cold certainty of a falling object, that he recognized quality when he saw it, and that quality was the worst thing it could have recognized.

"These are old," he said softly.

"They're my father's," Paloma said. "From before."

"Before." He smiled at her then, kindly, terribly. "Nothing is from before anymore, child. There is only after."

The sergeant was already prying up the false bottom with the flat of the hatchet, finding the space beneath where the remaining quires lay wrapped, and Paloma stood in the fog with her heart going like a press under full pull and calculated, in the last clear seconds she had, exactly what remained to her.

The final page. The dedication page. Her mother's secret, folded to the size of a prayer book and stitched with waxed thread into the lining of this stolen coat, between the wool and the canvas, over her left breast where a beating heart would explain any thickness a careless searcher might feel. Cazal had sewn it there himself, kneeling in his workshop with a sailmaker's needle, grumbling: *"If they search you properly, girl, nothing will save you. But if they search you lazily—"*

Soldiers searched lazily. Soldiers searched errand girls hardly at all.

"Name?" the thin man asked, writing already in a notebook.

"Coralie Farge," she said. The name Cazal had built for her out of dead women's paper. "Apprentice to M. Bonnefoy, bookseller, Rue aux Ours."

"Mm." He wrote it down, which meant she would exist in a file somewhere, a small dry seed in some archive, waiting. "And you were to see this delivered where?"

"To the bookseller at Saint-Aubin. Then on, I suppose. I only carry it to the crossing." She let her voice go dull with repetition, the voice of a girl reciting instructions she didn't understand. "I'm not paid to know what's in it."

No one laughed. The sergeant set the last quires aside. Two soldiers began reloading the crate with the brisk indifference of men confiscating rather than stealing—though Paloma noted, with a part of her mind that never stopped working, that the thin man handled the atlas himself, did not entrust it to any soldier's arms, and kept it open on top of the document case as though he could not stop looking at it.

"Load it," he said. "The boat too. Search the others."

They searched the others. The drover lost a flask of brandy and nothing else. An old woman with geese lost her dignity and kept her geese. The ferryman stood with his arms crossed and watched his barge fill with soldiers and seized freight and did not say a word, and when the sergeant jerked his thumb at the far shore he pushed off into the fog without protest, because the war had come to mean, for people like him, simply this: doing what boots told you to do.

Paloma stayed on the bank.

They had not ordered her anywhere. That was the mercy of being nobody—you were furniture until you moved, and furniture was rarely arrested. She stood among the emptied sawdust and watched the barge shrink into the whiteness, taking with it the weight of two summers of her mother's life, her father's guilt, Cazal's price, the promise made over brandy to a laughing foreigner before borders meant anything.

She watched until the fog closed over the wake.

Then she sat down in the mud, quite suddenly, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her, and put her face in her ink-stained hands, and wept—not prettily, not quietly, but the way a thing breaks when it has been holding itself rigid for four days and nights.

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough that the fog began to thin. Long enough that the old woman with the geese paused beside her on her way up the track and, without stopping, without looking at her, pressed something into the mud near Paloma's knee: a boiled egg, still warm from some pocket.

"Eat it," the woman said. "Crying dries you out worse than walking."

Then she was gone, geese complaining behind her, and Paloma sat with the egg in her palm like an offering at a shrine to ordinary kindness, and something in her chest turned over and began, slowly, painfully, to work again.

*Eat it.* As if the world expected her to continue.

She ate it. She wiped her face with her sleeve. She looked east.

The road ran along the river and vanished into the hills, the same road the barge was floating parallel to, the same destination, Saint-Aubin and beyond, the border country, the place where Véra Lindqvist waited—or did not wait, or waited for a delivery that would now never come. The atlas was gone. Gone into the hands of a thin man with a chained document case who could not stop looking at it, which meant it would be studied, which meant the inspector in the capital would learn of it within days, which meant the trail would lead back through the forge of Cazal's papers to the Delacroix name, to the shop, to her father standing among his cases of type with his gentle ruined hands.

*I've killed us,* she thought. *I've led them straight home.*

And then, underneath that thought, quieter, stubborn as water finding stone:

*You haven't finished it. You haven't done what she asked.*

Because the atlas was gone but not all gone. Because her mother had been a woman who cut metal into letters and maps and plans, and a woman like that did not leave the ending of her life's work in a crate to be confiscated. A woman like that sewed the ending where it could not be taken—at least, not easily, not by lazy searchers, not by sergeants with hatchets and thin men with notebooks.

Paloma put her hand flat against her left breast. Through the wool and the canvas she felt the small stiff rectangle of the page, and beneath it her own heart, still going, absurdly, faithfully, like a press that would not stop pulling.

The final page. The dedication she had not yet dared to read in full, because Cazal had warned her: *not on the road, not in daylight, not until there is no way back.* Well. There was no way back now. The way back was a confiscated crate floating east in the fog, and the way forward was a road and a river and a border she had never seen, guarded by men with orders about enemies of the state.

She stood up. Her legs shook. She made them hold.

The sergeant's party had left a sentry at the landing—a boy, really, seventeen at most, musket grounded, bored, stamping his feet against the damp. He glanced at her as she rose, and she gave him the dull look of Coralie Farge, apprentice, a girl whose father would beat her, a girl carrying nothing worth a sentry's trouble, and he looked away, and she walked past him up the cart track with her empty hands swinging and her stolen coat heavy around her, and he did not stop her, because invisibility was a performance and she had been performing it her whole life, and grief had only taught her to play the role deeper.

At the bend in the road, where the fog was lifting and the eastern hills showed gray-green under a breaking sky, she stopped.

There was a shrine there—one of the roadside ones, a niche in a wall with a chipped saint inside and rain-dark candle stubs—and she went to it not to pray but to think, because thinking needed a fixed point the way setting type needed a composing stick. She leaned her forehead against the cold stone and made herself list, item by item, the inventory of what she had lost and what remained.

Lost: the atlas. The shop, almost certainly, within the week. Perhaps her father. Perhaps everything.

Remaining: one page, sewn over her heart. A forged name. A road east. A godmother she had learned of nine days ago, in a snowbound house she could not yet imagine, at the end of a journey every reasonable voice in her head was screaming was impossible.

Her mother had crossed worse odds with a burin and a copper plate and the belief that a country could be kept alive in ink. Against that, Paloma thought, a girl with a page in her coat and fury enough to burn for heat was not poorly armed.

She straightened. She took one last look west, toward the river, toward the fog, toward everything she could not save, and let the look hurt as much as it needed to, and then she turned her back on it.

The road east climbed away from the water, and Paloma Delacroix—no, Coralie Farge, until the world changed again—began to walk.

Behind her, at the landing, the sentry stamped his feet and stared at the fog and saw nothing at all.

Ahead of her, in the lining of a stolen coat, her mother's last words waited to be read.
