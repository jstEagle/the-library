## 17. The Last Page

The hayloft smelled of dust and animal warmth and, faintly, of the river that ran somewhere below in the dark. Paloma had not meant to stop here. She had meant to keep walking through the night, to put another ten miles between herself and the ferry crossing where the soldiers had taken the crate, but her legs had betrayed her at the sight of the farm's low roof and its one lit window, and now she sat cross-legged on a bed of straw with a stolen candle stub guttering beside her and the last page of the atlas open across her knees.

It was all she had left of it.

She had sewn it into the lining of her coat with trembling fingers while the soldiers argued over the crate's manifest, while the ferryman swore he was only carrying type sorts for a printer in the east, while her whole heart stood still in her chest like a stopped clock. The page was heavy paper, hand-pressed, and it had ridden against her ribs ever since, warm from her body, so that she kept forgetting it was there and then remembering all at once, a coal tucked against her skin.

Now she looked at it properly for the first time.

It was a map — of course it was; every page of the atlas was a map — but it was unlike any of the others she had studied by lamplight in the empty drawer nights, in the guildhall library, in the back room of Cazal's shop while the old forger licked his thumb and turned the pages with something close to reverence. The others had shown the vanished country whole: its rivers braided silver, its mountains hatched in fine patient strokes, its towns named in her mother's small upright lettering. This last map showed nothing but the border country itself, the eastern marches where the country ended and the next one began, and it was drawn strangely — the rivers running off the edge of the page as if the cartographer had refused to acknowledge any boundary at all, as if the land simply went on being itself no matter what treaties said otherwise.

And along the bottom margin, in what appeared at first to be decorative flourishes, ran a line of tiny marks that were not decoration at all.

Paloma had noticed them before, on the ferry, in the moments when fear left her mind quiet enough to notice anything. She had assumed they were engraver's marks, registration points, the private shorthand of two women who had worked side by side over a plate. But now, with the candle close and her eyes tired enough to see honestly, she saw that they repeated. The same sequence, again and again, marching along the margin like a line of ants: a star, three dots, a crescent, a star. A star, three dots, a crescent, a star.

Her father's type cases had taught her the alphabet of symbols long ago — the daggers and asterisks and pilcrows that printers used when letters would not do. And her mother, she remembered suddenly, with a jolt that made her press her hand flat against the coat lining as if the page might fly away — her mother had loved ciphers. She used to set little puzzles in the margins of Paloma's copybooks when Paloma was six, seven years old, hiding messages among the practice strokes. *Find me*, they always said, in the end. *Find me.*

Paloma took out her pencil — the stub she carried for marking manifests — and copied the sequence onto the back of a scrap of wrapping paper. Star, three dots, crescent, star. It occurred four times across the margin. Four repetitions. She counted the symbols between them, looking for numbers, for coordinates, for anything that might be a key.

There was nothing. Only the repetition, patient as stitching.

She sat back against the hay and closed her eyes and let herself think about her mother's hands instead — because sometimes the way into a puzzle was not through the head but through the memory of the person who made it. Her mother's hands had been quick and ink-stained at the knuckles. She had hummed when she engraved, a tuneless little run of notes. She had taught Paloma her letters not with a hornbook but with pieces of type, pressing them into soft wax: *this is an A, this is a B, and this* — holding up the asterisk, turning it slowly — *this is a star, and stars are how you find your way home when everything else is dark.*

Stars are how you find your way home.

Paloma opened her eyes.

Not a cipher to be solved, then. A map to be read. She spread the page flat again and looked at it not as text but as territory — and there, when she looked for them, they were. Tiny stars pricked into the copperplate at intervals along the eastern rivers, so small she had taken them for paper flaws. Each star sat beside a crescent-shaped bend in the water. Between each pair of stars, three dots: three fords, or three villages, or three houses. A route. A route drawn in the only language an engraver could hide in plain sight, invisible to any inspector who searched the atlas, legible only to someone who knew to look — someone who knew her mother, who knew her mother's habit of teaching through stars.

Someone like a daughter.

Paloma laughed, once, a small broken sound in the dark loft, and then pressed both hands over her mouth because laughter in a hayloft after midnight could carry. The route began at the river crossing nearest the ferry where the crate had been seized — her mother had drawn this road twenty years before Paloma would need it, had marked the safe crossings and the villages between them, had stitched a path eastward out of stars. Her mother had known. Had planned for this. Had hidden the delivery inside the delivery, the true cargo sewn into the false one, waiting all these years for a pair of hands that knew how to read margins.

But why? Why hide the route at all? Why not simply send the atlas with directions?

Unless the sender could not be sure who would receive it. Unless the atlas might pass through many hands — hostile ones, indifferent ones — before it reached the right ones. Unless the person who finished it wanted the final page to be legible only to family.

*Family.*

The word arrived quietly, the way important words do, and Paloma sat very still with it.

She traced the route with her fingertip, star to star, and at the end of it — past the last ford, past the last village, up into hills that the hatchwork rendered softly — there was a mark she had almost missed. Not a star. A rose. Small, four-petaled, drawn with the same compass rose that was stamped on the brass key in her pocket, the key that had opened the locker in the guildhall, the key her mother must have cut and stamped herself. And beneath the rose, in letters so fine they were nearly imaginary, a single word in French:

*Filleule.*

Goddaughter.

Paloma did not move for a long time. The candle burned down; the horses below shifted and sighed; somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. She sat with the word in front of her and felt the ground of her life tilt beneath her, gently, the way a ship tilts when it leaves harbor.

*Filleule.* Goddaughter. Which meant a godmother. Which meant—

She turned the page over. On the reverse, in the corner, was a colophon — printers signed their work, even secret work, even work that could hang them — and she had glanced at it on the ferry without reading it closely, because reading closely had been impossible with soldiers' voices in her ears. Now she read it. Three names, in order of contribution, the way her father had taught her such things were done:

*Engraved by Marguerite Delacroix. Drawn after the surveys of Édouard Delacroix. For Véra Lindqvist, who kept the faith, and for her daughter by promise —*

—and there the ink changed. The first part was her mother's hand, unmistakable, the upright letters Paloma knew from a hundred proofs pulled at her elbow when she was small enough to stand on a stool to watch. But the last phrase was written differently. Slanted. Older. Pressed deep into the paper as if the pen had been held by someone whose strength was going, or whose feeling was too much for the nib to hold steady.

*— for her daughter by promise, whom I name here so that borders may never:*

The sentence stopped. The line simply ended, the pen lifted, the thought abandoned mid-flight — or interrupted. Below it, in fresh ink, in a hand Paloma did not know at all, someone had added a single line:

*Ask her yourself. Come east. We will wait as long as it takes. — V.L.*

Véra Lindqvist had held this page. Had written on it. Had waited.

Paloma pressed the page flat against her knees and found that her eyes were wet, which surprised her, because she had thought she was past weeping — past it since the ferry, since the crate, since the sound of soldiers' boots on the deck above the thing she had promised to protect. But this was not grief. She understood that dimly, the way one understands weather changing. This was something older than grief working its way up through her, something that had been buried so long it had forgotten its own name.

Her mother had planned this. Not her father — her father, who burned letters and emptied drawers and wanted to burn the atlas too, who had carried his guilt like a stone in his coat for twenty years. Her mother. Marguerite Delacroix, dead ten years, co-engraver, cipher-maker, star-hider, had built this entire journey into the atlas the way a builder builds a door into a wall that everyone else believes is solid. *For her daughter by promise.* Not *for the keeper of the Atlas* — the courier's letter had addressed her father, had spoken the language of debts and obligations between men. But the page spoke a different language entirely. The page said: I made this for you. I could not say your name aloud, so I hid it in stars. Follow them.

*Whom I name here so that borders may never—*

Never what? Never sever her? Never unname her? Paloma thought of the sentence's broken ending and understood, with a certainty that needed no proof, that her mother had been interrupted — that someone had come into the room, or the hour had come when writing such a sentence was dangerous, or that her mother had simply stopped, pen raised, unable to finish a sentence whose ending was a future she could not guarantee. And that Véra Lindqvist, receiving the unfinished page years later, had answered it the only way possible: not by completing the sentence, but by summoning its subject. *Come east. We will wait as long as it takes.*

A godmother. In all her life, no one had spoken to Paloma of godparents; the Delacroixes were not churchgoers beyond what respectability required, and her mother's death had folded that whole world away like a fan. She had grown up believing her family was small — a father, a shop, a trade, a name that meant nothing anywhere outside the print quarter. She had believed invisibility was her inheritance. And all the while, on the far side of the border, in a snowbound house in hills her mother had mapped with love, a woman had been waiting, keeping a faith, calling her — what? What had they called her, those three friends in the peacetime summer? What name had been promised?

Paloma looked down at the colophon again, at the slanted, pressure-deep handwriting, and read the unfinished sentence one more time. Then she did something she could not have explained if asked. She took her pencil and, beneath Véra Lindqvist's line, wrote in the margin, small and steady:

*I am coming. — P.D., daughter of Marguerite.*

It was the first time in her life she had signed anything as her mother's daughter rather than her father's. The pencil shook only a little.

Then she folded the page along its old creases, sewed it back into her coat lining with numb fingers and the needle she kept for mending, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, the route unfolded behind her eyelids: star, three dots, crescent, star. Eastward. Through the fords her mother had marked, past the villages her mother had named, up into the hills where the compass rose waited at the end of twenty years of patience. The soldiers had the crate. They had eleven maps and a forged manifest and whatever truth Cazal's conscience would or would not tell them. Let them have it. They had seized a body without its soul. The soul rode against her ribs, warm as a second heartbeat, and it was going home.

She lay back in the hay and did not sleep, but she was not awake either; she was somewhere between, in the country her mother had drawn, walking a road lit by stars.

Near dawn, a sound woke her fully: hooves on the frozen lane below the barn, more than one horse, moving slowly, stopping. A man's voice, low, asking the farmer something. Then silence, and then boots on the barn floor beneath her, and the beam of a lantern swinging up toward the loft ladder.

"Anyone up there?" the voice called. It was not a soldier's bark. It was quieter than that, and somehow worse for it — the voice of a man who already knew the answer. "Mademoiselle Delacroix. We know you came this way. There is someone here who wants to help you — if you come down before others arrive who don't."

Paloma pressed her hand against her coat, against the page, and did not move.
