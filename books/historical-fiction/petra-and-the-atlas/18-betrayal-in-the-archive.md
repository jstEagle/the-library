## 18. Betrayal in the Archive

The city of Vireloup had a way of swallowing people, and Paloma had learned to let it. For six days she had been no one—a printer's errand girl with ink-stained cuffs and a borrowed name, sleeping in a room above a chandler's shop where the tallow smell clung to her hair and the floorboards sang under every step. She had learned the rhythms of the street below: the milk cart at dawn, the knife-grinder's wheel at midmorning, the gendarmes' boots in pairs at dusk, always in pairs now, always slower than they used to be, as though the whole apparatus of order had grown tired of running after a country that was coming apart beneath it.

She kept the final page of the atlas against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat by her own hand in a ferry-town privy while her fingers shook so badly she pricked herself four times. It had become part of her in a way she could not explain even to herself—less a document than an organ, something she would bleed if it were cut away.

On the sixth day, she went to the archive.

She had told herself it was caution. A girl carrying nothing could walk into the Bibliothèque du Dépôt like anyone else, could sit among the ledgers of shipping manifests and road tolls and learn whether her crate had passed through Dornay, whether the ferry crossing had logged its seizure, whether the net was drawing closed behind her or ahead of her. But there was more in it than caution, and she knew it. The archive was the closest thing she had left to the atlas itself—the same paper-smell, the same hush, the sense of standing inside other people's careful records of a world that refused to stay still.

The reading room occupied the top floor of a former convent, its vaulted ceiling painted with saints whose faces had been whitewashed during some earlier fit of piety and never restored. Long tables ran beneath them, and along the walls rose shelves of boxes, each labeled in a clerk's faded hand: *Tolls, Northern Roads. Ferries, Department of the Meuse. Correspondence, Seized.* At this hour the room held perhaps a dozen readers—scholars mostly, gray men with gray notebooks—and one woman at the reference desk who did not look gray at all.

Her name, stitched on a ribbon badge, was Mme Aurelle Sanx. She was perhaps fifty, with spectacles pushed up into iron-gray hair and a voice pitched low enough that Paloma had to lean across the desk to hear it, which meant that everything Mme Sanx said felt like a confidence.

"You want ferry manifests," Mme Sanx said, when Paloma asked. "Dornay crossing."

"Yes, madame. For—" Paloma had prepared a lie about a merchant house, a lost consignment of paper stock, but Mme Sanx held up one finger, not unkindly.

"I don't require your reasons. I require your signature." She slid a ledger across the desk. "Readers sign. What they read is their own affair, unless someone asks me, in which case I have a poor memory and worse handwriting." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might live in a woman who had learned to keep them shallow. "Third shelf, aisle four. Boxes marked *Meuse, autumn*. Mind the dust; it gets into everything, including reputations."

Paloma signed. Her false name went down on the line in her true hand, and she thought, absurdly, of her mother's signature in the atlas margins—*Éloise Delacroix, engraver*—and wondered how many names a woman could carry before she stopped knowing which one was hers.

* * *

The manifests told her what she had feared and something she had not.

The crate had been seized at Dornay on the fourteenth—she found the entry easily enough, the clerk's hand neat and incurious: *One crate type-metal sorts, consigned Delacroix & Fils, Paris. Opened per directive. Contents as declared, plus one item undeclared: bound volume, maps, hand-colored. Removed to district headquarters.*

Plus one item undeclared. Her stomach turned over slowly, like a press taking too much weight. They had the atlas. All of it except the page against her ribs. Twenty years of her parents' work, her mother's maps, the vanished country drawn in colors no living chart remembered—all of it sitting in a district headquarters, waiting for some clerk with a good memory and better ambition to understand what he was holding.

But it was the second document, misfiled two boxes later among toll receipts, that stopped her breath.

It was a copy. Not the original—a fair copy made in a different hand, though a good one, confident, practiced. A map. And she knew it the way you know your own face in a dark window: the coastline first, then the river deltas rendered in her mother's particular stippling, then the marginal note in her father's upright script—*surveyed in peacetime; borders provisional*—copied letter for letter, flaw for flaw, even the little ink blot where her father's pen had stuttered one summer evening twenty years ago.

Cazal's page. Cazal's price. Copied perfectly, faithfully, beautifully—and filed here, in the archive, among seized papers, stamped along one edge in violet ink: *Dépôt Central. Received from Inspector B.-A. Roche.*

Roche. The name went through her like cold water through cloth. The inspector from the print quarter, the smooth-voiced man who had stood in her father's shop in the spring and asked his gentle questions about seditious material while his eyes catalogued every press and drawer. He had the page. He had traced it—of course he had; a map of a country that officially did not exist, surfacing through the hands of a known forger, would draw him like a lamp draws moths—and if he had traced the page forward to this archive, he had traced it backward too. To Cazal. To the bargain. To the crate. To *Delacroix & Fils*.

To her.

Paloma sat very still at the long table, hands flat on the manifest, breathing the way her father had taught her to breathe when a forme of type threatened to pie—slowly, evenly, panic being the enemy of everything delicate. Around her the scholars scratched their pens. Dust drifted in the light from the whitewashed saints. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed, and she flinched before she could stop herself.

*He knows the route,* she thought. *Dornay is behind me. Which means everything ahead of me is where he will look.*

And ahead of her lay only one thing: the border. The east. Véra Lindqvist.

* * *

She was re-shelving the box when the shadow fell across aisle four.

"Mademoiselle." Mme Sanx stood at the end of the row, a folio cradled in her arms like a sleeping child. Her eyes, behind the pushed-up spectacles, were doing something complicated—reading Paloma the way Paloma read proofs, quickly and without mercy. "You've been in the Meuse boxes for three hours. That is a long time to spend on toll receipts."

"I'm thorough," Paloma said.

"Mm." Mme Sanx glanced down the aisle toward the reading room, then back. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and every word came out flat and deliberate, like coins counted onto a table. "There is a man who comes here on Thursdays. An inspector. He sits at the corner table because the light is good and because he likes to be seen thinking. This morning he requested the seizure file from Dornay, and also the register of reader signatures for the past week." She paused. "I told him the register was out for rebinding. It was not. It is in my desk."

The dust seemed to thicken around Paloma. "Why would you tell him that?"

"Because I have worked in this room for nineteen years, mademoiselle, and I have watched men carry things out of it that should never have been carried anywhere." Mme Sanx adjusted the folio in her arms. "Maps, mostly. Borders are my profession's disease. Every time the government redraws a line, they send men up here to pull the old charts, and the old charts go into a stove, and I am left shelving empty boxes." Her mouth did that almost-smile again, thinner now. "I have developed opinions about stoves."

Paloma's heart was beating somewhere high in her throat. "The inspector. Roche. Does he know I'm here? Did he see my name?"

"He has your name. He has had it since the print quarter—he keeps lists the way other men keep snuff. What he does not have is proof you came here, and he will not get it from me." Mme Sanx stepped closer, and the smell of her was paper and lye soap and, faintly, candle wax. "Listen to me, girl, because I will say this once and then I will forget it, as is my habit. He copied the Dornay file this morning. All of it. He was smiling, which I have observed he does when he believes the hunt is nearly over. There is a description circulating from the ferry town—a young woman traveling alone, printer's hands, asking questions at the crossing before the seizure. He thinks you doubled back toward the capital. He is wrong, I think. I think you mean to go east."

Paloma said nothing. Saying anything would have been a confession, and besides, her voice had gone somewhere without her.

"The eastern roads closed to civilian travel yesterday," Mme Sanx continued. "Officially, on account of the disorders. Unofficially, because Roche requested it. He cannot seal a border—that requires a ministry and a ministry requires paperwork and paperwork requires a week—but he can make the roads slow, and slow roads give a pursuer time." She shifted the folio. "There is one exception. The archive runs a courier service. Records exchanged between depots—harmless, dusty, official. Couriers carry sealed satchels and are not searched at the toll stations, because searching them would require acknowledging that government archives contain anything worth stealing, and no one in this government wishes to look at what we actually hold." Now the smile arrived fully, and it was not a warm thing, but it was a real one. "We are short a courier. Our regular man drank himself into the Meuse last Tuesday. The requisition for his replacement goes through me."

Paloma stared at her. "You're offering me a courier's satchel."

"I am offering you employment, mademoiselle, which you may accept or decline, and which I will deny offering if anyone asks, which no one will, because no one asks me anything." Mme Sanx pressed the folio into Paloma's arms—actual work, actual ledgers, the perfect cover of a girl doing her job. "The depot at Saint-Aubin-le-Fort is forty miles east, near enough to the frontier to smell it. A courier leaves tomorrow at dawn. If a certain errand girl were to present herself at the service entrance at five o'clock with steady hands and no luggage, I might hire her on the spot, having a poor memory and worse handwriting."

"Why?" The word came out of Paloma raw. "You don't know me. You don't know what I'm carrying or why. You could lose everything—your post, your freedom—"

Mme Sanx looked at her for a long moment over the rims of her spectacles, and something in her face eased, some gate opening briefly onto a courtyard Paloma could not see.

"Nineteen years ago I had a brother who carried letters for people," she said quietly. "Not mine. Other people's. He believed that what one person promises another is none of the state's business, and he was shot for it outside Metz, in 'forty-one, and no one ever told me where they buried him." She straightened the spectacles on her nose, and the gate closed. "So. You will forgive me if I have developed opinions about promises as well as stoves. Five o'clock. The service entrance on the rue des Chartreux. Wear the coat you're wearing—it looks like every errand girl's coat in this city, which is the finest disguise there is."

She turned and walked back down the aisle, spine straight, folio balanced, a gray woman in a gray room, invisible as furniture and twice as load-bearing.

Paloma stood among the boxes with her arms full of ledgers and her heart hammering against the hidden page, and understood that for the first time since the letter had come to her father's shop, she was not alone in this. The knowledge frightened her more than the soldiers had. Things you carried alone, you could only lose alone. Things entrusted to others could be taken from them—and they could be betrayed by them.

*No longer alone*, she thought, testing the weight of it. *God help me. No longer alone.*

* * *

Across the city, in a rented office that smelled of cold tobacco and official ambition, Inspector Benoît-Auguste Roche laid two documents side by side on his desk and permitted himself the smile that Mme Sanx had described.

On the left, the fair-copied map page, beautiful as sin. On the right, the Dornay seizure report, and beneath it a list he had built himself over eleven days of patient, methodical work: the forger Cazal, arrested that morning at dawn and currently explaining, in the manner of his kind, everything he knew to everyone who would listen; the crate consigned *Delacroix & Fils*; the errand girl seen at the ferry crossing; and now, thanks to a rebinding clerk's small lapse and a concierge's smaller bribe, a signature in the archive register dated three days ago.

The name was false. The hand was not. He had watched the girl set type once, in her father's shop, a lifetime ago in spring, and he remembered how she held a pen the way she held a composing stick—precisely, defensively, as if the world might take it from her.

Roche dipped his pen. Outside his window, the city smoked from three separate fires, and the century was coming apart at its seams, and none of it interested him half so much as one line, which he wrote in his neat, patient hand:

*The daughter travels east. Close the roads behind her; leave the road ahead open. Let her run to the border. Everything is easiest to take from a person at the moment she finally reaches for what she wants.*

He blotted the page, rang for his clerk, and gave the order that would put mounted gendarmes on every eastern road by morning—every road but one, which he left deliberately thin, deliberately watchable, a corridor shaped exactly like a trap.

Five miles away, in a chandler's room above the tallow-smell, Paloma Delacroix laid out her one spare shift, checked the stitches holding the page against her ribs, and set her mind on five o'clock, the rue des Chartreux, and the road east—never knowing that the road had already been measured for her, like a grave, like a dress, like a trap closing gently, patiently, around the shape of her hope.
