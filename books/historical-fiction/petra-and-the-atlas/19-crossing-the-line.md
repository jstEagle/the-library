## 19. Crossing the Line

The chandler woke her before the bells.

He did it with a knuckle against the floorboards beneath her—two soft raps, the signal they had agreed upon—and Paloma surfaced from a sleep so shallow it was barely more than a held breath, her hand already at her ribs, finding the small stiff rectangle sewn there. Still whole. Still hers. The page lay against her skin like a second heartbeat, and for one disoriented moment she could not tell which of them was keeping the other alive.

She dressed in the dark by touch alone: shift, stockings, the grey wool dress that made her shapeless as a bundle of rags, the coat with its secret stitched into the lining over her left side, where a man's searching hand would be least inclined to press. She had re-sewn it twice since Cazal's betrayal had come to light through the archivist's warning—twice unpicked the seam and laid the page in fresh cloth, twice drawn the needle through with fingers that wanted very badly to shake. The stitches were small and even now. Her mother would have approved of them. That thought steadied her more than any prayer could have.

Below, the chandler pressed bread and hard cheese into her hands and would not take coin for them. He was a broad, slow man who had spent thirty years rendering fat into light, and he looked at her the way she imagined men looked at candles going out the door of their own shop into weather.

"The boy will meet you where I said," he murmured. "Tomas. He knows the marsh paths better than the gendarmes know their own horses' tempers. Do what he tells you. Even when it makes no sense. Especially then."

"And if he is taken?"

"Then you keep walking east until the ground stops being ours." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow the flatness made it worse and truer both. "Go now. The fog is on the river. It won't hold past six."

The fog did hold, though. It held like a blessing.

Paloma went down the rue des Chartreux with her collar up and her eyes lowered, and the city parted around her the way cities do before dawn—not empty but unwatchful, all its suspicion still abed. A milk cart stood outside the dairy with its brass cans catching lamplight. Somewhere a dog was being sick behind a wall. She walked past the corner where the sympathetic archivist had caught her sleeve two nights ago, whispering *they know about the ferry, they know about the crate, child, go east by the water or not at all*—and she made herself not look at the archive windows, dark above the street, not wonder whether he sat awake in there already regretting his kindness, already composing whatever explanation he would give when the inspector came asking how a fugitive had learned the shape of a trap before stepping into it.

She had left the city once before, disguised as cargo's shadow. This time she left as nothing at all—a girl in grey, carrying bread and cheese and a name she had only owned for a fortnight.

The river took her at the tannery steps.

* * *

Tomas was fourteen and built like a heron—all wrist and ankle and neck—and he stood up out of the reeds so suddenly that Paloma's heart slammed once against the page beneath it and went quiet again, oddly calmed.

"You're the printer's daughter." It wasn't a question. "My uncle said you'd have a face like you're listening to something far away."

"Did he." She kept her voice low, though the fog swallowed sound anyway; voices came back to them flattened, belonging to no one.

"He said you'd argue too."

"I'm not arguing yet."

"Good. Save it." He turned east without ceremony and began to walk along the waterline where the mud was firmest, and Paloma followed, and for the first hour neither of them spoke, because the fog required silence and because some part of her understood that this boy had done this walk before—for others, for strangers, for people whose names he never asked—and that his competence deserved the respect of quiet.

The marsh opened around them by degrees. First the river widened and went slack, shedding its current into blind channels; then the channels multiplied, braiding and rebraiding through reed-beds taller than Tomas's head, and the world contracted to a corridor of grey-green stalks and the sound of their own footsteps and, distantly, the complaint of waterfowl. Twice Tomas stopped, head tilted, and made her stand absolutely still while somewhere off to their left hooves went through shallow water at a trot—the gendarmes riding the dike road, exactly where the inspector wanted watchers to ride, exactly where two fugitives with any sense would not be.

"They'll ride that line till noon," Tomas whispered after the sound faded. "It's what they're paid for. Riding lines." There was contempt in it, young and clean. "My father rode lines for the customs men before the border ate our village. Then the line moved and ate him instead."

Paloma looked at him—at the set of his jaw, the reed-stains on his sleeves—and heard in his voice the thing she had been hearing all along the road east, in refugee kitchens and ferry queues and the chandler's warm room: the borders were not lines on paper to these people. They were animals. They grazed, they moved, they swallowed.

"Your village," she said carefully. "What was it called?"

"Treskow. On the old maps. On the new ones it's a district of somebody else's province." He shrugged as though shrugging off a weight he'd carried so long it had become part of his spine. "Why?"

Because, she thought, my mother drew your village. Because I have seen it in her hand, inked in peacetime, colored soft green for its meadows, with little trees rendered in a stipple so fine you had to hold the page to the light to believe a human wrist had made them. But she didn't say it. Not yet. Some recognitions needed daylight, and solid ground, and both parties alive.

"Someone I loved drew places like yours," she said instead. "Before they were erased."

Tomas glanced at her sidelong through the fog, and something passed between them that needed no elaboration—the look of two people who had each lost a country, hers by inheritance, his by decree.

"Then we'd better get you across," he said.

* * *

They walked until the light came up grey and sourceless inside the fog, and then they slept two hours in a peat-cutter's abandoned hut with a roof of sod, Tomas taking first watch without discussion, and Paloma lay on cold earth with her palm pressed flat over the hidden page and let herself, for the first time in days, think deliberately about what she carried.

Not the atlas itself—that was gone, seized at the ferry with the crate of type sorts, and the grief of it still ambushed her at odd moments, sharp as a stitch pulled tight. Only the final page. Sewn from the binding by her own hands in the ferry-master's chaos while soldiers overturned crates and a sergeant read aloud the manifest and her whole future hung by the question of whether anyone would think to search a fleeing errand girl's coat lining. They hadn't. The page had crossed the checkpoint against her ribs like a second soul, and everything since—the archivist's warning, the chandler's room, this marsh, this boy—had existed for one purpose only: to carry those few square inches of paper the last hundred miles.

Her mother's last page. Her mother's *hidden* page—she understood that now, had understood it since deciphering the dedication written in cipher between the map's compass points, the message meant to survive burning and seizure and the century's great closing of doors. *For V., who kept the western light.* And beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *and for the daughter, whoever she turns out to be.*

Whoever she turns out to be. As if her mother had known there would be a question about it.

Sleep took her mid-thought, dreamless and black as peat water.

* * *

They came to the line at dusk.

Tomas had warned her what to expect and the warning was useless, because expectation has no texture and the border did. It announced itself not with walls or wires—in 1848, in this sodden corner of the world, the frontier was still mostly an idea enforced by paperwork—but with absences. The farms thinned. The dogs stopped barking. A church tower rose ahead of them with its cross removed, leaving a bare stub against the last red light, and Tomas said quietly, "They took it down last year. Said it confused the surveyors." After that the land simply *ended*, in a belt of cut brush sixty feet wide, and beyond the cut brush another country began, looking exactly like this one, which was somehow the most obscene detail of all: same grass, same mist rising off the same kind of ditches, same evening star hanging over it. Two names for one sky.

"They patrol at half past nine and again at midnight," Tomas said, crouched at the edge of the last stand of alders. "Between patrols there's a sentry at the bridge—you see the fire?—and two walking the cut, but the cut is long and they are bored, and bored men walk slowly and look at the ground. My uncle's people cross here twice a month." He handed her a strip of smoked fish. "Eat. Crossing takes nerve, and nerve is meat."

Paloma ate. The fish was oily and delicious and she would remember it all her life.

"What happens," she said, "if they catch us?"

He considered the question with the seriousness of someone who had been asked it many times by many frightened adults. "If they catch us together, they send you back, because you're a woman and alone women get returned. They take me to the fort, because I've done this before and they know my face." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow harder to hear. "So when I say run, you run. Don't wait for me. Don't look back. Swear it."

"Tomas—"

"*Swear it.*"

The star had come fully out now, and the fires on the far side of the cut were lit, three of them, small and orange and patient. Paloma thought of her father, whom she might never see again; of the shop, lost; of Véra Lindqvist waiting somewhere north and east in a snowbound house for a delivery promised thirty years ago by three friends over brandy and engraving plates. Terror was in her—she could feel it sitting in her stomach like swallowed stone, cold and specific, weighing exactly as much as a cell, a trial, a firing squad. But underneath the terror, deeper than it, older, there was something else, and she finally had the honesty to name it.

Purpose. The page against her ribs. Her mother's hand guiding hers across a cut field in the dark.

"I swear," she said. And found, saying it, that the stone in her stomach had changed temperature. Not gone. Transfigured. Fear burned as fuel is still fear, but it moves you forward.

They waited for the walking sentries to converge at the cut's midpoint, where the boredom was deepest. Then Tomas touched her wrist—once, lightly, a touch she would keep forever—and they went.

The cut field was open ground under open stars and every step rang in her skull like a hammer on type metal. Halfway across, a challenge cracked out behind them—one voice, high with the particular panic of a bored man suddenly not bored—and Tomas shouted "*Run*" and the word broke into pieces as shots followed it, two, wide, cracking across the marsh, and Paloma ran as she had never run, arms pumping, the page a brand against her side, the far ditch coming at her like a promise, and then hands—she nearly screamed—hands hauling her down the bank and through freezing water to the chin, Tomas beside her shoving her under the culvert's lip, and the world narrowed to black water, her own thunderous breathing, and boots on the bank above, two pairs, three, and a lantern beam sliding across the ditch's surface inches from her nose.

She did not breathe. She did not blink. She became, with all the skill invisibility had ever taught her, absolutely nothing—a shadow among shadows, a girl-shaped absence in the dark water—and the lantern slid away, and the voices argued about direction, and gradually, gradually, the night closed back over the wound they had made in it.

When the last boot-sound had died, Tomas surfaced beside her, gasping, grinning, alive.

"You didn't look back," he whispered. It sounded like the highest praise his vocabulary contained.

"My mother," Paloma whispered back, teeth chattering, laughing or crying or both, "taught me to finish what I start."

They waded east.

The far bank, when they dragged themselves up it, was ordinary mud. Ordinary grass. A fence post with a number stenciled on it in a foreign hand. Paloma knelt on the ground of the second country, soaked to the skin, shaking, and put her hand flat against the earth as if checking a pulse, and somewhere behind them in the darkness Tomas laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and said, "You're across, printer's daughter. You're across."

She stayed kneeling a moment longer. Behind her: the trap, the inspector, the mounted roads, the shop that was no longer theirs, the whole weighted apparatus of a state convinced that paper was dangerous. Ahead of her: a hundred miles of unknown country, a snowbound house, an old woman with sharp eyes, and a page that needed only to be delivered to become, at last, complete.

Paloma Delacroix stood up. The star that had hung over both countries all evening seemed, from here, to hang over only this one.

"Which way to Véra Lindqvist?" she asked.

Tomas pointed north-east, toward the mountains just going blue against the last of the light—and that was when they both saw, on the road below, a single rider moving fast, not in uniform, not flying any colors, but carrying himself like a man with orders—and the rider, reaching the bend, paused, and raised a hand, and did not lower it.

He had seen them.
