## 21. The Century Turns

The inspector arrived an hour before dusk, when the light through the snowbound windows had gone the color of pewter and the whole house seemed to hold its breath.

Aubertin had not expected the journey to end here. He had imagined, in the long weeks since the ferry crossing, a border town of mud and suspicion, a fugitive dragged from a hayloft, papers scattered like startled birds. He had not imagined this: a stone house set back from a frozen lane, smoke rising straight and unhurried from its chimney, geraniums—of all absurd things—blooming red on a sill in February. He had not imagined that the woman he had crossed half a continent to accuse would open her own door before he knocked.

"Inspector Aubertin," she said, reading his credentials as though they were a calling card at a ball. "You've come a long way to freeze on my step. Come in before the cold does it for you."

She was older than he'd expected—sixty perhaps, or past it—but she stood the way fortresses stand, and her eyes were the pale, unblinking gray of winter water. Behind her, the hallway smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, faintly, of ink.

That last detail steadied him. Ink was his territory. Ink was where every case he had ever built began.

"You are Véra Lindqvist," he said, following her into a parlor lined with books. "Formerly cartographer to the crown surveys of—" he consulted his dossier with deliberate slowness— "a state that no longer exists."

"A state that no longer exists," she repeated, settling into a chair by the fire. "How neatly you say it. As if countries were candles one simply pinched out." She gestured to the seat opposite. "Sit, Inspector. You've ridden three days through snow to ruin my evening. The least I can do is let you thaw."

* * *

Paloma heard the knock from the kitchen, where she had been standing at the window for most of the afternoon, watching the road.

She had been in this house eleven days now, hidden in the attic room among trunks of Véra's dead husband's clothes, and in eleven days she had learned the rhythms of the old woman's days—the morning hour at the desk, the afternoon walk to the wall where the orchard ended, the evenings spent reading aloud in two languages as if neither tongue could be trusted alone. And all eleven days, Paloma had waited for this: hoofbeats on the frozen lane, a uniformed silhouette against the snow, the future arriving the way it always did, without apology.

Véra had heard it too. Paloma had watched her rise from her desk, cross to the window, and study the approaching rider for a long, untroubled minute. Then the old woman had turned and said, "So. They've sent someone at last. Stay upstairs, child, until I call you—and bring me the atlas. All of it, even the last page. It's time it earned its keep."

Now Paloma sat in the dark of the attic with the atlas across her knees, listening to the murmur of voices below. She could not make out words, only their music: the inspector's clipped official phrases, Véra's low, unhurried responses, patient as tide. Once she heard Aubertin laugh—a short, disbelieving bark—and once she heard silence stretch so long she thought the moment had come, the arrest, the end of everything her mother had planned across ten years and a hundred miles.

Her mother. Even now, after everything, the word still opened beneath her like a trapdoor.

*Marguerite Delacroix,* co-engraver. *Marguerite Lindqvist's dearest friend.* The marginal notes in her father's hand, and beside them, in a finer, surer script, notes in another hand entirely—a hand Paloma had grown up watching write grocery lists and birthday cards and never once suspected could cut a coastline into copper. The last page, sewn into her coat through a thousand miles of fear, had given up its secret slowly: a dedication hidden in the engraving itself, visible only when held to raking light, naming the atlas *for V., who taught us that borders are arguments, and maps are how we refuse to lose them.*

And below it, smaller still: *To be delivered by our daughter.*

*Our.* Not *my*. Her mother had written *our daughter*, and her father had cut the plate, and neither of them had ever said a word.

Downstairs, a chair scraped. Footsteps crossed the hall. Then Véra's voice, clear enough now to climb the stairs:

"Paloma. Come down and bring the book."

* * *

The parlor was warm and lamplit, and Inspector Aubertin looked very young in it suddenly, sitting rigid on the edge of his chair with his hat on his knee like a schoolboy called before a magistrate. When Paloma appeared in the doorway with the atlas in her arms, his eyes went to it the way starving men look at bread.

"So it exists," he said. "After all these months. After the searches, the seizures—" He stopped himself, but Paloma saw the flicker behind his eyes: the crate of type sorts seized at the ferry, the soldiers prying up false bottoms, the moment when the manifest had proven true and the atlas had proven absent, and somewhere in that confusion a girl in an errand girl's shawl had slipped away along the towpath with something sewn against her ribs. He knew. Of course he knew. He had been chasing her since autumn, and here she stood, dusted with attic dust, holding the thing itself.

"It exists," Véra said. "It has existed for nine years. Would you like to see why your government has made such fools of itself over it?"

Before Aubertin could answer, she took the atlas from Paloma's hands—not snatching it, but receiving it, the way one receives a child—and opened it upon the table.

Paloma had seen the maps a dozen times now and they still stopped her breath. Countries drawn in peacetime inks: rose madder and viridian, ochre and Prussian blue, borders rendered not as walls but as conversations—shaded zones where languages mingled, dotted lines marking markets that drew traders from both sides, tiny annotations in three hands noting where the wells ran sweet, where the bridges had tolls, where, in the summer of 1839, three friends had drunk brandy on a hillside and sworn the work would outlive every government that tried to unmake what it showed.

"This," Véra said, turning the pages with fingers that trembled only slightly, "is a country your ministry erased from existence six years ago. Its people have not been erased. They live in exile, in refugee camps, in your prisons, Inspector, or they die trying to cross borders that did not exist when they were born there. Your government calls the knowledge of this place seditious." She looked up. "I call it memory. And memory, unlike states, does not require permission to exist."

"The atlas is contraband," Aubertin said, but his voice had lost its footing. "Possession constitutes—"

"Constitute what? Treason?" Véra laughed then, a real laugh, sharp and unafraid. "Look closer, Inspector. Look at what your own archives will tell you, if anyone there still reads. This map was commissioned openly. Surveyed with the cooperation of both governments, in the years when they were pretending to be friends. My late husband brokered the arrangement. I engraved half of it myself." Her finger came down on the fine signature in the corner of the title page: *M. Delacroix.* "Your quarrel is not with a smuggler, Inspector. It is with a promise. And promises between friends are the one territory no army has ever successfully occupied."

Aubertin stared at the page. Paloma watched him do the arithmetic she had done herself a hundred times—that the names on the list, the seditious printers, the enemy agent, all of it led here, to a lamplit parlor and an old woman with ink under her fingernails and a book that proved, in hand-colored proof after proof, that two peoples had once trusted each other enough to draw each other truly.

"There is also," Véra added, almost gently, "the matter of my name. You'll find it in your ministry's records, if you look past the accusation filed against me. Advisor to the boundary commission. Decorated. Correspondent of three ambassadors who would be very interested to learn how their government treats its oldest friends." She closed the atlas. "You may take me, Inspector. I am old; prison holds few terrors. But you will have to explain to your superiors why the seizure of this book required storming the house of a woman whose testimony helped draw half the treaties your ministry depends upon. Or—you may sit down, drink the brandy I was going to offer you anyway, and consider whether some orders deserve to be carried out."

The fire ticked. Snow slid from the roof outside with a soft sigh.

Aubertin looked at Paloma then—really looked at her, for the first time, she thought, since the print shop, since the day he had questioned her father while she stood setting type with hands that refused to shake. There was no triumph in his face. That was the strangest thing. Only a tired man's reckoning, the look of someone discovering that the case he had built, plank by careful plank, had been load-bearing for a lie.

"The girl assisted in transporting forbidden materials across a war-time frontier," he said at last, to no one in particular.

"The girl delivered a legacy entrusted to her by two dead engravers and one living one," Véra said. "History will sort out which of those descriptions matters. Ministries rarely guess correctly."

For a long moment Aubertin said nothing. Then he reached into his coat, and Paloma's heart clenched—but what he drew out was not irons. It was paper. A warrant, she saw, folded twice, and beside it a second sheet, covered in his own cramped handwriting.

"I am required to file a report within the week," he said. "What it contains is... a matter of judgment." He laid the warrant on the table, unfolded it fully so they could all see it, and then—deliberately, watching them watch him—took a match from the fire's edge and touched it to the corner. The paper caught, flared, and he dropped it into the grate, where it curled and went to ash among the coals.

"The report," he said, standing, "will note that the party known as Véra Lindqvist was found to be in possession of historical documents of no current strategic value, duly surrendered and inventoried. It will note that the Delacroix girl was located deceased of exposure in the mountains, identification uncertain." His jaw tightened. "I have filed such reports before. They are regrettable but not unprecedented. What I will not do—" and here his voice roughened, and Paloma understood that she was seeing the whole architecture of the man at last, the careerist and the conscience at war in the same tired frame— "is deliver a cartographer and a child to the same ministry that erased her mother's country from the maps. Someone has to remain who remembers it was ever there."

He put on his hat. At the door he paused, his back to them.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your mother's line-work was better than your father's. Finer coastlines." And then he was gone, into the blue dusk and the falling snow, and the hoofbeats faded down the frozen lane, and Paloma discovered that she had been gripping the back of a chair hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that she was laughing, or crying, or both, and that Véra's arm had come around her shoulders, solid and warm as a hearthstone.

* * *

They were three days into the strange, suspended peace that followed—the house filling slowly with light, the atlas lying open on the parlor table like a patient convalescing—when the sleigh came up the lane.

Paloma heard the bells first. Then the horses, blowing white in the cold, and a voice at the gate speaking to Véra's hired man, and a voice she knew better than any voice in the world, hoarse from weeks of roads, saying her name.

She was out the door before she had decided to move, down the path with her shawl flying open, and there was her father climbing stiffly from the sleigh, thinner than she remembered, grayer, his left hand bandaged where the press had taken skin during the riots, and he looked at her across ten feet of snow as if he could not believe the arithmetic that had brought them into the same field of vision.

"Papa."

"Paloma." His voice cracked on the second syllable. He did not run—he was too old, too worn, too proud—but he came toward her fast, boots punching through the drifts, and when they met he held her so hard she felt the whole winter of their separation in the grip, every unanswered question, every burned letter, every mile.

"I thought—" he began, and could not finish.

"I know," she said into his coat, which smelled of lead and lamp oil and home. "I know. Me too."

Over his shoulder she saw the sleigh more clearly: a hired rig, one trunk strapped behind, nothing else. No tools. No presses. Nothing of the shop.

"The shop," she said quietly, drawing back to look at him. "Papa, what happened to—"

"Gone." He said it steadily, but his eyes were wet. "Burned the third week of January. Cazal got most of the type out first—he paid his debt after all, in lead if not in money—and the neighbors saved what they could carry. But the building..." He shook his head. "There is nothing to go back to, little one. Only me. If you'll have me."

She stared at him. In all her life her father had never asked for anything; he had dispensed rules and wages and silences like a small sovereign state. Now he stood in the snow asking to be allowed to follow his own daughter, and Paloma felt the last knot inside her—tied the night she had shouted at him over the burning-pan, *the promise was made to her too*—finally, gently, come undone.

"Véra has a spare room," she said. "And a library you could get lost in until spring. And Papa—" she took his bandaged hand carefully in both of hers— "there's something you need to see. Something Mama left in the last plate. She planned all of it. Every mile. She just—she wanted it to be me who finished it."

Her father looked at her for a long moment—at this stranger who had walked out of his print shop in the autumn and come back someone else entirely, someone with her mother's sureness in her spine—and then he bowed his head over their joined hands.

"Then show me," he said. "I have spent twenty years thinking I knew what she was. I should like to learn I was wrong."

Behind them the house door stood open, gold-lit against the gathering evening, and Véra Lindqvist waited on the threshold—an old woman with ink-stained fingers and a completed atlas and, at last, nothing left to wait for except the century, which was already turning, slow and bright, toward morning.
