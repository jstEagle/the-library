## 22. Setting New Type

The new shop was a third the size of the old one, and Paloma had come to love it precisely for that.

It sat on Rue des Ciseaux now, in a lane so narrow that two carts could not pass abreast, wedged between a bookbinder who smelled perpetually of glue and a widow who sold roasted chestnuts from November until the first warm days of April. The front window held room for exactly one press—a secondhand Albion with a cracked platen that Father had coaxed back to true—and a single case of type set into the wall like a reliquary. There was no cellar to speak of. There was no drawer that locked. After everything, Father had insisted on that: no locks anywhere in the shop. "A place with nothing hidden," he'd said, running his thumb along the empty keyhole he'd ordered filled with wax and sanded smooth, "has nothing to fear."

She had not argued. She understood it was not a philosophy but a penance.

The winter after the crossing had been long and white and strange, the city scrubbed of its barricades but full of new names on every official door—new prefects, new inspectors, men whose faces she did not know and who did not know hers, which felt like a kind of grace. The inspector Aubert had come once, in early spring, stood in the doorway of the small shop with his hat in his hands like a man visiting a grave, and said nothing at all for a full minute before leaving a card on the counter and going. The card bore no message. It bore only his name, and beneath it, in small type: *With respect.* Father had used the card as a setting gauge for months before Paloma quietly slipped it into the atlas's portfolio, where it lay now against the completed work like a pressed leaf—evidence that even fear could be outlived by patience.

* * *

On this particular morning—the first of March, with thaw water running bright in the gutter and the chestnut seller already calling her wares—Paloma sat at the graver's bench by the window, cutting her first original letterform.

It was a lowercase g.

She had chosen it deliberately, though she could not have explained the choice to anyone who asked, least of all to herself. The g was the most difficult letter in any alphabet to cut well: the double bowl, the ear, the link, the descender that had to fall just so or the whole form toppled into ugliness. Every apprentice feared it. Every master judged himself by it. Her father had cut his first g at nineteen, under his own master's eye, and kept the flawed proof framed above the stone sink for forty years as a lesson in humility—or so he claimed; Paloma suspected it was a lesson in tenderness, a way of keeping his young self near.

Her own g had taken eleven days. Eleven evenings after the last customer left and the press went quiet, she had sat with the steel blank clamped in the holder, the lamp trimmed low, and cut away everything that was not the letter. That was the whole art, her mother had written once, in a marginal note Paloma had read perhaps four hundred times since the snowbound house in the mountains: *Engraving is only subtraction. You do not make the letter. You remove what obscures it.*

She turned the punch now in the light, holding it up against the window where the morning came through gray and clean.

The bowl was true. The ear sat at the correct angle—not proud, not cowering. The descender fell in a curve that reminded her, absurdly and unavoidably, of the sweep of a river on an old hand-colored map, one of the rivers that no longer appeared on any current chart, flowing through a country that existed now only in steel and ink and memory. She had not planned that. But then, she was learning, very little of what mattered was planned.

"Let me see it."

Father stood at the press, sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow as always. He crossed the shop in three steps—it took only three steps now—and took the punch from her with both hands, the way one receives something breakable, or holy.

He said nothing for a long time. He tilted it toward the window. He tilted it away. He ran the ball of his thumb along the counters, feeling the cuts the way a blind man reads.

"It's better than mine," he said at last.

"It isn't."

"It is." He handed it back, and his eyes were bright in a way she had learned not to remark upon, because remarking made him retreat behind the press like a crab behind a stone. "Mine had a coward's ear. I angled it down because I was afraid of the master's criticism. Yours stands up." He touched his own knuckles briefly to the frame of the window, an old habit, a blessing without theology. "Yours stands up."

* * *

The matrix went to the founder that afternoon, and the sorts came back on Thursday, wrapped in wool shavings, still smelling faintly of hot metal: a full set of a letter that had not existed in the world before this month, in a face no foundry owned, drawn by no hand but hers.

She set them immediately, because there was a page waiting.

* * *

The atlas had gone east in January, carried by a diplomatic courier under seal this time—legitimate, documented, unimpeachable, the whole machinery of the state that had spent a season trying to destroy it now employed in delivering it safely, which Véra Lindqvist had arranged with a single letter and what Paloma imagined was a look. Before it went, they had done the thing Father had resisted through all the autumn arguments and all the winter silences: they had printed the dedication.

Not reproduced—printed. Set fresh, in Delacroix type, on paper from the mill at Essonne, run through the old Albion with the cracked platen, and tipped into the atlas's binding beside the original pages. A printed copy of the dedication would remain in the shop forever, in the case by the window, under glass. Proof. Witness. The thing itself, made ordinary and public, which was the only protection anything had ever truly possessed.

Father had wanted the dedication to carry his name alone. Paloma had let him argue for three weeks, patiently, the way one lets weather happen, and then had simply set the page differently one night while he slept, and shown him the proof at breakfast, and watched him go quiet, and watched him nod.

She set the type again now—for the shop's own copy of the record, the one that would stay—with the same words, in her own new letterforms wherever a g appeared:

*This atlas was engraved in peacetime by three friends who believed borders were drawings, and drawings could be remade. It was promised across thirty years and delivered across a war. It is dedicated to Marguerite Delacroix, engraver; to Étienne Delacroix, keeper of promises; and to the daughter of Véra Lindqvist, who never knew her godmother sent her a country in a crate of letters.*

Below that, the signature line. Two names.

*Paloma Delacroix.*
*Paloma Lindqvist-Delacroix.*

* * *

She had learned the second name in the snowbound house, sitting by a stove that ticked and breathed while outside the wind rearranged the mountain passes according to designs older than any government. Véra Lindqvist had held the final page under her reading glass for a long time—so long that Paloma had begun to count the stove's ticks to keep from weeping—and then had looked up, not at the page but at Paloma's hands, resting open in her lap.

"Your mother held her graver exactly so," Véra had said. "Thumb over the ferrule, little finger anchored. I told her once she gripped it like someone holding a door shut against the wind." A pause, the fire settling. "And your father holds it like someone prying a door open. You have both grips in you. I wondered whose hands you had." Then, softly, with the terrible gentleness of the very old: "Come here, child. Your godmother has waited a long time to see your face, and it seems I have been looking at your mother's instead."

Godmother. The word had landed in her chest like a struck bell and gone on ringing for weeks. In the margin notes, in her mother's hand, beside a map of a coastline erased from every living chart: *For P., when she is grown, so that she may know she was named twice.* Paloma had read it a hundred times before understanding. Named twice. Once for the saint's day, once for the promise. Paloma, after her mother's closest friend. Her godmother's own name, given in secret, sewn into a page, smuggled across a border in a coat lining while soldiers burned crates on a ferry dock.

She had lost the shop that winter—the big shop, the wide-fronted one on the Rue Saint-Jacques with the cellar and the locked drawer and the forty years of accumulated weight. Confiscated, formally, on charges that dissolved within the month but whose paperwork moved more slowly than justice ever does; returned eventually to the family of the printer from whom Father had leased it decades ago, and let to a young man who set posters for the new government and paid his rent on time. Father had visited it once, stood across the street in the rain, and come home without going in. "Shops are shells," he'd said, dripping on the mat. "I mistook the shell for the animal." And he had rolled up his sleeves the next morning and begun rebuilding in the narrow lane off the Rue des Ciseaux, smaller and lighter and unafraid, and somewhere in the laying of the first stone of the new composing stone he had become, visibly, a younger man.

* * *

The chestnut seller leaned in the doorway around noon, as she did every day, ostensibly to warm herself and actually to read over Paloma's shoulder, having appointed herself unofficial auditor of everything printed in the lane.

"What's that you're setting now? More maps?"

"A dedication," Paloma said. "For a book that's already been delivered."

"That's backwards, isn't it? Dedication comes first."

"Not this one." Paloma checked the line with the composing stick, tilting it to catch the light. "This one had to travel first. It needed to know where it was going before it knew what to say."

Madame Fournier—her name was Fournier, and she had been Fournier through three governments and regarded all of them as temporary tenants of her street—squinted at the type. "Those g's," she said. "They're new. They weren't new last month."

"No."

"Whose are they?"

"Mine."

The old woman considered this with the gravity she brought to matters of bread prices and regime change. Then she nodded once, decisively, the nod of a magistrate, and said: "About time somebody in this lane signed their work," and went back to her brazier, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

* * *

In the afternoon a letter came.

It came by ordinary post, which was itself remarkable—everything of consequence in Paloma's life lately had arrived by courier, by smuggler, by archivist in a hood—but this was a plain envelope, franked in the mountains, addressed in a hand that shook slightly at the ascenders but held firm everywhere it mattered.

*To Mademoiselle Paloma Delacroix (and Lindqvist), printer, Rue des Ciseaux.*

Inside, a single sheet, and enclosed with it a proof—an actual printed proof, pulled on some press in a house above the snowline, slightly crooked, gloriously crooked, the impression of hands that cared more for truth than for register.

Véra wrote: *The atlas is bound. The binder wept, which he will deny. I have placed it in the library where the guildhall keeps such things, in a case with glass on all sides, unlocked, per my instructions and against the librarian's protests. Anyone may look. That was always the point, though I confess it took me sixty years and your mother's death and a war and one stubborn girl to understand it fully.*

*Your mother drew a country that no longer exists. I have begun to suspect, in my old age, that she drew it so that it would exist somewhere, permanently, past the reach of anyone with an army and an eraser. Maps are promises too. She taught me that in a summer when none of us knew what was coming, and I have spent my life forgetting it and remembering it, and I am grateful beyond the power of this pen—which shakes, you will notice, but does not fail—that your generation appears to have learned it faster.*

*Come in summer. The passes are clear in July. Bring your father. Bring your new typeface; I want to see the letter that stands up.*

*Your godmother,
V.*

P.S. — *The binder says the tipped-in dedication is the finest piece of printing he has seen in forty years. He is lying about the forty years—he is seventy—but not about the printing.*

Paloma read it twice. Then she carried it to the window, to the light, and read the postscript aloud to Father, who laughed—one short bark, startled out of him, the laugh of a man relearning the motion—and went back to his press shaking his head.

But it was the line about the unlocked case that stayed with her through the afternoon and into the evening, through the pulling of proofs and the cleaning of rollers and the closing shutters, through the simple supper of bread and cheese and the last of the winter apples, and it was still with her when the shop was dark and she sat alone at the graver's bench with the lamp lit, turning the steel punch of her g in her fingers.

Unlocked. Glass on all sides. Anyone may look.

That was always the point.

* * *

Near midnight, she did a thing she had done perhaps a dozen times and never tired of: she opened the flat portfolio that lived on the shelf beneath the type case, and she looked at the record copy—the printed dedication, the card from Inspector Aubert pressed flat behind it like a fossil of fear, and beneath those, in a sleeve of acid-free tissue, the final page.

Not the atlas's final page—that had gone east, and was now behind glass in a mountain library, whole and complete and home. This was the proof. The printer's proof of the hidden dedication, pulled that last desperate autumn night in the old shop, in the hours after Cazal's betrayal surfaced and before the archivist Gaspard had come pounding on the shutters with his lantern and his warning, the hours when she had sat by candlelight deciphering her mother's cipher in the map's hatched border and felt her entire life turn on its axis like a press wheel.

Marguerite's cipher had been elegant and simple, as all the best work was: coordinates disguised as decoration, letters hidden in the hachures of a mountain range, spelling out, in her dead mother's careful invisible hand—

*If you are reading this, you have done what we promised. The atlas was always meant to find you, P. We made it for three reasons: because the world should remember what it destroys, because Étienne owed a debt he could not say aloud, and because a daughter should inherit something larger than a shop. Your godmother knows your name. Go and let her use it.*

And beneath, in smaller characters, almost shy: *You were never meant to keep your head down. Forgive us for teaching you to. Some doors only open for people who knock.*

Paloma touched the edge of the proof, very lightly, the way she touched finished type—thumb over the ferrule, little finger anchored, her mother's grip, holding the door not shut now but open.

Outside, the thaw water ran in the gutter, and Madame Fournier's brazier had gone cold for the season, and somewhere east of the passes a library case stood unlocked with the whole of a vanished country shining behind its glass, free to anyone who cared to look.

In the morning she would pull the final proof of the dedication, sign it with both names, and put it up under glass by the window where the light could get at it. In July they would cross the mountains together, she and her father, and an old woman would meet them at a station platform and grip their hands and call them family in front of strangers, loudly, the way one does a thing that can no longer be taken away.

But tonight there was only the lamp, and the bench, and the steel in her hands.

Paloma set her new type one more time—a single line, a test line, the line every cutter sets first from their first letter, the oldest sentence in the trade—and inked it, and pulled the proof, and held it up to the dark window where her reflection looked back at her, doubled now, overlaid with the ghost of a woman at a bench thirty years gone, gripping her graver against the wind.

The proof said, crisp and clean and standing up, in a letter that had not existed in the world until this month:

*Set by Paloma Lindqvist-Delacroix, engraver.*

Both names. One line. The first of many.

She hung the proof on the nail by the door—where her father's flawed g had hung in the old shop, where it would have hung still if walls kept what we give them—and banked the lamp, and stepped out into the cold bright night, locking nothing, because there was nothing left to hide, and the whole street smelled of spring coming on, and somewhere far to the east, behind unlocked glass, her mother's rivers went on flowing through a country made entirely of ink, permanent now, past the reach of armies, waiting for whoever came next to read it.
