## 1. The Ledger of True Names

The dressing station at the edge of the Ypres salient had been a school once, before the war decided otherwise. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the tall windows fitted now with sandbags instead of light, the coat hooks along the corridor where children's satchels had hung and where gas masks dangled in their rows like a crop of strange fruit. Sister Blackwood kept her supplies in what had been the headmaster's office. The desk was still there, scarred by shrapnel along one edge, and beneath it, locked, was a ledger that did not belong to the Army.

Silas Blackwood wrote in that ledger every night between eleven and midnight, when the last stretcher had come through and the first of the morning's wounded were still hours away. She told herself it was clerical habit — she was, after all, the only sister who could keep the station's records straight, who knew where every roll of gauze went and which orderlies owed which debts — and there was truth enough in that to satisfy anyone who might have asked. No one asked. In two years, no one had ever asked.

She unlocked the drawer with the small key she wore on a bootlace under her shirt, next to the locket that held nothing anymore but a curl of hair from a girl named Margaret who had died at Passchendaele believing Silas was exactly what the papers said.

*Silas A. Blackwood, Nursing Sister, Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service.*

The name had cost her everything. It had also saved her life, or at least given her a life worth saving, which amounted to the same arithmetic most days.

Tonight the station smelled of carbolic and mud and the particular sweet-rot smell that crept in from the dressing room when the wind came wrong off the salient, and Silas sat at the dead man's desk with a candle burning low and opened the ledger.

It was not an official document. That was the first thing to understand about it. The second thing was that it was the only honest record in Flanders.

---

She had begun it in the spring of 1915, at a clearing station near Poperinghe, on a night when a boy from Lancashire had wept into her hands and refused, even half-conscious, even bleeding out, to say his own Christian name aloud.

"Can't," he'd said. "Me mum calls me that. If I say it here—" He hadn't finished. He didn't have to. Every man in those beds understood the grammar of it: a true name was a thread back home, and if you let go of it in a place like this you might never find your way down it again. So they gave each other nicknames, Dinger and Smudger and Tosh, and they answered to their numbers, and somewhere in England mothers waited for sons whose real names the war had quietly swallowed.

Silas had understood better than any of them. She lived inside a false name the way the men lived inside their nicknames — except hers had to hold against paymasters and matrons and medical boards, against the daily intimacy of shared quarters and shared baths, against the whole machinery of a country that would have called her mad at best and criminal at worst for wanting to do this work as herself. *Sister Blackwood* was armor. She knew precisely how much a name could weigh.

So she had taken a spare ledger book, army issue, unmarked, and she had begun to write the names the men would not say.

Not their service numbers. Not their ranks. Their *true* names — the ones their mothers used, the ones whispered into pillows in the dark wards, the ones men gave up only when fever cracked them open or when morphine loosened the bolts on all the doors inside them. She collected them like contraband. And when a man died, she made certain the name went home: written into a letter to the mother, worked into the official correspondence where she could manage it, spoken aloud over the grave in the little cemetery behind the station with its crooked wooden crosses and its soil that never dried.

The ledger had grown fat. There were three hundred and forty-one names in it now, some with only a line — *Pvt. J. Ellery, "Ned," Cheshire Regt., d. 3 August 1917, sang hymns badly* — and some with pages. She did not write sentimentally. That was the discipline of it. Bureaucratic habit, she called it, even alone, even to herself, because tenderness needed a disguise too or it would drown you by September.

---

A knock at the doorframe. She closed her hand flat over the page without looking up, an instinct so old it had become muscle.

"Still awake, Sister?" Corporal Finch stood in the doorway with a mug of tea he had carried from the stove forty yards through the rain, which meant it arrived lukewarm and tasting of the tin, and which meant he had thought to bring it at all. Finch was twenty-two and had been at the station since June and had not yet learned to sleep through the sound of stretchers coming up the duckboards.

"I'm doing the returns," Silas said. It was her standard answer, delivered with just enough tedium to discourage conversation. Returns were the great sedative of military life; no man had ever followed a question about returns anywhere interesting.

"You'll ruin your eyes."

"My eyes are the least ruined thing about me, Corporal."

He grinned, set the tea down among the ink bottles, and lingered the way young men linger when they want something and haven't found the courage to ask for it. Silas let him stand there. Patience was another kind of nursing.

"It's Tosh," he said finally. "Tennant. He took a bit of shell tonight — arm, mostly — and he's asking for his mum, only he won't say her name either, won't say *anyone's* name, just keeps saying *her, her*, like we're supposed to know." Finch rubbed the back of his neck. "I thought — you've got a way with the books, Sister. Thought maybe you'd know what to write so the letter gets there proper."

Silas looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable, because that was how she kept her face from doing anything else.

"Leave it to me," she said. "Get some sleep."

When he was gone she turned back three pages in the ledger to where she already knew she'd find it. *L/Cpl. W. Tennant, "Tosh." Mother: Edith Tennant, née Marsh, 14 Foundry Lane, Bury.* She had taken it down in May, during the bad week, when Tennant had drifted on morphia and called her Edie and told her about the whippets. She copied the address onto fresh paper now, dated it, and began: *Dear Mrs. Tennant — Your son William asks me to tell you—*

William. There it was, the true name, laid out plain on the paper like a man washed clean. Somewhere in Bury a woman would read it and know her son was alive, and know that someone in France had bothered to learn what he was called when he was small, and that knowledge would be worth more than any medal the Crown could strike.

Silas wrote until the candle guttered. Then she blew it out, locked the ledger away, and lay down in the narrow cot behind the supply shelves, and slept the dreamless sleep of the double-lived.

---

Morning came in grey and wet and smelling of cordite from the direction of the ridge, where the guns had started again at four and had not stopped. Silas was in the ward by six, moving down the rows of beds with her sleeves rolled and her voice pitched to the flat, unhurried calm that the men trusted more than cheerfulness. Cheerfulness lied. Flatness told them the truth: *this is ordinary, we have done this a hundred times, you will live through the morning.*

Tennant was in the third bed, his arm bound and slung, his face the color of the sheets.

"Morning, Tosh," she said.

"Sister." His eyes went past her shoulder, the way they did. "Did you — Finch said you'd write her."

"I wrote her last night. Before you were awake." She held up the envelope so he could see the address. "Fourteen Foundry Lane, Bury. Shall I read you what I told her?"

He nodded. His throat was working. She read it aloud, quietly, the way one reads scripture: *Your son William is wounded but doing well. He is brave and comfortable and asks me to say he has not forgotten the whippets' names, though he would not trust himself to write them, so I shall: Nell and Captain. He speaks of you every day.*

"That's not—" Tennant's face crumpled and reassembled itself. "That's not what I said. About the whippets."

"No," Silas agreed. "You said it in May. I keep accounts." She tucked the letter into the outgoing satchel. "Anything you'd like added before it goes?"

He looked at her then, really looked, the way men did when they were deciding whether a thing was safe, and whatever he found in her face — and God knew what that was, she had spent years sanding it down to something unreadable — he decided it was safe.

"Tell her I'm coming home," he said. "Even if I don't. Tell her that part."

"I will," said Silas, and wrote it down.

---

At ten o'clock the ambulances came again, and the day became the kind of day that erased all other days: stretchers in a river through the corridor, the dressing tables running red and brown, Dr. Merrick's voice hoarse by noon and gone by three. An officer with his jaw held together by wire. Two boys from a Pals battalion who had gone out as forty and come back as nine, and who lay side by side holding hands across the gap because they were from the same street in Accrington and neither wanted to be the one who woke alone. Silas held a man's chest together with both palms for eleven minutes while Merrick dug for the bleed, and felt the heart stutter and quit under her hands like a bird deciding, and did not stop pressing for another full minute after there was nothing left to press for.

At seven that evening, in the hour when the light fails and the wounded go quiet, she walked out behind the station to the cemetery and added a cross to the row. The name she carved was the true one — the mother's name, the pillow-name — and she spoke it aloud once, into the rain, because a name that is never said out loud starts to die even faster than the body does.

Petra found her there on the way back.

---

The girl was a small figure in an oversized hospital coat, standing at the corner of the ward hut with her arms crossed against the cold, waiting — as she always waited — with the patience of something wild that has decided, provisionally, to be tame. She was eleven years old and had appeared at the station in August, pulled from the rubble of a farmhouse east of the town, and no one had ever come asking after her, and slowly, without anyone voting on it, the dressing station had grown around her the way a tree grows around a fence post.

"Supper's on," Petra said.

"So it is."

"You missed yours."

"I did not miss it. I am merely late for it, which is entirely different and much more dignified."

Petra considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Cook's got stew. Well. Cook's got something that used to be stew's grandfather."

Silas huffed — the closest thing to laughter she permitted herself on duty — and they walked back together across the duckboards, the girl a half-step ahead the way she always was, always leading, always watching. Rain ticked on the corrugated roof. From the ridge, the guns kept up their long argument, close enough now to feel in the floorboards.

Inside, the ward smelled of stew and iodine. The men had made room at the end table without being asked; there was a stool there that had appeared from nowhere months ago and belonged to no inventory, and Petra climbed onto it, and Corporal Finch dealt her into a hand of cards with the solemnity of a gambling house, and somewhere down the ward a man began, very softly, to sing.

Silas stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to, taking inventory of a different kind. Three hundred and forty-one names in a locked drawer. One girl on a stool. One name of her own that was a lie, and one family in England that had signed her death with a lawyer's pen eight years ago, and one war with no end in sight.

Later, when the ward slept, she would take out the ledger and turn to the blank pages at the back and look at them for a long time. She had begun to think, lately, that the book was not finished recording things. She had begun to feel, the way one feels weather coming, that something was on its way to her that would need writing down.

The candle burned. The guns talked. In the third bed, Tennant slept with his good hand curled shut, holding a letter he had asked to keep until morning, and inside the letter a woman in Bury was about to learn her son's true name from a stranger in France who kept accounts of such things.

Silas blew out the light and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the rain, and far away down the lines a telegraph key began to chatter — though she would not hear of it until tomorrow — carrying toward the salient a sealed envelope with a name on it she had not seen written down in eight years.
