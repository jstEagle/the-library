## 2. Petra of the Third Ward

The dressing station at the edge of the Ypres salient had three wards, and the third was the one nobody wanted. The first took the walking wounded, men who could be patched and sent back within a fortnight. The second took the surgical cases, the ones whose survival was measured in hours and whose screams were measured in decibels. But the third ward — the long ward, the men called it — took everyone else. The ones who would heal slowly or not at all. The ones who had been in the station long enough to learn its rhythms, its smells, the particular creak of the third floorboard from the door.

And it had Petra.

Silas Blackwood heard her before he saw her that October morning, the way he always did: a quick scuffing of boots on duckboards, a muffled curse, the clatter of something dropped. He looked up from his ledger long enough to see her skid around the canvas partition with an armful of bandage tins, catch them against her chest with her chin, and keep moving.

"Petra," he said. "Walk."

"I am walking," she said, not walking.

"You are falling forward with style."

She stopped anyway, grinning at him over the tins. She had a chipped front tooth and a cowlick that no amount of water would beat down, hair the color of wet sand, and eyes so dark they seemed borrowed from some older, more patient century. Two months ago those eyes had been full of dust and silence; they'd dug her out of the rubble of a farmhouse east of Verron with her mother dead beneath the same beam that had only bruised her skull. For three days she hadn't spoken at all. Then, on the fourth day, she'd walked up to Silas while he changed a man's dressing and said, quite clearly, *You do it wrong. Tante Marguerite wrapped it like this* — and demonstrated, small fingers sure as a surgeon's — and that had been the end of her silence and the beginning of everything else.

Nobody had ever formally decided she would stay. That was the thing about the third ward. Nothing there was formal. She had simply remained, the way a cat remains, and by the time anyone thought to ask questions she had become load-bearing.

"The letters today?" she asked now, setting the tins down beside his chair. She pronounced the English carefully, each word set down like a cup she didn't want to rattle.

"After rounds."

"You said after rounds yesterday."

"And I meant after yesterday's rounds. Today is a new day with new rounds." He closed the ledger and slid it into the drawer of the field desk, beneath a false bottom made from a biscuit tin lid, and locked the drawer with the little key he kept on the same chain as his dog tags. Petra watched this ritual with the flat interest she gave all his rituals. She knew about the ledger. Not what was in it — never that — but she knew it mattered, the way children know which objects in a room are sacred without being told. She never touched the drawer. She had once stood guard over the whole desk for an hour when a visiting colonel's adjutant lingered too close, arms crossed like a very small sentry, until Silas had laughed and shooed her off.

Rounds took an hour. Forty-one men in the third ward that week, forty-one sets of dressings, forty-one opportunities to lie gently about progress. Silas moved down the rows with Corporal Deen behind him carrying the tray, and Petra drifted along in their wake like a tugboat after a liner, collecting the used swabs, refilling the water pitchers, doing the hundred small jobs that kept a ward breathing and that no order had ever assigned to her because no order needed to. The men had organized themselves around her the way iron filings organize around a magnet. Private Okafor saved her the illustrated pages from his newspapers. Sergeant Bell, who had one leg and a face like a collapsed tent, had taught her piquet across the blanket-tray table, then cribbage, then a version of poker played with ration biscuits for stakes that she was winning at a rate Bell described as *suspicious*.

"He cheats," Bell announced as Silas reached his cot, jerking his thumb at Petra, who was at that moment stacking biscuits with the serene expression of a bank manager.

"She does not cheat," Petra said.

"She counts the discards."

"That is not cheating. That is *remembering*."

"It's cheating with extra steps," Bell said, but he was grinning under the wreckage of his face, and his hand — the good one — came up automatically to steady her elbow as she leaned past him for the pitcher. Silas noted it and said nothing. There were men in this ward who had not let another human being touch them in months except to cause pain. Petra had undone that without ever seeming to notice she'd done it. She touched people the way you touch a doorframe passing through — casually, constantly, as if contact were simply how bodies moved through a shared world — and somewhere along the line the ward had started believing her.

He finished the dressings. He wrote the notes. And then, because a promise was a promise even made to an eleven-year-old, he sat down on the upturned crate at the foot of Bell's cot, took out the slate and the stub of chalk he kept for exactly this purpose, and said, "Letters."

Petra appeared instantly, sliding onto the crate beside him with the biscuit winnings abandoned mid-stack, which told Silas more about the state of her heart than any confession could have. She loved the cards. But she *revered* the letters.

"Where were we?"

"*G*," she said. "*H*. I can do *H*."

"H is a ladder that fell over."

"A ladder that fell over," she repeated solemnly, and drew it — two strokes and the crossbar — with a concentration that wrinkled her whole face. The chalk squeaked. Down the row, Okafor lowered his newspaper to watch. This was the other thing about the letter lessons: they had acquired an audience. Half the ward listened in, offering corrections — *"No, miss, the tail goes below the line on a g, like it's diving"* — and Silas had long since stopped pretending to mind. It was the closest thing any of them had to a schoolroom, a church, and a comedy hour, all at once.

"What does H stand for today?" she asked. Every letter got a soldier. It had been Silas's idea in the first week, when she'd grown bored of words like *hat* and *hen* that belonged to some other, peacetime child. So H became Hobbes, who'd lost three fingers at Langemarck and could still write his own name with the remaining seven. G became Grieves. F became Farrell.

"Hobbes," Silas said. "Who sends his regards and complains about the porridge."

"Hobbes," Petra agreed, and printed the letter four times, tongue between her teeth, and then — because she always did this, and it always landed somewhere in the center of Silas's chest like a stone dropped down a well — she added, unprompted, "P. Petra." She drew the P with a flourish, loop and stem, and looked at it on the slate with an expression Silas had learned to recognize: the look of a girl confirming that she existed on paper, that she could be written down and therefore could not be erased.

Two months ago there had been nothing to write her name on. No birth certificate survived the farm. No parish record anyone could find. Her parents' papers had burned with the house. When Silas had asked her, early on, what her name was, she'd answered *Petra* and then, after a pause, added *I think*, as if the rest might have burned too. Silas had gone out that evening and found the quartermaster and gotten, through means he did not entirely explain even to himself, a blank identity card and a stamp. He'd written PETRA in block letters, left the surname line empty — *you'll fill that in yourself someday, when you find the one worth having* — and handed it over, and she had carried that card in her shirt pocket ever since, touching it the way other children touched lucky coins.

Now she held up the slate. "Good?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Better than Hobbes's handwriting," Silas said, "and he's thirty-two years old."

"I heard that," said Hobbes, three cots down, without opening his eyes.

Afternoons, if the convoys were light, the ward played cards, and Silas pretended to do paperwork while listening to Petra fleece sergeants out of their biscuits. It was during one of these afternoons that the shelling came close enough to matter — not the far-off drumroll they'd all learned to sleep through, but the sudden hard *crack-crack-crack* of guns finding a new range, closer, and the particular change in the men's breathing that meant everyone was counting the intervals without meaning to.

Petra went still over her cards. Silas watched her hands. They didn't shake. They did what they always did when the sound came close: they found the nearest solid object — Bell's wrist, in this case — and held on.

"Five-card stud," Bell said loudly, dealing another round with exaggerated ceremony. "Petra, you're blind. Everyone's blind. Nobody looks at anything until I say."

"Why?"

"Because I say. House rules. New rule, just invented."

It was nonsense, and everyone knew it was nonsense, and everyone played along, faces buried in their untouched cards, while outside the shells walked their slow way across the landscape like a drunk looking for a doorbell. Three fell. Five. Then the interval stretched, and stretched again, and the guns lost interest or found a better target, and Bell said, "Right, look at your cards," in exactly the voice he used for nothing else, and the game resumed, and Petra's hand slid off his wrist as naturally as it had arrived.

Silas dealt himself in. He lost spectacularly, on purpose, and paid his debt in biscuits with the gravity of a man settling a mortgage.

Later, when the lamps were lit and the ward had gone quiet in that particular way — not sleeping yet, but pretending to, the hour when the men who couldn't sleep stared at the ceiling and the men who could slept for them all — Petra appeared at his elbow with her blanket over her shoulders like a cape.

"I can't sleep," she announced.

"So I see."

"If I sleep I dream about the beam."

Silas set down his pen. He did not ask which beam. There was only one beam, and both of them knew it, and it lived under her eyelids the way it lived under the ruins east of Verron.

"Come here, then," he said, and moved the lamp, and got out the slate, and for twenty minutes she traced letters by lamplight — A, B, C, the whole alphabet marching — until her strokes grew slow and soft and the chalk drooped in her fingers, and then he walked her back to her cot in the supply alcove that had become hers sometime in September without anyone signing anything, and she was asleep before he finished tucking the blanket.

He stood there a moment in the dark. Around him, forty-one men breathed. In the alcove, a small girl breathed. On the chain against his chest, under his shirt, the key to the drawer lay warm from his skin, and beyond the canvas walls the guns muttered their endless arithmetic.

*This,* he thought, and could not finish the sentence even inside his own head, because finishing it would mean naming it, and naming things in wartime was asking them to be taken away. But the shape of the thought was simple enough. This — the slate dust, the stolen biscuits, a child's breath in a supply alcove — this was the only country he had ever actually belonged to, and he would defend its borders.

He went back to the desk. He unlocked the drawer. He took out the ledger, opened it to the page marked with a scrap of red thread, and by the low yellow light of the shielded lamp he read the names there — the true names, the frightened names, the names men whispered to him at two in the morning and asked him to keep. Thomas, who signed his letters home as Tommy and wept over every one. Yusuf, who had given Silas his mother's name to carry because no one alive should forget it. An entire hidden congregation, bound together by ink and secrecy.

At the bottom of the page, in his smallest, firmest hand, was written:

**PETRA. Age 11. Pulled from the rubble east of Verron, August 14th. Mother: Adèle Vermeulen (deceased). Father: unknown name, believed deceased. Ward: Third. Occupation: runner, card sharp, scholar.**

*Scholar.* He smiled at it. Then he added a line beneath, dated today, because the ledger's whole purpose was to prove that these lives had happened:

*Reads her first sentences. Cheats magnificently at cards. Is loved.*

He blotted the page, closed the book, locked the drawer — and froze, pen still in hand, as boots sounded outside the canvas and a runner from headquarters pushed through the partition with a sealed envelope in his fist, its wax stamp catching the lamplight.

"For Captain Blackwood. Priority."

Silas took the envelope. It was heavier than paper had any right to be.

Behind him, in the alcove, Petra turned over in her sleep and murmured something he couldn't make out — a word, maybe half a word, perhaps a letter of the alphabet, climbing toward the light.
