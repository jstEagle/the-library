## 3. A Letter Under Seal

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but me, because Tuesdays were the days I balanced the ledger.

I had a system, you see. Sundays I changed the dressings that needed changing most; Mondays the quartermaster came and I lied to him about our morphine stores with the easy fluency of long practice; Tuesdays I sat in the corner of the dressing station where the stove's heat pooled, and I reconciled my two accounts — the official one, kept in the regimental book with its printed columns, and the true one, kept in the ledger bound in grey canvas that lived inside my coat, against my ribs, where a man might carry a Bible or a love letter or nothing at all.

It was in that corner, then, with the ledger open on my knee and a stub of pencil behind my ear, that Corporal Finch found me.

"Post for you, Blackwood." He said it the way he said everything, as though the words had been issued to him along with his boots. "From the Division. Sealed."

Sealed post from Division was not, as a rule, good news. Sealed post from Division addressed by hand rather than type was worse. I took the envelope and turned it over. The wax was the deep red of arterial blood before it dries brown, stamped with an eagle whose wings had been pressed slightly crooked, as if the seal itself were trying to escape.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"No, sir." He hesitated. He was twenty and still blushed when he lied, which made him nearly useless at cards and endearing at everything else. "There's talk in the signals tent. Something about Verron."

"Talk," I said, "is the one thing this army never runs short of."

He grinned and went, and I sat alone with the letter while the ward breathed around me — the wet rattle of a chest wound in the third bed, the mutter of a man dreaming of his mother's kitchen, the clock over the door ticking with the patient insolence of all clocks in wartime, marking hours that no longer belonged to anyone.

I did not open it at once. That is a habit worth confessing: I have learned to sit with dread before letting it in, the way one sits with a wounded man through the worst of the bleeding, because panic kills faster than hemorrhage and is harder to staunch. Around me the light came down through the canvas roof in dusty shafts, and somewhere beyond the sandbags the guns spoke their slow, conversational thunder, three shells for every breath of quiet, like a man clearing his throat before saying something unforgivable.

I broke the seal.

*By direction of the Civil-Military Evacuation Board,* it began, in the round, careful hand of someone paid to make terrible things sound tidy, *the following civilians resident in the town of Verron (Nord) are designated Priority Category A for evacuation under General Order 114-c. Holders of this designation are entitled to reserved transport on scheduled evacuation services, priority billeting, and such protection of person and property as military resources permit.*

Then the names.

I will set them down here exactly as they appeared, because exactness is the only honesty left to me some days:

*Blackwood, Margaret — née Ashcombe.*
*Blackwood, Aldous R.*
*Blackwood, Edith (Mrs. A.R. Blackwood).*
*Blackwood, Thomas (infant).*

Four names. Four people who shared my name — my true name, the one no one in this army knew, the one I had buried in 1914 beneath a uniform and a shortened vowel and a lie told so often it had grown calluses.

My family. My mother, whom I had not seen since she stood on the step of the house in Verron and told me that whatever I thought I was, I was not her daughter, and that I should not write, and that if I ever came back I would find the door closed. My brother Aldous, who had written once, a single page, to tell me Father was dead, and had not answered my reply. Aldous's wife Edith, whom I had met twice and who had looked through me both times as though I were furniture of inferior manufacture. And a child. An infant named Thomas, born into a family that had amputated me and never once cauterized the wound.

I read the letter four times. On the fourth reading I noticed what I should have noticed first, and the noticing went through me like cold water through a torn coat.

The letter was addressed to *S. Blackwood, Acting Matron, No. 3 Casualty Clearing Station.*

Not to Sister Blackwood. Not even to the rank I wore. To *Blackwood* — and beneath the address, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, the way one adds a postscript to a threat:

*Delivered into the hands of the addressee only.*

Someone above me knew. Someone with access to the evacuation rolls and to personnel files and to whatever dusty registry in London holds the births of girls born to families like mine — someone knew that Acting Matron Silas Blackwood of the Royal Army Medical Corps had been born Margaret, and had chosen, out of the whole alphabet of possible recipients, to send this letter to me anyway. To me, with my family's name on it, like a key slipped under a door by a hand I could not see.

I put the letter down on my knee. The stove ticked. The clock ticked. The man in the third bed said his mother's name twice and slept on.

*

You will want to know what I felt, and I will tell you honestly: first, nothing. A white room behind the eyes. The body's oldest mercy, numbness before pain.

Then, in no particular order, these things arrived:

Anger, at the sender — whoever they were — for using the mail as a knife. Fear, sharp and specific, that my secret now had a second custodian besides myself, and secrets with two custodians are halfway to being public property. Grief, old grief, the kind that has been folded so many times it goes into the pocket flat, unfolding anyway now, crease by crease, until it covered my lap like a shroud.

And underneath all of it, faint but insistent, something I did not want to name and will name now because this ledger-keeping habit of mine does not stop at other men's names: relief. Relief that they were alive. Four people who had closed their door against me, alive, marked for rescue, and some traitor part of me — the part that was eight years old and had loved them without conditions — stood up inside my chest and shouted *thank God* before the rest of me could silence it.

That is the treachery of family. It keeps a garrison in your heart long after the war between you has officially ended.

*

I folded the letter along its original creases and sat with it while the afternoon went on without me. Sister Marsh called across the ward asking whether I'd signed the requisition for the new splints, and I heard myself answer yes in a voice that sounded like mine, and she went away satisfied, and none of it touched me. I was in two places at once, which is a skill this war taught me early: the body in the dressing station at the edge of the salient, the mind already walking the cobbled streets of Verron, past the fountain in the market square, up the hill where the plane trees grew, to the tall grey house with the iron gate that I had not opened in six years.

In that house there was a hall with portraits. In that hall there had been, when I was small, a portrait of me — done at seven, in a blue dress, holding a spaniel puppy I did not especially like but held obediently for eleven sittings. I wondered, sitting there among the moaning men and the smell of carbolic, whether it still hung. Whether my mother had taken it down the day she closed the door, or whether it hung there still, gathering dust, a record of a child who had never existed quite as painted.

And Petra — I thought of Petra, because I think of Petra the way a compass thinks of north, automatically, constantly, without deciding to. Petra, who had been pulled out of rubble in this same war, in a town not forty miles from Verron, who had lost her parents to a shellfall and been handed to us like a parcel no one claimed. If the Board was evacuating civilians from Verron by category, what category held the parentless? What berth, what billet, what line of the printed form had a place for a girl of eleven whose only papers were the ones I had forged for her myself?

The questions arranged themselves around the letter like barbed wire.

*

At dusk I walked the perimeter, as I do when my thoughts need marching to keep them in order. The salient at evening is a thing I could describe for a hundred pages and still fail to convey: the light going amber over the flooded ground, the poplars standing in rows like witnesses, the smell of mud and smoke and, beneath it, faintly, absurdly, the blossom of some orchard the shells had missed. The guns had settled into their night rhythm. Somewhere a lark sang, because larks are indifferent to our arrangements.

Finch fell into step beside me uninvited, which was his way.

"Beg pardon, sir, but you look like a man who's read bad news."

"I've read news," I said. "Whether it's bad depends on arithmetic not yet performed."

He considered this. "My mum's in Dover," he offered, after a while. "If there was a letter about Dover, I'd want to know it straight, even if it was bad. Especially then."

"You're a philosopher, Finch."

"No, sir. Just a son." He kicked a stone off the duckboard path. "The signals chaps were saying Verron's to be cleared. Whole sector. They're moving the civilians west before the front comes to them."

West. Away from the guns, toward the coast, toward ships and safety and the ordinary world where letters mean birthdays and not verdicts. I looked north, toward the darkening line where Verron lay, and felt the town pull at me like a tide.

"Thank you, Finch," I said. "Get some sleep."

He saluted and went, and I stood alone at the edge of the duckboards with the letter against my ribs now, next to the ledger, two documents pressing against each other through the wool of my coat like two strangers forced to share a bench.

Here is what I knew, laid out in columns as clean as any in my books:

They were alive. They were marked for rescue. And I was the only soul in this army who carried both their name and the power — small, bureaucratic, real — to influence how that rescue was conducted.

Here is what I did not know, and every hour it itched worse: *who sent the letter, and why to me.* Because orders of that kind go to liaison officers and town majors and the civil affairs section in Hazebrouck. They do not go, hand-delivered, sealed in wax, to acting matrons at casualty clearing stations — unless the sender wanted the recipient to feel precisely what I was feeling. Unless the letter was not only information but a test. Or a summons. Or a hook.

I am not a paranoid woman by nature. But I have survived six years in a man's war by assuming that when the impossible arrives addressed in my true name, someone means me to open the door.

*

I went back inside. The ward had gone quiet in the way wards do after lights-out — not silent, never silent, but hushed, the men's breathing and shifting and dreaming rising together like the sound of a sea that dreams of being calm. I checked the third bed first, out of habit; the chest wound was holding. I checked the morphine chart. I checked, last of all, the cot in the supply alcove where Petra sleeps, though check is a generous word for looking at a sleeping child and feeling the strange loosening in the chest that mothers must feel and that I, legally a man, have no honest word for.

She was asleep with the ledger's spare pencil clutched in her fist — she steals it nightly, believing I don't notice, practicing her letters in the margins of old bandage boxes by daylight. Her hair had come loose from its braid. There was graphite on her cheek. She had, sometime in the evening, dragged her blanket into a shape that suggested, if one squinted, a train, and she lay in the middle of it with her arms spread like a conductor taking the weight of an invisible lever.

Tomorrow, I decided, we would do the letter P. She had asked for it specifically — *"P for Petra, and then I'll be able to write my own name on things"* — and I had been putting it off, telling myself there was no urgency, that the war would wait, that childhood could be administered in weekly doses.

But the letter in my coat had taught me otherwise tonight. Names arrive on their own schedule. Papers get written in haste. And a girl who cannot yet sign herself may one day stand in a rail yard, in a crowd, in a wind, in front of a man with a list and a stamp, and need those four letters the way a drowning swimmer needs a plank.

I took the pencil gently from her fist. She frowned in her sleep and then let it go, the way children surrender things, absolutely and without grudge.

Then I sat down at the little table by the stove, took out the grey canvas ledger, and turned past the pages of soldiers' true names — past Whitfield who signs himself Wren, past Okafor who signs himself Cole, past all the frightened aliases of men who write home under borrowed names for reasons of their own — to the blank leaf at the back, the one I had always half-known I was saving.

And I wrote, in my smallest hand, the four names from the sealed letter. *Margaret. Aldous. Edith. Thomas.* My family, entered at last in the only book I trust, among the only people I trust, in the only inventory that has never lied to me.

Below them, after a moment, I added a fifth line, though it was not in the letter and needed no adding:

*Petra.*

No surname. She has not chosen one yet. When she does, I suspect she will not ask permission, and I suspect I will not offer any.

I closed the book. The stove had burned low. Through the canvas walls the guns kept their appointment with the darkness, steady as tax collectors, and forty miles north a town full of strangers and four people who used to be my family were sleeping under the same sky, unaware that somewhere a clerk with a crooked eagle stamp had already decided which of them mattered enough to save.

In the morning, I resolved, I would request leave. I would invent a reason — a supplies inspection, a convalescent transfer, some fiction plausible enough to survive a signature. I would go to Verron, or I would send word, or I would simply stand outside the iron gate and see whether the portrait still hung in the hall.

I did not yet know that the decision had already been taken out of my hands. I did not yet know that before the week was out, the question of the Blackwoods of Verron would arrive at my door wearing a major's insignia and carrying its own sealed orders.

I only knew that the letter lay on the table where I had left it, its broken seal gaping like a small red mouth, saying the one thing I had spent six years refusing to hear:

*Someone knows who you are. And they are not finished with you yet.*
