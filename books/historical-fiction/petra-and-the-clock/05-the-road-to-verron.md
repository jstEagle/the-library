## 5. The Road to Verron

Major Halloway signed the leave chit without looking up from his desk.

"Three days," he said. "Family illness. I've written it as such — your *aunt*, I believe, is gravely unwell." The word *aunt* was a gift and a test both; Halloway knew there was no aunt, no family worth the ink, and he knew that Silas knew he knew. That was the architecture of their arrangement: layers of courteous fiction, each one load-bearing.

Silas took the chit. "Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me. Thank the fact that you're the only nurse in this sector who hasn't wept on my uniform." He did look up then, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Verron is forty kilometres behind what used to be our lines and twenty ahead of where theirs will be by Friday if the weather holds. You understand what 'family illness' means when the whole town is marked for evacuation?"

"I understand it means I'm going anyway."

"I know you are." He tapped the chit once with two fingers, as if stamping it invisible. "Go. And Blackwood—" He almost said something else. Silas watched him decide against it, watched the major's mouth close around whatever mercy or warning he'd been carrying. "Mind the roads. They shell them at dusk for sport."

The dressing station was quiet in the way it was never truly quiet — the hush of men sleeping through pain, the tick of the station clock that someone had salvaged from Verron's ruined church and hung above the ward as a joke nobody laughed at anymore. Silas packed by lamplight: dressings, morphine tablets, the little tin of instruments she'd carried since Arras, and the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat where it rode against her ribs like a second heart.

She left before dawn.

* * *

The road out of the salient ran between poplars that had been stripped bare by shellfire until they looked like the masts of drowned ships. Silas walked the first ten kilometres because the lorry she'd hitched onto was full of wounded going the other way, and she would not take a seat that might carry a stretcher case later. She walked with her collar up and her cap low and her hands in her pockets, and she let the road do what roads do, which is make everything behind you smaller.

By midmorning she'd caught a ride on an ammunition cart driven by a French farmer's son who whistled opera badly and asked no questions, which made him the finest conversationalist Silas had met all year. By noon she was in Poperinghe drinking chicory coffee that tasted like burnt rope, and by two o'clock she was on a requisitioned ambulance bound south, wedged between crates of bandages with her knees against her chest, watching Flanders go by in shades of brown she hadn't known mud could be.

It was somewhere outside Abeele that the thought found her — the thought she'd been outrunning since the letter arrived under seal.

*Aunt.* Halloway had written *aunt*. Not mother. Not sister. As though the family were already half-erased, as though the name Blackwood had been quietly struck from the ledgers that mattered, the way Aldous — no. She would not think about Aldous yet. There would be time enough in Verron for thinking about Aldous, time enough for the whole cold inventory of what her brother had done, but not here, not on a road still technically friendly, with the sun making a weak yellow effort through the smoke haze.

What she thought about instead was the letter itself. The paper had been good paper — heavy stock, watermarked — and the list of names for priority evacuation had been typed in a clerk's careful hand. *Blackwood, E. — Verron, rue de l'Église.* Her mother's initial. Her mother's street. Someone high enough to touch evacuation lists had known to look for that name, and known to send it to a dressing-station nurse who officially did not exist as Elizabeth Blackwood at all.

Someone wanted her to see it. That was the part that kept turning over like a stone in her boot. Letters don't find their way to the wrong hands by accident in an army. Somewhere in the chain, a hand had placed it deliberately, and Silas could not decide whether that hand meant rescue or ruin.

The ambulance jolted over a culvert. A crate of bandages shifted against her shoulder. She closed her eyes and saw the rue de l'Église as it had been fifteen years ago: plane trees, a fountain with a lion's head, her father's house with its blue shutters and its portraits of ancestors who had never once been told about her.

She had sworn she'd never go back. The war had taught her that oaths were cheap currency; everyone in the trenches swore never to die, never to forget, never to come back to places like Verron, and broke every oath within the week.

The ambulance turned south.

* * *

She discovered the stowaway at the crossroads outside Watou, when she climbed down to stretch her legs and heard, distinctly, a sneeze from beneath the tarpaulin among the crates.

Silas stood very still. Then she reached under the canvas, closed her hand around a small bony wrist, and pulled.

Petra emerged blinking into the grey afternoon light, hair escaping its plait, one bootlace trailing, wearing a coat three sizes too large that Silas recognized instantly as having gone missing from the ward stores on Tuesday. The girl had the decency to look guilty for approximately one and a half seconds. Then her chin came up in that way that made orderlies twice her size step aside.

"You're going to Verron," Petra said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation of fact.

"I'm going to Verron," Silas agreed, "and you are going back on the next—"

"There isn't a next anything." Petra planted her feet. "I checked. The convoy went east yesterday. The supply wagons only run north till Thursday. If I get off here, I walk back alone, and Sister Agnes will skin me, and then you'll feel terrible, so really it's kinder to everyone if I stay."

Silas stared at her. Eleven years old, and she argued logistics like a quartermaster. Two months ago she'd been dug out of a cellar in Verron's east quarter with her parents dead above her, and now she stood at a Belgian crossroads negotiating her own insubordination with the calm of a woman discussing the price of eggs.

"How long have you been planning this?" Silas asked.

"Since you started packing last night. You packed the small tin. You only pack the small tin when you're going somewhere the big tin can't follow." Petra said it simply, without pride, the way she reported card scores. "You think nobody watches you. Everybody watches you. It's how you taught me letters — you watch everything and write it down."

Something moved in Silas's chest that she decided firmly was irritation and nothing else.

"You cannot come," she said. "There are orders. Civilians are being pulled out of that region, not pushed in. If a shell lands near you I cannot—"

"You can." Petra's voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; she never rose, she only got quieter and more precise, like water finding the crack. "You patched Corporal Ellis while he was screaming. You held Lieutenant Marsh's hand off at the wrist when it needed holding. You can do anything. You just don't want to do this thing, so you're pretending it's impossible."

A lorry ground past, its driver shouting something cheerful and filthy. The ambulance driver leaned out of his cab and called that they were losing light, and did the madame want to stand in the road all day arguing with a child?

"Yes," Silas said. "Apparently I do."

But even as she said it, she was looking down the road south — the road to Verron, empty in both directions, shelled at dusk for sport — and doing the arithmetic she hated herself for doing. Because the truth was that a child alone on these roads after dark was not a hypothetical danger. The truth was that the next convoy back was Thursday, and Thursday was a country and a lifetime away, and the truth was that she could not, in any world she was willing to live in, put Petra back under that tarpaulin and point her north.

"You'll obey me absolutely," Silas said. "You'll do what I say when I say it. If I tell you to run, you run and you don't look back and you don't argue, not even once. Do you understand?"

Petra's face did something complicated — triumph trying very hard to look solemn. "Yes, Nurse Blackwood."

"And you'll wear your cap. And you'll keep your hands in your pockets where soldiers can't see whether they shake." Silas boosted her up into the ambulance bed among the crates, more roughly than she needed to, because gentleness right now felt like a door she couldn't afford to open. "And when this is done, Sister Agnes skins us both."

"That's fair," Petra agreed cheerfully, and burrowed into the tarpaulin like a fox going to earth, and was asleep inside four minutes, which no human being should be able to manage in an ammunition truck, and which proved — Silas thought, climbing back into the bouncing dark beside her — that children trust the wrong things entirely, or possibly the right ones.

* * *

They crossed into the evacuated zone at dusk, past a military policeman who studied Silas's forged leave chit for a long moment and waved them through with the weary indifference of a man who had stopped believing paperwork described reality. The villages thinned. Then they thinned again. Windows became black squares. A church tower leaned at the angle of a man listening hard.

The ambulance could go no further than a shattered hamlet called Sainte-Marie, where the driver set them down with a shrug and a cigarette and the information that the road beyond was "not good for the engine, madame, nor, if I may say, for anyone." So they walked the last eight kilometres, Silas carrying the kit bag and Petra carrying nothing but the enormous seriousness of a child on an adventure, skipping over shell holes the way other children skipped over puddles.

"Tell me about them," Petra said, as the stars came out indifferent and sharp. "Your family. You never say."

Silas walked four paces before answering. "My father is dead. My mother keeps a house on the rue de l'Église. My brother—" The words snagged. "My brother is complicated."

"Aldous," Petra said. "You said it once in your sleep, in March. You sounded angry."

"I sound angry in my sleep about most things."

"Not most things." Petra kicked a stone into a crater. "One thing."

The road bent. Ahead of them, faint against the last grey of the sky, lay the shape of a town — roofs, a leaning tower, the suggestion of plane trees along what must once have been the boulevard. Verron. Fifteen years away and eight kilometres distant, and both distances true at once.

"Petra." Silas stopped walking. In the half-light the girl's face was pale and watchful, and suddenly Silas saw what she should have seen at the crossroads: that this was not adventure for her. This was homecoming. Every kilometre south had been carrying the child back toward the street where her parents died, and she had stowed away not for mischief but because something in her needed to stand in front of that house again, needed it the way a wound needs air.

"Why did you really come?" Silas asked quietly.

Petra looked at the town for a long time. When she answered, her voice had none of its usual quartermaster crispness. "I want to find my mother's ring. It fell behind the stove when the house—" She stopped. Started again. "When the house stopped. I want to find it before they knock everything flat. And I wanted—" Her chin trembled once and was mastered. "I didn't want you to walk into that town alone. Nobody should walk into a town alone. I know how it is when you do."

Silas crouched down in the middle of the ruined road, kit bag sliding off her shoulder, and took the girl by both arms. Up close she could smell woodsmoke and carbolic and the particular dust of hospital blankets that clung to Petra like a second skin. Two months ago this child had been pulled out of rubble that Silas's own brother had, by every account Halloway gave her, helped to arrange. Two months ago Silas had written her into the ledger — *Petra, eleven, of Verron; speaks French and Flemish and lies beautifully; afraid of thunder, not of guns* — because some names deserved better than the army's shorthand.

"We go in together," Silas said. "We find the ring. We get out before the shells start falling for sport, and we never speak of the small tin or the missing coat to Sister Agnes as long as either of us lives. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Petra whispered.

They walked the final kilometre side by side, and the town grew out of the dusk — shuttered windows, a barricade of furniture across the boulevard, a cat moving along a wall with the proprietary air of the last official resident. Somewhere on the rue de l'Église a lamp was burning behind blue shutters, the same shutters, Silas thought, that had framed her childhood like a portrait she'd been cropped out of.

Her mother was behind one of those windows. The woman who had received her last letter fifteen years ago and returned it unopened. And somewhere in that town, or soon arriving at it, was Aldous — complicated, angry-in-her-sleep Aldous — whose name sat on the evacuation list directly below their mother's, as though the war had filed them together again, a family, complete.

Petra slipped her cold little hand into Silas's, entirely without asking, and they went through the barricade into Verron as the first artillery rumbled somewhere far to the north, too far to worry about, everyone always said.

Too far, Silas noted, writing it down the way she wrote everything down, to believe.
