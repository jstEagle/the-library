## 6. The House That Forgot Her

The house had not grown smaller in eleven years; it had only grown quieter.

Silas stood at the iron gate at the end of the lime-tree walk and let the sight of it settle over her like a coat that no longer fit. Blackwood House had always performed its own dignity — the pale stone, the tall windows with their black shutters folded back like hands at rest, the clock above the stable arch that her grandfather had brought from Geneva and that had never once, in her childhood, told the correct time. It still didn't. The hands read a quarter past four against the church bell striking six.

Beside her, Petra shifted the canvas satchel higher on her shoulder and looked up.

"That's it?" the girl whispered.

"That's it."

"It looks like a house in a story where somebody dies."

Silas almost laughed. Two days on a troop lorry and a cart and finally this last mile on foot through orchards gone to seed, and the child's eye for truth remained unimpaired by fatigue. "It does rather," she said. She adjusted the collar of her officer's greatcoat — Halloway's leave chit said *Sgt. E. Blackwood, convalescent transit*, which was true in every particular except the ones that mattered — and pushed the gate open. The hinge cried out the way it always had, a small treachery she'd forgotten to expect.

"You don't have to come in," she said, crouching so they were level. "There's a café by the market square. I could give you money and you could—"

"I'm coming," Petra said, in the tone of someone announcing weather. She had been coming since the lorry depot at Amiens, when Silas had found her wedged between crates of dressings with a stolen loaf and an expression of profound unconcern. There was no use revisiting the argument. Silas had tried it for two days and lost every engagement. The men of the Third Ward would have told her: you don't order Petra anywhere. You merely inform her of your plans, and she decides whether to be there when they happen.

So they walked up the drive together, past the lime trees that needed pruning, past the sundial furred green with moss, and the front door opened before either of them touched it.

Her mother stood in the frame of light.

Eleven years, and Margaret Blackwood had aged the way marble ages — not softer but finer, more deliberate, every line carved with intent. Her hair was silver now and pinned with the same jet comb. She wore grey. Of course she wore grey. She was in half-mourning or full mourning or whatever the correct register was for a husband three months dead, and she looked at the tall figure in the officer's coat on her steps as though cataloguing a delivery.

"Mother," Silas said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. Eleven years of practice held it there, in the register of a man of perhaps thirty. "It's Edward. Edward Blackwood."

A silence long enough to cross a courtyard in.

"Yes," her mother said. "I can see the resemblance." And then, stepping back, holding the door: "You'd better come in before the light goes. There is shelling east of here now, you know. Everyone says it won't reach the town, and everyone said that about everywhere else."

* * *

The hall smelled the way it had always smelled: beeswax and cold stone and, underneath, faintly, the ghost of pipe tobacco that no one had smoked in years. Silas stopped just inside the door because some part of her body remembered being fifteen and standing exactly there, waiting to be summoned into the study, and the memory arrived whole and physical, like a hand between the shoulder blades.

Petra pressed close against her side, taking everything in with quick dark glances — the staircase with its wrought-iron balustrade, the portraits, the long case clock ticking in its corner with the confidence of a thing that has never been wrong about anything, unlike its cousin over the stable arch.

"The girl will sleep in the blue room," her mother said. It was not a question. She had already assessed Petra — the cropped hair, the too-large boots, the satchel patched with parachute silk — and filed her somewhere, and was moving on. "Cook is still with us. Half of France has left and somehow Mrs. Pell remains. She'll make up beds."

"I should explain—"

"You may explain at dinner." Her mother's eyes went briefly, coolly, to the window, where the sky over the eastern rooftops had the bruised amber tint it took on lately toward evening. "We dine early now. One doesn't know how long the lamps will be permitted."

She turned toward the stairs, then paused, one hand on the newel post, and for a moment something moved behind her face — not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition, the flicker of a woman seeing a stranger wearing her family's bones.

"You've got his shoulders," she said quietly. "Your father's." Then she climbed the stairs, grey skirts whispering, and was gone, and Silas stood in the hall of her childhood feeling precisely as welcome as the draft under the door.

"She's very polite," Petra whispered.

"Yes," Silas said. "That's the worst thing about her."

* * *

They dined at seven in a room built for twenty, at a table laid for three with the leaves removed, so that the candlelight reached only partway down the linen and the rest of the table lay in shadow like a pier extending into dark water. Three places. Silas counted them twice without meaning to. Herself, her mother, and — she understood before she was told — the empty place at her mother's right hand, kept dressed out of habit or refusal, the glass turned upside down the way one turns a glass for the dead.

"Aldous isn't here," Silas said. Not quite a question.

"No." Her mother ladled soup with terrible precision. The soup was a thin barley broth that tasted of the war even here, even in this room where the wallpaper had not changed since Victoria died. "Your brother comes and goes. He has interests in Amiens he says cannot wait. He arrived Tuesday; he left Thursday morning." A pause, measured to the grain. "You have missed him by two days. You always did miss each other."

"Where is Father buried? The letter said—"

"In the churchyard at Saint-Aubin. Behind the apse, with the other English. The priest was decent about it, considering." Her mother did not look up from her plate. "He went in his sleep, if that is a comfort to you. His heart simply concluded its business. He had been asking it to carry too much for years." Now she looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely level. "He asked after you, near the end. He asked whether we'd heard from Edward."

Silas set down her spoon. Under the table, Petra's knee pressed against hers — a small steady pressure, an anchor dropped without ceremony.

"And what did you tell him?"

"That we hadn't." Her mother said it the way one reports the loss of a glove. "It seemed kindest. He had made his peace with your going, such as it was. He used to say—" and here, for the first time all evening, her voice caught, a single hairline crack across the porcelain, "he used to say that a man who goes off to save strangers can't be entirely damned, whatever else he's running from."

"He knew, then." Silas's throat was tight. "About me. About what I — what Edward is."

"He suspected things he preferred not to name. Most of what your father knew, he survived by not naming." Her mother rose, began gathering plates though the meal was hardly done, movement as anesthesia. "You'll want to see the study. His books are as he left them. I haven't the heart to sell them and no son here to take them."

*No son here.* Silas felt the sentence land and pass through her like a bayonet through wet cloth — clean entry, no resistance, all the damage internal. Her mother had not said it cruelly. That was the artistry of Margaret Blackwood: she never once in her life had to say anything cruelly. Cruelty required acknowledgment that something was at stake. What her mother dealt in was arrangement. People were arranged — seated, housed, mentioned, omitted — and the omission was the message, delivered without a fingerprint.

Petra, bless her, ate her soup with the focused efficiency of a soldier on ration, and watched everything, and said nothing, and kicked Silas gently under the table when Silas forgot her own spoon for too long.

* * *

The study was exactly as it had been, and that was the unbearable part.

The same green-shaded lamp. The same globe with the British Empire picked out in red, the red faded now to the pink of an old scar. The same leather chairs, cracked along the arms where her father had gripped them reading. His spectacles still folded on the blotter beside a letter tray holding three months of unopened circulars, as though he might come down tomorrow in his dressing gown and complain that nobody kept the accounts current.

Silas stood in the doorway a long time. Petra slipped past her, respectful of the room in the way children are sometimes more respectful of grief than of furniture, and went to stand at the window instead, looking out at the darkening garden.

On the walls hung the portraits. Her grandfather, stern above his ledgers. Her grandmother, painted young and already disappointed. Her father, done at forty, kind-eyed and helpless inside his own frame. And at the end of the row, in the place of honor —

Silas crossed to it slowly.

Aldous, aged nine, in sailor whites, one hand resting on a globe smaller than their father's, painted by some provincial artist who had flattered him only slightly. Below it hung the companion piece commissioned at the same time, and Silas had known it would be there, had walked toward it knowing, and still the sight of it operated on her like cold water.

Herself. Age eight. In the portrait she was not yet Silas; she was the daughter, the second child, the girl with her hair in the style her mother favored, holding a book she had actually loved, painted by the same provincial hand. The plaque beneath read *A. Blackwood* on the first frame and on the second, simply, *his sister.*

*His sister.* Even in oil, even at eight years old, she existed in that house only in relation. Never *her*. Never a name of her own. The ledger in her coat pocket — forty-one names, forty-two now, soldiers who had whispered their true selves to her in the dark between dressings because she was the only person alive they dared tell — the ledger suddenly weighed like ballast.

"There's no picture of you from later," Petra said. She had come to stand beside Silas without sound, the way she moved through the ward at night. "Only the little one. Where are all the others?"

"They stopped taking them," Silas said.

"Why?"

"Because I stopped being the person they were taking pictures of." She crouched, as she did in the ward when a man needed the truth at his own height. "Do you remember your parents' faces? Truly remember?"

Petra considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions. "Mama's smile. Papa's hands. The rest is — like a photograph left in the sun. Mostly white where the important part was."

"That's what this room is," Silas said, gesturing at the portraits, at the globe, at the untouched spectacles. "A photograph left in the sun. Everyone in it stayed the age they were useful at."

From the doorway, her mother's voice: "Dinner is finished, then, is it?" And Silas straightened, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

Later — the clock in the hall striking ten with the smugness of a machine that answers to no one — Silas found her mother in the drawing room mending a shirt that belonged to no one present. The fire was small. The room was large. Somewhere east, so distant it might have been thunder deciding whether to become weather, the guns spoke, a soft irregular punctuation.

"Aldous sold something," Silas said. No preamble. She was too tired for preamble, and besides, preamble was another language her mother had never taught her. "The letter I received named this house among priority evacuations. Someone in the military administration knows our name, Mother, and knows it matters. That doesn't happen by accident. Either you wrote to someone, or Aldous is entangled with someone, and either way—"

"Your brother is a clever boy," her mother said to the mending.

"Clever boys don't get towns listed for evacuation."

"Verron is being evacuated because the front is unstable. It is in every newspaper. You read newspapers; you must have learned to in the army." The needle flashed. Steady. Metronomic. "I will not sit in my drawing room while my daughter invents scandals."

*My daughter.* The phrase landed strangely — offered now, after eleven years of *no son*, as if the word had been waiting in a drawer and had been taken out tonight for utility. Silas sat down across from her, deliberately, making herself a weight in the room.

"When did you last speak with him? Truly speak. Not the Sunday telephone performance."

Her mother's needle stopped. Just for a heartbeat. Then resumed.

"He is worried about the estate," she said. "About the accounts. Your father's affairs were — complicated."

"Aldous has never worried about an account in his life unless it was his own and overdrawn."

"You forget yourself." The needle came down through the cloth with unnecessary force. "You forget that you left this house. Whatever Aldous is, whatever he has done or not done, he *stayed*. He sat at this table. He buried his father. You sent a photograph from a training depot and then eleven years of nothing, and you arrive now in a major's greatcoat with a stray child and questions, and you will forgive me if I do not leap at your conclusions." She set down the mending at last, and looked at Silas fully, and her eyes were her father's eyes, kind-eyed and helpless, wearing a different expression. "You look like him. Do you know that? Since the war you look like him. It is very difficult to look at you."

Silas stood. The floorboards knew her weight; the house pretended not to.

"Get the girl ready to travel," she said. "However long the papers say we have, we have less. If I can't get you both onto the train, I'll walk you to Amiens myself. And Mother — ask Aldous, when he telephones his Sunday performance, what he was doing in March. Ask him what he sold, and to whom, and watch his answer. You raised us both. You'll recognize the sound."

At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and pride had spent enough of itself for one lifetime.

"There was never a place set for me at that table," she said quietly, nodding back toward the dining room. "Not once, growing up. Always three places, then four, and mine was the one nearest the door — the one that could be cleared quickly if it wasn't needed. I used to think that was my fault. Something I'd failed to earn." She pulled Halloway's leave chit from her pocket and set it on the mantel, next to the photograph of her father in younger years. "I know better now. Good night, Mother."

Behind her the needles did not resume. She counted the silence down the corridor — five seconds, ten, twenty — and then, faintly, the sound of the mending being folded, and set aside, and not picked up again.

* * *

The blue room had been made up for Petra: fire lit, hot brick at the foot of the bed, a nightgown that must have belonged to some cousin dead thirty years laid out with hospital corners. Silas checked the window latch, the shutters, the angle of escape — habits that would not release her — and found Petra sitting cross-legged on the quilt, watching her with that level dark attention that had unnerved three orderlies, two matrons, and one visiting general.

"You're angry," Petra said.

"I'm tired."

"You're angry *and* tired. Those go together in you. Like tea and the cup." Petra scooted over, making room on the edge of the bed, an invitation issued with the authority of a field commander. Silas sat. The springs protested. Somewhere below, her mother moved through the drawing room turning down lamps, and the house ticked and settled around them like a ship riding at anchor in bad water.

"Was it always like this?" Petra asked. "Before the war. This house."

"Worse. The war improved it. Gave everyone something honest to be frightened of."

Petra laughed — the small private laugh she spent on almost no one — and leaned her head against Silas's arm, and within four minutes was asleep, mid-sentence, the way she did, trust operating her body like a switch. Silas sat unmoving so as not to wake her, and looked out the window past the sleeping child at the eastern sky, where the guns had gone quiet in the way that was worse than noise, the loaded pause of an orchestra before the chord.

In her coat pocket, the ledger pressed against her ribs. Forty-two names. Tomorrow, she thought, she would add none and lose none, and that would be the day's entire victory.

Then, far off down the lime-tree walk, gravel crunched under tyres — a motorcar arriving well past midnight, engine cut too fast, doors handled by someone in a hurry to be quiet.

Aldous had come home.
