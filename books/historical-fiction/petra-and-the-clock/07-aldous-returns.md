## 7. Aldous Returns

The rain had stopped by the time Aldous Blackwood reached the edge of Verron, but the town still dripped like something recently drowned. He stood in the shadow of a burned-out cartwright's shop and watched water fall from eaves in long silver threads, and he thought, with the peculiar clarity that exhaustion sometimes granted him, *I used to know this street.*

He had known it as a boy, anyway — or he had known a version of it. The version where the baker's window glowed gold at dusk and Madame Ferrand's dog slept in the middle of the road because nothing ever came fast enough to hit it. That dog was gone now. So was the bakery's front wall. A shell had taken it clean away sometime in the spring, and behind the gap you could see the ovens still standing, absurd and domestic, like altars in a ruined church.

Aldous pulled his collar up and told himself, for the eleventh time since Calais, that he was not coming home. Home was a word with no address anymore. He was coming to collect his mother and his sister-in-law's children — well, his brother-in-law's, technically, the man had died at Verdun — and put them on a train, and then he would go back to Paris and to the ministry and to the soft gray corridors where men like him made the weather without ever getting wet.

That was the plan. It had been a good plan right up until the moment he'd seen the casualty lists.

* * *

He had seen them in an office that smelled of pencil shavings and cold coffee, three days ago, a colonel named Vasseur sliding a folder across the desk with two fingers, the way one might pass along something dead.

"East quarter," Vasseur had said. "Verron. You'll want the second page."

Aldous had wanted nothing of the kind. But his hands had turned the page without consulting him, because hands are loyal to their owner only in theory.

The numbers were not large, as such numbers went. Forty-one dead in the initial barrage. Sixty-three wounded, of whom perhaps twenty would survive the week given the state of the dressing stations. He had read worse figures off worse pages. He had *authored* worse figures — that was the thing about the artillery allocation memorandum, the thing he could never say aloud in any room: it had not been a mistake, not a miscalculation by some sweating staff captain. He had sat at a table in a hotel in Boulogne with a German intermediary whose cologne smelled of violets, and he had traded coordinates, timing windows, and the location of the town's civilian shelters for a guarantee of safe passage through the blockade for the Blackwood shipping interests, plus eleven percent. Eleven percent. He remembered thinking, as he signed, that the number had a pleasing ring to it. Eleven. Like a church bell.

The shelters. That was what the second page was about. The east-quarter civilians had gone into the cellars exactly as instructed — instructed by their own town council, following guidance distributed through channels Aldous knew intimately because he had helped write the guidance. The shells had been aimed to collapse the cellar roofs. Not deliberately, not with malice; simply as a matter of ballistics. If you knew where everyone would be, you did not need to hunt them. You needed only to be patient, and accurate, and paid.

Forty-one. Sixty-three. And somewhere in the arithmetic, unnumbered, unnamed: a girl. An orphan now, though Aldous didn't know that yet. All he knew, reading the list, was that among the forty-one were the names Ferrand, boulanger, and Ferrand, épouse, and that Madame Ferrand had once slipped him a warm roll across her counter when he was nine years old and hungry in a way his mother refused to acknowledge, and that she had said, *Tiens, petit monsieur, pour la route,* and winked, and he had loved her a little, the way children love people who feed them.

He had closed the folder. He had thanked Colonel Vasseur. He had walked out into the Paris rain and been sick against a wall on the Rue de Rivoli, in full view of the Tuileries, while a gendarme looked at him with the weary contempt reserved for drunk men, which was at least better than being looked at with suspicion.

Then he had wired his mother. Then he had bought a ticket north.

* * *

The house was dark except for one window on the upper floor, where a lamp burned behind a curtain the color of old cream. Aldous stood at the gate for a long moment. The gate still stuck in the same place, third hinge from the bottom, and he lifted it slightly as he pushed, an unconscious motion his body remembered even if his conscience had misplaced everything else.

Inside, the hall smelled of beeswax and cold ash. His mother's housekeeper — what remained of the household staff, one woman and a boy too young to be conscripted — had left a lamp lit on the hall table, and beside it, folded twice, a note in his mother's upright hand:

*Your sister came. I have sent her to the hotel. We will speak when there is something to say.*

Aldous read it three times. The words rearranged themselves each time and each time meant the same thing: *She was here, and I turned her away, and I am telling you this as a fact, not an apology.*

Esmé. He hadn't thought of Esmé in years — or rather, he had thought of her constantly, the way one thinks of a debt, always in the background ledger, never in the foreground where payment might be demanded. His sister who had walked out of this house at nineteen with nothing but a suitcase and a name she'd chosen herself, or rather half-chosen; Silas was the name they gave her, the family name, kept like a stolen coat. He had let her go. Worse: he had drafted the letter that formally severed her from the household accounts, because his father's hands shook too badly by then and someone with a steady hand had to do it, and Aldous had always been the one with steady hands.

Steady hands. That was the family talent. Steady hands for ledgers, steady hands for signatures, steady hands for holding other people's fates flat on the table while the ink dried.

He climbed the stairs. The fourth step still creaked on the left side. In his mother's sitting room, the lamp behind the curtain resolved into his mother herself, seated in her chair by the cold fireplace, fully dressed at eleven at night, doing nothing at all. Just sitting. Looking at the fireless grate as though expecting it to explain itself.

"Aldous." She said his name the way she pronounced all proper nouns: as though confirming them against a list. "You look terrible."

"It's good to see you too, Mother."

"I don't recall saying it was good." She rose, kissed the air an inch from both his cheeks — the war had curdled even her formalities — and studied him with those pale eyes that had missed nothing in this house for sixty years except the things that mattered most. "You've come about the evacuation."

"The trains leave day after tomorrow. There's a berth situation. I can pull strings in Paris—"

"You can pull strings." She sat again, folding her hands. "Yes. You always could. Your father couldn't; he was honest. Esmé wouldn't; she was proud. You, Aldous, can pull strings. It is the entire content of your character, and tonight I am grateful for it."

Something in him flinched. He covered it by crossing to the sideboard and pouring himself a brandy he did not want, from a bottle that was nearly empty, because the blockade had reached even here, even into the Blackwood decanters. He drank it anyway. It tasted like pennies.

"Where is everyone?" he asked. "Cousin Marguerite? The Delacroixs?"

"Gone west, weeks ago, those with sense. Those without—" his mother made a small gesture at the window, at the whole dark listening night, "—are still telling themselves the line will hold. The Germans are forty kilometers out, Aldous. Forty. In spring they were ninety."

"The line will bend before it breaks."

"That is precisely what a man who has already sold the breakage would say." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. It simply grew thinner and colder, like wire drawn fine. "I received a letter, Aldous. Under seal. Military channel. My name on an evacuation priority list, which means someone in your world knows this family exists, which means someone in your world has been discussing this family. I assume that someone is you. I assume whatever you did to earn us a place on that train also earned this town its present circumstances. Am I wrong?"

The silence went on so long that the clock in the hall struck the quarter-hour into it, four soft bronze notes falling like coins down a well.

"You're not wrong," Aldous said finally.

His mother nodded, slowly, as if he had confirmed a dinner reservation. "I thought as much. Your father sold timber rights during the '70 war under nearly identical conditions. It's the family gift. At least he had the decency to die before the bill arrived."

"The bill hasn't arrived."

"No," she agreed. "But I heard it coming. That's why the lamp is lit."

* * *

He should have left then. He understood that later, with the terrible hindsight of a man cataloguing his own ruin: there had been a door open, a train available, a version of events in which he collected his mother at dawn and never learned anything further, never heard the names read aloud, never saw the face of the child. There is always a door. The tragedy of a life is rarely that doors don't exist; it is that we stand in rooms arguing with furniture while they close.

Instead he went downstairs, because sleep would not come and the brandy bottle called to him, and he sat in his father's old study — his study now, though the chair still fit his father better — and opened the desk drawer where the important papers lived, because old habits of secrecy demand maintenance even when the secret is already bleeding out.

And that was where the girl found him.

Not immediately. First there was an hour of him drinking and reading his own signature on documents he could barely bear to look at — the Boulogne memorandum, a carbon copy he'd kept out of what he had told himself was prudence and knew perfectly well was evidence-hoarding guilt, the hoarder's instinct to keep the thing that damns you because destroying it would mean admitting what it says. He was staring at the violet-scented intermediary's cipher initials — *H.K.* — when he heard the stair creak. Fourth step, left side.

He froze. His mother did not use the stairs after ten; she had a bell-pull and opinions about gravity. The housekeeper had gone to her sister's in the next village. Which left—

The door opened six inches and a face appeared in the gap: a child, maybe ten or eleven, thin as a winter branch, with dark hair cut short in the rough fashion of someone working with bad scissors and no mirror, and eyes that were doing a great deal more work than the rest of her face. She looked at him. She looked at the papers on the desk, with a speed and specificity that startled him. Then she looked back at his face, and something moved through her expression like weather — recognition, calculation, and beneath both, a stillness far too old for her.

"Who are you?" Aldous said. "How did you get in?"

"The kitchen window doesn't latch," she said. Her French was fluent with a local grain to it; Verron-born, certainly. "Madame Lefèvre knows I come. I do the errands. Are you the son?"

"I'm—yes. Aldous Blackwood."

Something happened in her face then. He would spend months afterward trying to reconstruct it, and never quite succeed, because what crossed it was not one thing but several, moving fast: the flicker of a name landing in a place where it already had weight; the reflexive blankness of a child who has learned that adults watch faces; and underneath, faint as a watermark, something that in an adult he would have called judgment, and in a child looked almost like grief arriving early to an appointment.

"The nurse's brother," she said.

"The—"

"Esmé. The nurse. Everyone calls her Silas. She's at the hotel." The child's voice was flat, informational, a telegraph clerk reading off a form. "She came all this way. Your mother sent her to sleep in a strange bed in a town that's going to be shelled, because of a letter."

"How do you know about a letter?"

"I carry things," she said. "For people. People tell carriers things they don't tell their families. It's how I know the kitchen window doesn't latch, too." She stepped into the room then, fully, and he saw she was holding a cloth bundle against her chest the way children hold cats, and that her sleeves were rolled up over forearms marked with the small healed scratches of someone who worked among ruins. "You're the one from Paris. The one who does the shipping."

"My God," Aldous said softly. "You're Petra."

It slipped out before he understood why he knew it. But he did know it — that was the obscene thing. Somewhere in the machinery of his guilt, in the nights since the folder, the name had surfaced: the Ferrands' granddaughter, the girl from the east quarter, listed in the supplementary files as *surviving minor, whereabouts unknown.* He had read that line. He had read it and felt relief, the cheap animal relief of one fewer death on the slate, and he had not thought about her since.

Except she was here. Standing in his father's study, in the house his commission had purchased, looking at him with those old eyes, and she said:

"You knew my grandparents."

"I—did. Yes. The bakery."

"Monsieur Ferrand let me turn the mill wheel on Sundays." Her voice stayed level. Only her hands tightened on the bundle. "They're on your list. The dead ones. I keep the list too. Mine's longer."

"Petra—"

"Don't say my name like you're allowed to."

The words dropped into the room and lay there between them, small and absolute, like a coin spun to rest. Aldous set down his glass. His steady hands were not steady. He heard himself say — and would remember, always, the sound of his own voice making the confession before any decision to confess had been made, truth leaking out of a man the way light leaks under a door —

"What do you know? Tell me what you know. Please."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, far to the east, so distant it might have been thunder deciding whether to become weather, there came a sound like a heavy door closing underground. Once. Twice. The guns, finding their range in the dark, practicing their manners for morning.

"I know the shelters failed," Petra said. "All of them. On the same night. And I know shelters don't fail all together unless somebody drew the map of where everybody would be hiding." Her chin lifted. "And I know your name was on a paper in our cellar. A shipping paper. Papa kept papers. He thought they mattered."

Aldous stood up. The chair went over backward behind him and neither of them glanced at it. The guns sounded again, closer now, or maybe just braver, and in the hallway the grandfather clock began striking midnight, unhurried, indifferent, counting down something neither of them had agreed to.

"Petra," he said. "There is something I have to tell your — I have to tell Silas. Tonight. Before anyone sleeps. Can you take me to her?"

The girl considered him the way a judge considers a plea entered too late — not unmoved, but unmoved by movement.

"She won't want to see you," Petra said. "But she'll come anyway. That's the kind of person she is. You wouldn't know, because you weren't there when they made her."

She turned and walked out, and Aldous followed her down the stairs past the fourth step that creaked, out into the wet street where the lamps of Verron were going out one district at a time, and above the rooftops to the east the sky had begun, very faintly, very patiently, to glow.
