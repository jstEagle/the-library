## 8. Petra Knows the Name

Petra had learned early that houses spoke, if you knew how to listen. The house in Verron spoke differently from the dressing station. At the station the walls talked all night — groaning stretchers, muttering men, Sister Blackwood's boots on the boards like a patient clock. This house, her brother's house, the Blackwood house, barely whispered at all. Its floors were carpeted so thickly that footsteps vanished into them, and its doors were heavy as church doors, and behind those doors the adults said things they thought no one could hear.

But Petra had lived two months among soldiers, and soldiers taught you everything worth knowing without meaning to. They taught her that men who lowered their voices were saying the important things. They taught her that if you sat very still near a wall with your hands folded, like a child at prayer, the whole building would forget you were there.

So when Aldous Blackwood came home by night, Petra did not go to bed. She went to the stairs.

* * *

She had found him first from the window, before she ever heard his voice. She'd been supposed to be asleep in the narrow room Sister Blackwood had made for her — the little blue room with the cross-stitched pillow that smelled of lavender and of years nobody had slept in it. But sleep was a country Petra visited rarely these days, and the sound of hooves on the drive had pulled her up out of the bedclothes like a fish on a line.

Below, a horse she didn't know stood steaming under the bare plane tree, and a man swung down from it with the loose, showy grace of someone who had practiced dismounting for an audience. Even in the dark she could see he was fine-made, the way the officers at the station were fine-made, all polish over tiredness. He handed the reins to nobody, patted the horse's neck twice, and looked up at the house for a long moment before going in — looked at it the way you look at something you're not sure will still be there tomorrow.

Petra knew that look. It was the look the men gave the salient.

He came into the hall below her hiding place on the stairs, and the lamplight found his face, and Petra's breath went small in her chest, because he had Sister Blackwood's face. Not exactly — Sister Blackwood's face was weathered and watchful, carved by wind and work, while this man's face was smooth and handsome and somehow unfinished, like a portrait where the painter had done the eyes last and lost heart. But the bones underneath were the same bones. The same dark brows. When he smiled — and he smiled almost at once, at nothing, at the house itself — Petra saw the ghost of Sister Blackwood's rare, crooked grin wearing a stranger's skin.

"Aldous." The mother's voice, from the drawing room door. Cold as well water. "You might have written."

"Mother." He kissed her cheek; she permitted it the way one permits rain. "And miss the pleasure of seeing your face? I wrote. You burned my letters, I expect."

"I read them."

"That's worse." His laugh was soft and easy and did not reach anywhere near his eyes, and Petra, crouched in the dark with her chin on her knees, felt her stomach turn over, because she had heard laughs like that before — from the men who played cards too fast and bet money they hadn't got, laughing loudest when their hands were worst.

They went into the drawing room. The door did not quite close. It never quite closed in this house; the frames had swollen or sunk, and every door hung a finger's width shy of true, and through that gap poured light and voice like water through a cracked dam.

Petra crept down three stairs and settled against the banister post, where the shadow was deepest, and folded her hands, and listened.

* * *

At first it was only family talk — talk about money, which meant nothing to her, and talk about Paris, and talk about a cousin's marriage, and the mother's voice staying flat as a frozen pond whatever was thrown onto it. Petra nearly dozed. Then the brother said something about *the quarter*, and the word went through her like a splinter finding its old channel.

"—the east quarter was always going to be a write-off," Aldous was saying. "You don't weep over arithmetic, Mother. The guns were coming to Verron regardless. I merely ensured that when they came, certain parties were positioned to profit from the reconstruction contracts. Somebody would have. Better it be people who actually love this town—"

"Do not say that word in this house."

"Which word? Profit?"

"*Love.*"

A silence. Petra pressed herself smaller against the post. Her heart had begun doing the thing it did during shelling — not racing but slowing, going heavy and deliberate, each beat struck separately like a hammer on an anvil.

"You want to know what happened to the east quarter?" Aldous's voice had changed now. The charm had drained out of it, leaving something rawer, and Petra found, strangely, that this was worse. Charm you could see through. This new voice sounded like the men cried out at night when they thought no one heard. "I'll tell you what happened, since Father is dead and can't be troubled and Silas — since our soldier-sibling is upstairs sleeping the sleep of the virtuous. There was a mapping office in Lille. A clerk with gambling debts. And a broker in Amiens willing to pay for a corrected survey — the true positions of the cellars, the shelters, the civilian refuges marked as munitions stores so the French wouldn't use them, and the real storehouses marked as refuges so the German gunners—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The paperwork was elegant. That's what I remember most. How elegant it was. Three documents and a stamp, and eleven hundred people became a rounding error."

Eleven hundred. The number floated up the stairwell and hung there. Petra's mouth filled with the taste of dust — the particular taste of the east quarter, of plaster and ash and the flour-mill's burning grain, the taste that had lived in her nose for days after they dug her out. Two months ago. Two months ago tonight, nearly. They'd told her it was a shell that fell wrong, a mistake of war, war being full of mistakes, and she had believed it because children believe the shape of the story adults give them, the way water believes the shape of its cup.

Her parents' shop had been on Rue de la Tannerie. The tanners' street. In the east quarter.

She did not move. She had learned that too, in the rubble — the first hours under the beam, when moving meant the whole weight of the house settling — that stillness could keep you alive. Stillness now kept something else alive, some small creature in her chest that had just been struck and was deciding whether to die or to grow teeth.

"And the girl," the mother said. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Petra had learned was the mother's version of shouting. "This child Silas brought back. From the east quarter. Does she know?"

"How would she know? How would *anyone* know? The clerk is dead — I saw to his debts being paid and his silence being permanent. The broker has sailed for Buenos Aires with more money than God. The chain has no links left, Mother. Except me." A chair creaked; he must have sat, or slumped. "Except me, sitting in Papa's chair, drinking Papa's brandy, in the house that will be standing long after Verron's east quarter is a field of weeds. That's the joke, isn't it. I saved us. I am the reason there is still a house to be evacuated from, and a name on the list, and a future. I did it *for*—"

"For whom, Aldous? Say the word. You've said everything else tonight."

A long pause. When he answered, his voice was small in a way that frightened Petra more than anything yet, because it was suddenly a child's voice, and she recognized it — it was the voice she herself had used, once, under the beam, calling and calling.

"For the family. For the name. Father wanted the firm to survive the war and it was dying, he knew it was dying, and I found the only door out and I walked through it and I shut it behind me. That's all. That's the whole confession. I'm not sorry and I can't sleep and both of those are true, and I don't know what to do with either."

Petra heard the mother rise. Heard her cross the room — measured steps, unhurried even now, even at this. Heard her stop, presumably beside the chair where her son sat wearing his father's guilt like a borrowed coat.

"What is done is done," the mother said. "We will speak of it never again."

That was all. That was the verdict. No thunder, no weeping, no sending him away. *What is done is done.* Eleven hundred people, a whole quarter of a town, a tanners' street with its shop signs and its cats and its Sunday bells — filed away between two sentences, the way you file away a bill already paid.

Petra sat in the dark and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water filling her lungs, that this was how the world worked above the level of children. Adults did not lie the way she had imagined liars lied — with sly eyes and hidden papers. They lied the way this house lied: by hanging portraits of everyone except the daughter who'd run off, by setting four places at table instead of five, by saying *never again* instead of *I am ashamed*. They lied to be kind, or called it kindness, or couldn't tell the difference anymore. Sister Blackwood lied too — Petra had known since the second week, known and kept it like a stone in her pocket: the sister was a sister, the mister was a missus, and the whole ward conspired in the lie because the lie let a good person do good work. But that lie was a coat worn against the cold. This other kind — *what is done is done* — was a coat made of other people's skins.

She should hate the man in the drawing room. She tried to. She reached for hate the way you reach for a rung on a ladder, and her hand found it, and then — this was the terrible part — her hand also found his voice breaking on *I can't sleep*, and found the boy inside the monster, and hated that she could hear him, hated that pity arrived uninvited like a stray cat you couldn't kick.

She thought of Mama's hands, floured to the wrists. Of Papa singing off-key on delivery mornings. Of the cat, Margot, who had not come out of the rubble and whom nobody had ever mentioned again because mentioning her made Petra cry and adults preferred a quiet child. All of it gone, and the man responsible was drinking brandy two rooms away and calling his crime elegance.

Stillness. Be still. The beam hasn't finished settling.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the drawing-room door finally opened properly, and Petra flattened herself into the stair shadow as Aldous came out alone. He stood in the hall a moment, swaying slightly, the drink or the truth catching up with him. Then he moved toward the stairs — toward her — and Petra's blood stopped.

But he turned aside at the bottom step and simply stood, one hand flat against the newel post, head bowed, and in the dim light from the hall lamp Petra saw that he was crying — silently, horribly, the tears falling straight off his chin onto the polished floor, his body refusing to shake because shaking would make noise and noise would make it real. He cried the way she had cried under the beam. Without permission. Without any belief that anyone would come.

Then he climbed the stairs. He passed within an arm's length of her hiding place — close enough that she could smell the brandy and the horse and under both a faint thread of something like fear-sweat — and went on up, and a door closed somewhere above, and the house resumed its silence.

Petra stayed where she was a long time. Her legs were wooden. Her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles.

There was one thing she had not told anyone, not even Sister Blackwood. When they pulled her from the house on Rue de la Tannerie, she had been clutching a biscuit tin — the tin with the picture of the Eiffel Tower on the lid, Mama's sewing tin, which had been in the cellar and therefore survived. Inside, along with Mama's thimble and a photograph curled by damp, were papers. Papers Papa kept in the tin because he said the safest bank in France was a place no thief would think to look. Receipts. A lease. And letters — business letters, stamped and signed, that Papa had been keeping for a neighbor, the surveyor Monsieur Ferrand, who had asked him to hold them safe until he returned from Lille. Monsieur Ferrand had not returned from Lille.

Petra had read those letters a dozen times in the ward at Ypres, by candlelight, sounding out the hard words letter by letter while Sister Blackwood taught her her alphabet and never guessed why the child wanted so badly to read. The letters discussed a corrected survey. A payment. An office in Amiens. One of them bore a name Petra had seen nowhere else in all her salvaged paper — a name written in a fine, confident hand, signing himself as the broker's agent:

*A. Blackwood.*

She had not known what to do with it. She was eleven. She had held the knowledge the way you hold a coal — it warmed her on the worst nights and burned her on the others, and mostly she just kept it cupped and hidden and waited for it to become clear what it was for.

Now she knew. Now the coal had a shape.

She waited until the house had been silent a full half hour. Then she rose, legs aching, and went softly along the corridor to the room where Sister Blackwood slept — the old nursery, they'd said, though it held no toys now. Through the gap beneath the door: candlelight, and the scratch of a pen. Writing, at this hour. Of course writing. Sister Blackwood wrote everything down; it was the thing Petra loved best about her, that she believed names mattered enough to be recorded.

Petra put her hand on the door handle. Held it. Did not turn it.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would tell her. On the road, maybe, or in the yard — somewhere away from this house and its listening walls, because if the mother heard, the mother would find a way to make it *what is done is done*, and Sister Blackwood deserved better than a verdict. She deserved the tin. She deserved to see the name in the fine confident hand, and Petra needed to watch her face when she read it — needed to see whether love was stronger than blood in at least one grown person, or whether the whole world was this drawing room, larger.

Through the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat, she could hear the pen still scratching.

Far away to the east — so far it might have been imagination, so far the mother would certainly call it imagination — the night rumbled. Once. Twice. A long pause, like a breath drawn in.

Petra took her hand off the door, went back to her cold little room, and lay down on top of the coverlet with the biscuit tin hugged to her chest, listening to the sky clear its throat, counting the seconds between the far-off thunder and whatever came after.

The third rumble was closer.
