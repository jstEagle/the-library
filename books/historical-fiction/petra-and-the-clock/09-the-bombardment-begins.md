## 9. The Bombardment Begins

The first shell came at half past four in the morning, and it did not come from far away.

Silas had been awake for an hour already — she was always awake by then, her body still keeping the clock of the dressing station even here, in a house that smelled of beeswax and dead flowers — when the sound arrived. Not the distant rolling thunder she had grown used to at Ypres, the guns speaking across miles of mud. This was closer. This was *near*. The windowpanes shivered in their frames, and somewhere down the street a dog began to howl.

She sat up in the narrow bed in the room that had been hers as a girl, before it had been stripped of everything soft and given over to a guest who never came. Her hand went to the ledger beneath her pillow out of pure habit, fingers finding the worn leather edge, and she lay there listening.

A second shell. A third. Each one nearer than the last.

*Ahead of schedule.* Halloway's voice in her memory, dry as old paper: *The intelligence says Verron has days yet. But intelligence is a man guessing with a map.*

By the time she reached the landing, the house was stirring. Her mother stood at the top of the stairs in a dressing gown the color of ash, one hand on the newel post, her face turned toward the window with an expression Silas could not read — not fear exactly, not yet. Something older than fear. The look of a woman hearing a bill come due.

"The east quarter," Silas said. "That's where they're falling."

Her mother did not answer. Down the corridor, behind a closed door, Aldous coughed — the deep, wet cough of a wound going bad, the wound Silas had been dressing twice a day with the bitter economy of someone who has learned to hate the work and do it well anyway.

"They'll evacuate the town," Silas said. "There are orders. There's a train."

"There is always a train," her mother said, "until there isn't."

The fourth shell fell close enough to rattle the portraits in the hall. Silas heard one frame swing on its nail and stop, swinging, ticking against the wall like a small mad clock. She thought of Petra asleep upstairs in the nursery that had been converted to a servant's room, the girl curled around her salvaged tin of papers the way other children curled around dolls.

She went to wake her.

* * *

By seven o'clock the streets of Verron were a river running uphill.

Carts, prams, wheelbarrows, a piano abandoned in the middle of the rue Saint-Michel with its lid open and its keys glinting like teeth. Soldiers — a handful, mostly wounded men left behind when their unit pulled back — tried to impose order on the exodus and were swept along by it instead. An officer on horseback shouted something about the rail yard, about priority papers, about calm, and his voice cracked on the word *calm*, and nobody listened, because the shells had begun falling in earnest now, walking up the valley in great deliberate strides, each impact a fist against the earth that you felt in your back teeth.

Silas moved through it all with Petra's wrist locked in her grip.

The girl had woken without crying, which was worse somehow. She had dressed in thirty seconds, stuffed her tin of papers into her coat, and asked only one question: "Is it the east quarter again?" And when Silas said yes, Petra had nodded once, the way an old soldier nods, and said nothing more, and Silas had wanted to stop in the middle of the street and hold her until the world apologized.

There was no time for that. There was no time for anything but the next hundred meters.

"Where are we going?" Petra shouted over a detonation that sent a curtain of brick dust drifting across the rooftops.

"Home," Silas said, and heard the strangeness of the word leave her mouth. She had not called the Blackwood house *home* in eleven years. It came out anyway, smooth as a lie, because in a crisis the body reaches for whatever word is nearest.

They reached the house as a shell took the corner bakery two streets over. The blast wave shoved them both against the garden wall; glass rained into the flowerbeds; Mrs. Aubert's roses, which her mother had spent three summers perfecting, lay flattened under a lace of broken window. Petra's ears were bleeding slightly — Silas saw the pink at the girl's earlobes and pressed her palms over them without breaking stride, walking her through the gate, up the steps, into the hall where the portraits swung on their nails like hanged men.

"Aldous," Silas called up the stairs. "Get down here. Now."

He came down wrong — too fast, one hand on the banister, gray-faced, his bandage showing a bloom of fresh red at the shoulder. He looked at the windows, at the light flashing beyond them, and Silas watched him do the arithmetic every soldier does: *how long, how close, how many.*

"It's early," he said. His voice was hoarse. "The schedule said—"

"I know what the schedule said." She caught his arm and steered him toward the kitchen. "Listen to me. The town is going to pieces and the evacuation will take longer than anyone planned, which means casualties, which means people will start looking for any roof with a competent pair of hands under it. We have a cellar. We have supplies. I have—" she almost said *training*, said *experience*, said *two years of the salient* — "I know what I'm doing. So does Petra."

The girl looked up at the mention of her name, chin lifting, ready.

"You're wounded," Silas told Aldous flatly. "You'll carry things and you'll do what you're told and you will not argue with me in front of patients. Understood?"

For a moment something flickered across his face — the old brotherly indignation, the reflex of a boy who had never once been told what to do by his sister and survived it. Then another shell landed, nearer, and the china in the dresser sang all together like a struck chord, and the indignation died unborn.

"Understood," he said.

* * *

The cellar had been a coal store once, then her father's wine cellar, then nothing much — a cool dark place where furniture went to be forgotten. It ran deeper than most in Verron, brick-vaulted, with a second chamber behind the wine racks where her grandfather had kept his papers during the last war. It was, Silas realized with a clarity that felt almost obscene, the best aid post in the street.

They worked through the morning.

Aldous carried the dining table down in pieces and reassembled it as an operating surface; Silas boiled water on the kitchen range in every pot the house owned and lugged it down in pails. They stripped sheets off beds she remembered sleeping between, tore them into strips, rolled bandages until their thumbs ached. Petra became the runner she had been at the dressing station, darting up the stairs for this or that, memorizing in ten minutes the geography of a house she'd known for two days better than Silas knew it after eighteen years.

"Tincture of iodine is in the blue bottle," Silas told her. "Morphia tablets in the tin marked *tea*. If anyone asks, it's tea."

Petra grinned — quick, fierce, there and gone — and was gone herself, up the stairs two at a time.

The first patient arrived at noon, carried on a door by two neighbors: a man from the rue Saint-Michel with his leg opened from knee to ankle by a beam. Silas worked on him by lamplight while the house above them shuddered, and found, to her own surprise, that her hands were perfectly steady. That was the secret no one believed until they learned it: the shelling did not make the work harder. In a way it made it easier. There was no room left in the body for anything but the work. Resentment, grief, the cold archive of grievances she carried against the woman now holding the lamp for her — all of it drained away like water through sand, and what remained was simply the wound, and the hands, and the next thing, and the next.

Her mother held the lamp. She held it very straight, and she did not flinch when the man screamed, and Silas hated her a little less for the length of one debridement, which was perhaps the longest truce they had managed in a decade.

More came. A woman with a scalp wound, her children pressed against the cellar wall with their thumbs in their mouths. A gendarme with a shattered collarbone. Two soldiers separated from their retreating company, one of them blind from dust and shock, both of them seventeen if they were a day. By evening the cellar held nineteen souls, and the air was thick with iodine and coal dust and the low animal murmur of people trying not to cry where children could hear.

Silas moved among them the way she had moved among cots at Ypres, triaging with a touch and a glance. *You'll keep. You won't, tonight, unless— You'll keep.* Behind her, Aldous knelt beside a stretcher, holding a basin, feeding sips of water to the man with the leg, and doing it badly and then less badly and then, near midnight, with a gentleness that made Silas look at him twice.

He caught her looking.

"What?" he said. Defensive, tired, twenty-eight years old again.

"Nothing," she said. "Keep doing that."

Above them a shell landed close enough to bring down a fine white sift of plaster from the vaulting, and everyone in the cellar went silent, listening to the building decide whether to stand. It stood. Somewhere in the silence Petra appeared at Silas's elbow with the morphia tin and a pencil stub and a scrap of paper, and held them out without a word, and Silas understood that the girl had been keeping the ledger of the cellar in her head — who had been dosed, when, how much — because someone had to, and Petra had decided it would be her.

"You should sleep," Silas told her.

"So should you," Petra said, with the terrible fairness of children.

"I will. Later."

"That's what you always say." But the girl's eyes were soft when she said it, and she tucked the pencil behind her ear like a sergeant-major and went back down the row of stretchers, checking her list.

* * *

It was past two in the morning when the shelling paused — not ended, paused, the way a man pauses to draw breath — and in the ringing quiet Silas climbed the cellar stairs to see what was left of the house.

Less than there had been. The front drawing room had lost its windows entirely; the curtains hung in ribbons, breathing in the night air. Through the empty frames she could see the street, and the fires burning two blocks east, and the sky above Verron the color of a bruise. The portraits in the hall had stopped swinging. All those Blackwood faces looked out at the ruin with their painted composure, and Silas thought: *you were built for parlors, all of you, and the parlor is gone.*

Her mother stood at the broken window with her back to her.

"The train," her mother said, without turning. "The officer this morning said the last train leaves tomorrow night. From the rail yard. Priority berths for those with papers."

"Yes."

"And you have papers. Access. You can speak to whoever arranges these things." Now she turned, and the firelight from the east lay across her face, and Silas saw that her mother had rehearsed this speech, had built it plank by plank in the dark hours, the way she built everything — carefully, and for her own use. "Four of us, Auguste. Your brother cannot walk to the lines. I am fifty-nine years old. The servants' children—"

"I know the arithmetic, Mother."

"Do you?" Something moved behind her mother's eyes, quick and cold. "Do you know it, or do you only know the parts of it that suit you? Because I have watched you in that cellar all day, and you have been magnificent, and you have also been counting. Don't tell me you haven't. You count everything. You counted me out years ago."

The words landed precisely where they were aimed, and Silas stood very still and let them, because the worst of it was that they were true. She *had* been counting. Four family members. One berth she could legitimately secure, maybe two with forgery and luck. And a girl with no surname worth the paper it wasn't written on, waiting downstairs with a pencil behind her ear.

"I'll get you your berth," Silas said at last, evenly. "I'll do what I can. Now go down and hold the lamp. There's a man on the third stretcher who needs changing, and he doesn't care whose name is on our letters."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment — and Silas would remember, afterward, that the look contained something that might have been surprise, or might have been the beginning of respect, arriving eleven years late and dressed as disappointment.

Then the shells started again, walking back up the valley toward them, and below in the cellar a child began to cry, and Silas went down into the noise and the work, carrying with her the one number she could not put down: *one berth, and Petra entitled to nothing on paper.*

The last train left tomorrow night. She had one day to solve an equation with no honest answer.
