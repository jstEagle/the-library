## 10. Mother's Bargain

Margaret Blackwood had not slept in her own bed since the shelling began. She slept instead in the drawing room, fully dressed, on the chaise beneath the portrait of her husband — the one painted the year before his heart failed him, when he still had color in his face and opinions in his mouth. She had chosen that room deliberately. If the house was going to come down around her, she preferred it to happen among the faces of the dead, who at least had never lied to her.

Or so she had believed until this evening.

She heard Aldous before she saw him — the dragging step on the gravel path at the side of the house, the small animal sound he made trying to climb the porch steps without being heard. He had never been able to do anything quietly. Even as a boy, creeping down for stolen sugar, he had announced himself with his whole body.

She opened the door before he could knock.

He stood in the light of the storm lamp she held up, and Margaret Blackwood looked at her son and understood, in the space of a single breath, everything that Silas had tried to tell her in the cellar an hour ago.

His left arm hung wrong. Not broken — she knew broken; she had set Aldous's arm twice before he turned ten — but held close against his ribs, cradled, as if the limb itself were ashamed. His coat was torn at the shoulder and dark with something older than rain. There was mud to his knees and a scratch across his cheekbone that had stopped bleeding and started again. And his eyes —

His eyes were the eyes of a man who had seen the inside of his own soul and found it furnished entirely by other people's money.

"Mama," he said. His voice came out ruined, scraped thin. "Mama, I need —"

She stepped aside and let him in.

She would think about that later — that she let him in without a word, without a question, before her mind had finished assembling the facts her body already knew. Some decisions the blood makes on its own. That was the truth of motherhood they did not put in the books: you did not choose your children in the moment of crisis. You chose them years ago, over and over, until choosing them was simply what your hands did when a door opened into the night.

She led him to the kitchen because the kitchen was warmest and because the drawing room had portraits in it, and she found herself unable, tonight, to sit beneath her dead husband's painted gaze while she committed whatever sin this was going to be.

"Sit."

He sat. She heated water. She took the needle and the iodine from the tin where the cook used to keep them, back when there had been a cook, back when there had been staff, back when Verron had been a town instead of a casualty. She cut away his sleeve. The wound along his shoulder was long and shallow — shrapnel, or glass, or the edge of something meant for someone else. She cleaned it while he stared at the table and shook.

"Aldous." She threaded the needle. "Look at me."

He looked at her. He had her coloring and her father's jaw, and at thirty-four he still had the power to make her feel, briefly, like a girl again — like the girl who had believed that love was a thing you could bank, that devotion compounded, that if you gave enough of yourself to the right people they would someday give it back with interest.

"I sold them the grid," he said.

The needle did not stop. Her hands had been steady through two wars' worth of bad news delivered by telegram — her husband's death, her daughter's disappearance, the mortgage, the taxes, the slow peeling-away of the town's respectability one defaulted account at a time. Her hands were steady now.

"The artillery grid. For the east quarter." His breath hitched. "I didn't know — I told myself I didn't know what it was for. It was coordinates, Mama. It was maps and elevations and I told myself it was surveying. There was a man in Lille who paid —"

"How much?"

He flinched as though she'd struck the wound instead of sewing it.

"How *much*, Aldous?"

"Eleven thousand francs." A whisper. "And a passage out. To Argentina."

She tied off the stitch and cut the thread. Eleven thousand francs. Enough to clear the mortgages twice over. Enough to restore the house to what it had been in the portraits. Enough, in the arithmetic of their declining family fortunes, to buy back everything the war and her husband's pride and the slow rot of the century had taken — purchased with the east quarter of Verron. With the Dubois girl's parents. With the child sleeping downstairs in her cellar wrapped in a soldier's coat.

*Petra.* The girl had told her, in that flat, terrible, child's way of reporting weather: *The shells came at four in the morning and my house was the third one.* Third one. As precise as a ledger entry.

Margaret poured herself a measure of the cooking brandy and did not offer any to her son.

"Do you understand what you've done?" she asked. "Not what you've cost us. What you've *done*."

"Yes," Aldous said, and began to cry — silently, horribly, the way men cried who had learned it from fathers who disapproved of noise. "Yes, Mama. God help me. Yes."

* * *

It took her until dawn to arrive at the decision, and she arrived at it the way she arrived at all her decisions: walking the perimeter of it, testing every fence, looking for the gap.

She could turn him out. Let him take his chances with the military police already moving through the town's edges, checking papers, compiling lists of men of fighting age who had no regiment. She could turn him in herself. She imagined it — the walk to the gendarmerie post, her hand on his elbow, delivering him like a parcel marked *return to sender*. Justice, clean and cold. The kind of justice that would look very fine written down somewhere, in someone else's ledger.

And then what remained of her name would be attached to it forever. *The Blackwood woman whose son sold Verron.* They would take the house. They would take what little dignity the war had left her. Petra would go to an orphanage convoy with nothing but a soldier's coat and the knowledge that the mother of the man who orphaned her had done the only correct thing and been destroyed by it anyway.

Or she could protect him.

She sat in the kitchen as the sky went grey, and she examined that second door honestly, because she was too old and too tired to lie to herself about what she was. She was a woman who had protected her son his entire life — from his father's contempt, from his debts, from the consequences of a dozen small betrayals that had each seemed survivable at the time. Every protection had made him smaller. She knew that. Knowing it changed nothing. The blood chose. The blood had always chosen. It was the one inheritance she could not disown.

But there was a third element in her equation, and it had walked into her cellar yesterday wearing a British uniform and a stranger's name.

Her daughter.

Her — what was she even to call her? Silas. *Silas Blackwood.* The name on the sealed letter that had arrived like a hand reaching out of a grave. Margaret had spent eleven years constructing a story about that disappearance, and the story had been simple and self-flattering: the girl had run off, had disgraced them, had chosen her unnatural life over her family, and the family's coldness since had merely been the reasonable temperature of wounded pride.

Then the girl had appeared at the door in a man's coat, calling herself by a man's name, and had knelt in Margaret's own cellar and stitched strangers' wounds with hands steadier than any surgeon's, and had looked at Margaret with those black Blackwood eyes and asked for nothing.

Asked for nothing. That was the part that kept snagging, like a loose thread catching on a nail. Children who wanted something asked. Silas had not asked. Silas had come home — if it was home; Margaret suspected the word meant nothing to her — and worked, and watched, and waited, and wanted *nothing*.

Except, Margaret realized, sitting very straight in the cold kitchen, one thing.

The train.

* * *

She found her in the cellar at mid-morning.

The aid post had grown overnight. Word moved fast in a dying town, faster than official channels, and by dawn the Blackwood cellar held nine patients on straw pallets: two children with fever, an old man with a crushed foot, a young woman in labor who had arrived at three o'clock attended by her terrified husband, and a gallery of lesser hurts. Two of Silas's orderlies — boys who had followed her off the train on some unspoken soldier's logic — moved among them with basins and bandages, and Silas herself knelt at the center of it, sleeves rolled, delivering the laboring woman's baby with the unhurried competence of a woman doing something she had done a hundred times.

Margaret watched from the stairs. The child came just after she arrived — a boy, red-faced and furious at the world, and Silas caught him with the ease of long practice and did not smile, exactly, but something near the mouth, something banked, and handed him to his mother, and said quietly, "There's a strong pair of lungs. He'll be trouble," and the exhausted woman laughed through her tears, and Margaret felt something move in her chest that she refused to name.

She waited until Silas came to the stairs to fetch clean water. Then she spoke.

"We should talk."

Silas looked up. Up close, in the lamplight, the resemblance was unbearable — her husband's brow, her own mouth, aged eleven years past its right to be. Those eyes assessed her the way they seemed to assess everything: quickly, completely, and without warmth wasted.

"Five minutes," Silas said. "The next one may come fast."

They climbed to the pantry, and Margaret closed the door on the sounds below.

"You know about Aldous."

"I know what he did."

"And yet you're still here." Margaret folded her hands. She had rehearsed this in the grey hours, standing at the window watching the smoke columns rise over the east quarter, and she had rehearsed it because she knew — she had always known — that she could not win this conversation honestly. So she would win it the other way. The way she had won every argument in this family for forty years: by making duty do the work that love could not. "You could report him. You have the access. One word to your major, and my son hangs or shoots or rots. And what would it buy? The dead stay dead. The quarter stays rubble. My name — *our* name — is finished either way. But if you keep silent, we survive. All of us."

Silas said nothing. The silence had weight. Margaret pressed on, hating the sound of her own voice and pressing on regardless, because this was the shape her whole life had made: pressing on.

"The train leaves tomorrow night. Four of us need berths. You have military standing, papers, authority — more than you've admitted. Get us on it. That's all I'm asking. That is the smallest possible thing, and it costs you nothing but a signature."

"Four," Silas repeated.

"Your brother. Me. Your aunt Céline. Marta has nowhere else."

"Four berths." Silas's voice was very quiet. "How many do you imagine exist?"

"That isn't my question to answer. It's yours. You're the one with the access. You're the one who—" Margaret stopped. Steadied. Went for the wound, because she had always gone for the wound, and hated herself in the same motion, and did it anyway. "You're the one who owes this family something. Whatever you think we took from you — you left. You chose your exile. We lived with the shame of it. Eleven years, we carried—"

"*You* carried shame?" Something moved behind Silas's eyes — not anger, worse, a kind of tired recognition, as if Margaret had confirmed a prediction made long ago. "Mother. Do you hear yourself? Your son sold the coordinates that killed that child's parents downstairs. Your solution is that I spend my forged life securing seats for the people who disowned me, and send a homeless orphan to a convoy. And you call it what I owe."

"I call it family."

"No," Silas said. "You call it *Aldous*. You've called it Aldous my whole life. Family was just the word you hid him behind."

The pantry was very still. Below them, faintly, the aid post murmured — a moan, water poured, the newborn crying its furious welcome.

Margaret lifted her chin. This was the moment, she understood dimly, where a different woman might have said something true. Might have said *I was afraid for you*. Might have said *your father would have put you out and I let him, and I have regretted it every day of eleven years, and I don't know how to say so*. She felt the words assemble themselves somewhere below her sternum, old words, rusted shut.

What came out instead was:

"He's your brother."

"Yes," Silas said softly. "And she's mine too."

She nodded toward the floor — toward the cellar, toward the child below — and picked up the water jug, and paused at the pantry door.

"One berth, Mother. Maybe. I'll decide by tomorrow. Decide nothing tonight. You're only good at deciding for other people."

The door closed behind her.

Margaret Blackwood stood alone in the pantry among the preserves and the dust, and listened to the shells come again, far off, walking closer — and understood that her daughter had just handed her the one thing she had never once given anyone in this family.

A choice.

And she was terribly afraid that she had already made it, years ago, and that everyone in the house knew it but her.
