## 11. The List of Berths

The rail yard at Verron had been a place of timetables once. Silas could see its ghost in the chalkboard still bolted to the station wall — *Départ 14h40, Arrivée 16h10* — the tidy optimism of a world that believed trains went where they promised and came back again. Now the board was splintered along one edge, and someone had scrawled across it in three languages: **CIVILIANS ONLY. MILITARY PERSONNEL DO NOT BOARD.**

She stood before the evacuation officer's trestle table with her cap pulled low and the list in her hand, and she did the arithmetic.

It was the same arithmetic she had done a thousand times at the dressing station, only there the numbers had been men and morphine grains and the inches of gauze left on the last roll. There was always a number too small for the need. She had simply never before held both sides of the equation in the same family.

Four Blackwoods. One berth.

* * *

The list itself was nothing — a single sheet of flimsy paper, typed by some clerk in Amiens or Abbeville who would never smell the smoke over Verron. **PRIORITY EVACUATION — VERRON SECTOR.** Names in two columns. At the top, underlined twice in red pencil: *Blackwood, Mme. E.* Then beneath her, in the second column, as if an afterthought: *Blackwood, A. (wounded — stretcher case, priority).* And then two more names that made Silas's jaw tighten: an aunt, Célestine Blackwood, seventy-one, and a cousin's widow, Marguerite, with a child of four.

Five names now, not four. She counted them twice, hoping the ink would multiply the berths. It did not.

Against this: one authorized civilian berth remaining on the last train out of Verron, departing at dawn. Halloway's letter had been precise about it, in the way institutions were precise when precision cost them nothing. *One (1) berth, allocated per original survey of 3 October. No additional allocations will be authorized. Military escort is reminded that carriage of unauthorized persons constitutes—*

She hadn't finished reading the sentence. She'd folded the letter into thirds and put it in her breast pocket, over the ledger, where all the true things lived.

* * *

The yard was bedlam wearing the costume of order. Troops of the 41st were loading shell crates on the far sidings, their sergeant bellowing numbers like a man counting his own heartbeat. Refugees clogged the platform in a river of bundles and prams and white faces, held back by two gendarmes who looked barely older than Petra and kept their bayonets fixed mostly so their hands would have something to hold.

Silas walked the length of the platform twice, counting. Not refugees — she could do nothing about the refugees, and knowing it sat in her chest like swallowed coal — but counting the train. Eleven coaches. Two flatcars for livestock that would carry people anyway, whatever the manifest said. The medical coach at the rear with six stretcher racks already half-claimed by wounded from the dressing posts up the line.

One berth. She found it: compartment C, third coach, a single reserved seat with a paper tag pasted to the window. **BLACKWOOD.** Someone had already written the name. The universe, apparently, had a sense of humor.

She stood looking at that tag for a long moment while the crowd pressed and shifted around her like water around a stone.

* * *

"You're the military escort, then?" The evacuation officer was a rail captain with a waxed mustache and the eyes of a man who had spent three days saying no. "Captain Ferriol. You'll be responsible for the priority list. Anyone not on it doesn't board. I don't care what they offer you, what they cry, what they show you. Papers are papers."

"Papers are papers," Silas agreed, and heard how flat her voice came out.

Ferriol glanced at her insignia, then did the thing every officer did when he looked at her too long — the small hesitation, the flicker of doubt, quickly buried. Her papers said Lieutenant S. Blackwood, RAMC attached. Her shoulders said something else entirely, if you knew how to read them. Ferriol didn't know. He moved on. "Your party arrives when?"

"My party." She tested the words like bad coin. "They'll come up from the town this evening. Madame Blackwood, her sister-in-law, a child—" She stopped. Recounted without wanting to. "There's a girl. Eleven years old. Petra. She's been living with our household since October."

"Name on the list?"

"No."

"Then she doesn't board." Ferriol said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. He tapped the flimsy sheet. "I've got four hundred people on that platform and room for maybe sixty. You think I like the mathematics? Go argue with Berlin." He turned to the next supplicant, a farmer holding a goat on a rope, and became a wall again.

Silas stepped aside and took out her notebook, because when the world refused to make sense she wrote it down until it confessed.

* * *

She found a sheltered corner beneath the goods shed, out of the wind that carried ash from the burning eastern quarter, and made the column the way she made all her columns — honest, ruthless, unblinking.

**BERTHS AVAILABLE: ONE.**

Underneath:

*Mother. Seventy this spring. Will not survive a day in the roads; her heart is a clock with a cracked mainspring. Will not ask for anything, which is its own kind of demand.*

*Aldous. Stretcher case. Shell fragment in the thigh, wound going septic at the edges despite the carbolic. If the leg goes, he needs a surgeon within forty-eight hours. On foot, he dies. This is not sentiment; it is prognosis.*

*Célestine. Seventy-one, deaf in one ear, fierce as a kestrel. Stronger than Mother, God knows. Could walk to Calais if her feet held.*

*Marguerite and Théo. The widow and the boy. Théo is four. Four-year-olds cannot walk twenty miles through shelled country. But Marguerite has a sister in Boulogne, a whole other family to catch her, whereas—*

Whereas her mother had no one but the daughter she had erased. Whereas Aldous had no one but the sister he had orphaned a child to profit from.

And then the line she had been circling all afternoon, the way a tongue finds the broken tooth:

*Petra. Eleven. Entitled to nothing on paper. Not French-listed, not registered, not surveyed. An orphan the army never officially noticed, fed on army bread by soldiers breaking regulations for eight months, taught letters by a lieutenant who does not legally exist. Has no one. Has no claim. Has me.*

She stared at the page until the characters swam.

That was the whole of it, laid out like a surgical tray. One berth. Five names, six with the boy, seven counting herself — no, not herself, she was not on any list, she was the escort, the ghost, the S. Blackwood who signed forms and did not exist in any census. That was the bitter joke at the bottom of everything: she had spent three years making herself unlisted, and now the skill of vanishing was worth exactly one berth less than nothing, because the one berth could not go to a person who was not there.

* * *

A shell fell somewhere east of the town, and the ground carried it to her through the soles of her boots — that deep, patient thud, like a fist knocking on a door from underneath the world. The bombardment had been growing all afternoon, closer than the maps said it should be. The front was supposed to be eleven kilometers away. Shells did not respect timetables any more than trains did.

She thought of her mother's face in the parlor, three days ago, saying *you will manage it, Auguste, you have always managed things* — using the male name like a key turned in a lock, reminding Silas of the bargain between them: your secret for your service. She thought of Aldous gray-faced on the cellar cot, working beside her in silence, handing her forceps before she asked, and how she had hated how much that simple competence undid her.

And she thought of Petra on the train north from the front, stowed away among mail sacks, discovered furious and filthy and utterly unrepentant, saying: *You go, I go. That's the whole rule. I learned it from you.*

The child had no name on any list. But she had a name in a ledger — *Petra. Third Ward. Reads now. Counts to a hundred. Afraid of the dark and hides it. Brave in the open and doesn't know it.* Silas had written that entry in March by candlelight while the girl slept under a blanket in the next bay. It was the truest document in her coat.

* * *

"Lieutenant Blackwood?"

She looked up. A Red Cross volunteer, a stout Flemish woman with flour-white hands, stood over her holding a clipboard. "They're asking at the gate for the military escort. There's a family — Blackwood — arrived from the town. The old lady's collapsed on the platform, and there's a wounded man on a door they've been carrying since noon. And—" the woman hesitated, consulting her memory rather than any paper, "there's a girl holding onto the stretcher like if she lets go the earth ends. Is that yours?"

*Is that yours.* As if families were property. As if the word *mine* could settle anything.

"They're mine," Silas said, and stood, and put the notebook away, and felt the arithmetic close around her throat like a collar.

* * *

Her mother lay on a bench near the ticket office, Célestine fanning her with a newspaper, Marguerite clutching Théo against the press of the crowd. Aldous had been laid on the bench beside her, his bandaged thigh propped on a kit bag, his face the color of tallow. And Petra—

Petra stood at the end of the bench with one hand wrapped in the strap of Aldous's stretcher-door and the other holding a canvas satchel Silas recognized: the one with the salvaged documents, the burnt-edged papers the girl had carried out of her parents' house and never let anyone examine. She was watching Silas across the crowd with an expression Silas had never seen on her before. Not fear. Something older than fear. Something waiting.

Later — much later — Silas would understand that expression. Later she would learn what Petra had heard in the Verron cellar, whose name she had recognized on those scorched pages. But standing in the rail yard with the light going copper and the shells walking closer, Silas saw only a tired child watching her family assemble itself around one seat on one train, and understood with perfect clarity that the girl had done the same arithmetic Silas had just finished, and reached the same answer, and was preparing — with the terrible courtesy children learn — not to be chosen.

"Well?" her mother said, propped on one elbow. "You've seen the lists, Auguste. Tell us how we go."

Behind her, Ferriol chalked a final figure on the splintered board: **DÉPART — AUBE. LAST TRAIN.**

Silas opened her mouth to answer, and Petra spoke first — quietly, not to Silas at all, but to the space between them, in the voice of someone laying a card face-down on a table.

"I need to tell you something," Petra said. "Before you count us."
