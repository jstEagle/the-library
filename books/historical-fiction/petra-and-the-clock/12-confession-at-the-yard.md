## 12. Confession at the Yard

The rail yard smelled of coal smoke and fear.

Petra had never seen so many people in one place. Verron's square on market days held perhaps three hundred souls, and she had thought that a crowd, a sea of hats and baskets and elbows. This was different. This was the whole town turned inside out — every family she'd ever bought bread from, every face from the east quarter that still existed to have a face — pressed together between the tracks with their bundles and their birds in cages and their grandmothers in wheeled chairs, all of them staring down the rails as if they could pull the train out of the dark by wanting it hard enough.

She sat on a crate of oranges somebody had abandoned, her knees drawn up, and watched Silas across the yard.

Sister Blackwood moved through the crowd like a needle through cloth. She had a way of going where the need was thickest — a man with a bandaged eye here, a woman fainting against a luggage trolley there — and the crowd parted for the grey uniform without being asked. People trusted the uniform even now, even tonight, when the uniforms had failed them in every other way. Petra was proud of that. *That one is ours,* she wanted to tell the whole yard. *I found her first.*

Except she hadn't told her yet. And the not-telling was sitting in Petra's chest like a swallowed stone.

She had known since the cellar. Three nights ago — no, four; the nights had gone soft and shapeless since the shelling started — she had stood on the cold stairs with her hand over her own mouth and heard Mr. Aldous say the words that made her parents into a number on somebody's paper. *The east quarter. The guns will walk it grid by grid. They'll never know what hit them.* He had said it the way men said things that weren't quite real, the way you talked about weather in another country. And Silas had said nothing at all. That was the part that frightened Petra most, afterwards. Not the confession. The silence after it.

She had said nothing either. For four days she had carried it around inside her like hot slag, letting it burn through everything soft. She watched Silas dress wounds and write in the little ledger and give away her own bread, and Petra thought: *she doesn't know. She loves him and she doesn't know.* And then she thought: *when she knows, will she look at me the way I looked at the rubble? Will I be the thing that broke her house?*

Because that was the arithmetic a child learns early in a war. Whatever Petra said next would cost something. There were no words left in the world that cost nothing.

A whistle shrieked down the platform and the crowd surged, and for a moment Petra lost sight of the grey coat entirely, and panic rose in her throat so fast and so stupid that she was on her feet before she knew she'd moved. Then the crowd closed again and there she was, Silas Blackwood, pushing back through the press of bodies with a list in her hand and her jaw set like a door barred against weather, and Petra's heart came back down out of her ears.

*Tell her,* said the stone in her chest. *Tell her before the train comes and it's too late forever.*

Petra jumped down off the crate. Her legs felt like borrowed legs. She crossed the yard the way she used to cross the ward at the dressing station — quick, low, weaving — and caught Silas at the edge of the platform where the lamplight didn't reach, in the pocket of shadow between two freight cars.

"Sister." She used the name because it was safe. Because it was theirs. "Can I — can I talk to you? Just you?"

Silas looked down at her. In the dark her face lost its soldier-stiffness and became just a tired woman's face, kind around the eyes. "Always," she said. She glanced at the list in her hand, then folded it away. "Five minutes. Then I'm needed at the berth gate."

"It's not five minutes' worth."

Something shifted in Silas's expression — a small settling, like a house deciding to stand firm in wind. "Then we'll find more than five minutes." She steered Petra gently by the shoulder, back behind the freight cars, into a corridor of darkness between the rusted couplings, where the noise of the crowd went distant and woolly. A lantern hung from a nail on the far car, guttering. It lit half of Silas's face and left the other half to the night.

"Sit if you want," Silas said. "You've been running errands since dawn."

"I don't want to sit."

"Standing, then." She waited. She was good at waiting; Petra had learned that early, learning letters, learning cards. Silas never rushed a silence. She let it get big enough for the true thing to fit inside it.

Petra picked at the hem of her coat. The coat had been Marta Dubois's daughter's once, before the east quarter stopped existing. Everything she owned had belonged to someone who wasn't there anymore. Even her name, in a way. Even Sister Blackwood.

"You'll think I'm lying," she said finally. "Or you'll think I got it wrong. Kids get things wrong. Everybody says so."

"Do they?" Silas said quietly. "Who's everybody?"

"Adults." Petra's voice came out smaller than she meant. "Grown-ups always know better. Grown-ups decide what children saw."

Silas crouched then, slowly, until her eyes were level with Petra's — the way she did at the ward when a wounded man wouldn't speak unless nobody loomed over him. "Petra," she said. "Look at me. Four months ago you pulled a ration card out of a dead man's pocket and read his name to me when three orderlies swore there was nothing on him. You have eyes. I trust your eyes more than I trust most grown men's tongues. So whatever you saw, you say it plain, and we'll take it from there."

The stone in Petra's chest cracked. What came out was not what she had rehearsed. She had rehearsed beginning at the beginning, at the cellar stairs, at the words *grid by grid*. Instead she heard herself say:

"I know why my mama and papa are dead."

The lantern flame leaned. Somewhere down the yard a child cried and was hushed.

"It was your brother." The words fell out fast now, tumbling, the way rubble falls when you pull the wrong stone. "Mr. Aldous. I heard him. In the cellar, the first night. He said he sold the — the timings, the maps, he said the Germans would walk the east quarter and nobody would warn it because warning it would show whose information it was. He said it like — like reading a shopping list. And I know it's true because—" Her breath hitched. She made herself go on, made herself be the girl who read dead men's names. "Because I have the papers. From our flat. After. When I went back for Mama's ring, I took papers off the floor because paper burns good and I thought we might need fire. And one of them has *Blackwood* written on it. His writing. A letter, half-burnt, about *the arrangement* and *the eastern sector* and money. I couldn't read all the words then. I can read them now. You taught me."

Silas had gone very still.

Not disbelieving-still. Petra had prepared herself for disbelieving-still, for the tilted head and the gentle voice: *Petra, sweetheart, you were confused, it was dark, grief makes strange dreams.* She had a whole speech ready for that, angry and trembling, and a second speech ready for being sent away, and a third ready for nothing at all. She had not prepared for this stillness — the stillness of someone taking a blow they'd been expecting for years.

"Sister," Petra whispered. "Say something."

"The papers," Silas said. Her voice was level but Petra could hear the effort in it, like a man carrying a beam. "You still have them?"

"In my bundle. Under the spare socks. Nobody looks under spare socks."

Despite everything — or through everything — something moved at the corner of Silas's mouth. "You learned that from me."

"I learned hiding from you. You hide a whole book in your coat."

Silas closed her eyes. Opened them. When she spoke again, each word came out placed carefully, like stones across a flooded road. "Petra. Listen to me very carefully, because this is important and I am only going to say it once. What you heard in that cellar — I believe you. Every word. I believe you the way I believe my own heartbeat, which is to say completely, without checking, without asking anyone's permission." She reached out and took both of Petra's hands in hers. Her hands were cold and rough and steady. "You are not wrong. You are not confused. And you are not in trouble. Do you understand me? Whatever happens next — none of what happens next is your doing. You carried this alone for four days because you were trying to protect me. That is the bravest, kindest, most damnable thing—"

Her voice broke. Not much. A hairline crack, the kind you only see if you're eleven years old and watching someone's face from six inches away. But Petra saw it.

"You're not angry at me," Petra said. It wasn't a question. It was astonishment wearing a question's clothes.

"No." Silas laughed, wet and short. "No, love. I'm angry at a great many people tonight, and every single one of them is older than you and shares my blood. You are the one person in this entire yard I am not angry at."

"But he's your brother."

"Yes."

"And I'm telling you he killed my mama and papa."

"Yes."

"So how can you—" Petra's voice cracked and she hated it, hated being small, hated the tears coming up hot. "How can you hold my hands and not hate me?"

Silas drew her in then, right against the grey coat, into the smell of carbolic and cold wool and gun oil that Petra had slept inside of for two months and knew better than her mother's perfume. Above her head, Silas said, into the dark over the rails:

"Because believing you costs me a brother I already lost. Disbelieving you would cost me you. That's not a hard sum, Petra. I do arithmetic all day long. That one's easy."

Petra cried then, properly, the way she hadn't let herself since the rubble — great ugly sobs into the coat while the lantern gutted and the trains whistled and four hundred people waited for a train that could not carry them all. She cried for her mama's ring and the burnt letter and the four days of carrying the slag alone, and Silas held her through all of it, one hand cradling the back of her head, saying nothing, because Silas knew — she had always known — that some silences are bigger and kinder than any words.

When it was done, Petra wiped her nose on the sleeve that everyone already called the rag-sleeve, and stepped back, and felt lighter and heavier at once, the way you feel after a wound is dressed.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Now," Silas said, and her face had changed. The kindness was still in it, banked like coals, but over it lay something Petra recognized from the worst nights at the station — the look Silas wore when the casualties came in faster than the hands to treat them. Decision, arriving. "Now you're going to stay within arm's reach of me until this train leaves. Now you're going to give me those papers when we're somewhere with light enough to read them by. And now—" She stopped.

Down the platform, past the end of the freight cars, a figure was moving toward them through the thinning crowd. An officer's silhouette, cap squared, stride unhurried, cutting through the chaos like a man walking through a room he owned. Petra knew the walk. Everyone in the unit knew the walk.

"That's the major," she breathed. "Why is Major Halloway here? He's supposed to be at the junction."

"He is indeed," Silas murmured. She straightened up, and her hand found Petra's shoulder and stayed there, warm and heavy, an anchor dropped. "Which means either the war has come looking for me, or my leave is over early. Stay close, love. And Petra — whatever he offers me in the next five minutes, whatever he says, remember one thing."

"What?"

"You told me the truth and I believed you. Nothing that man says can unmake that. That's the whole of it. That's the family, right there, finished, done — the rest is paperwork."

But Petra, watching the officer's boots come closer across the cinders, watching the lantern light climb up brass buttons and stop below a face arranged like a locked drawer, felt the stone slide back into her chest — heavier now, colder, shaped exactly like a choice she hadn't heard yet.

The major stopped at the edge of the shadows and removed his glove.

"Sister Blackwood," he said. "We need to talk about your passenger list."
