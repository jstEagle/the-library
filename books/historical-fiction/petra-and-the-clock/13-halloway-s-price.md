## 13. Halloway's Price

Major Edmund Halloway had learned, over thirty years of soldiering and eleven months of this particular war's paperwork, that the truth always arrived in triplicate.

The first copy was the official one: the evacuation manifest from Verron, typed in a clerk's careful hand, listing four hundred and twelve civilians to be moved on the last train out before the sector folded. The second copy was the political one — the memorandum from the Ministry, stamped in red, instructing him that certain names were to be handled with discretion, that the Blackwood family was to be given every consideration, and that no inquiry into the circumstances of Verron's east quarter was to be entertained by any officer at any level. The third copy was the one he kept in his own head, which was the only version he trusted, and it read simply: *Aldous Blackwood sold the grid references, four hundred people are dead because of it, and the family that raised him is about to be carried out of the fire on a train his treachery made necessary.*

Halloway sat in his office at the rear echelon station with all three truths spread across his desk like a hand of losing cards, and he thought about Silas Blackwood — or Lieutenant Samuel Blackwood, as the army knew her, as the dressing station knew her, as he alone had known her for two years now — and he wondered how a man could feel so tired in a body that had done nothing all day but sit.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain came down in the flat grey sheets that Flanders produced the way other countries produced wheat. Somewhere beyond the rain, guns were talking to each other in their patient, conversational way, and somewhere beyond the guns was Verron, and somewhere inside Verron was a girl of eleven who did not officially exist, and a woman who existed on paper under a name that would hang her if anyone ever read it closely.

He had known for two years. He had known since the day a shell collapsed the aid post at Poperinghe and a stretcher-bearer tore open "Samuel's" uniform at the shoulder while dragging her clear, and Halloway — arriving twenty minutes later with the ambulances — had seen what the boy was before the boy had finished swearing at everyone in Flemish, French, and English. He had stood over her on the wet ground, looked into those furious grey eyes waiting for the sentence, and said only: "You'll want to sew that shoulder properly, Lieutenant. Can't have my best dresser coming apart."

She had understood. She had never thanked him — thank-yous between them would have been a kind of lie, an admission that a debt existed where actually there was only arithmetic — but she had never forgotten either. And when the war ground on and her reputation grew, when colonels asked after "that Blackwood lad" and generals signed commendations, Halloway had watched her carry the secret the way mules carried shells: steadily, without complaint, up roads that were being shelled.

Now the secret had become inconvenient. Now the secret had a price tag on it, and Halloway had been instructed to deliver the invoice personally.

* * *

He found her at the rail yard.

That was where he'd known she'd be. Three days' leave granted under a fiction he had signed without reading too carefully — he owed her that much discretion, at least, though the fiction had fooled no one, least of all her — and she had spent it not resting but hauling stretchers through a town that the Ministry had already written off in a memorandum nobody was supposed to read. Of course she had. That was the whole trouble with Silas Blackwood. She had never once in her life done the sensible thing, and the sensible thing was precisely what Halloway had come eight miles through the rain to ask her to do.

She was at the far end of the platform, arguing with a transport sergeant about berth assignments, her greatcoat soaked black at the shoulders, her cap pulled low. Even from thirty yards Halloway could see the exhaustion in her — not the theatrical exhaustion of the young, but the deep structural kind, load-bearing exhaustion, the sort that eventually brought buildings down. She had been running on duty and fury for a week. He could tell because she was gesturing with the manifest instead of holding it still, and Silas Blackwood held documents still the way surgeons held scalpels.

"Sergeant." Halloway's voice carried the way it was trained to. "Give us the platform."

The sergeant went. Silas turned, saw him, and something in her face closed — not hostility exactly, but the wariness of a person who has learned that senior officers arriving unannounced at rail yards bring weather with them.

"Major."

"Lieutenant." He nodded at the manifest. "How many berths?"

"One legitimate. Four if I forge, five if I forge well, and I forge well."

"And how many Blackwoods?"

Her jaw tightened. "Three. My mother. My aunt. Aldous, if we can get him walking, which we can't yet." A beat. "You know why you're here, then."

"I wrote half the memorandum," Halloway said. "Not the parts that matter. Those were written above me, by men whose names you'll never learn, in rooms with better carpets than this platform has got." He took off his own cap, shook the rain from it, put it back on. It was an old habit, a small ritual, buying himself three seconds. "Walk with me."

They walked. The platform ran alongside a cutting full of standing water, and beyond the cutting the fields went dark toward Verron, where the horizon pulsed orange at intervals like something breathing under a blanket.

"The train leaves tomorrow at eighteen hundred," Halloway said. "After that, the line is cut. After that, Verron is a name on a map that we will spend the next fifty years pretending was never important. You understand the schedule. So let us understand the rest of it." He stopped walking. Rain ticked against his cap brim. "Here is what is being offered, Lieutenant. Not by me. Through me. There is a difference, and I want you to hear the difference, because I am going to say some things next that you will want to hit me for, and I would prefer you remember that I am only the postman."

"I've never once wanted to hit you, Major," Silas said. "I've wanted to shoot you twice, but never hit you."

"There's hope for you yet." He drew a breath. "One berth. One name on the list. The name is yours to assign — that is the concession they've made, and it cost them nothing, because they know what you'll do with it. Put your mother on the train. Put your aunt in the luggage van, I can arrange that much quietly, no one counts luggage vans. Your brother stays. He's wounded, he's compromised, and frankly, Lieutenant, if he boards that train there are people in London who will make sure he never arrives anywhere. Dead men can't testify, and Aldous Blackwood is the last living man who knows where the artillery orders originated."

"You're asking me to leave my brother to die."

"I'm telling you the Ministry intends him to die, and asking whether you'd rather it happened here, quickly, among people who might mourn him, or later, slowly, in a room in London where no one will ever find the body. Those are the choices. I didn't build them. But they're the ones on the table."

Silas said nothing. Her face did the thing it always did — went very still, very level, the emotion driven down somewhere below the waterline where he couldn't see it. He had commanded enough young officers to know that stillness was not calm. Stillness was a hull taking on water and refusing, out of sheer pride, to list.

"And the girl," Halloway said, and hated himself for saying it, and said it anyway, because the whole point of him was to be the man who said the thing no one else would.

"What about Petra?"

"She goes on the orphanage convoy."

"She has no papers."

"The convoy takes undocumented children. That's rather its entire function. There's a convoy forming at Hazebrouck, church-run, Quakers mostly, decent people. She'd be registered, fed, evacuated south, and in two years she'd be in a school in England learning to curtsy and spell. Which is a damned sight more than this war offers any other eleven-year-old in Flanders." He kept his voice level, administrative, almost gentle. "And you — you go back to your dressing station with a clean record. The leave becomes official, the forgery becomes clerical error, the girl in your ward with the wrong name on her enlistment paper becomes a clerical error too. Everything I have spent two years sitting on, everything that would end you — prison, disgrace, worse — all of it gets filed away and forgotten. You keep your work. You keep your name. You keep your life."

The rain filled the silence.

"And in exchange," Silas said quietly, "I put the family that disowned me on the train, and I put the child who chose me on a cart to an orphanage, and everyone lives happily ever after except the one person in this whole rotten equation who ever loved me."

"In exchange," Halloway said, "the institution survives, and so do you."

"That's not an exchange, Major. That's an amputation."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned on him then, and for the first time in two years he saw the waterline break — saw the fury come up through the stillness like a wreck rising. "Do you actually know what you're asking? Because I don't think you do. I think you've got a memorandum and a conscience and you've decided the conscience loses, because it always loses, because that's what institutions are *for* —" She stopped herself. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to something worse than shouting. It had dropped to precision. "Petra's parents died in the east quarter of Verron. Do you know what destroyed the east quarter, Major? My brother's signature. She is an orphan because of my blood. Every reason the world gives me to protect the Blackwoods is the same reason she has nothing. And you're asking me to reward them for it. To hand them the last seat on the last train out of the town my brother sold, and send the child they orphaned away to strangers, and call it mercy because the strangers are Quakers."

"I'm not calling it anything," Halloway said. "I'm delivering it."

"Then take it back."

"I can't take it back. That's the nature of offers from ministries. They're like artillery. Once they're fired, all you can choose is where you're standing when they land." He looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't since Poperinghe, since the mud and the torn shoulder and the eyes that had waited for a sentence. "So stand wherever you like, Lieutenant. That's the part of the offer that's real, even if nothing else is. Nobody is watching this platform. Nobody is checking the list until eighteen hundred hours tomorrow. Whatever you write on that manifest, whatever names, however many — the train will carry them, and I will sign the paperwork afterward, and if it comes apart I will stand in front of it and it will land on me. That is the offer. Not theirs. Mine."

She stared at him. Rain ran off her cap and dripped from her chin.

"Why?" she said finally. "Two years you've covered for me. Why does a major in the rear echelon burn his career on a dressing-station nurse with a false name?"

It was the question he'd hoped she wouldn't ask, because he'd never once answered it honestly, even to himself. He looked out past the cutting, past the dark fields, to where Verron breathed its orange breath against the sky, and he thought about the son he'd lost at Loos two years ago — the son whose effects had come back to him in a parcel with a nurse's note tucked inside, unsigned, describing how a young man named Thomas Halloway had died holding another man's hand and saying his mother's name, and how someone had stayed with him to the end so he wouldn't die alone. He had never found out which nurse. He had sometimes suspected. He had never once dared confirm it.

"A long time ago," Halloway said, "someone did for me what I'm doing for you, and never told me, and I've spent my whole career trying to pay it forward without knowing who to pay. That's all the answer you get. It isn't sentiment. It's accounting." He put his hand in his pocket and brought out a folded paper — blank, official letterhead, the kind that could become anything with the right ink and the right hand. He held it out. "Clean record. Signed and dated tomorrow morning, contingent on nothing. If you put every Blackwood in Flanders on that train and the girl in the luggage van beside your aunt, it's valid. If you put the girl in the berth and your mother in the road, it's valid. If you put yourself on the train under your own true name and dare the whole army to argue, God help us both, it's valid."

Silas took the paper. Her hands, he noticed, were shaking — just slightly, just at the edges, the tremor of a woman who had held too many dying men steady for too many days.

"You realize," she said, "that you've just handed a known forger a blank pardon."

"I realize," Halloway said, "that I've just handed a known forger the only weapon left in this war worth carrying, and I'd better pray she aims it better than her brother aimed his." He settled his cap. Down the platform, a whistle shrilled; a locomotive coughed itself awake somewhere in the dark, impatient, indifferent, already counting the hours. "Eighteen hundred tomorrow, Lieutenant. The train won't wait. Neither will the guns."

He turned to go. Behind him her voice caught him, quiet and stripped of all its armor:

"And if I choose her? If I give Petra the berth and let my mother walk?"

Halloway didn't turn around. He watched the rain fall into the cutting, into the standing water, into the dark.

"Then tomorrow morning," he said, "you'll find out what I already know — that some records can't be cleaned, only burned." 

He walked away down the platform, into the rain, and did not look back, because he had seen her face at Poperinghe once already, waiting for a sentence, and he could not bear to see it twice.

Behind him, in the lamplight, Silas Blackwood stood alone on the emptying platform with a blank pardon in one hand and a manifest in the other, and began, very slowly, to write.
