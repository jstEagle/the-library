## 14. Night in the Cellar Aid Post

The first stretcher came down the cellar stairs at half past seven, and by nine there was no floor left to see.

Silas worked by lamplight — three lamps, then two when the oil ran short, arranged so their circles overlapped and no wound lay in shadow. The house above them groaned each time a shell landed in the eastern quarter, and fine dust sifted down through the joists like flour, settling on the blankets, on the bandage rolls, on the faces of the wounded who had been pulled from the rubble of the rue des Tanneurs and laid out in rows that bent around the wine racks because the room would not hold straight lines.

She counted as she worked. It was how she kept her hands from shaking. Eleven civilians with lacerations. Two with fractures she could splint. A boy of perhaps six with a scalp wound that bled magnificently but meant nothing, and an old woman whose silence meant everything, whom Silas checked every quarter hour by pressing two fingers beneath her jaw and waiting for the small stubborn knock of a heart that intended to keep going.

"More water," she said, not looking up.

A bucket appeared at her elbow. She took it without thought, wrung the cloth, went back to cleaning grit out of a carpenter's shoulder where the plaster of his own house had driven itself into the meat like shrapnel's coward cousin.

It was some minutes before she registered that the bucket had not been carried by Petra, or by Madame Fournier from next door, or by any of the neighbors who drifted in and out carrying what they could.

She looked up.

Aldous knelt on the flagstones beside her, sleeves rolled to the elbow, holding the bucket with both hands as though it were something he had been given permission to carry and did not intend to surrender. There was plaster dust in his hair. His shirt was ruined. He looked, for the first time since his return, like neither the charming brother of her childhood nor the desperate man who had confessed by candlelight — he looked like a hospital orderly, which was to say, like a man who had found the one honest job left to him.

"I can tie a tourniquet," he said. "Father made me learn. He said every gentleman should." He stopped, hearing himself, and set the bucket down. "I'll do whatever you tell me."

Silas looked at him a long moment. Above them the house groaned again; somewhere in the dark a child began to cry and was hushed by a woman's low voice. She held out her bloodied hand and pointed at the boy with the scalp wound.

"Hold his head still. Talk to him if you can think of anything true to say. Don't lie to children tonight — they've had enough of that."

* * *

He was clumsy for the first hour. He tied knots too tight, fumbled the safety pins, flinched when a man cried out, and once he stepped back from the table with his face gone grey and had to stand with his hand pressed flat against the stone wall until the wave passed. Silas did not spare him a glance. She had seen that grey face ten thousand times — on boys fresh from England, on veterans on their worst nights, on herself, though never in front of anyone. You let it pass through you or you let it break you. That was the whole of the lesson, and it could not be taught, only survived.

But he came back. That was the thing she noted, filing it away beside the ledger entries in her mind: he finished being sick behind the cask of nothing in particular in the corner, rinsed his mouth, and returned to the boy's side and picked up exactly where he had left off.

By midnight he had learned to fold a triangle bandage in four seconds flat. By one he could hold a limb steady while she probed, without being told, adjusting to her smallest pressure of the fingers, the way good orderlies learned to read a surgeon's hands the way sailors read weather. Neither of them spoke except in the language of the work. *Hold here. More light. Turn him — gently.* He answered in nods and motion. Once, when she needed a second pair of hands for the carpenter's shoulder and said "counter-pressure, both hands, lean your weight into it," he set himself against the man's back and bore down with all his strength, and Silas felt through the wounded man's body the tremor of her brother's arms and thought: *he is strong. When did he get strong? What has he been doing with that strength?*

She knew the answer. She had known it since the letter under seal. The strength had been spent on Verron's east quarter, sold by the pound like any other commodity, and now it was being spent lifting a carpenter off a cellar floor, and both expenditures were equally real, and only one of them could be forgiven, and perhaps not even that one, and none of this changed the fact that right now, at this hour, on these stones, she needed his hands.

So she used them. That was the mercy war allowed, and also its cruelty: it gave you work instead of words, and work kept you alive when words would have killed you both.

* * *

Around two o'clock the bombardment slackened — not ended, never ended, but thinned to a rhythm a body could almost breathe inside — and Silas permitted herself the luxury of standing upright for ninety seconds. She rolled her shoulders. Her spine crackled like a fire. In the corner, Aldous sat on an upturned crate with a sleeping child across his knees, the boy with the magnificent scalp wound, who had decided somewhere in the night that this tall stranger with shaking hands was safe, and had climbed into his lap without asking.

Aldous did not look up at her. He was looking down at the child's bandaged head, and his thumb moved slowly, back and forth, across the boy's shoulder.

"He asked me my name," Aldous said quietly.

"What did you tell him?"

"Aldous." A pause. "I nearly didn't. I nearly gave him a false one. I've gotten — practiced at false ones." His jaw worked. "He said it back wrong. Al-doo. Then he went to sleep."

"Children forgive pronunciation faster than adults forgive anything else," Silas said, and turned back to the old woman, and pressed two fingers to her throat, and found the small stubborn knock still there.

* * *

At three, the door at the top of the stairs opened and their mother descended.

Silas saw it in the way Aldous changed — the slight straightening, the instinctive smoothing of a ruined shirt, the reflexes of the drawing room asserting themselves even here, forty feet below the falling world. Their mother came down the steps one hand on the rail, lamp held high, surveying the cellar the way she had once surveyed dinner tables: assessing, allocating, finding everything slightly less than correct.

"You should be resting," she said to Aldous. "You were out in it all day."

"So were they." He nodded at the stretchers.

"That is not the same thing, darling, and you know it."

Silas kept her eyes on the bandage roll she was rewrapping. She heard her mother cross the floor, heard the pause at the carpenter's stretcher, the soft intake of breath that was as close as Margaret Blackwood ever came to acknowledging suffering she could not manage. Then the voice again, closer, pitched lower, meant only for family.

"There will be a train tomorrow. I have it from the mayor's office. One train, at dusk. I need to speak with you about the arrangements, Silas — about the berths. There are four of us, and I am told—"

"Not here."

"It cannot wait. These things must be settled early, before the crowd—"

"*Not here.*" Silas said it without heat, which somehow made it worse; her mother actually stepped back. Silas lifted her chin toward the rows of wounded, the sleeping child, the old woman whose heart knocked on. "These people hear every word spoken in this room. Tomorrow exists. We will speak of it tomorrow, upstairs, in daylight, if daylight comes. Tonight we work."

Her mother stood very still. For a moment — one strange, unrepeatable moment — Silas thought she saw something move behind the familiar cold face, something that might have been shame or might have been arithmetic, and then it was gone, shuttered away, and Margaret Blackwood said only, "Very well," and ascended the stairs, and the door closed, and the cellar breathed again.

Aldous exhaled slowly. "She'll settle it," he said. "She always settles it. Mother can arrange anything except the truth."

Silas did not answer. She was thinking about the word *four*, and doing arithmetic she did not want to do yet, and pushing it away because there was still a shoulder to dress and a fracture to splint and a night to survive before any list could be made.

* * *

Toward dawn the shells found the church.

They felt it more than heard it — a concussion that came up through the earth into the stones, through their knees, through the teeth, and the hanging lamps swung on their hooks and the shadows wheeled around the cellar like startled birds, and someone screamed, and then everyone was talking at once, and Silas was moving before her mind caught up with her body, calling names, assigning tasks: *you, pressure there; Petra—*

Petra.

The girl materialized out of the dark with a blanket over her shoulders like a small general wearing a cape, wide awake, having slept in snatches against the wine rack as she had done every night since the bombardment began, ready. "Water's boiling," she reported. "I put the last of the soap in it. And Madame Fournier says the rue des Tanneurs is gone — the whole street — and there's people in the schoolyard and nobody's gone for them."

Silas closed her eyes for exactly one second. The schoolyard. Three streets over. Open ground, in the dark, under intermittent fire.

"I'll go," Aldous said.

"No."

"Yes." He was already pulling his coat on, and there was something in his face she had not seen in twenty years — not charm, not desperation. Resolve. "I sold the guns that emptied those streets. You don't get to keep all the penance for yourself, Silas. Some of it's mine to carry, and carrying it looks like this." He nodded at the door. "Tell me what to bring."

She wanted to refuse him. She examined the want honestly, turned it over the way she turned a probe in a wound, and found underneath it not strategy but the old childish pleasure of *no, mine, you may not help* — and she had buried that child long ago in a house that had no place set for her, and she would not dig her up tonight to spite a man trying to be useful.

"The stretcher by the wall. The folding one. And take the lantern with the wire guard — the others break." She tore a page from the back of her mind's notebook and gave him the route: down the lane, along the garden walls, never the open road. "If the shelling starts again heavy, you drop flat and you stay flat, and you come home when it lifts. Do you understand me? *Home.*" The word surprised them both. She did not take it back.

He understood. He went. And Petra watched the door close behind him and then looked up at Silas with an expression too old for eleven years, and said nothing, and helped boil water.

* * *

He came back twice. Each time he carried someone.

The first was a woman with a broken ankle, delivered to the top of the stairs with such careful tenderness that the neighbors who had gone out with him simply stood aside and let him manage it alone. The second was a man, middle-aged, concussed, bleeding from the ear, whom Aldous brought down the steps in a fireman's carry with his teeth clenched and his breath whistling, and when he laid the man out on the last clear patch of floor his legs folded under him and he sat down hard on the stones, gasping, laughing a little at his own weakness, wiping plaster and other men's blood from his face with the back of his wrist.

Silas crouched beside him with the smelling salts for the concussed man and stayed crouching after, a moment longer than the task required.

"You're getting good at this," she said.

"Don't say that." He shook his head. "Don't ever say that. Good is the one thing I don't get to be. Useful, maybe. Useful is as far as the road goes." He looked at his hands, which had stopped shaking sometime in the small hours, and seemed surprised to find them steady. "Do you know what I keep seeing? Every time I lift one of them. I keep seeing the map. My map. The overlay with the grid squares, and the little pencil marks, and I remember thinking — God help me, I remember *thinking* — that the east quarter was mostly warehouses. Paperwork. I moved a line on a page, Silas. Lines on pages don't bleed." His voice cracked on the last word, and he mastered it, barely, the Blackwood mastery that had been bred into all three of them and had saved none of them.

"No," Silas said. "Lines on pages don't bleed. People do. That's why I keep the ledger." She touched her coat, where it hung heavy over her heart. "Names are the opposite of lines on maps. A name can't be moved. It stays where it fell, and it stays who it was."

Aldous looked at her for a long time. The lamps had burned down to amber; the shadows were kind now, softening everything they touched.

"Will you write theirs?" he asked. "The ones from the east quarter? From Verron? I don't — I couldn't recite them all. But some. The ones I know. The families the brokers named, when they were being efficient." He swallowed. "In my hand. If you'd allow it. If it isn't — if I haven't forfeited the right—"

Silas thought of the ledger, of its rule, unwritten but absolute: *true names, freely given, remembered aloud.* She thought of chapter and verse, of who deserved entry and who did not, and found that the ledger had never cared about deserving. Deserving was a drawing-room word. The ledger only knew true.

"Bring me the names," she said. "Spelled correctly. All of them."

Something went out of his shoulders that he had been carrying since the night of his confession — not guilt, which would stay, but the lonelier weight of guilt with nowhere to put itself. He nodded. He got up. He went back to work.

And that was all the forgiveness either of them offered or asked for, and it was enough, and it was not enough, and it would have to last the night.

* * *

Dawn came grey and trembling through the coal chute, laying a pale stripe across the floorboards of the world above. The bombardment had withdrawn to the horizon, grumbling like an animal denied. In the cellar, eleven had become fourteen — two dead, three arrived — and the dead lay at the far end under blankets, and Silas had already written their descriptions in the margin of her memory for the ledger she would open the moment there was light enough to see ink.

Petra climbed into her lap without warning, the way she did, boneless with exhaustion, and Silas wrapped her arms around the girl and rested her chin on the bandaged crown of her head.

"Is he staying?" Petra asked. Her voice was small and sleep-blurred. "Mr. Al-doo. Is he staying with us?"

"For now."

"And the train?" The question came quietly, carefully, the way Petra asked all the questions that mattered — sideways, watching, a child who had learned that adults lied to be kind and had resolved to catch them at it. "Madame Fournier says there's a train today. One train. She says everybody's talking about who gets to go."

Silas felt the arithmetic stir again in her chest, the numbers she had refused all night: four Blackwoods, one legitimate berth, orders forbidding civilians, a girl entitled to nothing on paper because paper had never heard of her. She felt her mother upstairs, awake already, arranging. She felt the ledger against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Everyone's talking about it," Silas said. "Which means somebody has to decide. And I expect—" She stopped. Over Petra's head, across the cellar, she saw her mother descending the stairs with a piece of paper in her hand, unfolded, creased sharp, her face composed into the expression she wore when a bargain was about to be struck.

"—I expect we're about to find out who," Silas finished softly.

Margaret Blackwood reached the bottom step and held out the paper, and her voice, when it came, was smooth as poured cream.

"Silas, darling. We need to talk about the list."
