## 15. The Mother Decides Nothing

Marguerite Blackwood had spent her whole life being told what to do by men who never explained themselves, and she had learned, in the way of women of her generation, to survive it by becoming very good at waiting. Waiting for her husband to finish his sentence. Waiting for Aldous to come home. Waiting, in the early years, for a daughter who had run off to wear a dead boy's name — though she had told herself even then that this was not waiting but disapproval, that she sat by the window every evening out of principle rather than hope.

Now the war had compressed all her waiting into a single afternoon, and the question that had come to sit across from her at the kitchen table was small enough to hold in one hand and heavy enough to crush a house.

*One berth.*

The officer's clerk had said it without looking up from his list, the way men say things they have said ten thousand times: *One berth remaining on the last civilian transport. Priority as marked. Confirm your party.* And then he had waited, pen poised, while Marguerite stood in the rail yard's cold with her family arranged around her like pieces on a board she had never learned to play.

She had said *I must consult my family,* because that was what one said, because one did not decide such things standing up, and because — though she would not have admitted it under torture — she needed someone else to make the choice so that it would not be hers.

So now they were home again, all of them crammed into the kitchen that smelled of dust and yesterday's soup, and Silas had laid the arithmetic out flat on the table between them like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Four of us," Silas said. "One berth."

Silas. She still could not say the name aloud. Her mouth made the shape of *Sibyl* every time — her daughter's true name, the one she had given at the font, the one that had belonged to the girl who used to fall asleep in the pew during sermons — and every time the shape refused to become sound. It was easier not to speak at all.

"Aldous is wounded," Marguerite said. "He cannot walk to the lines. He takes the berth."

It came out quickly, almost before she had decided it, and she was grateful for that — grateful that her mouth had moved ahead of her mind, so that the decision felt less like a decision and more like a fact she had merely reported. That was how she had survived fifty years: by treating her choices as weather.

Silas looked at her. There was something in that look Marguerite could not read — or perhaps could read too well and refused to. "Aldous can't sit upright without help," Silas said quietly. "You've seen him. The stretcher-bearers won't take him unless I carry him to the yard myself, and if I do that, I'm carrying him past three checkpoints where my papers say I'm traveling alone."

"Then you'll manage it," Marguerite said. "You always manage things."

The words left her mouth and she heard them, too late, for exactly what they were. Not praise. An instruction. *You always manage things* — which meant *the managing is yours; it has always been yours; I gave you nothing and called it strength.*

Silas's face did not change. That was the worst of it. Somewhere in the years since the girl had left, her youngest child had learned to receive blows the way a wall receives them, and Marguerite understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that she herself had built that wall. Brick by brick. Every silence at dinner when Sibyl spoke. Every time she had let Edmund laugh at the girl's ambitions and had poured the tea and said nothing. Every evening she had sat by the window and called it principle.

"I am asking," Silas said, and the voice was steady, terribly steady, "who yields the berth. Because there is Petra, Mother. She is eleven. She has no papers that count, no convoy that will take her, no one. If we give her the berth—"

"The child is nothing to us." The words came out sharp and cold, and Marguerite heard her own mother in them, heard three generations of women saying hard things so that the men would not have to. "She is a stray the army picked up. You owe her nothing."

"She owes me everything," Silas said. "Or I owe her. I've stopped keeping accounts."

"You kept accounts your whole life," Marguerite said, and hated herself for it even as she said it, because it was cruel and because it was the only language she had left. "Ledgers. Lists. You were always writing in that little book of yours instead of—" Instead of what? Instead of being a daughter? Instead of sitting where I could see you?

Instead of making me choose you, when choosing you would have cost me everything I knew how to be?

The kitchen went quiet. Aldous lay on the settee in the parlor, feverish, and through the door Marguerite could hear his uneven breathing. Outside, somewhere beyond the hills, the guns had started again — a different sound than yesterday, closer, more insistent, like a creditor at the door.

Silas gathered the papers from the table. Slowly. Squaring their edges with a care that Marguerite recognized as the care of someone keeping their hands busy so they would not shake.

"There's a train at dawn," Silas said. "The clerk needs the name by midnight. Think about who goes." A pause, and then, softer, in a voice Marguerite had never heard from this person in a uniform who had replaced her child: "Think about whether you've ever once thought about it before."

And Silas left — out the back door toward the cellar, where the wounded from the east quarter had been carried all afternoon, because of course Silas would go to the wounded, Silas had always gone where the bleeding was, that had been the whole trouble with her from the beginning, she bled for strangers and would not bleed for us—

Marguerite stood alone in the kitchen.

* * *

She did not sit by the window that night. She sat at the table, where the lamp made its small circle of yellow, and she took out the thing she had not opened in nine years: the drawer beneath the bread box, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the letters.

Not the letters Sibyl had sent — those had been burned, all but one, on Edmund's orders, in the first winter, and she had watched them go into the fire and had put another log on top because Edmund was watching her watch them. No. These were older. These were the letters she had written and never sent, back when the wound was fresh, back when she had still believed that silence was temporary, that pride was a season that would pass, that her daughter would come home when the stubbornness wore thin the way it always had before.

*My darling girl, your father says—*

She read four lines of the first one and put it down.

The trouble — and here it was, the truth she had been circling for nine years, the truth that had followed her down the stairs tonight like a cat she kept feeding — the trouble was that Silas had asked her a real question, and she had discovered, sitting alone with the lamp, that she did not know the answer. Not because the answer was difficult. Because she had never once, in her entire life, made a choice of this shape.

Edmund had chosen her university. Edmund had chosen the house. Edmund had chosen which child's education mattered and which child's spirit would be broken gently, over years, for form's sake — and she had stood beside him pouring tea, and she had told herself that standing beside him was love, that obedience was a kind of faithfulness, that a woman who kept the household running through a man's cruelty was performing heroism of a quiet domestic kind.

And when Sibyl left — when the girl had walked out with a dead boy's name in her pocket and a look on her face that Marguerite still saw sometimes behind her eyelids, a look not of anger but of finished-ness, of a door closing from the inside — when that had happened, Edmund had said *she is dead to this family*, and Marguerite had said nothing, and her silence had been a choice, and she had spent nine years pretending it wasn't.

Now Edmund was two years in his grave and the choosing had finally come to her, alone, unmediated, entirely her own — and her hands lay flat on the table like a dead woman's, and she could not move them.

Because the choice was not really about the berth.

The berth was a train seat. The choice underneath the berth was this: after fifty-eight years, did she know how to choose anyone at all? Could she say a name — one name, out loud, in front of the others — and bear the weight of what it meant about all the names she had never said?

If she chose Aldous, she chose the son who had sold a town full of families to the artillery, though she did not yet fully understand how she knew this — she only knew it the way one knows weather coming, from the set of Silas's jaw, from the way Aldous had not met her eyes since he came home limping. Choosing Aldous was safe. Choosing Aldous was what Edmund would have done. Choosing Aldous required nothing of her but continuation.

If she chose Silas — but Silas wasn't asking to be chosen. That was the unbearable part. Silas had laid out the arithmetic and included everyone but herself, had already quietly subtracted her own name from the column, had already decided to walk, and what she was asking her mother for was not rescue. It was witness. She was asking: *choose the child. Say it. Let me hear you choose someone for once.*

And Marguerite had sat with the lamp burning low and the unsent letters spread before her and had found, in the whole inventory of her heart, no muscle for it.

There was a knock at the kitchen door — two soft taps — and Petra came in.

The girl was carrying a basin of water she had fetched for the cellar, arms pink with cold, hair escaping its braid, and she paused in the doorway with the instinctive caution of a child who has learned to read rooms before entering them. Then she saw the letters, and Marguerite saw the girl see them, and something flickered across the small face — not curiosity. Recognition. As if the child knew exactly what unsent letters were.

"The water for the aid post," Petra said. "Missus. I didn't want to wake you if you were resting."

"I wasn't resting," Marguerite said.

"No," the girl agreed gravely, setting the basin down. "Nobody is."

She turned to go, and at the door she stopped, and she did not turn around, and she said — in the matter-of-fact voice of an eleven-year-old stating arithmetic:

"He'd give you his berth, you know. The captain. If you asked him. He'd give it to any of you. He already gave mine away once — no, that's not right." The girl frowned, corrected herself. "He's *going* to. I can tell. He does sums like that in his head all the time, and the sum always ends the same: everybody else first."

The girl looked back at her then, just once, over her shoulder, and her eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be.

"So somebody has to choose *him*," Petra said. "That's how the sum works. Somebody has to."

And she was gone, out into the dark, back toward the cellar where the wounded lay and the lamps burned all night, leaving Marguerite alone with the letters and the clock ticking on the mantel — Edmund's clock, the one that had ruled every meal of her married life, whose chime had meant *sit down, be silent, the hour belongs to him* —

and Marguerite sat, and listened to it tick, and understood at last that she had spent her whole life inside that clock, and that the train left at dawn, and that the clerk wanted a name by midnight,

and she did not write anything down.

She did not choose.

At half past eleven she climbed the stairs to tell the clerk's runner there was still no decision, and her voice, when she gave the message, was perfectly composed, perfectly correct, the voice of a woman who has kept a household through a war and a tyrant and a disappearance and has never once, not once in fifty-eight years, said the word that was in her heart —

and downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, she could hear Silas moving among the wounded in the cellar, and the sound of a small girl reading names aloud from a ledger in the lamplight, practicing her letters, and Marguerite stood in the dark hallway with her hand on the banister and wept — silently, correctly, without disturbing anyone —

because dawn was coming, and the train with it, and the name had not been given, and she understood now, with terrible finality, that the silence was itself the verdict: that a woman who cannot choose will lose everything twice — once to the world, and once to herself —

and that her daughter had already packed the ledger, and filled the canteen, and checked the straps on the stretcher, and made every arrangement for a journey on foot through shellfire,

which meant, Marguerite realized, standing in the dark, that the choice had never actually been offered to her at all.

Someone had already made it.

And it was not her.
