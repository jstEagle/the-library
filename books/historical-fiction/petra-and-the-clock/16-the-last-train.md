## 16. The Last Train

The rail yard at Verron had been a place of timetables once. Now it was a place of arithmetic.

Silas stood on the platform with the morning fog burning off around her and counted what was left: one train, four cars, a single passenger berth still unclaimed in the last carriage — a compartment meant for officers that Halloway's requisition order had grudgingly converted to civilian use. Around her the town poured itself onto the platform in a thin gray river: old men with their life savings sewn into coat linings, women carrying birdcages with no birds in them, children asleep upright against their mothers' knees as though sleep could be banked like coin against whatever came next.

The bombardment had reached Verron two days early. Everyone knew what that meant. The front was folding like wet paper, and the paper was on fire.

In her left hand Silas held the ledger. In her right, a travel warrant she had forged by lamplight three hours ago, her surgical penmanship turned for once to crime. *P. Blackwood, age eleven, dependent minor, priority evacuation, medical escort required.* She had written it in the same careful capitals the War Office used, and if her hand had shaken on the surname, no clerk would ever know. Blackwood. She had spent twenty years trying to give that name back. Now it was the only thing she had worth giving.

"Silas." Petra tugged her sleeve. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting things you don't want to count."

Silas looked down at her. Petra had washed her face in a rain barrel that morning without being told, and braided her own hair, and buttoned her coat to the throat, because somewhere in the last week the child had understood — the way children understand such things, completely and wordlessly — that she was about to be measured against something. She stood very straight on the platform, chin up, hands folded, a small soldier awaiting inspection by a world that had never issued her a uniform.

"The berth is yours," Silas said. "That's all you need to know."

"You said the family gets first—"

"I said nothing of the kind."

"Mother Blackwood said—" Petra stopped herself, hearing the name she'd used, and flushed. In the cellar aid post, over three nights of holding lamps and tearing bandages, Petra had taken to calling Silas's mother *Mother Blackwood* with a child's blunt diplomacy, and Mrs. Blackwood had accepted the title the way she accepted everything — as an inconvenience borne with dignity.

"My mother," Silas said, "will be on this train because there is a seat for her and she knows it. Aldous—" Her voice did something she hated; it caught. "Aldous has made his arrangements."

*His arrangement* was a stretcher in the freight yard, a shrapnel wound through the thigh he'd taken helping load wounded civilians two nights ago, and a stubbornness so profound it could only have been inherited from the same bloodline that had disowned her. He refused the berth. He had refused it in front of their mother, in front of Halloway, in front of God and the loading sergeant, with the theatrical nobility of a man who had discovered conscience late in life and intended to wear it out in public.

She hadn't argued. That was the truth she carried like a stone. When Aldous waved off the berth, some cold practical thing inside her had done the arithmetic before her heart could object: *one berth, four claimants, and the one who matters most cannot speak for herself.*

Petra was looking up at her with those gray eyes that missed nothing, that had watched a confession through a cellar door and kept it sealed for days like a good field nurse keeping a wound closed until the surgeon was ready.

"Silas," she said carefully, "if I get on the train, will you be behind me?"

"Yes."

"That's not what I asked."

Silas crouched, making herself small enough for honesty. Around them the crowd pressed and murmured; a whistle shrieked somewhere down the line, long and impatient. "I will follow on foot with your Uncle Aldous, who cannot walk well and therefore requires someone to bully him. I am very good at bullying him. It is a family tradition."

"And the ledger comes too?" Petra touched the shape of it under Silas's coat, proprietary, the way she'd been touching it since the night Silas first read names aloud to the wounded. "All of them? Even the ones who didn't make it?"

"*Especially* the ones who didn't make it. A record isn't finished when the person stops breathing. It's finished when someone says the name after."

Petra nodded, solemn as a vicar. Then, in a smaller voice: "Will you write me down again? In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case the train loses me. In case France loses me. In case everybody forgets which parts are true." She lifted her chin. "My name is Petra. Not the ward girl. Not the runner. Petra. You said a name is the one thing nobody can shell."

"I said that, did I?"

"At Calais. First week. You were changing Mr. Ellery's dressings and you thought I wasn't listening."

Silas had learned, these eleven months, that Petra was always listening. She drew the ledger out now, right there on the platform, heedless of the crowd, and opened it against her knee. Pages of names in pencil and ink, some crossed with a single line — dead — and some starred — *survived, last seen, believe alive.* She found the page near the end, the newest entries, and beneath *Blackwood, Aldous — brother, guilty, human* she wrote:

*Blackwood, Petra — daughter. Chosen. All of it true.*

"There," she said, closing the book. "Now you exist twice. Once in the flesh and once in ink, and ink is harder to kill."

Petra looked at the ledger the way other children looked at reliquaries. Then she threw her arms around Silas's neck and held on with the desperate strength of a creature who had already lost one set of arms around her and knew precisely what arms were worth.

* * *

They came for the boarding at half past seven, a sergeant with a list and a voice like gravel sliding downhill. Names were called. Families surged and sorted themselves. The fog had burned away entirely now, and the sun lay flat and merciless on the platform, showing everything: the weeping, the hoarded luggage, the old woman praying aloud with her lips moving faster than any prayer could go.

Mrs. Blackwood stood apart from it, as she had stood apart from everything for sixty years. She wore her best black traveling dress, brushed clean despite the cellar dust, her hat pinned at the correct angle, her gloves on. Even evacuation, even apocalypse, and the woman would arrive presentable. Silas felt the old familiar ache — not hatred, not anymore, just grief wearing hatred's old clothes — and put it down beside the stretcher she was guiding toward the freight car.

"Aldous," their mother said, when she reached the platform's edge. "You will board."

"No, Mother."

"You are wounded. You are *foolish*. There is a berth."

"There is one berth," Aldous said, "and it is spoken for." His leg was bound and splinted under his greatcoat, and he stood leaning on a crate with the sweat of fever at his temples, but his voice carried the strange lightness of a man who had finally set down something heavy. He looked across at Silas. "It's spoken for, isn't it."

"It's spoken for."

Their mother's eyes went from her son to her daughter — to her *son*, the name the world used, the name Silas wore like armor and like apology — and something moved behind them, some calculation forty years in the running. Silas watched her arrive at the truth of it, watched the arithmetic complete itself behind the composed face: one berth, one grandchild-by-no-blood, one daughter-who-was-not-a-daughter, one son bleeding through his bandage.

And then Mrs. Blackwood did the thing Silas would remember at odd moments for the rest of her life, usually at dawn, usually unbidden: she straightened her gloves and said, "Then I shall take my seat, and I shall expect the child to write to me, and she will use proper margins." A pause. Her gaze flicked to Silas, and for half a second — half a second only — the composure trembled, and underneath it was not love exactly but something older and more frightened, some ancestral animal thing that recognized its own blood and did not know how to say so. "Augusta," she said, so quietly the sergeant's list-calling swallowed it.

Not Silas. Not the name she'd built. Augusta — the name she'd been given in a warm room in another century, the name that had been buried with the girl who owned it.

Silas did not correct her. Some names were armor. Some were wounds. This one was both, and there was no time to bleed.

"Margins," Silas said. "I'll instruct her myself."

Her mother boarded. She did not look back. Silas had never expected her to, and yet some childish cellar of the heart had waited, all these years, for the sound of footsteps pausing. The carriage door closed. Through the grimy glass, Mrs. Blackwood's profile sat rigid above the seatback, facing forward, always forward, toward whatever came next that she could endure correctly.

* * *

Loading Petra took longer than loading anyone else, because Petra fought it the way small fierce things fight — with dignity first and then without it.

"I can carry stretchers," she said. "I've been carrying stretchers for months. Ask anyone. Ask Corporal Finch, ask—"

"The war is over for you," Silas said, lifting her bodily onto the step of the last carriage. "That's an order. My first lawful order to you, and you will obey it, because unlike everyone else in this army you actually do what I say."

"Because you say things worth doing!" Petra twisted in her grip, gripping her lapels. "Silas. *Silas.* Listen. Come with me. Squeeze into the luggage rack, they never check the racks, I've seen—"

"And when they do check?"

"Then you'll be arrested and I'll tell them you're my brother and they can't arrest brothers, it's not—"

"Petra."

"—it's not *legal*, they'd have to—"

"*Petra.*"

The name landed like a hand laid flat on a panicked chest. The girl stopped. On the platform the crowd was thinning, the whistle building toward its final scream, and down the line German shells were walking closer through the morning — Silas could feel them now through the rails, faint tremors, like a pulse in a wrist.

"When the train stops," Silas said, "you will get off, and you will find the Calais hospital, and you will ask for Matron Vance, and you will show her this." She pressed the folded page into Petra's hand — not the whole ledger, she couldn't give up the whole ledger, but a copy, three pages in her tightest script, every surviving name transcribed. "Tell her Silas sent you. She'll feed you, house you, and let you pester the convalescents until they adore you. And when this war finally does what wars do — ends — I will walk through those doors, and you may scold me for as long as you like."

Petra stared at the pages. Then at her. "Promise?"

"I don't promise lightly."

"I know." The girl's chin wobbled, and she mastered it, visibly, the way Silas had seen her master it over her parents' grave. "That's why I'm asking."

Silas pulled her close, one arm, brief and hard, pressing the small skull against her shoulder the way she'd press a dressing onto a wound — firm, sealing, buying time. "Petra Blackwood," she said into her hair. "Daughter. Chosen. All of it true. Now *go*."

The sergeant slammed the door. The whistle screamed. And through the window, as the train lurched and began to crawl out of the yard, Silas saw Petra pressed to the glass with her palm flat against it, mouth moving — and Silas read the words rather than heard them, because the engine drowned everything, but she knew them anyway, knew them the way you know your own heartbeat.

*Come back. Come back. Come back.*

She raised her hand and held it raised until the carriage slid past the water tower, past the wrecked signal box, past the last of the platform, and the train took her daughter south and west toward the sea while Silas stood in the smoke with a ledger against her ribs and a wounded brother on a stretcher and ninety miles of shelled country between her and everything she had promised.

* * *

Aldous was watching her from the crate. He'd seen all of it. Of course he had; he'd always had their mother's talent for observing what he could not bear to engage with.

"You gave her the name," he said.

"She earned it."

"You gave her *our* name." Something moved through his fevered face — not resentment, not quite. Wonder, maybe. Or envy wearing wonder's coat. "Twenty years you wouldn't touch it. You'd sooner have died under a false one than sign yourself Blackwood."

Silis turned to check the freight car straps, buying a moment, then another, then gave up lying to herself and answered. "It was poisoned for me, Aldous. Every memory attached to it. And then a girl crawled out of rubble in Verron's east quarter with no name anyone could find, and I looked at her and thought — here is a name that hasn't hurt anybody yet. Here is a name going spare." She tightened the strap. "So I gave it to her. Turns out that's what it wanted all along. To be handed to someone who'd use it kindly."

A shell landed somewhere east of the yards, close enough to sift dust from the freight car roof. Neither of them flinched. They were past flinching, both of them, in the particular way of people who had decided the next hour mattered more than the next minute.

"The road out," Aldous said. "You're still set on it. Ninety miles, my leg, no papers that would survive a sergeant's squint, and the whole salient coming down on our heads."

"Yes."

"They'll hang you if they catch you. Desertion, forgery, abduction of military property—" he nodded at the direction the train had gone, and Silas realized he meant Petra, *military property*, and chose not to strike him solely because he was bleeding, "—and impersonation, if anyone looks closely enough at Silas Blackwood to wonder why the records call him a man."

"Then we'd best not be caught."

He laughed — a short, cracked, astonished laugh, the laugh of a man discovering that his sister was the bravest person he'd ever known and that he had spent thirty years failing to notice. "God help me," he said. "You're actually going to walk us out of this."

"Not us." Silas shouldered the pack, checked the ledger one last time against her ribs, and looked east, where the shells were walking, patient as a clock. "I'm going to walk *you* out of this. You owe a debt, brother, and I intend to collect it in miles."

She took the handles of his stretcher and began, through the smoke and the falling dust and the screaming of a world coming apart, to haul him toward the Allied lines — and toward the checkpoint on the Cambrai road where Major Halloway was already waiting, though neither of them knew it yet.
