## 17. The Road Out on Foot

The last thing Verron gave me was its dust.

It hung behind us in the grey morning light where the town had been — or where it thought it had been, because half of it was smoke now and the other half was learning to be. I did not look back. Looking back is a luxury for people who are not carrying a grown man on their shoulders.

Aldous weighed more than he should have. Not from flesh — there was less of him than I remembered from the boy who used to race me down the orchard rows — but from the way a wounded man goes loose and heavy all at once, trusting you entirely whether he means to or not. His arm was bound tight against my collar. My hands were locked under his knees. The shell fragment had gone through his thigh an hour before we left the cellar, and I had done what I could with forceps, iodine, and the particular fury of a nurse whose patient refuses to be sensible.

"Put me down," he said, for the fourth time.

"No."

"I can walk."

"You can limp," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is called bleeding to death in a beet field."

He subsided. Somewhere east of us, the guns had found their rhythm again — that long, patient conversation between batteries that sounds almost conversational at a distance, until you remember every sentence is a house. The road north was empty of everything except crows and the things crows were interested in. I kept us off it anyway, cutting through the stubble fields where the soil came up black and soft and swallowed our footsteps.

The ledger rode against my ribs inside my coat, wrapped in oilcloth. It had ridden there through two years of war, but today it felt different. Heavier. As if the names inside had shifted in the night and settled against my heart like stones finding their place in a wall.

*Petra Blackwood,* the new page said, in my own careful hand. Forged papers, forged surname, one berth on a train already gone west with my mother aboard and my sister-in-law's complaints aboard and whatever remained of the family fortune sewn into a carpet bag. And Aldous, who should have been on that train, who had put a stretcher onto it instead — some other man's stretcher, some stranger's son — and then collapsed in the rail yard with his leg pouring red.

So now it was the two of us, and eleven miles of shelled country between Verron and the Allied picket lines, and my brother's blood soaking through my bandages at roughly the rate of my patience.

* * *

We stopped at noon in the ruins of a farmhouse that had lost its argument with an artillery piece sometime the previous autumn. One wall still stood. It gave the illusion of shelter, which is sometimes enough.

I laid him down on a door someone had dragged outside months ago and never claimed. His face was grey as the sky. I cut the bandage back and looked, the way I have looked at ten thousand wounds, and told myself the truth: it was ugly, but it was not killing him yet. Infection would do that work later if the fever started. We had perhaps two days. Perhaps three.

"Water," he said.

I gave him the canteen and watched him drink like a man remembering how. Then I rewrapped the leg, and he watched me do it, and neither of us spoke, because there are conversations that happen entirely in the hands — mine competent, his still, both of them agreeing on something our mouths hadn't found words for yet.

It was Aldous who broke first. He always did. Even as boys, he could not stand a silence longer than a hymn verse.

"You could have taken the berth yourself," he said. "You know that. Halloway would have looked the other way. You've earned —"

"Don't." I tied off the bandage harder than necessary. "Don't tell me what I've earned. You don't get a vote anymore."

"She's a child, Silas. A French orphan. You owe her nothing on paper —"

"On paper." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You want to talk about paper? There's a ledger in my coat with four hundred names in it. Four hundred men who were too frightened to die under their real names, so they gave them to me instead. Do you know why they were frightened? Because somewhere behind the lines there was a family — a mother, a father, a brother — who had decided who they were allowed to be, and they couldn't bear to die as that person's idea of them." I leaned close. "You sold a town, Aldous. You sold the quarter where a girl's parents died in their own kitchen. And Petra — Petra, who has nothing, no name, no paper, no claim on anyone — she is the only member of this family who has never once lied to me."

He flinched. Good. Let him flinch.

"I didn't know," he said quietly. "What the coordinates were for. I knew it was artillery registration. I told myself it was — everyone tells themselves something. That's the whole trade, Silas. Everyone tells themselves something, and then a town burns, and the telling-themselves keeps them warm at night."

"And does it?"

He looked at his hands. They were shaking, and not only from blood loss. "No," he said. "It doesn't. It hasn't since the cellar. Since I watched you work." He swallowed. "You were magnificent in there. You were always going to be magnificent at something. I just never thought —" He stopped.

"Never thought what?"

"That it would be saving people. While I was selling them." His voice cracked down the middle like old plaster. "Mother named me Aldous after the bishop. She said I'd be the one who mattered. And I did matter, didn't I. Look how much I mattered."

For a moment — one treacherous, unguarded moment — I saw the boy under the man. The one who had held my hand when the fever took our governess, who had cried easier than I did, who had loved me before love became a thing our house rationed. I wanted to hit him and hold him in the same motion, and I did neither. I sat back on my heels and said the truest thing I had:

"The berth wasn't charity, Aldous. I gave it to Petra because she asked for nothing. I'm carrying you because you're my brother. Those are different equations. Don't confuse them."

He nodded slowly. "Understood," he said. And then, because he was still, underneath everything, my brother: "Your bandage technique has improved. Mother would say it's unladylike."

"Mother," I said, "is on a train."

* * *

We walked — or rather, I carried and he endured — through the afternoon.

The land between Verron and the lines was a country that had forgotten it was ever farmland. Craters had filled with rain and become ponds; ponds had become graveyards for carts and, once, horribly, for a horse that had been standing there so long the water had gone clear around it. Shell-holes made a kind of stepping-stone geography. I learned to read the guns the way Petra had taught me to read cards — by their temper. When the distant batteries went quiet, we moved. When they woke, we lay flat in the beet rows and let the earth argue above us.

Aldous talked, in the way of men who are afraid that if they stop talking they will start screaming. He talked about the orchard. About the clock in the hall that Father had wound every Sunday and nobody had wound since. About our father's funeral, which I had not been permitted to attend, and which he described to me now in detail — the vicar's cough, the wreaths, the way Mother had stood like a portrait of herself.

"He asked for you," Aldous said suddenly. "At the end. Father. He said —" He hesitated. "He said, 'Tell her the ledgers were wrong.' I didn't understand it then. I thought he was raving."

I stopped walking. Or rather, my legs stopped without consulting me, and Aldous's weight swung and I caught us both against a fence post that had survived everything except meaning anything anymore.

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd tell you. And then I didn't, because telling you meant admitting you existed, and admitting you existed meant explaining to Mother why I'd written to you at all, and —" He made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You see the shape of it. The whole architecture of cowardice. One lie holding up the next one holding up the roof."

*Tell her the ledgers were wrong.*

Our father had kept accounts his whole life. Accounts of harvests, of debts, of daughters. Somewhere in the last pages of his life, with the arithmetic finally finished, he had audited himself and found the column short.

I adjusted my grip on my brother's legs and walked on, and if my eyes were wet, the wind took the evidence, and neither of us mentioned it. That, too, was a family inheritance — the agreement never to mention the weather of each other's faces.

* * *

The river nearly killed us at dusk.

It shouldn't have. On the map it was a stream, a blue pencil-line called the Serein by people who had never seen it in November after three days of rain. But maps are written in dry weather, and the Serein had come up over its banks and spread itself across the low ground in a brown, muscular flood, dragging fence rails and a drowned gate and something white that I chose not to identify.

"We go around," Aldous said.

"There's no around. The bridge is a mile east and there's a picket on it, and the picket will ask questions your papers can't answer, because your papers are ash in a stove in Verron." I had burned them that morning, along with the letter under seal. What remained of our identity fit in a coat pocket: a ledger, a forged evacuation chit, and a nurse's instinct for triage.

"So we swim."

"You can't swim. You can barely sit up."

"Then leave me on the bank," he said, "and come back for me."

I looked at him. He looked back, and there was something new in his face — not charm, not the broker's smile he'd worn into the cellar like a uniform. Just a man asking to be worth the trouble.

"No," I said. "Nobody gets left on a bank. That's not how this family works anymore. We're making up new rules as we go, and rule one is: nobody gets left."

The ford was fifty yards upstream where the flood spread shallow over a gravel bar — shallow being a relative term, meaning waist-deep and moving like a crowd leaving a burning building. I bound his arm tighter around my neck, took the ledger out of my coat and shoved it inside his shirt against his chest, where it would stay dry longest.

"Hold that," I said. "Hold it like it's the only thing in the world. Because right now it is."

"What's in it?"

"Everyone," I said. "Everyone who was too afraid to say their own name. Hold it."

Then I stepped into the water.

The cold went through me like a bayonet. The current took my legs sideways on the third step and I leaned into it, angling upstream the way you fight any current, walking a diagonal that felt like walking backward. Aldous's weight shifted and I heard him gasp against my ear, and then — God help me — I heard him laugh.

"This is insane," he shouted over the water. "This is completely — you're the most stubborn —"

"Save your breath!"

"— I sold a town and my sister is carrying me across a river like a sack of —"

His foot found a hole. I felt it happen through his whole body, the sudden lurch, and then his arm slipped from my collar and the river had him.

I have pulled drowning men out of the Ypres canals with shells falling. I have never moved faster than I moved in that brown water. I got a fist into his collar, turned him face-up — the first law, face-up, always face-up — and the current took us both then, slamming us down the flood line toward a fence that had become a weir of rails and debris. I got one hand on a post. The water tried to peel me off it the way you'd peel a label. My shoulder screamed. Aldous was coughing, alive, clawing at my coat, and the ledger — I felt it against his chest, still there, still held, because even drowning he had held it.

Somehow — I will never reconstruct it honestly, and I have decided not to try — I got us both onto the gravel bar on the far side, where we lay side by side in the shallows like two landed fish, breathing in great tearing gulps while the Serein went on about its business without us.

After a long time, Aldous said, wheezing: "The ledger. Is it —"

I fumbled inside his shirt. Oilcloth. Soaked at the corners, but sealed. I unwrapped it there in the mud, by the last violet light, and checked the pages, and they were dry, every name, four hundred names plus one, *Petra Blackwood*, safe as scripture.

"Dry," I said.

He stared at the sky. "Good," he said. "Good. Because I was thinking — if we drown, at least the names float downstream toward home."

"They're not going anywhere," I said. "Neither are we. Up. We have four miles left and you've used up all my tenderness for the day."

He laughed — really laughed, a broken, soaked, astonished sound — and let me haul him upright, and we went on.

* * *

We came down off the ridge in full dark, and there they were: the picket fires of the Allied lines, small and orange and impossibly domestic, like windows in a house where someone is waiting up.

Four miles had taken us three hours. Aldous had stopped talking a mile back, which frightened me more than the bleeding did, and I had answered his silences with fragments of the ledger, reading names aloud as we walked — *Whitlock, Amos J.; Ferreira, Tomas; Okafor, Daniel* — giving him my voice to walk inside of. He had understood. He had kept his feet by listening, the way soldiers keep step to a drum.

Now, at the edge of the dark, I set him down against a shattered tree and went through the ritual in my head: the challenge, the password Halloway had wired ahead, the story we would tell. Wounded officer, civilian nurse, evacuated from Verron, papers destroyed in the bombardment. Every word of it true except the ones that mattered, which is the best kind of lie.

Aldous caught my sleeve as I bent to lift him again.

"Silas." His voice was thin. "When they ask who I am — what name do I give?"

I looked at him. My brother, who had spent his life trading in names — buying them, selling them, hiding behind his own. Asking me, now, which name to wear.

"The true one," I said. "Aldous Blackwood. Say it plainly and let it mean what it means. We'll carry the rest together."

Ahead of us, out of the darkness, a lantern swung up and a voice called the challenge — and the voice, when it came closer, was one I knew. Measured. Unhurried. The voice of a man who had spent a career deciding exactly how much mercy the regulations could survive.

Major Halloway stepped into the lantern light, looked at the two of us — the bleeding brother, the soaked nurse, the oilcloth bundle pressed to a traitor's chest — and said, very quietly:

"Well. Let's see what you've brought me."
