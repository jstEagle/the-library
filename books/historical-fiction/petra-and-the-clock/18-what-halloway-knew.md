## 18. What Halloway Knew

The lantern light did not waver. That was the first thing I noticed about Major Edmund Halloway, and the last thing I would ever forget: that in a war where everything trembled — the ground, the sky, the men, their names — his hand holding the lantern was perfectly still.

He had come out alone. No escort, no sentries trailing behind him into the mud. A checkpoint this far forward should have had four men and a Maxim gun; it had a folding table, two crates, a brazier guttering against the wind, and one major of fifty-three years standing in the rain with his greatcoat buttoned to the chin as though he were presiding over a parade rather than the edge of a collapsing front.

"Major," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. Beside me, Aldous swayed on his feet, his weight pulling at my shoulder, blood from his thigh soaking through the improvised bandage in slow dark pulses. The oilcloth bundle pressed between us held the ledger, and the forged papers, and everything I had decided to be.

"Captain Blackwood." He said it evenly, without inflection, and I felt Aldous stiffen beside me at the surname. Halloway's eyes moved from my face to Aldous's leg, cataloguing the wound with the speed of long habit. "You're bleeding through. Sit him down."

"There's no chair, sir."

"There's a crate." He nodded toward it. "There has always been a crate. That is what crates are for." And there was something almost gentle in the dryness of it, the way a man might speak to a frightened animal — or to a subordinate he had decided, months ago, never to frighten again.

I lowered Aldous onto the crate. He went down hard, teeth clenched, and Halloway watched without moving to help, which I understood: help offered too quickly becomes charity, and charity is a debt. This man did not deal in debts. He dealt in decisions.

"You're eight miles from Verron," Halloway said. "Off leave. Off orders. Out of uniform regulation in three particulars I could name if I were feeling pedantic." He set the lantern on the table. Its light threw our shadows huge against the hedge behind us, three figures fused into one. "And you are carrying a wounded civilian whose name appears on a list I have been instructed to memorize."

The rain ticked against the oilcloth. Somewhere east of us, the guns had paused — that awful held breath they took sometimes, like a man drawing back his fist.

"I can explain, sir—"

"No," Halloway said. "You can't. Not in any language that would survive being written down. Which is why we aren't going to write anything down." He held out his hand. "The papers, Captain."

I did not move. Behind us the road ran black toward the lines; ahead, past the checkpoint barrier, lay the flooded country and the river and Calais somewhere beyond all of it. One word from Halloway and every path closed at once.

"Sir," I said carefully, "those papers are—"

"They are signed by a Lieutenant S. Blackwood," Halloway said, "in a hand I have watched sign casualty reports for fourteen months. They name the bearer as Aldous Blackwood, civilian, priority evacuation, authorized by an order number that does not exist because I invented its prefix myself last spring when I needed a fiction to move a widow out of Armentières." He looked at me, and in the lantern light his eyes were tired in the way stone is tired — not worn by use but simply old, patient, immovable. "I know exactly what those papers are, Captain. I am asking you to show them to me anyway. There is a difference between surrendering contraband and showing your work."

So I gave them to him. My fingers were stiff with cold and river water, and the packet came apart easily — too easily, as if it had already surrendered. He unfolded the pages under the lantern and read them the way he read everything: once, completely, without hurrying or slowing, a man who had learned that documents do not change when you stare at them harder.

Aldous spoke first. Of course he did; charm was the only muscle he'd ever kept trained.

"Major Halloway. I don't suppose—"

"Don't," Halloway said, without looking up. It was not cruel. It was the voice of a man closing a door on a room he did not wish to enter. "I have read your file, Mr. Blackwood. Or rather, I have read the version of your file that will exist after tomorrow, which is shorter, and which ends with the words *presumed dead, Verron sector*. Your family's name is being erased from the record as we speak — efficiently, quietly, above my pay grade and several grades above the men who arranged it. You are not a person tonight, Mr. Blackwood. You are an omission." He folded the papers along their original creases. "It is a peculiar freedom, being an omission. Very few people get to experience it."

"And her?" Aldous said. "She's an omission too, isn't she? If anyone reads those close enough—"

"Aldous." I said it low, a warning, but Halloway raised one hand and stopped me, and it was not the gesture of a superior officer. It was the gesture of a man who wanted to hear the answer himself.

"He's right," Halloway said. "That is the arithmetic of it. If these papers pass through curious hands — if some clerk at the rail office compares the signature to the casualty rolls, if someone asks why a serving officer is walking a wounded traitor across open country — then the inquiry does not stop at the forgery. Forgeries are investigated backward, Captain. Signature to author. Author to identity. Identity to—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—to the ledger you keep. Yes, I know about the ledger. I have known since Passchendaele, when I watched you write in it by candlelight and understood that no dressing-station inventory requires that much tenderness."

The rain came harder. I stood very still. Fourteen months I had believed the secret was mine alone — shared, perhaps, with the night, with the candle, with God in whatever form He takes at the front. To learn it had been seen, witnessed, carried silently by this man through a year of inspections and reports and moments when a single word from him would have ended everything —

"Why?" I asked. My voice cracked on it. "Why protect me?"

Halloway set the papers down on the table. He straightened them so their edges aligned, a small precise act, the habit of a man who kept his soul the way he kept his desk.

"Because I have spent thirty years inside an institution," he said, "and I have watched it do two things without ceasing. It spends men. And it files them." He looked up. "It filed you incorrectly, Captain. On purpose, by your own courage, at cost I cannot imagine and will not ask you to describe. And the institution, if it learns the truth, will not admire the courage. It will correct the file. It will unmake you with a stroke of a pen and call it accuracy." His jaw tightened. "I have spent my career watching that pen fall. I have signed with it myself more times than I can count. I told myself each time that the machine required it — that mercy given piecemeal is chaos, that rules are the only wall between ten thousand frightened people and ruin." He was quiet a moment. The brazier spat. "All true. All true, and insufficient. Because tonight I am looking at a woman who forged government documents to carry a dying man she had every reason to hate across a river in shellfire, and the regulations have nothing — *nothing* — to say about her. The regulations were written by men who never imagined anyone would act like this."

* * *

He picked up the papers then. Held them at arm's length, considering, as though weighing them on some scale only he could see.

"I want you to understand the price," he said. "Not to dissuade you. To witness it. Someone should witness it, and it may as well be me." He turned the pages in the light. "If I burn these, there is no record that Aldous Blackwood passed this checkpoint. No record that Captain Blackwood left her post. When the audit comes — and it will come; the erasure of the Verron name guarantees an audit — I will have no papers to produce. No order, no authorization, no fiction to hide behind. I will stand in front of men whose entire profession is the discovery of omissions, and I will have nothing except the truth, which is the one thing that institution cannot process." He smiled faintly, without humor. "They will take my command. Possibly my commission. Almost certainly the pension, and my wife has opinions about the pension that could stop artillery."

"Then don't," I said. The words came out before I could weigh them. "Sir — don't. Petra's on that train. She's safe. My mother is on it. The berth was used, the child is evacuated, the letter of the orders was honored as far as it could be honored. Let me take him across the country and vanish, and you keep your hands clean. You've kept them clean for me for fourteen months. You don't owe me—"

"That," Halloway said, "is precisely what I have been telling myself since Passchendaele." He said it mildly, but underneath the mildness was something that had been grinding for a long time, like water under ice. "That I don't owe you. That protection freely given costs the giver nothing. That a senior officer shielding a subordinate is merely good administration." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what I knew, Captain? Let me tell you what I knew, since you ask. I knew your ledger existed. I knew the girl in your ward had no papers and no name on any roll, and I let her stay, because the ward functioned better with her in it and because no one asked me directly. I knew your brother's name appeared on the intelligence summaries connected to Verron, and I said nothing to you, because saying something would have meant admitting I read things I pretend not to read. I knew your mother boarded that train under a forged surname and I approved the manifest without reading it closely, which is the oldest cowardice in the army — plausible deniability, the sin of the half-closed eye." His voice dropped. "I have been spending your secrets for a year, Captain, spending them a coin at a time, telling myself the account was mine to manage. Tonight is simply the settlement. The whole sum, paid at once. It was always going to come due. Better here, in the rain, than in some whitewashed room with men who enjoy their work."

Aldous shifted on the crate. When he spoke, all the charm had gone out of his voice, and what remained was rawer and younger and, for the first time in my memory of him, honest.

"She doesn't deserve what it costs you," Aldous said. "I do. Burn mine if you have to burn anything. Give her the clean record. She's the only one of us who's earned—"

"Oh, be quiet," Halloway said, almost wearily. "You sound like a court-martial. Everyone wants to allocate the guilt to the correct party, as though guilt were rations." He looked at Aldous for a long moment, and something in his face softened by a degree — not forgiveness; I don't believe Halloway forgave easily, if at all — but recognition. "Your sister pulled you eight miles through a flooded country with a bullet in your leg. Whatever else you are, Mr. Blackwood, you are now evidence of something the war has produced very little of. I find I'm unwilling to destroy the evidence."

* * *

He struck the match himself.

I remember thinking it strange that my heart did not pound — that I stood in the rain beside a guttering brazier watching a major of the British Army hold my forged papers to a flame, and felt not terror but a kind of terrible calm, the way one feels at a funeral for someone whose suffering has finally ended.

The paper caught reluctantly — damp at the edges, the flame having to work for it, crawling up the page past the order number that didn't exist, past the signature in my own careful hand, past the surname we had chosen for ourselves. Halloway held it until the fire reached his gloves, then dropped the last burning corner into the brazier, where it curled among the coals and went out.

"There," he said quietly. "No record. No omission to discover. As far as the army is concerned, Captain Silas Blackwood is at his post, exemplary, unwounded, and uninspected." He stripped off one glove finger by finger. "And I am a man who burned official documents at a forward checkpoint during an active engagement, which, when they ask me — and they will ask me — I shall answer with the only defense available to an officer of my years." He drew himself up slightly, and to my astonishment there was something like pleasure in it, something almost young. "I shall say I made a judgment call. Thirty years of service buys you perhaps three judgment calls. I hoarded the first two. I am spending this one on you."

"Sir," I began, "if they take your commission—"

"They will not take my commission," Halloway said. "They will take my *command*, which is different, and worse, and survivable. Command is what a man does. Commission is what a man is. They can reassign what I do." He picked up the lantern again. "What I am cannot be filed, Captain. I learned that watching you."

Behind him the eastern sky had begun, very faintly, to gray — not dawn yet, but the memory of dawn, the promise the sky makes each night and so often fails to keep. Somewhere far off, a train whistled, long and low, carrying toward the coast a girl with forged papers in a coat too big for her and a grandmother who had never chosen anything in her life. I thought of Petra's hands around the ledger I'd pressed on her at the platform, and my throat closed, and I was grateful for the darkness.

"The river crossing is ahead," Halloway said, all business again, turning toward the barrier. "The pontoon bridge at Marest is gone — went up Tuesday. But there's a weir two kilometers south, and the millrace is passable if the water hasn't risen since yesterday, which it may well have done." He looked at Aldous's leg and frowned. "He'll never swim it."

"We'll cross together," I said. "We always seem to."

"Hm." Halloway studied us both in the lantern light a moment longer — the bleeding brother, the soaked nurse, the ashes cooling in the brazier — and then he did something I had never seen him do in fourteen months of inspections and silence: he extended his hand. Not to me. To Aldous.

Aldous stared at it. Then he took it, wincing as he rose from the crate.

"Mr. Blackwood," Halloway said. "I knew what you did at Verron before your sister did. I have known for six weeks. I could have told her. I chose not to, because I judged she had enough weight to carry." His grip tightened, and Aldous's jaw tightened with it. "Understand that I am shaking your hand not because you are forgiven, but because you are *carried*. There is a difference. Learn it, if you live long enough."

"Yes, sir," Aldous whispered.

Halloway released him, retrieved a canvas satchel from behind the crates, and pushed it into my arms — morphine, sulfanilamide, fresh dressings, a flask of brandy.

"Field kit. Unofficial. Unrecorded." He almost smiled. "Everything tonight is unofficial. That is rather the point." He lifted the lantern high so its light fell full on my face, and for a moment the measured voice faltered, just barely, like the flame in the wind. "Captain Blackwood. When the bells ring — and they will ring soon, whatever the papers say — I hope someone reads you your true name aloud, in a place where it is safe to hear it."

Then he stepped back from the barrier, swung it wide, and stood aside — one man, alone at his post, holding the door of the institution open with his body while the rain fell on both our heads.

"Go," he said. "South, to the weir. And Captain—" I turned at the edge of the lantern light. He was already straightening the papers on his table, aligning their edges, restoring order to the small kingdom he was about to lose. "Tell the child the clock in my office runs fast. Tell her to wind hers slow. Some of us measure time wrong on purpose — it's how we make room."

I walked out of the light with my brother leaning on my shoulder and the ledger heavy against my ribs, and behind us the lantern shrank to a spark, then a memory, and the road south opened black and endless and ours.
