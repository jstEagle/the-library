## 19. Reunion at Calais

The clock over the matron's desk in the Calais hospital had stopped at twenty past four, and no one seemed inclined to fix it. Petra decided this was the finest quality the clock possessed. A stopped clock could not hurry anyone. It hung there on its nail like a promise that the afternoon would last as long as she needed it to.

She had been in Calais for six days.

The orphanage convoy had left on the second day — a line of grey lorries rumbling out of the yard while an officer with a clipboard read names off his list and children climbed aboard clutching bundles they had not been allowed to pack themselves. Petra's name had been on that list. *Petra Vasseur, age eleven, unclaimed minor, destination: orphanage at Rouen.* She had heard the officer say it twice, once to himself and once to a sergeant, and both times her name had sounded like a parcel being weighed.

So she had hidden in the linen cupboard behind Ward C, sitting very still among the folded sheets, breathing through her mouth so the smell of soap wouldn't make her sneeze. She had gotten good at hiding in two months of war. Hiding in rubble, hiding in ditches, hiding behind Silas's coat when the shells came close. The linen cupboard was easy by comparison. It was warm, and nothing in it was trying to kill her.

When the lorries had gone, when the engines faded down the boulevard and the hospital settled back into its long groan of groaning men, she had come out, brushed the lint off her dress, and presented herself to the matron with her chin up and her best invented story ready.

"My aunt is coming for me," Petra said. "She's a nurse. She's at the front. She said if ever I got sent to Calais I was to wait here, because she'd come."

The matron — a broad woman named Madame Fournier with forearms like a laundress and eyes that missed nothing — had looked at Petra for a long moment. Petra had held the look. This too she had learned from Silas: how to hold someone's gaze without blinking, how to let silence do the arguing for you.

"Your aunt," Madame Fournier had said slowly. "A nurse at the front."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And her name?"

And here Petra had made the decision that had kept her warm every night since. "Blackwood," she had said. "Petra Blackwood. She gave me the name herself. It's on my papers."

Because it *was* on her papers. Folded in the pocket of her dress, softened at the creases from handling, was the evacuation document Silas had pressed into her hand on the platform at Verron — forged in a dressing-station tent in a careful clerk's hand, naming her *P. Blackwood, dependent, priority berth.* Silas had written the name out letter by letter while the shells walked closer across the town, and she had said, *You hold this and you don't let go of it, and you don't let go of me either, do you hear?*

Petra had heard. She was eleven years old and she had buried her parents under the east quarter's rubble, and she knew precisely what she did and did not intend to let go of.

Madame Fournier had looked at her another long moment, then jerked her thumb toward the ward. "If your aunt doesn't come within the week, I'm putting you on the next convoy myself. Understood?"

"Yes, ma'am," Petra had said, and meant: *she will come.*

* * *

The days arranged themselves into a shape Petra recognized from the front — work filling the hours where fear would otherwise pool. Madame Fournier, having decided to keep her rather than turn her over to the Red Cross bureau, put her to use the way the men at the dressing station had: carrying bedpans, folding bandages, running messages between wards. The wounded soldiers called her *la petite infirmière* and taught her bits of English and one bit of Flemish that made the orderlies laugh. An American with a bandaged jaw taught her a card game called gin rummy using half a deck, and she beat him three times running before he accused her of counting cards, which she was.

But every hour, between tasks, she found her way back to the entrance hall, to the bench beneath the stopped clock, and sat where she could see the doors.

That was where she was on the sixth day, knees drawn up, watching the light shift across the tiled floor, when the ambulances began arriving.

She knew the sound of them before she saw them — the particular grinding gear-change on the hill outside, the way the engines labored under weight. Convoy after convoy had come through Calais in the six days she'd been there, and each time her heart performed the same treacherous arithmetic: *maybe, maybe, maybe* — and each time the stretchers carried strangers, men with faces she didn't know, and she learned again to fold the hope flat and small and put it away.

But this time, as the first ambulance backed toward the receiving doors and the orderlies pulled the canvas, Petra saw a figure step down from the cab — a tall figure in a mud-caked greatcoat, hair cropped short, cap pushed back, moving with that deliberate stride Petra could have picked out of a crowd of thousands, out of a crowd of millions, because she had spent two months learning it the way you learn a prayer.

Silas.

Petra was off the bench before she had decided to stand. Her legs did it for her, and then her whole body did it, and she was crossing the tiles at a dead run, weaving between stretcher-bearers, ignoring the matron's startled cry — "*Jeune fille!*—" — and then Silas turned at the sound of the shout and Petra watched the exhaustion fall off her face like a dropped coat.

"*Petra—*"

She hit her at full speed. Silas caught her, staggered, caught her again, arms closing around her hard enough to lift her clean off the floor, and Petra wrapped her arms around her neck and buried her face against the collar of the filthy greatcoat and cried the way she hadn't let herself cry since Verron — loud, ugly, hiccupping sobs that shook her whole ribs.

"You're supposed," Silas managed, her voice breaking apart in the middle, "to be at an orphanage in Rouen."

"I refused," Petra said into her shoulder.

"You *refused.*"

"I hid in the linen cupboard." She pulled back far enough to see Silas's face, and what she saw nearly started the crying all over again — the hollows under the eyes, the grime worked deep into the lines of her neck, the rawness of lips cracked by wind and river water. But underneath it, unmistakable, was the look Petra had seen only a handful of times and hoarded every one: the look Silas wore only when the mask slipped, the look that was not Nurse Blackwood at all but simply *hers.*

"You crossed a river," Petra said. "Halloway told us about a river."

"Half a river. The other half we walked." Silas set her down but kept a grip on her shoulders, looking her over with clinical thoroughness — checking her arms, her face, turning her gently by the chin. "You're thinner."

"You're filthier."

A laugh broke out of Silas, ragged and real, and she pulled Petra in again and pressed her lips to the top of her head. "Six days," she murmured. "You waited six days."

"The matron said a week." Petra tilted her face up. "I'd have waited a year. I'd have waited until the clock fell off the wall."

* * *

It was only afterward, walking beside Silas through the receiving ward while orderlies streamed past with the new arrivals, that Petra noticed the second ambulance. And the man on the stretcher.

He lay very still, one leg splinted and bound, an arm wrapped thick in bandaging, face turned toward the ceiling lights. Even hollowed out by fever and blood loss, even with a beard of several days' growth, Petra knew him. She had stood in the parlor of the house that forgot her mother-in-all-but-name and listened to this man's voice pour charm over the room like oil over water. She had stood in the dark of the cellar stair and heard him confess what he had done to the east quarter, to her street, to her parents' house, in the flat dead tone of a man finally telling the truth.

Aldous Blackwood.

Her stomach clenched, old and hot. She felt Silas stop beside her, felt the change in her — the way her hand came to rest on Petra's back, light and steady, an anchor and a question at once.

"He's the reason," Petra said quietly. Not asking. Telling. "He's the reason my mama and papa are dead."

"Yes," Silas said. There was no flinching in it. That was one of the thousand things Petra loved about her: she never lied to soften anything, never lied at all if she could help it. "He knows it too. He's known it since Verron. He stayed behind to carry stretchers when he could barely stand, and he took a shell splinter doing it, and he hasn't once asked anyone to forgive him."

Petra looked at the man on the stretcher. Up close he seemed smaller than he had in the parlor, the way everything cruel seemed smaller once you'd seen it confess. His eyes were open now, fixed on the ceiling, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen there before — not charm, not desperation. Just a man lying very still under the weight of what he'd done, letting it press down.

His eyes moved. Found her. Something went through his face like a current.

"You're the girl from Verron," he said. His voice was gravel-raw. "The east quarter. Your parents—"

"I know what happened to my parents," Petra said.

Silence stretched. Orderlies moved around them; somewhere a man moaned; the great machine of the hospital went on grinding. Aldous Blackwood looked at the child whose street he had sold, and Petra watched him try to find a word for it and fail, watch his mouth open and close, watch his eyes fill with something that had no business being tears and yet was.

"I can't undo it," he said finally. "There isn't enough of my life left to undo it. But I wrote their names down. All of them. Every name I could find or remember. In a ledger." He turned his head painfully on the pillow, looking at Silas. "In *your* ledger. I thought— it seemed right they should be recorded somewhere true."

Petra's breath caught.

She had heard about the ledger for two months — the little book Silas kept sewn into her coat, the book of true names, the names soldiers were too frightened to say aloud. She had never been allowed to see inside it. She had imagined it a hundred times: a book full of men who wanted, just once, to be called what they actually were.

And now Aldous Blackwood had written her parents into it. Into the book of remembered things. Into the family of the counted and the mourned.

She didn't forgive him. She understood, suddenly and completely, that forgiveness might take years, or might never come at all, and that no one — not Silas, not God, not the war itself — could demand it of her. But something in her chest shifted, some load-bearing grief adjusting itself a few degrees, making room.

"He can have the bed by the window," she said abruptly.

Both Silas and Aldous stared at her.

"Not because I forgive him," Petra added fiercely, chin up. "Because window beds get more sun, and he looks like he needs to be somewhere honest, and sunlight is honest." She turned to Silas. "Is that all right?"

Silas was looking at her with an expression Petra couldn't name — something between heartbreak and astonishment and a fierce, blazing pride. "Petra," she said, and her voice cracked clean through, "you are the most extraordinary person I have ever known, and I have known generals."

"That's not saying much," Petra said. "Generals are mostly paper."

Silas laughed again — wet-eyed this time — and put her arm around Petra's shoulders, and together they watched the orderlies wheel Aldous Blackwood to the bed by the window, where the afternoon light came through the glass in long dusty bars and lay across his blanket like something given rather than earned.

* * *

That evening, when the ward lamps were lit and the worst of the day's work was done, Silas sat on the edge of Petra's cot in the nurses' annex where Madame Fournier, with magnificent selective blindness, had assigned her a pallet "temporarily, until the paperwork sorts itself out." Outside, the port boomed faintly with distant guns — the front was only fifty miles off, close enough to feel in the floorboards some nights.

Silas reached into her coat and drew out the ledger.

Petra had never seen it outside of rumor. It was smaller than she'd imagined, shabby, swollen with inserted pages, its spine stitched and re-stitched with thread of three different colors. It smelled of mud and carbolic and smoke.

"You asked me once what was in it," Silas said.

"I asked every day for two months."

"And I said you weren't old enough." Silas turned the book over in her hands. "I was wrong. You've been old enough since Verron. Maybe since before." She opened it. Pages upon pages, dense with writing, names in dozens of hands — some careful, some shaking, some pressed so hard the paper had torn. "These are the true names. The ones men are afraid to say. The ones nobody else remembers." She paused. "There's room in it. There's always room in it. If you wanted—"

"If I wanted what?"

"A page of your own. Your parents' names. Where they lived. What they grew in their garden, if you remember. What they called you when you were small." Silas's voice had gone very quiet. "Nobody else in the world is going to write it down, Petra. Except us."

Petra looked at the ledger. Through the open doorway, down the ward, she could see the bed by the window, where Aldous Blackwood lay awake in the lamplight, staring at the ceiling, waiting — she realized — for morning, when he had asked, humbly, whether he might add the rest of the names he still carried in his head.

Somewhere beyond the port, the guns went on talking their endless language. The stopped clock in the hall downstairs was still holding the afternoon at twenty past four, keeping it safe.

"Yes," Petra said. She took the pencil Silas offered, and opened the book to a clean page, and began at the top with her father's name, writing it large and slow and perfectly, so it would last.

Down the ward, in the bed by the window, Aldous Blackwood turned his face toward them and began, silently, to rehearse his own confession of names — and Petra understood that tomorrow, when he read his list aloud, the family would sit together to hear it: the sister, the brother, and the girl who had chosen them both.

The ledger lay open between them, filling, page by page, with everyone the war had tried to erase.
