## 20. Reading the Ledger Aloud

The ward at Calais had a smell Silas had learned to read the way other men read weather. Carbolic and iodine meant a clean morning. The green-sweet rot beneath it meant gangrene had found someone in the night. And the particular hush that hung over the long rows of cots on the last evening of October — a hush like held breath, like a church before the organist's first note — meant that the men knew what night it was.

Saturday. Ledger night.

She had begun the ritual at Ypres without meaning to. It had started as nothing: her habit of reading back the names she'd recorded, checking her entries against her memory the way an accountant checks his sums, murmuring aloud at the little table by the stove while the ward slept. She'd thought no one heard her. Then one evening a boy from Lancashire — Marlowe, seventeen, both hands bandaged into white mittens — had said from the dark, "Say mine again, Sister," and when she'd said it — *Thomas Edwin Marlowe, of Wigan, who played cornet* — he'd turned his face to the wall and wept without noise, the way men wept when they'd forgotten how.

After that it was a thing the ward did. Every Saturday, lights low, Silas sat on the stool by the stove with the ledger open on her knees and read the true names of the wounded aloud: not the ranks and regimental numbers the Army kept, but the names underneath them — the names men were too afraid to speak because speaking them made them sons again, brothers again, boys again, and boys died easier than soldiers did. She read the name and then the line beneath it, the small biography each man had dictated to her in his pain: *who played cornet; who kept bees; who was engaged to a girl named Ada and had never told his mother; who could whistle any tune after hearing it once.*

Tonight the ward was fuller than usual — forty-two cots, three of them new arrivals from the convoys that had been running since the armistice rumors began, though no armistice had come. The rumor mill ground day and night now. *Any day. Any hour.* The men didn't quite believe it, and so they held their breath, and they waited for Saturday.

Silas dressed for it as she dressed for surgery: sleeves rolled, hair pinned hard, the ledger carried flat against her ribs like something anatomical. At the stove she paused. Aldous was already there.

He sat where he always sat now, on the upturned crate beside the coal scuttle, rolling bandages. He'd been rolling bandages for eleven days — since the stretcher-bearers had carried him through the Calais gates with a shattered thigh and a face gray as ash, and Silas had signed him into her own ward under the name *Blackwood, A., volunteer orderly* rather than let him vanish into some general hospital where no one would know what he'd done or what he was trying to undo. His leg was splinted and propped on a stool. His hands, which she remembered as soft hands, a broker's hands, a traitor's hands, were raw at the knuckles from work.

He looked up when she came in. He never asked if he could stay for the reading. He simply stayed, every week, silent until the end, and at the end he said the same thing: "Thank you, Sister." As if she were still only a nurse to him. As if he hadn't yet earned the right to call her anything truer.

She set the stool by the stove and opened the ledger.

* * *

"Men of the third ward." Her voice carried the way it needed to — not loud, but pitched low and even, the voice she used over wounds, the voice that said *I am here and I am not leaving*. "You know the custom. If I speak your name, you needn't answer. You needn't do anything at all. It is enough to be named."

A cot near the window creaked. Somewhere down the row a man whispered *here*, out of habit, and another shushed him gently, and the hush settled again.

Silas wet her thumb and turned to the first page.

"Whitfield, James Arthur. Of Leeds. Who kept pigeons on his mother's roof, thirty birds, and taught the youngest one to sit on his cap." In the fourth bed a broad-shouldered gunner closed his eyes. Silas had taken that entry in June, at Ypres, while the shells walked closer and closer and the man had gripped her wrist and said *write the pigeon part, write the pigeon part first, write it before the other parts.* She had written it first. She had written it largest.

"Okafor, Daniel Chukwuemeka. Of Lagos, by way of Liverpool. Who spoke four languages and translated jokes between companies so that everyone laughed twice."

"Marlowe, Thomas Edwin. Of Wigan. Cornet."

"Here," Marlowe breathed, and this time nobody shushed him.

She went down the page and down the ward, and the room changed shape around her voice. That was the thing she could never explain to Halloway, who had once caught the tail end of a Saturday and stood in the doorway with an expression like a man watching water flow uphill: that the ledger did not diminish these men into anecdotes. It enlarged them. An army file said *Pvt. J.A. Whitfield, RFA, gunshot wound, femur, fair prognosis.* The ledger said *pigeons, cap, youngest bird,* and suddenly Whitfield was not a wound in a numbered bed but a boy on a roof in Leeds with the whole sky going gold above him — and the whole ward could see it, and for a moment the ward was not a ward at all but a long street with all their roofs on it, all their skies, all their mothers calling them in to supper.

She reached a page she had been turning toward all evening, the way you turn toward a door you're afraid of.

"Verron," she said quietly. "I have names from Verron."

The ward stilled. Half of them had come through Verron's evacuation; two of them had helped load stretchers at the rail yard; one, a corporal named Finch, had stood guard on the platform the night the last train pulled out, and had seen a woman in a nurse's coat put a child aboard and stay behind. He'd never spoken of it. He watched her now across the lamplight with steady eyes.

"These were given to me by a survivor," Silas said. "A girl of eleven. She wrote them herself. I copied them in her hand as near as I could make it."

Petra's list. Petra had written it in the cellar aid post during the worst hours, by candle stub, on the backs of shell wrappers, while the walls jumped and the dust came down like snow: the names of the dead of the east quarter, street by street, house by house, as many as a child could gather. Forty-one names. Silas had copied all forty-one into the ledger in October, sitting up all night, and had not yet been able to read them aloud until now.

"Boucher, Marguerite. Who sold bread on the rue de l'Est and gave the ends of loaves to children free, and called it *the crust tax in reverse*."

"Boucher, Henri. Who tuned pianos. Whose own piano was the finest instrument in the quarter, and who let anyone play it who asked politely."

She read on. The baker's daughter. The schoolteacher who had kept teaching in the cellar while the ceiling cracked. Two brothers, ages six and nine, whose entry was a single line in a child's careful French-inflected English: *who raced snails on the garden wall and always let the smaller one win.* Silas read that line and had to stop, and press her thumb flat against the page, and breathe, and the ward waited with her, forty-two men breathing together in the carbolic dark.

At the far end of the room, bandage rolls had gone untouched for ten minutes. Aldous sat with his head bowed and his hands open in his lap, palms up, the way men sat in chapels. He had asked her once, early, whether the Verron pages would be read aloud. She had said: when you can bear it. He had said: don't wait for me. She had waited anyway. Tonight she judged — looking at his bowed head, his ruined hands — that waiting had done its work.

"Aldous," she said, and his head came up sharply. Not *Mr. Blackwood.* Not *orderly.* Just the name, bare, the way she read all the others. "Come here."

He came, awkward, crutchless, dragging his splinted leg with both hands on the cot frames, taking the long journey down the ward past the faces of men who did not know — none of them knew, only Halloway and Silas — what this limping volunteer had cost the town whose dead she was reading. He arrived at the stove. He stood swaying. She moved off the stool and made room for him, and he understood, and he sat, and she put the ledger on his knees and turned the page to the blank space after the last of Petra's forty-one names.

"The record isn't finished," Silas said to the ward, and to him. "Records kept by one hand are half a record. There are names missing from Verron. Names only one living man knows."

His breath went out of him. "Sister—"

"You'll say them," she said. "Or you'll sit there with them inside you your whole life, and they'll rot, and that will be the second death you gave those people. Speak them instead. Give them the dignity of being difficult."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. His jaw worked. His eyes went down the ward — to Finch, to the gunner with his pigeons, to Marlowe with his mittens — forty-two men who would have torn him apart if they knew, and who were now, unknowing, offering him the only absolution that had ever counted: their attention.

He took the pencil she offered. His hand shook so badly the first mark skidded. Then he steadied it against the page the way she'd seen a hundred men steady a hand against a wound, and he began to write.

"I only ever saw the lists as numbers," he said. His voice was low and rough, a stranger's voice, or perhaps his real one, surfacing at last. "Grid squares. Tonnage. That is how I made it possible, you understand — I made it arithmetic, so I wouldn't have to make it people. But there was a woman—" The pencil stopped. "There was a café on the corner of the rue de l'Est. Blue shutters. The owner's wife sang while she worked. I passed it twice a week for a month while I was in Verron arranging the survey. She sang opera badly. Puccini, mostly. Off-key and loud, and everyone on the street smiled at it."

He looked up at Silas.

"I don't know her name. I never learned it. I know everything about the artillery positions within nine kilometers of that café and I never learned her name."

"Then write what you know," Silas said. "*Woman of the blue shutters, rue de l'Est, who sang Puccini badly and made a whole street smile.* The ledger keeps what it is given. A name unknown is not a name refused."

He bent over the page. The pencil scratched. The ward was so quiet Silas could hear the stove tick and, outside, the distant sea working the harbor wall, and somewhere beyond that, impossibly, faintly, a church bell ringing the hour — eight o'clock, ordinary time, time going on.

He filled a page. Then another. The blue-shutter woman. The gendarme who'd tipped his hat to him every morning. The boy who'd run messages for his survey team and been paid in chocolate and had shown him, proud as a colonel, a toy boat carved from a shell fragment. Name after name recovered from the wreckage of his arithmetic — not names, most of them, but the next best thing, the truest thing he had: the small particular evidence that these had been people, that he had seen them, that seeing had not saved them but might now save their memory.

When he finished, his forehead was damp and his shoulders sagged as though he'd carried the ledger itself up a mountain. Silas took the book back, read what he'd written, and found it good — found it, God forgive her, better than good: it was the first honest document she had ever known a Blackwood to produce.

"There are nineteen entries here," she said. "They stand in the record beside Petra's forty-one. Neither list cancels the other. They are simply the truth, whole."

Aldous nodded. He tried to rise; his leg betrayed him; Silas took his elbow and took most of his weight, and for a moment, standing there in the stove-light, brother and sister held each other upright, which was more than their family had ever managed in the great cold house with all its portraits.

"Read the rest," he whispered. "Yours. All of them. Let me hear what a record sounds like when it's kept out of love instead of fear."

So she sat back down, and she read.

* * *

She read for an hour. The ward emptied into sleep one man at a time, gently, the way an audience drifts out of a concert — not leaving, just going where the words had carried them. She read the pigeon-keeper and the beekeeper and the whistler and the man engaged to Ada. She read the entries from the retreat, from the rail yards, from the cellar in Verron where she'd stitched strangers by candlelight while her mother stood frozen on the stairs. She read the entry she had made for her father, dead two years, whom she had never been allowed to mourn properly: *Blackwood, Edmund. Of Verron. Who taught me chess and lost gracefully, every game, on purpose, and I only understood why twenty years later.* She had never dared read that one aloud before. When she finished it, the silence had a different texture — warmer, she thought, or perhaps she only needed it to be.

Last of all, as always, she read the page at the very back, the one headed in her own hand: *Chosen.* It was the shortest page and the longest to read, because she read it slowly, deliberately, looking at one person per name.

"Petra. Of Verron. Eleven years old. Who survived what should not be survivable, who learned her letters in a dressing station and taught this ward more about courage than any commission could. Who chose her own name, which is the oldest and hardest right there is."

Down at the end of the ward, in the nurses' alcove where Petra had claimed a cot among the probationers, a small shape sat bolt upright in the dark, pretending to be asleep, glowing.

"Halloway, Gerald. Major. Who carries more secrets than a field dispatch case and has broken every rule he swore to except the ones that mattered." In the shadows by the door, where he'd been standing for the last twenty minutes with his cap in his hand, unseen, the major cleared his throat softly and studied the ceiling.

"And Blackwood, Aldous. Of Verron. Who broke what cannot be mended, and is spending the rest of himself trying to mend it anyway. Entered into the record not as what he did, but as what he is doing. The ledger keeps both. So must we."

She closed the book.

The stove had burned low. Out in the harbor a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and beneath the horn, threading through it from the town — from every church in Calais at once, it seemed, one after another catching fire with sound — bells. Not the hour. Not any hour. Bells rung wild and unscheduled and joyous, pealing over the water, and then the ward was on its feet — the men who could stand were standing, the men who couldn't were shouting, and someone was crying *it's over, it's over, they've signed it, the war is over* —

Silas sat very still with the ledger against her chest.

November the eleventh. The eleventh hour. She had read the dead into memory on the very morning the dying stopped, and the bells would not stop, and Marlowe was holding his bandaged mittens up to the light as if testing them for flight, and Petra had burst from the alcove and was running down the aisle of cots toward her, and Aldous was laughing — actually laughing, a sound she hadn't heard since childhood, wrecked and wondering — and Major Halloway stood in the doorway with tears running openly down his face and his cap crushed in his fist.

Petra reached her and climbed onto the stool and into her arms, all elbows and triumph, and shouted over the bells: "It ended! Silas — it *ended* —"

"Yes," Silas said into her hair. Around them the world was roaring its relief, and the ledger lay warm against her ribs, full of names, and tomorrow — tomorrow there would be demobilization papers, and records to correct or not correct, and the question of the name sewn into her collar, and the whole vast peacetime problem of who she was permitted to be.

But tonight there were bells.

She lifted Petra down, and stood, and raised her voice over the din: "Third ward! One more name!"

And forty-two men, hoarse and laughing and weeping, turned to hear it.
