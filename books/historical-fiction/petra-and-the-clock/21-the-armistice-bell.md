## 21. The Armistice Bell

The eleventh hour of the eleventh day began, as all our days had begun for two years, with the sound of a bell — though this one was not ours.

It came from the town below the hospital ridge, from the cracked tower of Saint-Aubin, which had lost its spire in the spring shelling and kept only its frame, like a man who has given up everything but his voice. One stroke, then another, then a wildness of them all together, and beneath it a sound I had almost forgotten existed: cheering. Human voices, thousands of them, rising out of the mud of France like steam off a winter field.

I was kneeling at the foot of Corporal Ellis's bed, rewrapping his ankle, when the first shell of silence fell.

That is what it was. A shell. The guns along the whole front simply stopped — not dwindling, not fading, but stopping, mid-breath, mid-order, mid-arc, so that the air itself seemed to stumble. For eleven months there had not been a single hour in which I could not feel artillery through the soles of my boots. Now my boots stood on still ground, and the stillness went up through me like cold water, and I put down the bandage scissors because my hands were shaking too badly to be trusted with anything sharp.

"Is it—" Ellis started.

"It's over," I said. "Sergeant, it's over."

He lay back on the cot and looked at the ceiling beams, and then he did what half the men in that ward would do before noon: he cried without any noise at all, tears running sideways into his ears, as if grief and relief had arrived at the same door and neither would give way to the other.

* * *

By ten o'clock the ward smelled of tobacco smoke, which Matron permitted for one day only and which hung in the rafters like a ghost granted leave. Men who could walk were standing in the doorway in their hospital blues, watching the road into Calais fill with lorries flying every flag anyone could find — French tricolours cut from bedsheets, a Union Jack someone produced from a kit bag where it had waited four years for exactly this morning. A boy from the Cheshire regiment played a mouth organ badly and no one asked him to stop.

Aldous sat propped against his pillows near the window, his leg in its splint, a cigarette he wasn't smoking held between two fingers. He had been silent most of the morning, watching the celebration the way a man watches a fire in a house he once owned.

"You should be down there," I said.

"Should I." He turned the cigarette. "There are names in your book, Silas. Forty-one from Verron alone. None of those men can walk down to the road either." He looked at me. "Do you think they'd want us celebrating?"

"I think," I said, "that they'd want us to notice we're alive, since they paid for it."

He considered that for a long moment, and then he got his crutches — he was not supposed to have them yet; Sister Okafor had been explicit — and swung himself upright with a grunt and said, "Well. Then let's notice properly," and hobbled out into the corridor to find the men who couldn't stand a reason to stand.

I stayed by the window a while longer.

Below, past the wall and the bare plane trees, the road ran west toward Calais, and somewhere along that road, in a convalescent hospital she had refused to leave, was Petra. Three letters had come since October. In the first she reported her own recovery with the brisk authority of a ward sister twice her age. In the second she confessed she had been caught teaching the younger children their letters using the pages of my old notebook, and did not apologize. In the third, written in her careful new cursive, she had asked one question: *When the war is done, where do I go?*

Not *may I come to you*. She was eleven, and she had learned already that the world did not answer questions asked plainly. She had learned it in a cellar in Verron, under the weight of a house Aldous Blackwood's arithmetic had brought down on her parents. She had asked instead: *where do I go* — leaving the answer entirely to me, trusting me with it even now, even after everything, and I stood at that window with the bells going mad in the valley and understood that this was the last morning of my life in which the answer could be deferred.

* * *

At noon, Major Halloway came to the ward.

He wore his full uniform, medals polished, which meant nothing good; Halloway dressed like a Christmas tree only when he intended to deliver bad news wrapped in ceremony. He walked the aisle slowly, nodding to the men, letting them see him, and stopped at my table by the window where the ledger lay open — because I had been reading aloud that morning, between bandages, the way I always did on the worst days and now, apparently, on the best ones.

"Blackwood," he said.

"Sister Ward says you've been discharged from medical hold."

"Effective tomorrow." He picked up an apple from the bowl, turned it over, set it down again. "There will be demobilization boards starting in the spring. Medical review boards. Pay books checked against enlistment papers. Four years of records being read by clerks who have nothing better to do than read records." He looked at me over the apple. "You understand what I'm telling you."

"The war ends today, Major, and the paperwork begins."

"The paperwork," he agreed, "begins."

We had had this conversation before, or versions of it — in a rail yard at Verron, in a checkpoint hut with forged papers burning in a brazier between us. He had spent his career on my secret the way a miser spends coins he cannot account for, and each time the ledger balanced by some miracle neither of us had engineered.

"There's a route," he said. "There always is. A clerk in Boulogne owes me more than he'll ever repay. A transfer to the Indian Medical Service, a posting far enough away that no board will trouble itself. You'd serve out two quiet years and come home with a record cleaner than mine." He set the apple down. "Or you walk into that board in the spring as yourself, and we discover whether England has grown kinder while none of us were looking."

"And if it hasn't?"

"Then you lose everything except the thing you actually value," he said, "and I lose my pension, and we shall both have to live with it." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; Halloway's face had never fully recovered from the war's tuition. "I'm told living with things is supposed to be possible. I've seen you do it for four years."

He straightened, became the major again for the benefit of the ward, wished the men a happy peace in a voice pitched to carry, and left. At the door he paused.

"The girl arrives Thursday," he said. "Convalescent transport, Calais to Étaples. Someone signed the requisition." He did not say who, and I did not ask, and the door closed behind him on the sound of the bells, which had not yet stopped anywhere in France, and I do not believe stopped all day.

* * *

The afternoon passed in small surrenders.

Men who had hoarded chocolate since Ypres gave it away. The sergeant who had taught Petra euchre laid the cards out on a blanket in the yard and dealt hands to men who could barely hold theirs, and when a hand was won he read out the winner's true name from memory — not the rank, not the number, the name — and made everyone drink to it in tea. Word had gotten round about the ledger; it always does, in wards; and now men I had never treated came to my table shyly, caps in hand, and asked, some of them, if I knew their real names. As if after four years of being a serial number a man might doubt he had ever been anything else.

One of them was a gunner named Whitcombe who had lost both eyes at Cambrai. Sister Okafor led him to my table and he stood with his ruined face turned toward me and said, "They told me there's a book. They said you write down what a man wants to be called."

"That's right."

"Nobody's called me anything but 'mate' since April," he said. "I'd like to hear my mother's name for me. If it's not too much trouble."

"It's never trouble," I said, and took his hands, and told him: Wilfred. Wilfred Amos Whitcombe, called Will by his brothers, called Wilf by his mother, who sang to him about the bonny banks of Loch Lomond though the family had lived in Birmingham for three generations. His face crumpled and remade itself into something like peace, and he thanked me as if I had returned something stolen rather than merely spoken aloud, and perhaps I had. Perhaps that was all the ledger had ever been: a place to keep what the war tried to confiscate.

Aldous watched from his chair by the window. When the yard emptied he swung over on his crutches and stood at the edge of my table, looking down at the open page, at the forty-one names from Verron in my handwriting and the newer column beneath in his — the names he had gathered himself, street by street, from parish registers and survivors' accounts, his penmanship neat and schoolroom-perfect, each entry initialed *A.B.* as if signing a confession over and over.

"Read mine back to me," he said quietly. "All of them. I want to check I haven't missed anyone."

So I read them, and he listened with his head bowed, and twice he corrected a spelling, and once he added a name I did not have — a schoolmistress, Céline Aubry, who had gone back into the east quarter for her class register — and when I finished he said, "Thank you," in the voice of a man accepting a sentence rather than a kindness.

"Aldous." I closed the ledger. "Thursday, when Petra comes—"

"I'll be gone by Wednesday." He said it lightly, arranging it as a favor. "She shouldn't have to learn to look at me. Not yet. Maybe not ever; that's hers to decide, not yours and not mine." He gripped the crutches. "I'll take a convalescent post in Rouen. There's a shortage of orderlies and I've discovered I'm rather good at carrying things."

"She'll need to know where you are. Someday. That's how it works — the truth keeps its appointments whether we do or not."

"I know." He smiled, and it was the first smile of his I had seen in twenty years that didn't perform anything. "You always were the honest one. God help you, it nearly killed you half a dozen times, and here you are, still at it." He looked out at the yard, at the flags. "Silas. What will you do? After? Go back to being—" He stopped, hearing the trap in his own sentence, and I watched him step around it carefully, the way you step around a wounded man. "Go back to the house?"

"There's no house to go back to. Mother sails Friday on the hospital ship. She asked me to come to the pier." I turned the ledger over in my hands. Its cover was soft as leather gloves now, worn to the shape of my pocket. "I haven't decided."

"You have, though," he said. "You decided years ago. You just haven't said it out loud yet, and saying it out loud is the part that costs."

He was right, as he had been wrong about everything else and right about this. But I did not say it out loud, not yet, not that afternoon with the bells still ringing. Some words need a quieter hour than victory provides.

* * *

Evening came early, as it does in November, and with it a frost that silvered the duckboards and made the sentries' breath visible at the gate. The ward lamps burned low. Half the walking wounded had gone down to the town and not returned, and no one went after them; there would be no more desertions worth prosecuting, no more charges worth preferring. The war had released its grip and even discipline seemed embarrassed to still be holding on.

I sat at my table with the lamp turned up and opened the ledger to the first page.

*Thomas Ellison, called Tommo by his sister, afraid of the dark.* Dead, Passchendaele. *Samuel Okonkwo, called Sam, writes poems he hides in his kit bag.* Discharged, home to Cardiff. Page after page — four years of them, the living and the dead interleaved without distinction, because the book had never cared which was which; it only cared what people wanted to be called.

Near the back, in fresher ink: *Petra Marchand, called Petra. Found in the rubble of the Rue des Écoles, Verron, 9 August. Age eleven. Wants to be called brave, and is.*

And beneath it, added in September in a smaller hand, hesitating over the letters as though they might still refuse him: *Silas Blackwood. Called Silas. Chose this name herself, and it fits, and it stays.*

My name. In the book. Aldous had entered it without asking, sometime during those cellar nights when we worked side by side binding wounds and not speaking, and I had found it only afterward and never mentioned it, and he had never mentioned that I hadn't. It sat on the page now like a stone placed on a grave or a cornerstone — I could not tell which, and perhaps it was both.

Legally, of course, the name did not exist. Legally I remained whatever the enlistment papers said, some clerk's invention, a masculine fiction maintained by luck and Major Halloway's expensive mercy through four years of inspections and paybooks and one demobilization board still to come in the spring. The war was over. The armor could come off. That was what everyone said, wasn't it — *now you can go home, now you can be yourself again*, as if the self were a uniform folded away in a trunk, as if the trunk had a lock, as if the world outside had changed its locks along with the calendar.

But the world had not changed. The world had only stopped shooting.

In the spring there would be clerks with my file. And I thought of Wilfred Whitcombe standing at my table wanting his mother's word for him, and of Petra's letter asking *where do I go*, and of the ledger's soft worn cover, and I understood what I had known all along and had not yet said out loud:

Some names are not disguises. Some names are load-bearing walls. Take them down carelessly and the whole house comes with them — and the house, this time, has a child in it.

Petra needed a guardian the law would recognize. The law recognized Silas Blackwood. Whatever the truth of my body, whatever reckoning the spring might bring, the name was armor still, and armor is not vanity; armor is what stands between the ones you love and the shells. There would be a season for truth. There always is, if you live long enough to keep its appointments. But it would come on my schedule, chosen freely, not extracted by a board of strangers with stamps.

I dipped my pen and wrote, beneath Aldous's entry, a line of my own:

*To be corrected when the world is ready — and not one day before.*

Then I blotted it, and closed the book, and held it a moment against my chest the way you hold anything that has kept you alive.

* * *

The bells had finally stopped somewhere past midnight, and the frost had thickened, and I was banking the lamp when I heard footsteps in the corridor — quick ones, light ones, utterly wrong for a hospital at midnight, and then Sister Okafor appeared in the doorway holding a lantern and wearing an expression I had never seen on her capable face: pure, undecorated delight.

"There's a girl at the gate," she said. "Stowed away on the ambulance convoy from Calais. Refused all evening to give a name — sat on her kit with her arms crossed like a small general." She stepped aside, and the lantern light spilled into the corridor, and onto a figure standing behind her in an oversized coat, dusted with frost, chin lifted, eyes already finding mine across the dark ward.

"They said Thursday," Petra announced, to the whole room, to the whole sleeping world. "But the war's over, and nobody checks trains anymore."

The ledger was still in my hands. I set it down on the table — open, deliberately, to her page — and she crossed the ward at a dead run, and I caught her, eleven years old and heavier than grief and lighter than everything else, and above us the cracked bell of Saint-Aubin, unprompted, unexplained, rang out one single clear note across the frozen fields, as if the year itself were marking the page.
