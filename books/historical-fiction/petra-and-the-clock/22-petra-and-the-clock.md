## 22. Petra and the Clock

The clock had stopped again.

It always stopped in February, when the damp crept through the hospital's east wall and settled into the clockwork like an old soldier taking a chair by the fire. Petra Blackwood stood before it with the winding key in her hand and felt the familiar small irritation, the familiar small love. Eleven years she had owned this clock, and eleven years it had behaved exactly as it pleased.

"Stubborn thing," she said aloud, though the ward behind her was empty at this hour except for the sleeping men and the one lamp burning low over the nurses' station.

She fitted the key into the face and turned. The mechanism resisted, then caught, and the pendulum swung back into its patient rhythm — tick, tick, tick — the same sound it had made hanging on the wall of the dressing station at the edge of the Ypres salient, where Petra had been eleven years old and the world was made of mud and bandages and the voices of men who were afraid to say their own names.

She had learned to wind it before she had learned long division. Silas had put the key in her hand one evening in the third ward — "A clock is just a promise you keep twice a day," she'd said, in that dry voice she used when she was saying something tender and didn't want anyone to notice — and Petra had kept the promise ever since, through two hospitals and one long sea voyage and every year since.

* * *

The night shift at St. Agatha's Military Hospital, Orpington, began the way all her shifts began: with the ledger.

Petra sat down at the station desk, opened the drawer, and took out the book. It was new — this year's book, its cover still stiff, its pages still white — but she opened it the way she opened all of them, which was slowly, with both hands, the way you might open a door into a room where someone you loved was sleeping.

On the first page, in her own careful hand, was written:

*A ledger of true names. For the ones they are afraid to speak.*

She had copied that line from the original. The original lived at home, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin biscuit box, wrapped again in a shawl, kept in the bottom of her wardrobe behind her winter boots. She did not take it out often. When she did, she read a few names only, the way you eat something sweet a little at a time so it lasts.

Silas had given her the ledger in 1919, at Calais, three days after the stretcher came through the doors with Aldous Blackwood on it, grey as ash but breathing, and Silas walking beside it on feet that were bleeding inside their boots from forty miles of shelled road. Three days after Petra had refused the orphanage convoy and sat in the hospital corridor for two days and nights, telling every matron who tried to move her that she was waiting for her family, and daring them — an eleven-year-old girl in a borrowed coat — to tell her she wasn't.

When Silas woke properly, the first thing she did was ask for the ledger. The second thing she did was hold out her arms. Petra had run to her hard enough to knock the breath out of her, and Silas had held on with both arms, which she almost never did, and said into her hair: "You waited."

"You came," Petra said.

"That too."

Later — much later, months later, on the boat home — Silas had pressed the ledger into her hands and said, "One day I'll be too old or too dead to remember them all properly. You'll have to do it better than me." And Petra, who understood by then that Silas expressed love the way other people expressed weather — constantly, and mostly without announcing it — had simply said, "Yes," because there was no other possible answer.

* * *

Tonight's page began, as always, with the new admissions.

Private Ellis, R., twenty-two, Manchester. Shot through the shoulder at — no, not shot; there was no war now, not the big one; these were the garrison men, the accidents, the fevers, the ones whose wounds came from lorries and training grounds and the slow collapse of bodies that had been broken once already and never quite mended. The Great War had been over for six years, but Petra had learned that wars did not end; they only changed address. They moved into men's chests and stayed there, coughing in the winters. Half her ward was full of men fighting battles that had finished in 1918.

Private Ellis had been admitted that afternoon, feverish, thrashing, calling for his mother. And when Petra had leaned close to give him water, he had grabbed her wrist with surprising strength and said, "Don't tell them my name. Please. Don't write it down."

"Not even your real name?" she'd asked, gently, because some of them wanted their real names spoken precisely because no one else would use them.

He'd stared at her, confused, and then — the way they sometimes did, surfacing from wherever the fever kept them — he'd said, "Ronald. But everyone calls me Ron. My mum calls me Ronald. If she comes, tell her Ronald's here."

So tonight Petra wrote, in the second column of the fresh ledger, in her best hand:

*R. "Ronald" Ellis. Wants his mother to know Ronald is here. Afraid of the dark. Good teeth, bad cough. Sang two verses of "Pack Up Your Troubles" before the fever took him under — sang them wrong, on purpose, to make the man in the next bed laugh.*

That last part mattered. Silas had taught her that. *Anyone can record what a man suffers,* Silas used to say. *The ledger is for what a man is.* A name was the start of it, but never the whole.

* * *

At midnight she made her rounds.

The ward breathed in the dark — thirty men breathing at slightly different speeds, the sound of it like a tide. She moved down the rows with her little shaded lamp, checking dressings, straightening blankets, pausing where Private Ellis muttered and smoothing his hair back the way she remembered hands being smoothed over her own hair in another ward, another country, when she was small and newly orphaned and did not yet know that the word *orphan* was about to become the least important thing about her.

Because that was the secret she carried, the one she had decided long ago was actually the largest truth she carried: she had not been orphaned into nothing. She had been orphaned into *them*. Into the third ward, into the card games and the stolen biscuits, into Silas's letters lesson at the end of each day — *P is for Petra, and Petra means rock, and rocks are what everything else builds on* — into a family assembled out of choice rather than blood, which Silas had told her was the only kind of family that never lied to you about what it was.

Her parents' names were in the original ledger. Étienne Marchand. Colette Marchand. Of Verron, east quarter. She had copied them into the front of every new ledger since her fifteenth year, so that they would open each book first, before any living name, the way you set a place for the dead at the table. Her father, a clockmaker — that was where Silas had found the station clock, in the ruins of his shop, and had dug it out of the rubble with her own hands and carried it across France because, she said, a clockmaker's daughter ought to have a clock. Her mother had sung while she cooked. That was all the ledger could hold of them, and all it needed to. What a person is, in the end, fits in surprisingly few words, if the words are chosen honestly.

Petra finished her rounds and came back to the desk, and the clock ticked on the wall above her — she'd had it hung in the ward itself, where the men could see it, because men coming up from anesthesia liked to know the time had continued without them.

* * *

At two in the morning, Sister Mills came through the door quietly, coat still buttoned, snow melting in her hair. She was due off shift, but she crossed to Petra's desk and stood there a moment with an odd expression — not worry, exactly. Something more like ceremony.

"Post came late," she said. "There's one for you. Hand-delivered, if you please, by a taxi driver who wouldn't leave until I signed for it."

Petra took the envelope. Heavy paper. Her name written across it in a hand she knew better than her own — a strong, slanted, economical hand, a hand that had written ten thousand chart notes and one famous ledger and had once forged an evacuation pass on a rail-yard platform with shells falling on Verron, forging a surname onto a child's papers so smoothly that the officer checking documents never once looked up.

Blackwood, P.

She opened it.

The letter inside was short. Silas had never believed in long letters. *"Brevity is a kindness,"* she used to say. *"Say the true thing and stop."*

*Petra —*

*I am writing this on Tuesday because by the time it reaches you it will be Saturday, and on Saturday I intend to do a thing I have been putting off for eleven years. I am going to walk into a registry office in the town where nobody knows me, and correct the record. All of it. Every document, every name, every lie we needed to survive. The world has had six years of peace to get ready, and I am tired of my own name being armor instead of skin.*

*You will understand what this costs, better than anyone alive.*

*I am not asking permission. I am telling you, because you are the keeper of the ledger, and keepers must be told true things.*

*Also: Aldous sends his regards, and asks — again — whether you will finally let him teach you chess. He says you cheat. I have informed him that you do not cheat, you simply refuse to lose, and that this trait was acquired fair and square from your elders.*

*Wind the clock. Eat something green. Come south when the wards allow it — I want to show you the sea from the right side of it for once.*

*Your —*

And here the pen had paused. Petra could see the pause, the faint hesitation in the ink, eleven years of chosen family compressed into the space between two strokes. Then:

*— aunt,*

*— S.*

Petra laughed, alone in the lamplight, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and laughed again. *Aunt.* After everything — after the forged papers and the forty-mile road and the river crossing where Silas had held Aldous's head above the water with one arm and the ledger above her own head with the other — after all of that, Silas Blackwood, who had spent a lifetime being precise about names, had hesitated over the word *aunt* and then committed to it like a woman signing her own surrender and her own coronation on the same line.

Sister Mills was still standing there, watching her with polite curiosity.

"Good news?" she asked.

"The best kind," Petra said. "The kind that's been a long time coming."

* * *

At four o'clock, Private Ellis surfaced from his fever, and Petra was there.

She gave him water. She checked his dressing. And then, because she was who she was, she sat down on the edge of his bed and said, quietly, "Your mother telephoned this evening. I told her Ronald was here, and resting, and that he asked after her."

His eyes filled. He didn't say anything — men of his generation seldom did, at moments like this — but his hand found hers and gripped it.

"I'll write down that you're brave," she said. "If you like. In my book."

"What book?"

"A family book," she said. "Everyone in this ward is in it. Everyone who ever was."

He looked at her as though she might be mad, and then, because the fever had burned away whatever pride usually stood guard, he whispered, "Would you? Nobody's kept anything about me before. Not anything worth keeping."

"They're all worth keeping," Petra said. "That's the entire point of the book."

* * *

Dawn came grey and clean over the Kentish fields, and Petra's shift ended, and she walked home through streets where the lamps were going out one by one and bakers were lighting their ovens, and the whole ordinary machinery of a peacetime morning ground forward around her. Six years of it, and still she noticed. Still, every morning, she noticed — the absence of sirens, the presence of children, the sheer unremarkable miracle of a street where nobody was shouting numbers.

At home she wound the mantel clock — the little sister of the great station clock, which she had bought with her first wages so the big one wouldn't be lonely — and she took the biscuit tin down from the wardrobe, and she unwrapped the oilcloth, and she read the original ledger's last page.

Silas had written it at Calais, in November of 1919, and Petra knew it by heart, but she read it anyway, the way you visit a grave that holds good news:

*Let it be recorded that families are not found. They are built, plank by plank, in cellars and dressing stations and railway yards, out of whatever hands are nearest and willing. Blood is one material among many. Choice is the stronger timber.*

*Entered herein: Petra Marchand Blackwood, who chose us first, and thereby taught us how it is done.*

Petra closed the tin. She sat for a moment in the quiet house, listening to both clocks tick in their different rooms, keeping their double promise.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped her pen, and began the reply she had been composing in her head since Saturday's date first appeared in the letter — the reply that would begin, *Dear Aunt Silas, come north instead, I have something to show you* — because yesterday, at the registry office in Chatham, in her lunch hour, between one ward round and the next, Petra Blackwood had done a thing of her own.

She had filed the paperwork to change her name officially, legally, permanently — striking *Marchand* from the record not in grief but in honor, folding her lost parents into the past tense of her heart where they would be safe forever, and signing herself, at last and entirely and on purpose, in the surname of the woman who had forged it for her on the worst night of both their lives.

Not armor. Skin.

The pen hovered. Somewhere a church bell rang seven, and both clocks answered it.

Petra Blackwood smiled, and began to write.
