## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The floorboard in the third ward, the one beneath the cot nearest the sea-facing window, lifted at its north end if you knew to press your thumb against the knot of old pitch and pull. Paloma Kavanagh had discovered this on her second week at Greywater garrison, when she'd dropped a spoon and it had skittered beneath the frame, and she had gone after it on her knees with a candle stub while the wind worried the shutters outside. The board came up like a held breath released. The cavity beneath was shallow and dry and smelled of salt and cedar shavings, and for a long time she kept nothing there but the spoon.

Now it held forty-one names.

She counted them the way other people counted coins or days — not because she doubted the number, but because counting was itself a kind of prayer, and prayers, in her experience, worked better when they were repeated until they wore smooth as river stones. Forty-one men whose deaths had happened in this hospital, in these cots, under these blankets that smelled of carbolic and the sea, but whose names appeared nowhere in the garrison's official register. Not in the morning reports. Not in the casualty lists that went south to the Admiralty in their careful envelopes. Not in the letters dictated by officers to grieving families, because those letters were never written at all.

Forty-one men who had simply stopped existing, as far as the war's paperwork was concerned.

Paloma wrote them down in a ledger no bigger than her palm, its cover scuffed leather gone soft at the corners, bought from a peddler on the harbor road for two coppers and a promise she hadn't intended to keep. She wrote in pencil because ink could be read by lamplight through paper, and pencil smudged, and smudging felt safer somehow, though she knew this was superstition dressed up as strategy. She wrote small. She wrote fast. She had gotten good at writing without looking down, her hands steady over a bandage roll or a basin while her fingers moved inside her apron pocket, transcribing a name she'd heard Sister Bram say aloud only once: *Corporal Hennet, died at four this morning, tell his people he wasn't in pain* — except nobody would ever tell his people anything, because Corporal Hennet had been struck off the rolls before he'd finished dying.

The wind came up off the water that evening in long gray sheets, rattling the hospital's loose clapboards, carrying the smell of kelp and coal smoke and, beneath it all, the faint sweet-rot of the tide flats. Paloma stood at the window of the third ward and watched the last light fail over the harbor. Three transport ships rode at anchor out past the breakwater, dark shapes against the darker water, waiting. Everyone knew what ships like that meant. The front had shifted again — it was always shifting now, in these late months, like a sick man turning in bed — and when it shifted toward the coast, the orders came down: evacuate the wounded who could be moved, burn what couldn't be carried, and leave.

Burn the records first. That was always the instruction. *Destroy all field documentation before withdrawal.* She had seen it done once, two years ago, at a dressing station further up the coast — officers feeding ledgers into a drum fire while the orderlies loaded stretchers, pages curling black and lifting on the heat like small escaping souls. She had stood there holding a basin of water and thought, with a clarity that frightened her even then: *no one is going to remember any of these men.*

So she measured her days differently now. Not in shifts or in deaths, though both still marked time — but in weight. In what could be carried.

A woman could carry perhaps thirty pounds at a hard walk, more at a slow one, less if she was also carrying fear. Paloma had begun to think in these terms constantly, almost compulsively, the way a miser thinks in coins. A tin of bread: two pounds. A change of stockings: half a pound. The good scissors, the ones that cut canvas cleanly: three ounces, and worth their weight doubled. Water for a day: six pounds, though thirst would make her spend that weight foolishly before noon. And the ledger — the ledger weighed almost nothing. Four ounces of paper and pencil stubs, wrapped in oilcloth. Four ounces against forty-one lives, and the number grew.

She did not think of it as defiance. Defiance was loud; it marched; it got people reassigned, or worse. What she did was quieter than that. It was closer to mending. When a garment tore, you didn't shout at the tear — you took up needle and thread and you closed it, stitch by stitch, so that whoever needed the garment afterward would find it whole. The war had torn a hole in the record of these men, and someone, someday, would need to know they had existed. So she sewed names. One at a time. Small stitches, invisible ones.

* * *

Sister Bram found her at the dispensary window near midnight, grinding chalk tablets because the mortar wouldn't grind itself and sleep wouldn't come either. The older woman moved through the wards the way she moved through everything — heavily, decisively, like weather — but she paused at the doorway and looked at Paloma for a long moment before speaking.

"You count them," Sister Bram said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Count what?"

"Don't." The sister came closer, set her lantern on the shelf. Its light made the chalk dust glow between them like a swarm of tiny pale insects. "Don't do that. You know what I mean. I've seen you writing in that little book. I've seen you lift that board."

Paloma's hands went still in the mortar. The pestle clicked against the bowl, once, and then silence, except for the wind.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Good." Sister Bram's face, half in shadow, might have been carved from the same gray wood as the hospital walls. "Because if you did know — if such a book existed — then you'd be a fool, Kavanagh. A brave fool, which is the worst kind. Brave fools get sent east. And you've seen what happens to nurses they send east."

Everyone had seen what happened to nurses sent east. They came back different, or they didn't come back, and either way their names were spoken softly, if at all, as though loud speech might summon the attention that had taken them.

"They're men," Paloma said quietly. "They died here. Somebody wrote *died of wounds, returned to duty, transferred* — lies, all of it — and then the line just stops. Their mothers are still setting places at tables."

"And what will your book do for them? Bring them back?"

"No. But it will prove they weren't nothing."

Sister Bram was quiet a long time. Outside, the surf worked at the breakwater stones with the patience of something that had all eternity and intended to use it. Then the older woman picked up her lantern, and her voice, when it came, was lower than the surf.

"The third ward board sticks in wet weather. Oil the hinge side, not the middle — the middle swells and gives you away." She turned to go, then stopped. "I never saw a book, Kavanagh. And neither did you. But if a book existed, it ought to have the dates. Names alone prove nothing. Dates and wards and the treating surgeon, if you can get them. Paper is only as strong as what's written on it."

And she was gone, her lantern swinging away down the corridor, leaving Paloma alone with the chalk dust and the sudden fierce tightness in her chest that was either gratitude or grief or some alloy of the two she had no separate name for.

* * *

The next death came on a Tuesday, in the fourth ward, in the hour before dawn when the lamps burn lowest and the sea fog presses hardest against the glass.

His name was Aldous Wren, and he was nineteen years old, and he had been brought in nine days earlier with a shell wound to the thigh that should have killed him in an hour and instead chose to kill him slowly, generously, giving him nine days in which to talk about his mother's plum orchard and his intention, after the war, to learn the cornet. He talked about the cornet the way other men talked about heaven. At three in the morning his breathing changed, and Paloma, who knew that change the way sailors know weather, sat with him and held his hand and told him the truth, which was that he was not alone, and let him go when the going-time came.

At seven, Sergeant Odo Pell came through with the morning list, his boots leaving wet prints on the boards. He was not a cruel man; he was a tired one, and tiredness had worn him smooth of everything except procedure. He glanced at the fourth ward, consulted his papers.

"Wren," Paloma said, before he could pass. "Aldous Wren. Died at oh-three-forty. Wounds, left thigh. Dr. Imre attending."

Pell looked up. Something flickered behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, or apology, or just the reflex of a man who has already been ordered to do a thing and does not enjoy being reminded of it.

"He's not on my list," Pell said.

"He was in this ward for nine days. He died in this ward this morning. I held his hand."

"Then he's not on my list twice over." Pell lowered his voice, and here was the terrible thing, the thing Paloma would turn over in her mind for nights afterward: he sounded embarrassed. Ashamed, even. "Look — Kavanagh. There's a requisition problem. There's a numbers problem. I write what I'm given to write. His name will be in the battalion book, maybe. Somewhere. It'll exist somewhere."

"Somewhere isn't anywhere. Somewhere is where names go to be lost."

Pell held her gaze for a moment, and she saw that he understood her perfectly, and that understanding changed nothing, and that this — the perfect comprehension married to total helplessness — was the true machinery of the war, running beneath the guns and the transports like a millrace under ice.

"I'm sorry about the boy," he said, and moved on down the ward, calling other names, living names, checking other papers.

Paloma waited until the shift changed and the ward emptied of everyone but sleeping men and the fog at the windows. Then she knelt beside the third-ward cot, pressed her thumb to the pitch-knot, and lifted the board. The oilcloth bundle came up into her hands, soft and familiar as a sleeping animal. She unwrapped it on her knees, took out the pencil, and wrote:

*Aldous Wren, private, 19 yrs. Died 0340, Tuesday, fourth ward. Shell wound, left thigh. Dr. Imre attending. Nine days in care. Talked of his mother's orchard and meant to learn the cornet.*

She hesitated over the last sentence. Names, ranks, times — those were evidence. Dates and wards and surgeons, as Sister Bram said; paper was only as strong as what was written on it. But the orchard, the cornet — what were those? Sentiment. Weight without function.

She wrote them anyway. Because the officers' lists recorded what the men had suffered, and nothing of what they had been, and if her four ounces of paper were going to carry forty-one — forty-two now — men across whatever water lay ahead, then it would carry them whole. Not just the fact of their deaths. The proof of their lives.

She wrapped the ledger, slid it back into the earth-smelling dark beneath the board, and pressed the wood down until it seated with a sound like a door closing gently in another room.

* * *

That afternoon the supply wagons came up from the harbor, and Paloma stood in the yard with the other nurses while crates were unloaded, and this was ordinary, this happened every week, and she had never paid it much mind beyond noting whether the crates held the morphine they'd been promised or the quinine or the clean lint. But today, standing in the thin cold sun with her hands chapped from washing, she found herself watching the men who handled the crates. Watching the way the quartermaster's clerk moved along the wagon beds with his clipboard, ticking items, his pen scratching in the cold. Watching the numbers stenciled on the crate sides — shipment markings, lot numbers, the dull administrative poetry of logistics.

One of the crates slipped as it came off the tailboard. It cracked open on the cobbles, and out tumbled — not the dressings the manifest said it held, Paloma saw the manifest pinned to the crate lid, *field dressings, 200 ea* — but ballast stones. Smooth gray harbor stones, packed in straw like eggs, like something precious.

The clerks were on it in a heartbeat, laughing, swearing, repacking it before anyone else could look too closely. *Mislabelled at the depot,* one of them called out, easy as anything. *Happens all the time.* And the crate went into the storehouse with the others, and the manifest stayed pinned to its lid saying *dressings, 200 each*, and the yard went back to its noise and its cold and its ordinary work.

But Paloma stood very still in the middle of it, and felt the ground tilt slightly beneath her, the way it tilts on a ship when the swell changes direction.

Ballast where dressings should be. Two hundred field dressings that existed on paper and nowhere else — unless they existed somewhere they shouldn't, sold or diverted or traded, while the paper traveled on to hospitals like hers, to wards like hers, to boys like Aldous Wren, whose thigh wound might have been held by a proper dressing instead of boiled rags if two hundred of them had actually arrived when the manifest swore they had.

It was one crate. It could have been one mistake. Depots made mistakes; the clerk had laughed as though mistakes were the most natural thing in the world.

But Paloma had spent two years learning to read patterns the way surgeons read wounds — and a single wound might be chance, but a pattern was a disease, and diseases had sources. Already, unbidden, her memory was moving backward through the weeks, through the mornings, through the casualty lists and the missing names, searching for a thread. Forty-two men, struck off the rolls. And shipments, shipments, always shipments, logged and manifested and stamped —

She caught herself. Deliberately, she smoothed her face into the blank competent mask the sisters wore, picked up a crate-end with the other nurses, and carried her share into the storehouse. Inside, among the shadows and the straw and the smell of tar, she passed close to the clerk's clipboard where it hung on its nail by the door, and let her eyes move across it without appearing to look at all.

Every crate in the yard, every shipment of the past month, logged through the same office. The same hand.

*Quartermaster's office — E. Brandt,* said the stamp at the top of the page, neat and blue and official as a signature on a treaty.

That night, after lights-out, Paloma knelt at the third-ward board with her heart beating hard and slow, like footsteps coming up a stair, and opened the ledger, and began — carefully, in her smallest hand — to copy shipping numbers beside the names of the dead.
