## 2. What the Officers Omit

The rain came in off the harbor the way it always did in late autumn, sideways and mean, rattling the hospital's east windows like a beggar demanding entry. Paloma Kavanagh had learned to read weather by sound before she'd learned to read words, and this rain said: days of it, mud deepening in the yard, the stove coal damp, the wounded arriving wet.

She stood at the dispensary counter with the morning casualty list spread before her, a single sheet of foolscap covered in an adjutant's cramped hand, and she counted.

It was a habit now, the counting. It had begun innocently enough, the way most dangerous things begin — as arithmetic done to pass the hours between one bleeding man and the next. The garrison hospital at Cape Ferren held two hundred beds and usually three hundred patients, and every morning the adjutant delivered the official list: men admitted, men discharged, men transferred, men dead. The dead were listed with their companies and their causes, and at the bottom of each page came the total, written twice and initialed, as if the number itself required witnessing.

For months Paloma had simply copied the totals into the back of her own notebook, the way she copied pulse rates and dosages — a nurse's instinct for record-keeping, nothing more. But somewhere in the sixth week, without deciding to, she had begun comparing. The morning count of empty beds against the evening count of filled ones. The ward ledger's tally of the dead against the adjutant's total. The names Sister Agnes spoke over the burial cart against the names that appeared on the official sheet.

They did not match.

Not wildly, not suspiciously so at first glance — that was what made it clever, whoever had arranged it. The discrepancy was never more than four or five men in any week. A clerk glancing at both documents would see two lists agreeing within the margin of ordinary error, because errors happened; men died of wounds recorded as dysentery, died in transfer and were logged elsewhere, died unrecorded entirely in the chaos of a field station. Any single week's difference could be explained a dozen ways.

But Paloma had been keeping her own numbers for eleven weeks now, and eleven weeks of small differences added up to thirty-one men who had died at Cape Ferren and did not exist on paper.

Thirty-one men whose mothers would receive no letter. Whose pay would stop without explanation. Who had been, in the eyes of the army that fed them bad bread and pointed them at enemy guns, quietly subtracted.

* * *

"You're doing it again."

Sister Agnes's voice came from behind her, dry as the lint they pressed from old bandages. Paloma slid the casualty list under a stack of linen invoices and turned. The old nun stood in the doorway with a basin of gray water balanced on her hip, her wimple dark with rain.

"Doing what?"

"Counting." Agnes set the basin down with a clank. "You get a look on your face when you count. Like a magpie that's found a coin." She began wringing out a cloth, her knuckles swollen and sure. "The beds need stripping before the new convoy arrives. Ward Three has lice again, which means Ward Three has been lying about the lice for a week, which means you'll strip those beds yourself because I've done my years of lying about lice."

Paloma almost smiled. Almost. "Sister. When Private Ondrusek died last Tuesday — the one with the belly wound from the ridge action — was he entered in the ward book?"

Agnes's hands did not pause, but something in her shoulders did, a stillness passing through her like a draft through a door left ajar. "He was."

"And the official list says he died Wednesday. Of fever. At the annex."

"The annex," Agnes repeated. There was a long moment in which the only sounds were water dripping from the wrung cloth and the rain against the glass. The old woman finally looked up, and her eyes were not unkind but they were wary, the way a person is wary near a thing that might go off. "Girl. What are you asking me?"

"I'm not asking anything. I'm telling you what I wrote down." Paloma kept her voice level, kept her hands busy sorting gauze, because busy hands made honest-seeming faces. "Ondrusek died Tuesday at half past nine in the evening. I held his hand. I wrote the time myself. And the army says he died a day later, a mile away, of a different thing entirely."

"Then the army made a clerical error."

"Eleven weeks of them?"

The cloth stopped moving. Agnes crossed the room in three steps, took Paloma by the wrist — not cruelly, but with a grip like a tourniquet — and drew her close, so that the smell of carbolic and wet wool was all there was in the world.

"Listen to me," the old nun said, very quietly. "I have nursed through three wars and one famine, and in every one of them there were men who vanished off the books, and in every one of them there were women like you who noticed, and I will tell you where those women are now. They are in garrisons a hundred miles from anywhere, counting bedpans until their hands fall off, or they are married off to quartermasters, or they are gone entirely, and nobody asks where, because nobody remembers them, because that is rather the point." Her grip tightened. "You are twenty-four years old and you are good at your work and the wounded love you. Do not make yourself into a question."

"A question?"

"A question mark. That is all a troublesome woman is, in the end. A punctuation problem for some major to solve." She released Paloma's wrist and stepped back, smoothing her apron, her voice returning to its ordinary dryness as though nothing had passed between them. "Ward Three. Lice. Go."

Paloma went. But she went with the word *eleven weeks* sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she knew that Agnes had heard it too, and had chosen, in her way, to warn her rather than report her — which meant that Agnes had counted too, once. Perhaps still did.

* * *

The pattern revealed itself on a Thursday, in the laundry yard, of all places.

Rain again — it had hardly stopped in a fortnight — and Paloma was pinning sheets beneath a leaking awning when Corporal Devane of the transport section ducked under cover to light the pipe he wasn't supposed to smoke on duty. Devane was a talkative, decent sort with frostbitten ears and a wife in the northern provinces, and he complained the way other men breathed, continuously and without malice. Paloma handed him her matches because he always lost his, and he grunted thanks, and then he groaned at the sky.

"Look at this muck. Third shipment this month come in soaked through. Crates marked as sealed, mind you — waxed canvas, tarred rope — and inside, everything sodden. Bandages ruined. Splints warped. Half the flour gone green." He shook his head, sending rainwater flying. "And yet old Brandt signs for it all clean as Sunday. Not a claim filed, not a protest raised. Just 'received in good order' and his signature, every blessed time."

"Brandt," Paloma said. The name snagged on something. "Elias Brandt? The quartermaster?"

"That's him. Twenty-two years in service, they say. Man's a fixture. You could set a clock by his requisitions." Devane puffed his pipe alight, cupping the flame against the wind. "Between us, I think his eyes are going. Signs whatever's put in front of him. Easier that way, maybe. A man his age doesn't want the fights that come with refusing a shipment."

"He receives supplies for the whole garrison?"

"Everything but ammunition. Medical stores, rations, clothing, fodder. Comes down from the depot at Vellmark on the coast road, and Brandt logs it in, and half of it rots or rusts or walks off, and none of it is ever anyone's fault." Devane shrugged with the vast fatalism of a career soldier. "War's a leaky bucket, miss. You just hope you're drinking upstream of the holes."

He touched his cap and sloshed back into the rain, and Paloma stood alone among the flapping sheets with her heart going oddly fast.

Because she had seen the name Elias Brandt before. Not on supply manifests — she had no reason to read supply manifests — but in the margin of a different document, in her own careful pencil, in the notebook that lived beneath a floorboard under her cot, wrapped in oilcloth beside a spare pair of boots.

She had begun, three weeks ago, recording more than names. The first time had been an accident: Private Halloway, omitted from the rolls, had been buried with a slip of paper in the pocket of his greatcoat — a chit for a replacement uniform, requisitioned through the quartermaster's office, stamped with a date and a batch number. Paloma had copied the numbers without thinking, a nurse's reflex toward completeness, and then had thought: *why not the others?* Every omitted man had passed through the stores at least once, drawn boots or a kit or a blanket. Every death left a paper trail of small transactions, and transactions had numbers, and numbers could be compared.

That night, after lights-out, kneeling on the cold flags of the nurses' quarters with the floorboard pried up and a candle stub shielded by her body, Paloma laid eleven weeks of records side by side and looked, really looked, for the first time.

Thirty-one omitted names. Beside each, where she had managed to find one, a transaction number from the quartermaster's office — a blanket issued, boots replaced, a kit bag drawn, a medicine chest opened. Thirty-one men, forty-some numbers.

And when she arranged them in sequence, oldest to newest, her breath went thin.

Every number fell within a range. Not scattered across the year of garrison accounts as chance would scatter them, but clustered — every transaction traced to stores received during a particular run of shipments, a stretch of some five months, deliveries logged in from the Vellmark depot under a specific series of manifest numbers. Shipments signed for, she now understood, by Quartermaster Elias Brandt. Received in good order. No claims filed.

The men who did not exist had all, in their final months, eaten the bread, worn the boots, slept under the blankets, and taken the medicine from the same leaky bucket. From Brandt's bucket.

Paloma sat back on her heels in the freezing dark and felt the shape of the thing settle over her — not yet its meaning, but its weight. Clerical errors did not cluster around one man's ledgers. Rot did not choose which shipments to spoil. And dead soldiers did not arrange themselves, thirty-one strong, along the margins of a single officer's signature unless someone wanted them arranged there — or unless what killed them had passed through his hands.

She copied the manifest numbers into the ledger. All of them, in her smallest hand, column after column, dates and batch marks and the initials stamped at the Vellmark depot, until the candle guttered and her fingers ached and the page looked less like a nurse's notes than like the cargo plan of a ship carrying something terrible.

Then she put the oilcloth back, and the board, and the rug over the board, and lay awake listening to the rain and to the breathing of the six other nurses in the room, and thought about Devane's easy shrug: *a man his age doesn't want the fights that come with refusing a shipment.*

Or didn't want the fights that came with reporting one.

* * *

Three days later, Dr. Ilves cornered her in the surgery corridor.

He was the senior surgeon, a heavy, gray-faced Livonian who worked eighteen-hour days with the grim economy of a man bailing a boat he expected to sink, and he had never once spoken to Paloma except to issue instructions. So when he stepped out of the dispensary doorway as she passed and said, quietly, "A word, Nurse Kavanagh," she felt the floor tilt beneath her before he said another thing.

He walked her ten paces down the corridor, to the window where the rain ran silver down the glass and the harbor fog was coming in to swallow the masts.

"I'm going to say something once," Ilves said, looking out at the water, "and then we will both forget it. You have been asking the transport men questions. You have been reading the morning lists longer than reading requires. And yesterday you requested, through the orderly, access to the ward admission registers going back to spring — registers that are stored in the adjutant's office." He turned his head, and his eyes were tired and old and, unexpectedly, not hostile. "Am I wrong?"

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "I maintain accurate patient records, Doctor. It's part of—"

"It is not part of your duties to cross-reference the dead." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "And before you decide how to answer that, understand what I am not saying. I am not accusing you of anything. I am telling you what happens next." He turned back to the window. "In March, there was a nurse here named Katrin Voss. Clever girl. Quick with figures. She noticed that the morphine counts never reconciled — that we were drawing more doses than the wounded should have required, and losing more men to pain than the stocks explained. She asked the adjutant. Politely, in writing, with columns." His jaw worked. "In April she was transferred to the mountain station at Greben. You know what the roads to Greben are like in April. In May, a letter came for her, misdirected, and I read the direction before returning it. It was addressed to a convalescent home in the south. For a nurse recovering from — I never learned. Something that happened at Greben, or on the way there."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, a burial detail's boots squelched past the window in slow rhythm.

"She asked about medicine," Ilves said. "Medicine, Nurse. The least political substance in the world. And she was removed from the board within the month, and no one spoke of her again, and I did not speak for her, and I have regretted it every day since, and I am telling you this not out of kindness but because I cannot bear to watch it happen twice." Now he did face her fully, and took her by the shoulder, surgeon's grip, brief and hard. "Whatever you think you have found — and I do not know what you have found, and do not tell me — understand that this garrison is not where such things are untangled. This is where such things are *buried*, in every sense, and the shovels are issued by the same office that issues the bandages."

"Then where?" The question escaped her before she could weigh it. "If not here — where does it get untangled?"

Ilves let go of her shoulder. For a long moment he studied her, and something moved behind the exhaustion in his face — something that might have been respect, or pity, or the ghost of a younger man who had once asked the same question.

"Nowhere," he said at last. "Nowhere, usually. Which is why sensible people keep their heads down and their hands clean and thank God when the war ends and they're alive to be silent in." He straightened his apron. "Ward One is waiting. And Nurse—" he paused at the dispensary door, not turning around, "—if you must write things down, write them somewhere no adjutant's clerk would ever think to look. And never, under any circumstance, let anyone know how much you know. Knowledge is only dangerous when it can be located."

He went in, and called for forceps, and the ordinary machinery of the hospital resumed around her.

* * *

That night the rain stopped for the first time in a week, and the cold came down clear and starry, and Paloma knelt on her floor with the candle and the ledger open across her knees.

She had spent the day doing her work, flawlessly, invisibly — changing dressings, holding a dying gunner's head while the morphine did its mercy, laughing at the right moments in the mess tent. All the while, beneath the competence, her mind had turned the day's pieces like a lock mechanism: *Voss, morphine, Greben. Buried, in every sense. The same office that issues the bandages.*

The same office.

She looked down at the ledger, at the thirty-one names marching down the page in her careful script, and beside them now the manifest numbers, the batch marks, the dates, the depot stamps — and at the head of every column, recurring like a refrain, the same initials entered in the receiving log: **E.B.**

Elias Brandt. Twenty-two years of service. A fixture. A man who signed for soaked bandages and green flour and never filed a complaint, whose signature sat at the top of every chain of transactions that ended in an unrecorded grave behind the chapel.

She did not yet know what it meant. Perhaps the man was a fool. Perhaps a thief, skimming stores and letting the shortage eat the weak. Perhaps something worse — perhaps the shortages themselves, the rotten bread and the spoiled dressings and the medicine chests that arrived already broken, were the reason some of these men had died at all, and the omissions were not negligence but architecture, a wall built one missing name at a time.

What she knew, kneeling in the dark with her fingers going numb, was that she was no longer a nurse who happened to notice things. She was the keeper of a parallel record — the only true account, perhaps, of thirty-one men — and the knowing had changed her, the way a splinter changes the whole body around it.

She dipped her pen. Below the last manifest number she wrote, in letters smaller than any others on the page:

*All omissions trace to stores received under E.B., Vellmark series, spring–autumn. Surgeon warns questions = reassignment. Continue. Quietly.*

She blotted the page, folded the oilcloth over the book, and lowered it into the dark beneath the floorboard.

From the men's wing across the yard, faint through the walls, came a sound that pulled her upright — the high, ragged call of a man in fever, rising above the coughing and the restless turning, a voice cracked down to its boards. An orderly's footsteps hurried past her door. Someone said, low and urgent, "Send for Dr. Ilves — it's the quartermaster, it's Brandt, he's burning up, he keeps asking for—"

The voice dropped below hearing. Paloma knelt there in the cold, her hand still resting on the floorboard, her heart striking slow and hard against her ribs, and listened to the fever-shredded voice across the yard calling, over and over, a name she couldn't quite catch —

— and then, distinctly, her own.
