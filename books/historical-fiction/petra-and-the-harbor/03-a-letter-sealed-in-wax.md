## 3. A Letter Sealed in Wax

The fever ward smelled of camphor and salt, and beneath those, of the particular sweetness that Paloma had learned never to name aloud, not even in her own head. She called it *the turn* when she spoke to the other nurses, and they all knew what she meant: the moment a man's sickness stopped fighting him and started keeping him, like a tide that had decided to stay.

Elias Brandt had reached the turn three days ago.

She found him in the corner cot by the drafty window, the one nobody wanted because the wind came through the warped frame all night. He was a quartermaster, which made him unusual among the dying — most of the men in this ward were infantry, their bodies broken at the front or rotted from the trenches along the coast road, but Brandt's war had been fought with ledgers and manifests and the great wax seals of the army supply office. He had come down with the fever two weeks after arriving at the garrison, as if whatever he'd been carrying in his papers had finally gotten into his blood.

"Kavanagh." His voice was a dry rattle, like a cart on a frozen road. He hadn't opened his eyes, but he'd known her footsteps. Everyone in the ward knew her footsteps; she'd been told once by a dying sergeant that she walked like someone counting.

"That's me," she said, settling onto the stool beside him. "How are we this morning, Quartermaster?"

"We're past morning." He almost smiled. His lips were cracked and gray. "Past most things."

She took his wrist — thin now, the pulse running quick and shallow under skin gone the color of old tallow — and did not contradict him. Lying to the dying was a kindness she had never learned to perform. She wet his lips with a cloth dipped in barley water, and he drank what little she offered with the greed of a man who understood exactly how much time he had left to taste anything.

"You were at the supply office yesterday," he said.

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. Yes — she had been there, standing in the corridor outside Captain Aldous's door for the better part of an hour, waiting to ask about requisition order 4471, the one whose numbers matched the shipping marks she'd copied into her ledger. The captain had kept her waiting until she gave up. She had begun to suspect that this was the point of making her wait.

"You walk into rooms," Brandt said, "like you already know something. That's dangerous here."

"I don't know anything."

"No." He opened his eyes then, and they were fever-bright but steady, fixed on her face with an intensity that made her want to look away. "That's the worst of it. You don't. You only half-know. Half-knowledge is what gets people reassigned."

The word again. *Reassigned.* The surgeon Wren had used it two nights ago, leaning close over the operating table where a boy of nineteen was being opened up for shrapnel, saying it quietly enough that only she could hear: *Nurse Kavanagh, some questions are answered for you, whether you ask them or not.* She had thought about that sentence every day since, turning it over the way one turns over a stone to see what lives underneath.

"I'm not asking you anything, Quartermaster," she said.

"No. But you're going to." He coughed, a long, tearing effort that bent him nearly double, and when it passed she held the basin while he spat rust-colored sputum into it. "And I'm going to be dead before I can decide whether answering you would be a mercy or a murder. So I've decided to let the letter do it."

Paloma went still. Outside the window, gulls wheeled over the gray water of the harbor, screaming their idiot hunger, indifferent to everything inside this room.

"What letter?"

He didn't answer immediately. Instead he lifted one trembling hand and pointed, or tried to point, toward the foot of his cot, where his kit lay — a canvas bag stamped with the supply office's mark, half-hidden beneath a folded blanket. "Under the false bottom," he whispered. "I haven't the strength. You'll have to."

Every instinct she had — and she had spent eighteen months sharpening them in this place, where instincts were the only instruments that couldn't be confiscated — told her to stand up, smooth her apron, and go check on the man in the next bed. But her hands were already moving, pulling the blanket aside, unbuttoning the canvas flap. The false bottom came away with a sound like a sigh.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a single envelope. Heavy paper. A blob of sealing wax the color of dried blood pressed with no seal at all — just the flat impression of a thumbnail, as if whoever had closed it had wanted to be certain no one could pretend it came from anyone official. It was addressed to no one. There was no name on its face, no regimental number, nothing. Just the paper, the wax, and whatever waited inside like a stone at the bottom of a well.

"Quartermaster Brandt," she said carefully, "I can't take—"

"You can." He had pushed himself up against the headboard with what must have cost him the last reserves of a body already bankrupt. "You will. Listen to me, nurse. Listen, because I won't say this twice — I may not have the breath to say it twice."

She leaned closer. Around them, the ward breathed and moaned and slept its feverish sleep, and no one looked their way. Dying men had no curiosity left over for other people's secrets.

"There was a convoy," Brandt said. "Two years ago. Winter convoy, out of Corvane, bound for the southern garrisons. Crates marked as quinine, morphine, surgical silk, carbolic. Do you understand? The winter convoy. The one that arrived with ballast stones instead."

Paloma's mouth went dry. She knew about the winter convoy the way everyone at the garrison knew about it — as a story told in low voices, a disaster blamed on storms and enemy raiders and the fog of war, a tragedy that had filled wards like this one with men who died of gangrene because there had been no carbolic, of malaria because there had been no quinine. She had held the hands of men who'd been shipped to die by that empty space in the crates. She had written names in her ledger — eleven of them, twelve, she couldn't remember without looking, and she would not look, not now, not in front of him — of soldiers who had starved and rotted and burned with fever while the official reports recorded their crates as *delivered in good order.*

"The manifests were signed," Brandt went on, his voice dropping until she had to bend her ear nearly to his lips to catch the words. "Signed at every station. Signed by clerks who never counted, by officers who never looked, by port officials who—" He broke off, coughing again, and this time the fit left him gasping, his chest heaving, sweat standing out on his forehead like beads of oil.

"Port officials," she repeated softly. It wasn't a question either. It was a hook, and she felt it go in.

"In that envelope is everything. Names. Dates. The substitution schedule. Who ordered it, who signed, who got paid, and how the payments moved through the harbor office at—" He stopped. His eyes had drifted past her shoulder, toward the ward door, and something in his face changed — not fear, exactly. Recognition. Resignation. "They'll tell you it was wartime necessity," he said. "They'll tell you the crates were short-weighted, miscounted, lost at sea. They have been telling themselves that for two years. But there are copies, Kavanagh. Copies that weren't supposed to survive. And names."

"Whose names?"

His hand closed around her wrist — surprisingly strong, the grip of a man holding on to a rail in high seas. "I need you to promise me something first."

"I'm not promising a dying man anything before I know what it costs."

A ghost of a smile crossed his ruined face. "Good girl. All right. Then hear this instead. When you read it — and you will read it, tonight, alone, with the door barred — you're going to wish you'd never touched it. You're going to wish you'd let me die quiet and let the paper burn. Promise me you won't burn it. Whatever you find in there. Promise me the paper doesn't burn."

"Why?" Her voice came out thinner than she intended. "What could possibly be in it that—"

"It names the Kavanaghs."

* * *

The words landed in her like cold water poured down the back of the neck. For a moment the ward seemed to tilt, the cots and the moaning men and the gray light all sliding sideways, and she gripped the edge of his cot to keep her balance.

"That's not possible," she said.

"Nothing is possible," Brandt whispered. "That's why it works. Nobody checks the impossible. Your uncle signs manifests at Corvane harbor — don't look at me like that, nurse, quartermasters read personnel postings the way priests read scripture. Tomas Kavanagh, deputy harbormaster, countersigns outbound military freight. His name is on four of the winter convoy documents. Four. And there's a fifth signature, a family signature, that appears on the payment routing. A K. Initial only. But initials open doors when someone wants them opened."

"My uncle countersigns hundreds of documents a month. That means nothing. It proves nothing."

"It proves nothing," he agreed. "Until it's laid next to the other names. Then it proves everything, or it damns everything, depending on who's doing the laying. That's the thing about paper, Kavanagh. Paper doesn't care about innocence. Paper only cares about proximity." He sagged back against the pillow, spent, his breathing going ragged. "Your brother's initial too. Devlin. He works the tally house, doesn't he? Apprentice clerk?"

"He's nineteen," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the number. "He counts barrels. He doesn't know what's in them."

"Neither did most of them." Brandt's eyes were closing. "That's the whole architecture of it, nurse. A hundred men each knowing one small piece, none of them seeing the shape. The shape was seen by maybe six people alive, and I'm one, and by tomorrow I won't be."

She sat very still. Somewhere across the ward a man began to cry out in his sleep, calling for his mother in a dialect she half recognized, and no one stirred to comfort him because everyone had learned that the sleeping were beyond comforting. Paloma held the envelope in both hands and felt its weight — nothing, really, a few sheets of heavy paper, less than a deck of cards — and yet it seemed to pull downward like an anchor.

"If this is true," she said slowly, "then my family's name is on the same paper as the names of every man I've watched die of bad water and no medicine. Do you understand what you're giving me?"

"Yes," Brandt said. "Better than you do, yet."

"And you're giving it to me anyway. Why me? Why not the chaplain, or the provost, or—"

"Because the chaplain reports to the same colonel who signed the third document. Because the provost's brother-in-law is on the payment routing. Because I've spent two years watching every honest channel get sealed shut one by one, like portholes before a storm." He opened his eyes one last time, and they were clear now — the fever burning away whatever was left of the man and leaving only the quartermaster, the keeper of accounts, determined that the final entry in his own ledger should balance. "But you write down the names they leave off the reports. Don't deny it. I've seen your little book, nurse. I've known about your little book since the day you started it. You think you hide it well. You hide it adequately."

Heat crawled up her neck. "You spied on—"

"I audited," he said, and this time the smile was real, crooked and tired and strangely gentle. "Someone in this army has to. You're the only person I've met in two years who writes down what actually happened. So. The paper doesn't burn. Swear it."

"I swear," she said, because she could not do otherwise. Some oaths happen to you rather than being chosen, and this was one of them.

"Good." He exhaled, long and slow. "Then take it and go, before the change of watch. And Kavanagh — when you find out what your family did or didn't do — remember that finding out is not the same as knowing. Read it twice. Then read it again. And then decide who you are."

* * *

She finished her shift in a haze. She changed dressings she did not remember changing, spooned broth into mouths she did not remember feeding, and twice caught herself standing motionless in the middle of the ward with the envelope's weight pressing against her ribs where she had hidden it, flat against her stomach, beneath her uniform blouse and the wide bandage she wore — legitimately, she was glad to note even now — around her waist from an old injury. The bandage made a pocket. The pocket made a hiding place. Eighteen months of habit had built her body into a filing cabinet, and now it held the one document she feared more than any inspection.

At the change of watch, Sister Marguerite stopped her at the door. "You look ill, Nurse Kavanagh."

"Just tired, Sister."

"Tired is a kind of ill." The older woman studied her with pale, unhurried eyes. "Whatever you're carrying, put it down somewhere safe before you try to sleep. Things carried too long start to carry us."

Paloma managed a smile that she hoped looked like agreement, and went out into the evening.

Her quarters were a converted linen closet off the laundry corridor, six feet by eight, with a cot, a stool, and a window the size of a hymnal that looked out on the drying yard. She locked the door. She wedged the stool under the handle, which was theater — the lock was adequate, the stool was ritual — and then she sat on the edge of the cot for a long moment, the envelope on her knees, the lamp turned low.

Outside, the garrison settled into its night noises: boots on gravel, the distant challenge of sentries, the sea working at the harbor pilings below the cliff. Somewhere in the officers' wing, a piano played badly, someone attempting a waltz with more enthusiasm than accuracy. The world continued being ordinary. It seemed obscene.

She broke the seal.

The thumbnail impression split cleanly, and the flap came up, and inside were nine pages in Brandt's cramped, precise hand — the hand of a man who had spent decades writing figures that other people's lives depended on — plus three documents that had clearly been copied, line for line, from originals: a manifest from the winter convoy, a requisition amendment, and a payment authorization routed through the Corvane harbor trust. She read the manifest first. Forty-two crates entered as medical stores. Arrival inventory: thirty-one crates of ballast stone, eleven crates missing entirely. Delivered in good order, per receiving officer. Countersigned at origin: T. Kavanagh, Deputy Harbormaster.

*T. Kavanagh.*

Her uncle's handwriting was on the copy — she knew it, had known it since childhood, the distinctive way he crossed his sevens, the little flourish on the *T*. She had watched that hand sign birthday letters. She had watched it carve the roast at Sunday dinner. She had watched it, two years ago at Devlin's apprenticeship celebration, raise a glass of plum brandy and toast the family: *May we always be worth our signatures.*

She read the second document. The requisition amendment, dated three weeks before the convoy sailed, substituting "miscellaneous harbor materials" for "medical stores, priority class" — authorized by a signature she didn't recognize, witnessed by a clerk's mark, and noted at the bottom in a different ink, added later: *payment per arrangement, ref. K.*

*Ref. K.*

Referral K. Reference K. The letter sat there on the page like a splinter working toward the heart. An initial. Nothing but an initial. And yet she could think of three people it might mean and two of them shared her blood and the third was herself, which was absurd, which was impossible, which was the sort of thought that came from reading damning pages alone at midnight with a low lamp.

She read the third document, the payment authorization, and this was where the scheme showed its full anatomy: sums drawn against phantom losses, insurance claims on cargo that had never existed, the money moving through the harbor trust in increments small enough to escape audit, and at the end of each routing line, the same terminal notation — *per K., approved* — in a hand that was not Tomas's. Smaller. Tighter. A hand she almost, almost recognized, and could not place, and the failure to place it frightened her more than everything else combined, because it meant the hand was near. It meant she saw it often.

The ninth page was Brandt's own summary, written in the style of a man composing a deposition he expected to be read after his death. He named five conspirators by role rather than rank: the Originator (the requisition amendment's author), the Harbor Hand (Tomas Kavanagh), the Tally Clerk (unnamed, "a youth, unwitting"), the Receiver (the officer who certified ballast as medicine), and the Paymaster ("the hand behind ref. K."). Of the five, only the Harbor Hand was named outright. Only the Harbor Hand was, as far as Brandt knew, innocent of understanding. "Kavanagh signed under threat," Brandt had written. "Threat of what, I never learned. But a threatened man's signature is still a signature, and history does not footnote coercion."

*History does not footnote coercion.* She read the sentence four times. She thought of her uncle's face at Sunday dinners, the careful way he never discussed work, the year he'd stopped laughing easily — she had attributed it to age, to the war, to the gray weather of middle life. She thought of Devlin counting barrels in the tally house, believing himself part of the war effort, part of something honorable, his name reduced to an initial on a payment route he'd never heard of.

And she thought of the ledger under her floorboard. Eleven names from the winter convoy. Twelve. Thirteen if you counted Sergeant Imre Halloran, who had survived the gangrene by losing the arm and then died anyway, of fever, in March, unnamed in every report ever filed.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick with fingers that were not quite steady.

Brandt was right about one thing: reading it was not knowing it. She read the nine pages twice more, as instructed, and by the third reading she had stopped hoping to find an error, an ambiguity, a merciful gap. The papers were coherent. Coherent the way a machine is coherent. Every gear meshed: the amendment, the swap, the ballast, the signatures, the money, the silence. Two years of silence, maintained by threat above and ignorance below, and in the middle of it, holding the door open with his terrified, coerced signature, her uncle Tomas — and trailing behind him like a child holding his coat, her brother Devlin, guilty of nothing except the crime of being countable.

Near dawn, when the garrison bugle sounded the first call and the sky over the harbor went the color of dishwater, Paloma did the thing she had done every night for a year and a half. She knelt, pried up the floorboard beneath the cot, and lifted out her ledger — the small, grease-stained book in which she had written, in a hand as neat as prayer, the names of the omitted dead.

She opened it to the winter convoy page. Thirteen names. She read them once, aloud, barely voiced, the way one reads over graves.

Then she took up her pen, and beneath the thirteenth name, in the margin, she wrote a single line:

*The paper does not burn.*

She put the ledger back beneath the floorboard, and the letter — wrapped again in its oilcloth — went into the lining of her spare uniform coat, sewn flat between wool and cotton where no search would think to feel. Not under her floorboard. Under her floorboard was where things went when she trusted the room. This, she realized, she already trusted to no room at all.

In the ward that morning, Elias Brandt's cot stood stripped, the blanket folded at its foot with hospital neatness. Sister Marguerite told her he had died at four in the afternoon the previous day — hours, Paloma calculated, before he had pressed the envelope into her hands, before the ward's records said he could have spoken at all. She mentioned this to no one. The dead kept their own schedules in places like this, arriving at the last moment they could manage and leaving before the paperwork caught up.

At noon, a runner brought word down to the wards: the supply office requested any personal effects Quartermaster Brandt might have entrusted to staff. Routine, the runner said. Inventory reconciliation.

Paloma smiled at the young man, thanked him, and said she'd be sure to ask around.

That evening she wrote the first new entry her ledger had taken in weeks, and it was not a soldier's name. It was a question, underlined twice:

*Who is K?*

And beneath it, because dread always travels with its twin, she wrote the second question, the one she had been refusing since midnight:

*How soon can I get home?*
