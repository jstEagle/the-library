## 4. The Family in the Middle

The leave pass was signed by a surgeon who never looked up from his instruments. *Sister Kavanagh, ten days, family emergency.* There had been no family emergency — not one anyone could name aloud — but Paloma had learned that a lie written in a hospital's own ink traveled farther than any truth she could carry in her mouth. She folded the pass into her bodice, next to the letter sealed in black wax that had lived against her skin for six days now, warm as a second heartbeat.

The coach north took two days and a night. She sat wedged between a fishwife who smelled of brine and a young lieutenant with a bandaged hand who talked ceaselessly about the coming peace, how the historians were already circling like gulls over a gutted whale. Paloma nodded at intervals. The letter pressed its small hard rectangle into her ribs each time the wheels struck a rut, and each time she thought of Elias Brandt's fingers on her wrist — hot, trembling, insistent — and the word he had spent his last breath on.

*Kavanagh.*

Not Kavanagh's. Not the Kavanaghs, plural, scattered across some clerk's ledger. He had said it the way a man names a single thing he has carried too long: *the Kavanaghs.*

She watched the coastline slide past the fogged glass — the same coast she had grown up counting ships on, Devlin beside her with his arm raised against the sun, calling out flags before their father could. Somewhere behind her, south along that gray water, lay the garrison hospital with its omitted men, its quiet graves, its ledger hidden beneath a floorboard where only she knew to kneel. Somewhere ahead lay home, which had always been the place where difficult things went to be forgiven rather than examined.

She did not want to arrive. She arrived anyway.

* * *

Harrow Point had not changed, which felt like an accusation. The harbor still curved like a cupped hand; the customs house still squatted at its lip with its green shutters and its brass plaque, *PORT AUTHORITY — T. KAVANAGH*, polished so faithfully that it caught the light from a hundred yards off. Gulls wheeled over the drying nets. A boy led a mule loaded with salt barrels down the cobbled ramp, whistling something tuneless.

Uncle Tomas was in his office above the weigh-house, exactly where he had been every working day of Paloma's childhood, bent over manifests with a ruler and a pot of black ink. He looked smaller than memory kept him. His hair had gone entirely white at the temples, and there was a tremor in his left hand that he hid by pressing it flat against the desk when he thought no one watched.

He saw her in the doorway and stood too fast, knocking the inkpot so it bled a slow black star across the manifest beneath it.

"Paloma." Her name came out of him strangely, half welcome and half wound. "Nobody said you were coming."

"Nobody knew." She closed the door behind her. The latch clicked with a sound far louder than it should have been. "I need to talk to you, Uncle. Alone."

He wiped his hands on his coat, though they were clean. It was an old habit of his, she remembered — wiping hands that were already clean, as if cleanliness were a thing that required maintenance. "Your mother—"

"Is at market. I passed her. I'll see her after." She crossed the small room and stood before his desk, and found she could not sit, because sitting would make this a visit, and it was not a visit. "Elias Brandt," she said. "Quartermaster, Third Supply Division. Do you know the name?"

Something moved behind his face — quick, submerged, gone — like a fish turning under ice. "Should I?"

"He's dying. Fever. And before he—" She stopped. Steadied. "Before he died, or near enough, he gave me a letter. Sealed. In it, according to him, are shipping records. Medicine consignments out of Harrow Point, logged and signed, that never contained medicine. Ballast instead. Crates weighted with sand and stone, stamped and manifested as quinine, as morphine, as surgical lint." Her voice held. She was surprised by that. "He said the name on those manifests was Kavanagh."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the boy with the mule had stopped whistling. Down on the ramp, someone called a price, and someone else laughed.

Tomas sat down slowly, the way old men sit when the body has decided before the mind has. He looked at the ruined manifest, at the spreading black star, and made no move to blot it.

"Where is the letter now?" he asked.

"On me."

"Then listen to me carefully, girl." He raised his eyes, and what she saw in them frightened her more than anger would have — it was exhaustion, deep and structural, the weariness of a wall that has been holding back water for years. "Burn it. Unopened. Tonight, in your mother's stove, and put salt on the ashes."

"You knew." It was not a question. She heard it land in him like a stone in a well.

"I knew what I knew," he said. "Which was less than you think and more than I wanted."

"Then tell me. All of it. Because I have sat at the bedsides of men whose wounds went septic for want of the medicine that was supposed to be in those crates. I have written letters to mothers describing deaths I could not explain, because the official explanation was *fever* and the true explanation was that we cut into men without chloroform and sewed them with thread we boiled twice because there was nothing else." Her voice cracked at last, and she let it. "Three hundred men, Uncle. Maybe more. Brandt couldn't count them all before the fever took his numbers away."

"They told me it wouldn't be like that." The words came out of him low and fast now, a dam failing in small places all at once. "In the beginning it was — you understand how these things begin? A man comes to your office. Well-dressed. Army collar, but the wrong army, if you follow me — the kind of officer who exists in the space between regiments. He has papers. He has recommendations signed by people whose signatures open doors you've knocked on your whole life. And he says: the port authority here is understaffed, underpaid, and about to be reorganized. There will be a review. Men lose posts in reviews. Your brother's widow lives in this town. Your nephew wants a commission." He rubbed his clean hands together. "And then he says: all you must do is sign what's placed in front of you. You're signing for goods received. That's all. Goods received, goods forwarded. The weights are checked by other men. The seals are applied by other men. You are a signature, Tomas. Nothing more."

"And you signed."

"I signed." He said it flatly, without asking forgiveness. "For two years I signed. And then the war turned, and the convoys started going north to the front instead of along the coast, and I began to understand what I was signing for. I tried to stop. I wrote a letter of refusal." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter ever went to funerals. "The man came to my house. Sat in your mother's kitchen. Drank her tea. Talked about my girls — about your cousins — the way a man talks about weather. Then he left, and the next week the review board cleared me, praised me even, and the manifests kept coming, and I kept signing, because I understood at last that the choice was never between signing and not signing. It was between signing and being erased. And an erased man cannot protect anyone."

"So you protected yourself," Paloma said, "and buried strangers."

"Yes." He met her eyes. It cost him visibly; she watched the cost move through his face like weather. "Yes. And I have told myself every night since that I had no choice, and every morning I have known that was a lie, and I have signed anyway. That is what I am now. A man who knows the difference and signs anyway." He pushed back from the desk. "So burn the letter. Because the people behind Brandt — and Brandt was nothing, a middleman, a pair of hands — the people behind him are not going to stand in a tribunal and confess. They are going to do what was done to me, but faster and finaler. They will erase you, Paloma. Not kill you — erase you. Reassign you, discredit you, lose your records, lose *you*. And your name—" He stopped. Started again, softer. "Our name. It's the only thing your father left us intact. Don't hand it to them to break."

"It's already broken," she said quietly. "It broke in the mouths of dying men who waited for medicine that was sand." She stood there a moment longer, looking at him — at the ink-star bleeding across the manifest, at his white temples, at the tremor he had stopped hiding now that there was no point. "I loved you my whole life, Uncle Tomas. I need you to know that whatever happens, that doesn't change. But I'm not burning anything."

She left him sitting in the small room with his ruined paperwork, and went down the stairs into the salt-smelling afternoon to find her brother.

* * *

Devlin was on the beach below the point, mending net with their cousin's boys, and seeing him there — sleeves rolled, hair bleached lighter by the same sun that had bleached it when they were children — nearly undid her. He looked up, saw her, and his whole face opened the way it always had, unguarded, immediate, a door that had never once been locked against her.

"Pally!" He dropped the net and came up the shingle two steps at a time and lifted her clean off the ground, the way he had when she was nine and he was fourteen and the world was only tides and races and whose boots were muddier. "Ten days! Mother will feed you until you can't walk. Why didn't you wire?"

"No time." She laughed despite everything — his joy was a tide, it pulled at her whether she willed it or not. Then she stepped back and looked at him, twenty-two years old and broad through the shoulders, wearing their father's old jersey, and thought: *how do I say this to you. How does anyone say this to anyone.*

They walked the beach while the light went long and gold. She told him in pieces, watching his face change. First the garrison, the omitted names, the ledger — and he frowned at that, troubled but abstractly, the way one is troubled by distant storms. Then Brandt, the fever, the letter. Then the manifests. Then the name.

He stopped walking.

"No." He said it simply, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "No, Pally. That's not — you've got it wrong somewhere. Some clerk misheard him. He was feverish, you said so yourself, out of his head. Dying men say all kinds of things."

"He said it clearly. Twice."

"Some other Kavanagh, then. There are Kavanaghs in three counties."

"Not Kavanaghs. *The* Kavanaghs. Our Kavanaghs." She kept her voice level. "And I spoke to Uncle Tomas this afternoon, Dev. He didn't deny it. He confessed it. Two years of manifests. Signed under threat, yes — threatened, coerced, trapped, call it what you want — but signed. In our name."

Devlin stood very still, looking out at the water where the fishing boats were coming in with the evening, their lamps just lit, small and patient on the darkening sea. When he turned back to her, his face had closed — not against her, she realized, but around something. Around the idea itself, the way a body closes around a wound.

"Tomas was threatened," he said. "You said so. He was a victim, not — he didn't *do* anything. He signed papers he couldn't read the truth of. That's not our name being tainted, that's our name being *used*. There's a difference, and you of all people should know it."

"There is a difference," she agreed. "But the mothers receiving the letters won't read it that way. And neither will the tribunals, when they come."

"*If* they come." He kicked at the shingle, and his voice rose, and she heard in it not anger but fear wearing anger's clothes. "Pally, listen to yourself. You're a nurse on leave. You've got a dead man's letter and a — what, a notebook? — and you're talking about tribunals. Against whom? Against officers with connections in the capital, against port authorities, against the whole supply chain of an army that's about to *win this war*. You know what happens to winners' wars? Winners write them. Nobody's going to thank you. Nobody's going to publish you. They're going to bury you alongside the story, and our name goes in the grave with you, and Mam—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Mam has buried enough. Da, and the twins, and Grandad. Don't make her bury the family name too."

The words went into her like cold water into a boot — slow, total, unavoidable. Because he was not wrong. That was the terrible thing. Every practical thing he said was true, and she could feel the truth of it pulling at her the way the riptide pulled at swimmers off this very beach, the way it had drowned two tourists when she was a girl while the whole town stood on the shore unable to reach them.

"Devlin," she said. "There were men. In the ward, on the convoy route, in the field hospitals up and down that coast. Men who died holding a nurse's hand and believing someone would tell their mothers why. I have their names. I have two hundred and eleven of them in my own handwriting. If I burn that letter — if I go back and keep my head down and let the archives seal and the historians write their clean little chapters — then I am doing exactly what Uncle Tomas did. Signing anyway. Knowing the difference, and signing anyway."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. But fairness isn't what loyalty means, Dev. Loyalty isn't protecting the family's good name. Loyalty is telling the truth inside the family first, so that when the world comes asking, we aren't caught lying to each other." She took his hands. They were rough with net-work and cold from the evening air, and they trembled slightly in hers, and she understood that he was not refusing her — he was terrified for her, and terror in a young man speaks as certainty. "I'm not asking you to fight. I'm asking you to believe me. That's all. Believe that I read what I read, and heard what I heard, and that our name is already in this — and that the only question left is whether it appears in the record as a signature or as a confession."

For a long moment he said nothing. Behind him the boats came in, lamp by lamp, and the gulls settled on the tideline, and somewhere up the hill their mother was lighting the stove and setting a place for a daughter she believed was home for rest.

"I believe you heard something," he said at last, and it was not surrender, and they both knew it. "I believe you think you know what it means. But Pally — promise me you won't do anything. Not yet. Stay the ten days. Eat Mam's food. Let me write to some people — I know people now, army people, I can ask questions quietly, find out if there's another side to this. Just give me that. Give me time to prove our name clean before you go making it dirty in public."

She looked at her brother, whom she loved more than the harbor loved the sea, and understood that she could not give him what he asked, and that saying so tonight would break something between them that might not mend.

"I'll stay the ten days," she said.

He exhaled, and smiled, and pulled her into his arms, and held her the way he had when they were children — as if holding were the same as keeping safe.

But that night, lying in her narrow childhood bed with the letter a hard secret against her ribs and the floorboard-memory of her ledger two hundred miles south, Paloma listened to the tide working the shingle below the window and counted what she could carry. The letter. The names in her head. The truth, which weighed nothing and everything.

On the third day of her leave, a military courier came up the coast road to Harrow Point with dispatches, and among them was a telegram addressed to Sister P. Kavanagh, care of the Port Authority. She read it standing in her uncle's office, in the very room where he had confessed, while Tomas watched her from behind his desk with the expression of a man watching weather turn.

*GARRISON UNDER EVACUATION ORDERS STOP FIELD RECORDS TO BE DESTROYED ON SITE STOP ALL PERSONNEL REPORT SOUTH BY MONTH'S END STOP*

The army was burning its paper. Everything except what she carried.
