## 5. Orders Come

The orders arrived on a Tuesday, though Paloma would remember it afterward as the day the gulls went quiet.

She was folding bandages in the linen room when the bugle sounded—not the morning call, not mess call, but the long triple note that meant assembly for all ranks and all civilians attached to the garrison. The sound came through the salt-fog the way sounds came through everything at Greywater Point: muffled, delayed, as if the world itself were reluctant to deliver bad news.

She set down the bandage in her hands. It was a good roll of linen, boiled clean, still warm from the press. She had been counting them. She counted everything now—bandages, doses of laudanum, names.

Outside, the parade ground had filled with men who moved wrong for a formation. They stood in loose clusters, talking low, and when Major Corrigan climbed the steps of the administration block with a paper in his hand, a hush went over the yard like frost going over a field.

"By order of the Army of the Coast," Corrigan read, and his voice carried flat and clear across the fog, "the garrison at Greywater Point is to be evacuated within ten days. All personnel will proceed to the embarkation point at Saltmere. All materiel designated Category One will be transported. All remaining records—" he paused, and Paloma saw his eyes lift from the page and sweep the crowd once, quickly, before returning to it, "—all remaining records will be destroyed by fire prior to departure."

No one spoke. Somewhere near the kitchen a pot lid clattered, absurdly loud.

Corrigan folded the paper. "Ward sections will receive their schedules this evening. Dismissed."

The men did not disperse so much as dissolve, drifting back toward their duties in twos and threes, heads bent together. Paloma stood among them feeling the words settle into her body one by one. *Evacuated.* Ten days. And then fire—the army's oldest housekeeper, tidying away everything it did not wish to carry.

*All remaining records.*

Her hands had gone cold. She pressed them flat against her apron and made herself walk, not run, back toward the ward.

* * *

The ward smelled of carbolic and seawater, the two smells that had soaked into her clothes for three years until she could no longer tell which belonged to the hospital and which to her. Forty beds, thirty-one occupied. She knew every man in them by name and by wound, and she knew—which was the thing she could never say aloud—she knew which of them existed and which of them did not.

Private Hollis, bed fourteen, fever down to something survivable. Private Anselm, bed nine, who would keep his arm if the rot stayed where it was. And in the ledger beneath her floorboard, in her own careful hand: Corporal Dennet, dead of dysentery on the fourth of March, unrecorded. Gunner Faye, dead of the bad flour, unrecorded. Thirty-eight names now. Thirty-eight men the war had eaten and the paperwork had declined to notice.

She had started the ledger without deciding to. That was how she would always think of it afterward—not a decision but a symptom, the way a fever starts. A soldier died and no entry followed, and the absence sat in her chest like a stone swallowed, and one night she had pried up the board beneath her cot and begun to write. Name, rank, date, cause. Where they'd been when it happened. What they'd said, if anything, near the end. She wrote small. She wrote in pencil first and inked only what she was sure of.

And since Brandt's letter—since the wax seal cooling under her palm while a dying man whispered *the Kavanaghs* into the dark—she had written faster.

Now she lay on her cot during the midday lull, staring at the ceiling beams, and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing.

Ten days. Then fire. Every shipping manifest, every requisition form, every hospital register, every death return—all of it, ash. The official record would be born clean, compiled afterward in some ministry office from whatever documents the victors chose to preserve, and the preserved ones would show exactly what the officers here had wanted shown all along: that the supply chain had functioned, that the deaths had been fewer, that nothing needed explaining.

Brandt's letter named her family. It was evidence against the Kavanaghs—and it was also, she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water, the only proof in the world that there was anything to prove. If the letter burned, the fraud burned with it. If the ledger burned, thirty-eight men became forty men who had never lived.

Everything would survive except the truth. Everything was designed to survive except the truth.

She got up, knelt, and pulled the floorboard.

* * *

That evening she packed.

Not her own things—those took five minutes, a change of clothes, her mother's shawl, a photograph gone soft at the corners. She packed the way she had learned to pack in the first year of the war, when the garrison had fallen back twice in a month and every nurse had learned the discipline of the small bundle: what can be carried, what can be left, what must be carried even if it costs you something else you need.

But tonight the arithmetic had changed. Tonight she measured differently.

She emptied her medical kit onto the cot and looked at it honestly. The scissors—army issue, replaceable. The spare needles—replaceable. The tin of carbolic powder—replaceable, or nearly; there would be supplies at Saltmere, supplies everywhere, supplies were the one thing the army never failed to move. She repacked the kit with less than half its contents.

Into the space she laid the ledger, wrapped first in oilcloth, then in a folded bandage, then another bandage, until it was a thick roll indistinguishable from any other roll in a nurse's kit. It fit perfectly. It had been made, in a sense, for this—thirty-eight entries of small, tight handwriting, compressed into a rectangle no larger than a hymnal. She had measured her days in what could be carried, and here was the answer the measuring had been building toward: the whole weight of what mattered fit inside two hands.

The letter was harder. She could not fold it again without cracking the wax further, and the wax was already scarred where Brandt's thumb had pressed it, trembling, three weeks ago. She thought about the boot she wasn't wearing, the hollow heel the cobbler in her hometown had built for smuggled lace in another lifetime, and decided against it—boots were searched, boots were thrown, boots were lost in mud. Instead she unpicked the seam of her kit's inner flap with a needle from her own sleeve, slid the letter flat against the canvas, and sewed it closed with stitches so fine she could barely find them again. Her mother had taught her those stitches. Her mother would have wept to know what they carried.

She worked by the light of a shuttered lamp, past midnight, past the snores and coughs of the nurses' quarters, and the work steadied her the way work always had. Hands busy, mind quiet. This was the mercy of nursing: there was always a next task, and the next task kept the larger questions at bay.

But around two in the morning the tasks ran out, and the questions came.

She sat on her cot with the kit between her knees and let them come.

*Who do I give this to?* Not Corrigan—Corrigan had read the destruction order with his eyes lifted, watching the crowd watch him, and she had understood in that moment that he was not an officer burdened with an unpleasant duty but an officer relieved of an unpleasant secret. Not the chaplain, who was kind and weak. Not the surgeon, Aldous, who had warned her—kindly, gravely—that questions got nurses reassigned, and whose warning she now reread in memory as what it had truly been: a map of the machine, drawn by a man who had chosen to live inside it.

Saltmere, then. The embarkation point. And after Saltmere—

Petra. The name rose in her mind the way a landmark rises out of fog. Neutral harbor, neutral ground, printers and deserters and foreign consuls, a city where paper was currency and nobody's archives were safe from anybody. She had heard soldiers talk about it the way sailors talk about ports they'll never see. If the record was going to survive anywhere, it would survive there—or nowhere.

Ten days. She had ten days to get a bread tin's worth of truth off a peninsula that was about to be sealed, sanitized, and set alight.

A floorboard creaked in the corridor. Paloma's hand went flat over the kit, over the place where the letter lay, and she held her breath until the footsteps passed. When they faded she exhaled slowly and noticed her heart was going like a drum in a retreat.

This was what it had become, then. This was the shape of her life now: a woman sitting alone at night, guarding papers, flinching at footsteps. She almost laughed. Three years ago she had come to Greywater Point to bind wounds. Now she bound secrets, and the wounds were in the country itself, and no bandage she owned would reach them.

* * *

Morning brought Ada Reyes.

Paloma heard her before she saw her—the particular brisk tread, the jingle of the tin cup hanging from her belt that Ada refused to stop carrying because, she said, a soldier who couldn't get her own water deserved whatever happened to her. Ada came into the linen room with an armful of fresh dressings and stopped dead.

"You've heard," Paloma said.

"Heard? I was at assembly." Ada dumped the dressings on the table and looked at her, and Paloma watched the calculation move behind her friend's eyes—the same quick inventory Ada ran over every wound before she touched it. "You look like you've been up all night."

"I have been up all night."

"Packing?"

"Thinking about packing."

Ada leaned against the table, arms crossed. Outside the window the fog was burning off, and the light fell across her face in stripes through the shutters, and Paloma thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that the person she trusted most in the world was someone she'd known only two years, while the family whose name was stitched into a dying man's confession she had known all her life.

"They're burning the records," Ada said. It wasn't a question.

"All of them."

"I heard Corrigan read it. 'Destroyed by fire.'" Ada's mouth twisted. "You know what that means? Half these men are going to die twice. Once for real and once on paper." She said it lightly, the way she said most sharp things, but her eyes weren't light. "You know what else it means?"

Paloma waited.

"It means nobody's coming back to check. Whatever happens in these last ten days, happens in the dark." Ada picked up a dressing, put it down again. "So. Floorboard. Empty yet?"

Paloma didn't answer, and Ada nodded as if she had.

"I'm not asking what's under there," Ada said. "I'm asking whether you've got a plan, because I've watched you for two years, Paloma Kavanagh, and you've got the face of a woman with a plan and no one to carry it with."

The offer sat between them, plain as bread. Paloma looked at her friend—at the chapped hands, the cup on the belt, the steady eyes—and felt the old warmth rise, and felt, underneath it, something new and unwelcome: a hesitation. Since Brandt's letter she hesitated at doors she used to walk through. She hated herself for it and did it anyway.

"There may be things that need carrying," Paloma said carefully. "To Saltmere. Possibly farther."

"Farther meaning Petra."

"Meaning I haven't decided."

Ada smiled, slow and crooked. "You decided the day the orders came. You just haven't told yourself yet." She pushed off from the table and gathered her dressings, and at the door she paused. "Whatever it is—you don't have to carry it alone. That's all I'm saying. Some things are too heavy for one pair of hands."

She left. Paloma stood in the striped light a long moment, hearing the words both ways, the way you can hear a bell both ways depending on which side of it you stand.

* * *

The days that followed had a doubled quality, like walking on a frozen river—every ordinary task performed above, every dangerous thought moving below.

She nursed. She changed dressings on bed nine and fed soup to bed fourteen and held the hand of the boy in bed twenty-two who was going to lose his leg and knew it, and she did all of it well, because the work deserved to be done well regardless of what was sewn into her kit. But underneath, always, the other current: she noted which officers inspected the records room, and how long they stayed. She noted that Sergeant Imbrie, who had signed two of the false returns, drew extra ammunition from stores and said nothing to anyone. She noted that the fires were to be lit in the courtyard behind the administration block, and that a detail of engineers had already stacked oil drums there, waiting, patient as pallbearers.

On the fifth day she walked past the stack at dusk and stood looking at it until a sentry shifted his weight and reminded her where she was.

Oil drums. For paper. Someone had calculated how much paper the garrison held and how much oil it would take to make certain of it. That was the part that chilled her most—not the burning, armies burned things all the time—but the precision. Somewhere a clerk had done sums on erasure, and the sums had been approved, initialed, filed. Even the destruction of the record would have a record, somewhere, in some office, and that record would say *routine disposal*, and it would be true, and it would be a lie, and no one would ever have to choose between the two.

She went back to her cot and lay awake doing her own sums.

One nurse. Two documents. Ten days minus five. A road to Saltmere choked with evacuating columns, checkpoints, provosts with lists. And at the end of it, a ship, and beyond the ship a harbor where she had never been, where she knew no one, where the only plan she had was the vaguest and most stubborn kind: *find someone who prints things, and make them print this.*

It was a terrible plan. She turned it over anyway, examining it the way she examined a wound—honestly, without flinching, because flinching never helped anyone.

Below her, through the floorboards, the ledger lay wrapped in its oilcloth, thirty-eight men sleeping in the dark. Against her ribs, in the kit she would carry out the door, the letter lay flat against the canvas, Brandt's broken seal pressed to the fabric like a hand.

On the eighth day, the fog came in thicker than usual, and with it, down the coast road, the first of the refugee columns—carts and handcarts and children walking beside oxen, the war's debris flowing ahead of the army's tide. Paloma stood at the gate and watched them pass, and among the faces in the gray light she found herself searching, without quite admitting it, for one face in particular—for her brother, for Devlin, who did not know yet what their name was worth and to whom.

He would know soon. Everyone would know soon, or no one ever would, and the difference between those two futures was currently weighing four ounces and riding in a bread tin she hadn't bought yet.

She turned back toward the wards. There were bandages to boil, and names to copy, and six days left in which to measure the world one final time—in what could be carried, and what must be.
