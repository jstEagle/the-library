## 6. The Friend Who Stays

The rain came in off the harbor that evening the way it always did in late autumn — sideways, patient, as if it had all night and intended to use every hour of it. Paloma stood at the ward's one window with a chipped mug of chicory going cold in her hands, watching water sheet down the glass and thinking about weight.

She had been measuring things again. It was a habit now, older than she could say precisely when it had begun — perhaps the day she'd first slid the ledger beneath the floorboard of her quarters, or perhaps long before that, back when she was a girl on the docks watching her uncle Tomas weigh crates of salt fish by eye and never once be wrong. Everything had a weight. A tin of bread. A folded letter sealed in black wax. A book of names written small enough to fit inside a palm. The question was never whether a thing could be carried. The question was whether *she* could carry it, and for how far, and what she would have to set down to make room.

Behind her, the ward breathed its familiar breath — the wet rattle of lungs, the creak of cot frames, someone moaning softly through a dream that was probably better than his waking hours. Forty-one men tonight. Three would not see morning; she knew their faces without looking, knew them the way she knew the weather coming off the water.

And Ada Reyes was coming up the hill through the rain to find her, because Ada Reyes always came, and Paloma did not yet know — could not yet know — that this was the last evening she would trust her completely.

* * *

Ada arrived smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool, shaking out her oilskin cloak in the doorway of the nurses' quarters like a large dog shedding water. She was shorter than Paloma by half a head and broader through the shoulders, with a face that seemed built for laughing and hands that were not — hands scarred from shell fragments at Marrow Point, knuckles permanently swollen, fingers that could splint a femur in the dark or break a man's grip at the wrist if it came to that. It had come to that twice, that Paloma knew of. Ada never spoke of either time.

"You look," Ada announced, hanging the cloak on the single nail by the door, "like a woman doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"No." Ada dropped onto the end of her own cot, across the narrow room, and began working off her boots. "You do sums all day — dosages, rations, who can walk and who can't. This is different. This is the other kind." She looked up, and there it was: the directness that had made Paloma love her within a week of their posting, three years ago, when Ada had found Paloma crying in the linen store after losing her first patient and had said nothing at all, only sat down on a pile of sheets beside her and stayed until the crying stopped. "You've been doing it since Brandt died. Since you came back from wherever you went that afternoon with your face shut like a door."

Paloma set her mug on the sill. Rain ticked against the window between them.

"Tell me," Ada said. Not a request. Ada didn't make requests; she issued invitations you couldn't refuse.

So Paloma told her. Not everything — not yet, though she would tell herself later that she'd meant to, that the words had been there waiting — but enough. She pulled the ledger from under the floorboard and laid it on the blanket between them: a school exercise book, cheap paper gone soft at the edges, filled edge to edge with names in her small careful hand. Two hundred and six names. She watched Ada's thumb move along the spine of it, the way you'd touch something living.

"Two hundred and six," Paloma said. "Men who died here, or in the convoys, or in the wards at Torren and Cape Aldis — men whose names aren't in any report. I've checked. I wrote to the records office twice, pretending to be verifying pension claims. Nothing. As if they'd never been born, let alone buried."

Ada turned pages slowly. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the roof slates. When she reached the columns of shipping numbers — the ones Paloma had copied from manifests, from waybills, from the quartermaster's own requisition slips — her thumb stopped moving.

"Elias Brandt," Ada said quietly. "He was the one who told you? Before he—"

"He gave me a letter. Sealed. He couldn't speak above a whisper by then; the fever had him. He said—" Paloma heard her own voice go flat, careful, the voice she used for delivering bad news to families. "He said the letter named the Kavanaghs. My family. That medicine went into the ships and ballast came out, and men died of it, and my name is somewhere in the middle of it whether I put it there or not."

Ada looked up. In the lamplight her face was very still, and Paloma realized she had expected — what? Horror? Pity? What she saw instead was harder to read, a stillness like deep water, and for the first time in three years of friendship she felt the small cold drop of not knowing what Ada Reyes was thinking.

"Say something," Paloma said.

"I'm deciding what to say." Ada closed the ledger gently, both hands on its cover, as if closing a coffin lid. Then she exhaled, and the warmth came back into her face like a lamp being turned up. "I'm saying yes. Whatever you're planning — and you're planning, don't insult me — I'm in it. You're not carrying that thing alone."

Paloma hadn't known, until that moment, how badly she needed to hear it. Her eyes stung and she blinked hard and pretended it was the lamplight.

"Petra," she said. "It has to be Petra. Neutral ground. There are printers there, foreign consuls, people who'll still be free when the victors seal every archive on our side of the water. The evacuation orders will come soon — everyone knows it — and when they do, the army burns its field records. All of them. Every scrap of paper in this garrison goes into a barrel and a fire, and two hundred and six men become nobody's problem forever." She tapped the ledger's cover. "This is what survives. If I get it out."

"Then we get it out." Ada said it simply, the way she'd say *then we change the dressing*. "Roads are chaos already with the refugee columns forming. Two women traveling together draw less notice than one. I know the coastal road as far as Sennet Ford, and after that—" She shrugged. "After that we improvise. We're good at improvising. We've kept forty men alive on supplies meant for sixty."

"We?"

"Unless you'd rather I stay behind and embroider." But she smiled when she said it, and Paloma laughed despite everything, and for a while the room held nothing but the rain and the lamplight and the old easy shape of the two of them against the world.

Later — much later, boots off, cloaks hung, the ledger returned to its hiding place beneath the boards — Ada lay in the dark and asked the question.

Paloma was nearly asleep. The rain had softened to a whisper. And Ada's voice came across the narrow space between the cots, casual as anything, pitched just below the register of ordinary conversation:

"The letter. Brandt's letter. What color was the wax?"

Paloma lay still.

"Black," she said.

"Mm. Black wax — that's chancery stock. Expensive. Quartermasters don't usually buy chancery wax; they get the common red by the pound." A pause, the length of a slow breath. "Was there an impression? A seal mark? Some offices have them — port authority, provost corps. If there's a mark, you can trace where he bought it, maybe even when."

"Why?" Paloma kept her voice as light as Ada's. "Planning to write to the wax merchants' guild?"

"Just wondering how identifiable it is. If we're carrying a thing across half the country, I like to know what it looks like if someone asks us to open our bags." Another pause. "You'll want to keep it separate from the ledger, anyway. Don't keep everything in one basket."

"That's sensible advice," Paloma said. "I'll take it under advisement, Corporal."

Ada laughed softly. "Go to sleep, Nurse."

But Paloma did not sleep, or not well. She lay listening to Ada's breathing slow into sleep, and she turned the conversation over and over like a stone in her hand, feeling for the crack in it. Because it was true that Ada liked to know things — Ada catalogued the world the way Paloma catalogued names, compulsively, as armor against chaos. It was true that asking about the wax was exactly the kind of practical question Ada would ask.

And it was also true that Ada had never once, in three years, asked a question she didn't already have a reason to ask.

* * *

In the morning the fog came up off the water so thick the garrison bell sounded muffled, as if rung underwater, and the world shrank to twenty paces in any direction. Paloma walked the ward rounds with the ledger's absence under the floorboard like a missing tooth — she had moved it before dawn, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked into the false bottom of her kit trunk beneath a layer of bandages. Bandages. Everything important in her life now traveled dressed as bandages.

Ada met her at the dispensary door with two mugs of chicory and a list.

"Ferry at Torren runs Thursday if the fog lifts. Refugee column's assembling at the crossroads mill — I counted eleven carts this morning, oxen, families, the usual misery. Nobody searches misery." She handed over the list. "What we need. Bread tins — a good deep one, the kind bakers use. Oilcloth. A needle and waxed thread. And you should write to your brother."

Paloma looked up sharply. "Devlin?"

"You said yourself he doesn't believe the name is tainted. Fine. But if the letter names the Kavanaghs, Devlin Kavanagh may wake up one morning to find intelligence officers drinking his tea and asking polite questions. Better he hears it from you than from them." Ada sipped her chicory, watching Paloma over the rim. "Unless you think he's involved."

"Devlin isn't involved in anything except being angry at the war and drinking too much in ports where ships are laid up. He signs nothing. He files nothing. He—" Paloma stopped. She thought of Devlin's last letter, cheerful and evasive, mentioning that Uncle Tomas had gotten him a berth as a supercargo on a coastal run, steady work, good money. Steady work. Good money. Ships that sailed heavy and logged light.

"I'll write to him," she said.

"Good." Ada folded her own copy of the list away. And then, in the same mild tone, eyes on the fog beyond the dispensary window: "One more thing. The letter — you keep it on you? On your person?"

"It stays where it stays, Ada."

"Of course." A smile, quick and warm. "Just — if we're searched at a checkpoint, and they find it, and it's got your family's name in it, I'd like to know what story we're telling. Stories work best when both of us know the same one."

It was reasonable. It was entirely reasonable, and Ada delivered it reasonably, and Paloma nodded and said they'd settle on a story before the crossroads mill, and the moment passed the way a fish passes under a boat — a shadow, a suggestion, gone before you could swear to what you'd seen.

But that afternoon, checking dressings in the third ward, Paloma caught herself watching Ada across the room. Watching her wrap a stump with those scarred, capable hands. Watching her joke with a boy of nineteen who'd lost his left arm at the elbow, making him laugh around the morphine haze. Watching her glance — once, briefly, without apparent purpose — toward the nurses' quarters, where the kit trunks sat packed against the wall.

*She likes to know things,* Paloma told herself. *That's all it is. That's all it's ever been.*

And it was true. And it was not enough. Because doubt, once planted, grew the way the fog grew off the harbor — silently, from below, until it had swallowed the shapes of things you'd sworn you could see clearly yesterday.

That night she took Brandt's letter from its hiding place inside her boot — she had moved it there two days ago, sewn into the boot's inner seam, flat against the ankle bone — and held it over the lamp. Black wax, chancery stock, unbroken. No impression, no seal mark; whatever office had supplied Elias Brandt with his wax, he had pressed it plain. Untraceable. Anonymous. A dead man's silence, sold to her for the price of a fevered whisper.

*It names the Kavanaghs.*

She thought of Devlin reading her warning letter, his furious scrawl already composing itself in her mind: *Our name? Our name in what? Ulla, you've been at that hospital too long, the fevers have gotten into your head.* He would not believe it. He would refuse to believe it, loudly, loyally, the way he refused to believe in anything that shamed the family — and God help them both, she loved him for it and feared it in equal measure.

She rewrapped the letter, pushed it back into the seam, laced the boot.

Across the room, Ada slept soundly, one arm flung over her eyes, breathing the deep even breaths of a woman with a clean conscience. Paloma watched her for a long time. Then she whispered, so quietly that even the rain barely carried it:

"If you're mine, Ada, prove it on the road. And if you're not—"

She didn't finish. She didn't know how to finish. Instead she lay back, measured the weight of the day ahead — one bread tin, one ledger, one letter, one friend — and slept in snatches, waking at every shift of the wind, until the gray light came and with it, three days early, the sound of boots in the corridor and a staff officer's voice calling for the senior nurse.

Evacuation orders. The army was burning its records in the morning.
