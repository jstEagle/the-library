## 7. Road to Petra

The column began to move before the sun cleared the headland, and by then the smoke from the garrison's burning records was already a black seam against the pale sky.

Paloma walked at the rear of the third cart, where she could see the whole length of the road unspooling behind her: the hospital wagons, the limping soldiers who had been judged well enough to walk, the families with their bundles and their children and their goats. Above it all hung that thread of smoke. Somewhere in it were the shipping manifests she had spent two years copying into her ledger — the numbers, the dates, the neat columns of Elias Brandt's signature. The army was erasing itself as they watched.

She kept her hand on the bread tin in her lap and did not look back again.

The tin had been Ada's idea. *No one searches food twice,* Ada had said the night before, prying the lid from an empty biscuit tin and lining it with oilcloth. *Bread goes stale, tea goes sour, but nobody opens a bread tin looking for treason.* They had packed the ledger flat along the bottom, wrapped Brandt's letter in a scrap of bandaging above it, and filled the rest of the space with actual bread — a dense gray loaf Ada had charmed out of the cook, still warm when it went in. Then the lid, then a clean cloth tied over it, so that it looked like what it pretended to be: a nurse's provisions, nothing more.

Now it sat under Paloma's hands like a sleeping animal. She could feel its weight through the canvas of her skirt, could feel the letter inside it like a second heartbeat. Somewhere ahead lay Petra, three days' march through hills that had changed hands four times since spring. Somewhere behind lay everything the tin contained: two hundred and six names, and one family name she could not stop hearing even now, even in her own silence — *the Kavanaghs.*

"Sit forward," Ada said quietly, without turning her head. "You're hunching over it like it's gold."

"It is gold."

"It's bread until someone makes it not-bread. Sit forward. Look bored. Boredom is armor."

Paloma sat forward. Ada had a gift for this — for the small theater of survival, the angle of a shoulder, the dullness of a gaze. Paloma had always thought of herself as brave in the way nurses are brave, which is to say steadily, privately, in places no one salutes. But Ada wore her courage differently, worn smooth like a river stone, and watching her now, Paloma felt the first flicker of something she would not name for days: that she did not know all the shapes Ada's smoothness could take.

"You never asked," Paloma said, low, as the cart jolted over a culvert. "What's in the letter. You've had two days."

"I know what's in the letter." Ada's eyes stayed on the road. "You sleep-talk, Kavanagh. Did you know that? First night after Brandt died you said a name over and over." A pause, measured precisely. "I didn't catch which one."

The lie was gentle, and it was a lie, and Paloma heard it land between them like a coin dropped on stone. Ada knew nothing about the letter except what Paloma had told her — that it was evidence, that it named names, that it had to reach Petra before the historians sealed the archives. That was all anyone needed to know. So why the small performance? Why say *sleep-talk* at all?

"Whose name?" Paloma asked anyway, testing.

Ada glanced at her then, and smiled, and the smile was the old smile, the one from the fever tents, the one that had pulled Paloma through the worst winter of the war. "Yours," she said. "You were saying yours. Over and over, like a rosary." She nudged Paloma's knee with her own. "Rest while you can. Checkpoint at the ford by noon, if the refugees coming the other way are telling it straight."

*Refugees coming the other way.* Paloma turned and looked properly at the road ahead for the first time: the long line of carts and walkers descending toward a river valley, and above them, thin and patient, a smudge of dust that might have been traffic or might have been soldiers. She thought about asking how Ada knew there would be a checkpoint at all. She decided, for the moment, to be bored instead.

Boredom is armor.

She put her hands in her lap, away from the tin, and let the road carry her.

* * *

Ada counted rifles.

It was a habit older than the war — her father had been a harbor watchman, and he'd taught her that you survive by counting: boats, crates, men, exits. Now, riding beside Paloma with the bread tin sitting between them like a shared secret, she counted. Eleven soldiers at the head of the column. Two officers on horses that were too fine for their uniforms. One field telephone on a mule, which meant orders came from somewhere, which meant someone wanted this road watched.

And at the ford, exactly as the fleeing miller's family had said: a checkpoint. Sandbags, a barrier made from a felled poplar, and a tent with a staff pennant that fluttered in a wind too weak to deserve it.

*Someone wants this road watched,* Ada thought, and felt the cold settle into her chest where it had lived since the garrison fell. Because she already knew who wanted it watched. She had known since the night she'd written her first report, folded it inside a letter to her sister, and sent it down the wire to a man named Vasseur in exchange for one sentence: *Your sister will be moved off the punishment list.*

That was all it had taken. One sentence. One sister. And ever since, every kindness she had done Paloma had come doubled — once as friendship and once as currency — until Ada could no longer tell which hand was giving and which was taking.

She had reported the departure from the garrison. She had reported the destination: Petra. She had reported, in careful neutral language, that Nurse Kavanagh carried documents of unspecified nature. She had *not* reported the bread tin, because reporting the bread tin meant surrendering the last thing she had that was only Ada's — the thing she had chosen, freely, without a gun at her sister's name held behind it.

At the checkpoint, an officer stepped into the road and raised one hand, and the whole column sighed to a halt.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined Vasseur's people as thick-necked and loud. This man was slight, gray at the temples, with the unhurried air of a clerk doing sums. His uniform bore the intelligence branch's discreet silver pin. He walked the line of carts slowly, hands clasped behind his back, peering into baskets, lifting a shawl here, a tarpaulin there, apologizing to a grandmother in a voice pitched to be overheard — *forgive me, madam, forgive us all, the war makes thieves of gentlemen* — and the refugees, hungry and tired and afraid, forgave him, because what else does forgiveness cost when you have nothing left?

"He'll come to us," Ada murmured. "We're uniform. Uniform gets looked at longest."

"Why?"

"Because we're supposed to have nothing to hide, and men like him don't trust what's supposed to be clean." She shifted so her body half-shielded the tin. "When he asks, I talk. You're a field nurse with fever fatigue; your voice is gone. Nod. Look hollow. You've done it a thousand times."

"That's not acting."

"No," Ada said softly. "It isn't."

The officer reached them. Up close his eyes were pale and quick, and they went first — Ada noted it with a lurch — not to their faces but to the space between their knees, where the tin sat under its cloth.

"Nurses," he said. It wasn't a question. He read the armbands, then their faces. "From the coastal garrison?"

"Third Field Hospital," Ada said. "Evacuated this morning. We were ordered out with the walking wounded." She gestured at the soldiers plodding past on foot, and the officer followed her gesture with his eyes, and in that half-second Ada moved her boot, casually, so it rested against the cart's side rail — closing the gap where a man might lean and lift.

"Names?" He produced a notebook. Small, black, well-thumbed. A working book, not a decoration.

"Corporal Ada Reyes. Nurse Paloma Kavanagh."

The pen scratched. Ada watched him write the name *Kavanagh*, and watched something flicker — not recognition, she told herself, not yet, just the ordinary pause of a clerk meeting a common name. But the flicker was there, and she filed it away in the place where she kept all the things she would someday have to confess, a place growing crowded as a refugee column itself.

"And your baggage?" The pale eyes returned to the tin.

"Bread," Ada said. "Tea. Bandage linen. Would you like some? There's plenty, and your men look like they've been standing here since yesterday."

Something crossed the officer's face — surprise, maybe, or the ghost of amusement. Men with clipboards rarely got offered bread. He hesitated, and in the hesitation Ada saw the whole machinery of the morning tilt on an axle: he could refuse and seem petty, or accept and seem bought, or search anyway and confirm to himself that he was right to be suspicious. She watched him choose. She had learned, in the long apprenticeship of her double life, that most men most of the time chose whatever let them feel generous.

"Not today, Corporal." He snapped the notebook shut. "Petra road is closed to military traffic after dark. Move along, and move quickly."

The barrier was dragged aside. The cart rolled forward through the ford, water churning brown around the wheels, and only when they were a hundred yards up the far bank did Paloma exhale, long and shaking, her hand finding the tin's lid and pressing it flat as if the bread might rise and give them away.

"He wrote my name," she whispered.

"They write everyone's name."

"He wrote my *name*, Ada. My surname. In a book."

Ada said nothing. Ahead, the road climbed into pine shadow, and the smoke of the burning garrison had finally dropped below the horizon, and somewhere behind them — though neither woman turned to look — a field telephone was already crackling, and a colonel with a careful voice was learning that a nurse named Kavanagh was on the road to Petra.

* * *

They made camp that night in a quarry cut into the hillside, among forty other fires, because a single fire is a confession and forty fires are a crowd.

Paloma sat with her back against a warm stone and rationed the bread from the tin — carefully, from the top layer, disturbing nothing beneath — and passed pieces down the row of walking wounded who had adopted their cart as theirs. An artilleryman with a burned neck. A boy of maybe seventeen who claimed to be twenty and whose hands wouldn't stop moving. A sergeant named Oduya who had carried a comrade eleven miles and now couldn't bend his spine and laughed anyway at everything, quietly, as if laughter were contraband too.

"You going all the way to Petra, Sister?" Oduya asked.

"All the way."

"Good town, they say. Neutral. Nobody asks where you came from." He chewed, wincing. "Nobody asks where anybody came from. Whole harbor full of people being nobody from nowhere."

"That sounds like heaven," the boy said.

"It sounds lonely," Paloma said, and Oduya shrugged, and the fire popped, and for a little while the quarry held nothing worse than cold and hunger and the ordinary griefs of retreat.

Ada found her after full dark. She came around the stone without a sound — she always moved without sound, another thing Paloma was only now cataloguing, adding it to the list of small strangenesses — and crouched beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Below them the forty fires made a fallen constellation.

"The boy's feverish," Ada said. "I gave him the last of the willow bark. He'll be useless tomorrow, so we should think about whether we stay with the column or push ahead alone."

"Alone?"

"Faster. And—" Ada paused. In the firelight her face was hard to read, which was unlike her; usually her face broadcast everything, generously, wastefully. "There were riders on the road behind us at dusk. Four of them, staff horses. They passed the column without stopping anywhere. Just came through and went on ahead."

Paloma felt the cold return, settling into the same place it had sat at the checkpoint. "Going where?"

"Where does anyone go, on this road?" Ada's voice stayed level. "Petra."

They were both silent. Down the slope, the boy coughed in his sleep, and someone's goat complained, and the night went on being an ordinary night, indifferent, enormous.

"Paloma." Ada turned, and now her face was readable after all — and what was written on it frightened Paloma more than the riders had. It was guilt. It was unmistakable, naked guilt, wearing the mask of concern and failing at the edges. "If anything happens on this road — if we're stopped again, if someone comes for you specifically — promise me you'll let me do the talking. Promise me you won't run, and you won't fight, and you won't—"

"Why?" Paloma asked quietly. "Why would someone come for me *specifically*, Ada?"

The question hung between them. Around it the quarry breathed, forty fires, forty small companies of the defeated. Ada opened her mouth. Closed it. For one suspended moment Paloma watched her friend stand at the edge of a confession — saw it happen, the way one sees a bird decide against flight, the gathering and the settling — and then Ada exhaled and shook her head and became, visibly, deliberately, the old Ada again.

"Because you're carrying something worth killing for, and I love you, and I'm better at talking than you are. Those are the reasons. Take them or don't."

"I take them," Paloma said.

But she did not sleep. She sat with her arms around her knees and watched the stars wheel and turned the moment over and over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: *she almost told me something.* And beneath that thought, quieter, colder, the other one she could not make go away:

*Or she almost warned me.*

* * *

On the third day Petra announced itself as a smell — woodsmoke, tar, fish, and under it all the particular brine-and-rust breath of a harbor — long before the fog lifted enough to show the town.

They crested the last ridge in late afternoon and stopped, the whole column stopping without any order, because everyone did, everyone always did, the way pilgrims stop at the first sight of a shrine. Below them the city spilled down its hillsides to a great gray bowl of water: masts in their hundreds, cranes like gallows and like prayers, warehouses, bell towers, and everywhere the fog drifting through in slow ribbons so that ships appeared and vanished like thoughts. A bell somewhere was ringing the hour. Gulls argued overhead. On the quay, even at this distance, they could see crowds — deserters in mixed uniforms, women selling coffee from urns, a knot of men in civilian clothes but military posture standing outside a warehouse with fresh paint on its sign, and even from the ridge Paloma could read the painted word: *ARCHIVE*.

"They're already here," she said.

"Who?"

"The historians. The victors." Her hand found the tin through the cloth. "Writing it all down. Sealing it all up."

Ada stood beside her, and for a long moment neither spoke, and the wind off the sea pushed the fog around below them like slow white sheep. Then Ada said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"Well. You brought the truth all this way, Kavanagh. Let's hope somebody down there still knows how to read."

They started down the hill together — and neither of them noticed the four staff horses tethered outside the harbormaster's office, nor the slight, gray-templed officer standing at the window above them, notebook open, watching the refugee column descend into his net.
