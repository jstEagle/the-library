## 8. Harbor of Second Chances

The fog came first.

Paloma had been told that Petra announced itself by fog before it announced itself by land — a gray wall rolling off the cold current to meet the harbor, so thick the pilots navigated by sound, ringing bells mounted on the breakwater and counting the seconds between their echoes. She heard those bells for two hours before she saw anything at all: a slow, mournful pealing out of the white, closer and closer, until the fishing smack carrying them up from the coast road's end slid into a silence so complete she could hear water dripping from the rigging.

Ada stood at the bow with her collar turned up, saying nothing. She had said very little since the checkpoint. Paloma had decided not to decide what that meant. The bread tin sat between them on the thwart, wrapped in oilcloth, and every time the boat rolled Ada's hand drifted toward it and then away again, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

Then the fog thinned all at once, the way a curtain lifts rather than parts, and Petra was simply there.

A harbor shaped like a cupped hand, holding a hundred masts. Fishing boats and coastal tramps and two fat merchantmen flying no flag Paloma recognized, riding at anchor as though they had forgotten where they were going. Beyond them, a city climbing steep hills in terraces of pale stone, its roofs tiled green with damp, its chimneys breathing smoke into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. On the seawall, cranes swung crates down onto cobbles. Somewhere a ship's bell rang the hour. Gulls argued over the fish market's leavings. And over everything lay the particular hush of a place that belonged to no one — no guns had fired here in forty years, no blockade had ever quite closed around it, and the whole town wore the ease of furniture in a house whose owner has died but whose heirs have not yet arrived to quarrel.

"Neutral," Ada said, as if testing the word's weight. "Actually neutral."

"For now." Paloma watched a naval launch — grey, purposeful, flying the victors' pennant — thread between the anchored tramps with an insolence that did not need translation. "They're already here."

* * *

They came ashore among the fish stalls because Ada said customs officers watched the main quay, and because after three weeks of refugee columns Paloma trusted Ada's instincts about uniforms more than her own about maps. The harbor district received them the way a tide receives driftwood: without comment. Women gutted herring on benches worn hollow by generations of hands. Boys sold roasted chestnuts under a striped awning. A man with half his face scarred by powder burn sat mending nets and did not look up when they passed, and Paloma understood that this was the local courtesy — everyone here had arrived from somewhere worse, and no one asked.

The streets behind the quay climbed in steps. Every third doorway seemed to hold a trade that fed on war without touching it: a saddler making holsters for two armies' revolvers; a chandler whose window displayed signal lamps in three navies' patterns; a pawnbroker's shelf of medals, ribbons, and false papers arranged with the loving disorder of a museum. Deserters moved through the crowds like fish through kelp — you spotted them only when you were one yourself, by the way they walked, shoulders braced against nothing, eyes doing arithmetic on every uniform.

"They'll seal the archives within weeks," Paloma said quietly. "Vasseur's people. That's why we came fast."

"You say his name like he's already found us."

"He doesn't need to find us. He needs only to be faster than the printers." She touched her boot where Brandt's letter rode flat against her ankle, sewn into the seam by a hotel chambermaid in the last river town who had asked no questions and taken no coin. "Ink is slower than soldiers. That's the whole war now."

Ada laughed — short, surprised, not entirely kind. "You've become a strategist."

"I've become tired." It was truer than she meant it to sound, and Ada's face flickered with something — hurt, or guilt — before smoothing itself back into travel-worn competence.

* * *

The boarding house was called the Anchor of Sorrows, which Paloma took at first for gallows humor until she saw it was simply accurate: seven rooms, each let to someone mid-flight. The landlady was a broad Vessian woman named Frau Ostermayer who counted their coin, glanced once at the oilcloth bundle Ada carried, and said, "Laundry line's in the yard. Nothing dries in this fog, but everything hides." Then she handed over a key stamped with the number six and went back to her ledger, and Paloma felt the strange vertigo of being somewhere that kept honest books.

Their room was narrow, clean, and smelled of salt and lamp oil. There was a bed, a chair, a washstand, and a window looking down into an alley where fog pooled like spilled milk. Ada set the bread tin on the floor beneath the bed, then straightened, then bent and moved it deeper into the shadow of the bedframe, and Paloma watched her do it and felt the doubt move through her chest again, patient as weather.

"The letter stays on me," Paloma said. Not asking. Telling.

"I know." Ada didn't look up. "You've said."

"And the ledger—"

"—stays in the tin, and the tin stays where I can see it. I know, Paloma." Now she looked up, and her dark eyes were wet-rimmed with exhaustion or something else. "Three weeks I've slept sitting up next to that tin. Three weeks of checkpoints. Do you think I don't understand what it weighs?"

"I think," Paloma said carefully, "that at the checkpoint you asked me four times what the wax seal looked like. And I've wondered since why it mattered."

The silence stretched long enough for a gull to cross the window's gray square of light twice.

"Because I wanted to know how bad it would be if we lost it," Ada said finally. "That's all. A soldier's habit. You count what you can't afford to lose so you know how careful to be." She picked up her coat. "I'm going to find food. Real bread. Don't open the door for anyone but me."

She left, and Paloma sat on the edge of the bed and listened to her boots descend the stairs, and told herself, again, that exhaustion made everyone strange, that fear read as secrecy, that Ada Reyes had pulled her out of a burning dressing station at Corrennes with shrapnel in her own shoulder and had earned the benefit of every doubt Paloma owned.

She almost believed it.

* * *

Finding a printer in Petra took less time than finding bread.

Frau Ostermayer, asked over supper whether any presses still ran in the harbor quarter, gave Paloma a look of frank appraisal — the look of a woman pricing information — and then jerked her chin toward the hills. "Sol. Marek Sol. Above the ropewalk, where the smell of tar covers the ink. If your business is honest he'll feed you. If it isn't, he'll feed you anyway and remember your face forever."

The ropewalk ran along the hillside like a long green trench, and above it, up stairs that groaned, Paloma found a door painted the blue of deep water with a brass plate polished nearly illegible: **M. SOL — JOB WORK — PAMPHLETS — BILLS OF LADING — NOTHING ASKED**.

Inside was a cathedral of paper. Reams stacked to the ceiling in towers softened by dust. A flatbed press squatting in the middle of the room like some iron ox at rest. Type cases ranked along the walls in hundreds of shallow drawers, each labeled in a cramped hand. Two apprentices — boys of perhaps fourteen, one missing an arm at the elbow — sorted type by lamplight with the absorbed serenity of monks.

And at a desk buried under proofs, a small man with wire spectacles and ink to his second knuckle looked up and said, without preamble: "If you're selling something, I buy nothing. If you're buying something, I sell everything. If you're neither—" He took off his spectacles, and his eyes behind them were quick and kind and entirely awake. "—then you're the first interesting visitor this month, and you'd better sit down."

"My name is Paloma Kavanagh."

Something crossed his face — not recognition, exactly; calculation. "Kavanagh. That's a name from the war, isn't it? From the supply columns." He rose, waved her to a stool, shouted at the one-armed apprentice to bring tea, and then, when she was seated, folded his ink-stained hands and gave her his whole attention the way poor men give money: completely, and expecting nothing back.

"I have documents," Paloma said. "A letter, sealed, written by a dying army quartermaster named Elias Brandt. It names names — officers, port officials, a scheme that swapped medicine for ballast while soldiers drank fouled water and took fever. Hundreds died. The official reports record none of them." Her voice stayed level; she had rehearsed this in doorways all the way across three borders. "And I have a ledger of my own. Names. Four hundred and eleven names of soldiers who died and were never reported dead. Their families are still waiting for letters that will never come, because the men who erased them are now the men writing the history."

The tea arrived. Sol ignored it. The lamplight caught the dust motes turning slowly above the type cases, and Paloma thought: *this room has waited for me without knowing it.*

"Four hundred eleven," Sol repeated softly. He was not looking at her anymore; he was looking through her, at some interior column of figures only he could see. "Do you know what arrives in this harbor every week? Historians. Official ones. Men with government commissions and first-class berths and contracts to write the war as it ought to have happened. They've booked the Hotel Aurora entire. They interview generals. They copy dispatches selected for them by Colonel Vasseur's office — yes, I know the name, everyone in this town knows the name, he dines at the governor's table twice a week." He smiled without humor. "In eighteen months there will be a beautiful book. Maps in three colors. No fouled water anywhere in it."

"That's why I'm here."

"Yes." He stood abruptly and began to pace between the towers of paper, hands working. "Here is my problem, Frau Kavanagh — or Comrade, or Sister, whatever you are. I can print anything. That is what this press is *for*. But a pamphlet printed tonight is seized by morning unless it has behind it something the authorities cannot seize. Do you know what that is?"

"Witnesses."

"Witnesses." He stopped pacing and faced her, and the lamplight put a shine on his spectacles so that for a moment his eyes vanished and he seemed all attention, all listening. "Paper can be burned. Printers can be jailed — I have been jailed twice, and the cells improved my character not at all. But a sworn testimony, given publicly, witnessed publicly — that is a different animal. The victors can suppress a document. Suppressing a *fact* that fifty dockworkers heard spoken aloud, well." He spread his small, capable hands. "That is like suppressing the tide. Difficult. Embarrassing. Occasionally impossible."

"There are survivors," Paloma said. Her heart was beating hard, and she realized it was hope, arriving after so long an absence that she barely recognized it — thin and painful, like blood returning to a numbed limb. "Men from the poisoned convoy. Some reached the coast ahead of us. There's a gunner who took a throat wound and cannot speak, but he writes a fine clear hand. I saw his testimony myself."

"Names?"

"Some. Addresses of a sort — taverns, a mission house on Rope Lane."

"Then we have work." Sol was already pulling proofs aside, clearing a space on his ruined desk, moving with sudden ferocious energy. "But hear me, Kavanagh, because I will not pretend to be braver than I am. What you propose ends careers and possibly lives. Mine included. So we do it properly or not at all. Sworn statements. Signatures. Dates. And not a sheet leaves this room until we print enough copies at once that seizure becomes a farce — a thousand broadsheets in a night, scattered before their marines finish their breakfast." He glanced at her boot, at the place where the letter rode. "The quartermaster's letter. Is it authentic? Can it be authenticated?"

"It was sealed in his presence. He dictated it knowing he was dying. There's a witness — a nurse who attended him."

"Get her statement too. Get everything." He pushed the teacup toward her, suddenly gentle. "Drink. You're gray as the fog outside. Truth-keeping is heavy labor, and you look like a woman who hasn't set it down once in months."

Paloma wrapped her hands around the cup. The tea was cheap and strong and tasted faintly of the tar drifting up from the ropewalk below, and it was the best thing she had tasted since the war began. Through the window she could see the harbor lights coming out one by one below the fog line, and beyond them, faint on the water, the grey silhouette of the victors' flagship, riding at anchor like a promise.

"They'll close the archives," she said. "Weeks. Maybe sooner."

"Then we won't need the archives," Marek Sol said, and smiled, and began pulling type.

* * *

She came down the hill in the dark with her coat pockets full of Sol's sample pamphlets and her mind full of lists — witnesses, signatures, paper, the gunner's clear handwriting — and it was not until she turned into the alley below the Anchor of Sorrows that the hope cooled into vigilance.

A man stood at the mouth of the alley. Civilian clothes, good boots, a posture that no tailor could disguise. He was smoking, unhurried, and as she passed he removed his hat with perfect courtesy and said, in accented but fluent trade-tongue:

"Comrade Kavanagh? Forgive me. Colonel Vasseur sends his compliments, and asks whether you've settled in comfortably. He hopes to call on you soon — there are matters he'd value your help with. Matters concerning your family."

He tipped his hat again, merged with the fog, and was gone.

Paloma stood a long moment in the cold, hearing the harbor bells ring nine, hearing above them the faint rhythmic clatter of Marek Sol's press beginning its work in the dark. Then she climbed the stairs to room six, knocked the signal she and Ada had agreed upon, and prepared to lie to her closest friend about where she had been — and about how little time they had left.
