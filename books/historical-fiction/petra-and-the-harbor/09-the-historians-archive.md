## 9. The Historians' Archive

The invitation came folded inside a delivery of printer's stock.

Paloma found it on a Tuesday morning, tucked between two reams of rag paper that Marek Sol had ordered from a chandler who owed him favors. The card was heavy cream stock, embossed at the corner with the seal of the Provisional Commission for Historical Records — a laurel wreath around an open book, gilded so thickly it caught the light like a coin. *The Commission requests the pleasure of your company at a reception honoring the archivists and historians of the victorious powers, Customs House Hall, Thursday evening, seven o'clock.*

There was no name on it. No addressee at all, in fact. Marek had pressed it into her hands with the expression of a man handing over something slightly unclean.

"They printed three hundred of these," he said. "The harbor authority distributed them to every merchant house, every shipping office, anyone with a name worth knowing. Nobody checks. It's a reception, not a fortress." He wiped ink from his fingers with a rag that had been gray so long it might once have been white. "Vasseur will be there. So will half the men deciding what the war was."

"And you want me to walk into the same room as Vasseur."

"I want you to see what's happening to the archives before they're gone behind wax and lock and law." Marek's voice dropped, though the print shop was empty except for the two of them and the hiss of the kettle on the stove. "I've heard it from three separate sources this week. The Commission is sealing the field records. Everything from the coastal campaigns — hospital registers, supply manifests, court-martial files, burial reports. Sealed for fifty years. Maybe a century. By the time any historian opens those boxes again, everyone who could contradict the official account will be dead of old age."

Paloma turned the cream card over in her hands. The paper was beautiful. That was the thing she noticed first, and the thing she would remember longest afterward — how beautiful the paper was, how much it must have cost, how many trees had been felled to announce the burial of documents.

"You're asking me to spy," she said.

"I'm asking you to attend a party." Marek smiled without warmth. "What you do there is your own affair."

* * *

She spent Wednesday preparing, and the preparation felt like nursing — the same attention to detail, the same quiet triage of what mattered. She took her good dress from the bottom of her trunk, the dove-gray one she'd worn to her mother's funeral, and brushed the travel out of it. She pinned her hair up in the style women wore in Petra's better districts, borrowed a pair of gloves from the landlady at the boarding house, and practiced walking past the cracked mirror until her shoulders forgot the hospital and remembered the parlor.

The ledger stayed under the floorboard. The letter stayed where it always stayed now, sewn into the seam of her boot, flat against the bone of her ankle. She had stopped noticing its weight during the day; at night she noticed nothing else.

Ada watched her dress with narrowed eyes.

"A reception," Ada said. "You hate receptions."

"I hate being useless."

"Those aren't the same thing, but I take your meaning." Ada sat on the edge of the bed, still in her corporal's coat even though the war had released her from it, or she had released herself, or some arrangement of the two that neither of them discussed anymore. "If Vasseur sees you—"

"He saw me once, across a checkpoint yard, in the dark, for four seconds."

"He has a memory like a customs ledger."

"I'll keep my back to him." Paloma pulled on the gloves. They were a size too large; the landlady had hands like shovels. "And if he does see me, what then? He can't arrest a guest at his own reception. Not here. This is Petra. There are laws here, even if they're thin ones."

"Laws are exactly as thin as the men enforcing them want them to be." Ada stood, crossed the small room, and straightened Paloma's collar with two brisk tugs — a gesture so like their old selves, so like the hospital tent and the shared watch and the years of friendship, that Paloma's throat tightened before she could stop it. "Come home early," Ada said. "That's all. Come home early, and tell me everything."

* * *

Customs House Hall had been built in the years when Petra's merchants believed architecture could make neutrality look like grandeur. Its ceiling was painted with allegories of Commerce and Concord, its marble floors echoed like a drum, and its long windows looked out over the fog-bound harbor where the fishing boats and the victors' gunboats lay at anchor within sight of one another, tolerating each other the way the city tolerated everything.

Inside, the reception was in full voice. Perhaps two hundred people moved through the rooms in slow orbits: naval officers in dress uniform, civilian officials of the Commission in black, foreign historians with the particular hungry alertness of men cataloguing a corpse. Waiters circulated with wine the color of garnets. A string quartet played in the corner, something measured and minor-keyed, music for people who wanted to feel solemn without having to think about why.

Paloma took a glass of wine she didn't drink and let the crowd carry her.

It didn't take long to find Vasseur. He stood near the great fireplace in the second room, and he was not, as she had half expected, holding court. He stood slightly apart from the clusters of conversation, a tall man in a colonel's uniform with the red-and-gold braid of the intelligence service, holding his own glass untouched, watching the room the way she imagined he watched checkpoints — not for what was happening, but for what might happen next. He had a face made for patience. When a historian approached him and bowed slightly, Vasseur inclined his head with a courtesy so precise it communicated nothing at all.

Paloma turned her back to him, as promised, and listened instead to the room.

"—the transfer ceremony is set for the end of the month," a woman in Commission black was saying to a knot of listeners near the window. "All field records from the coastal theater, crated and sealed under joint commission. Fifty-year closure, standard terms."

"Fifty years," someone murmured. "My students will be old men."

"The war deserves dignity," the woman said. "Dignity requires order. Order requires that the record be complete before it is consulted. Incomplete records produce confusion, and confusion produces—" she made a small gesture, as if brushing away a fly "—*claims*. We've had enough claims."

Paloma drifted closer, close enough to hold her glass among theirs, anonymous in her gray dress and oversized gloves. The woman speaking was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the unhurried authority of someone who had never once been interrupted. Her badge read **MME. ARLETTE DUCLOS, COMMISSION SECRETARY**.

"The families," another listener ventured — a young man, foreign, with the accent of the northern universities. "Surely provision is made for family inquiries. Deaths, pensions, that sort of thing."

"Inquiries are processed through the standard channels." Duclos smiled. It was a well-made smile, symmetrical and empty. "Which is precisely why the record must be orderly. You cannot answer a widow's question from a file full of discrepancies. Better a clean record than a contradictory one. A clean record is a kindness."

*A clean record is a kindness.* Paloma held the words in her mind the way she'd once held a feverish soldier's wrist — feeling for the pulse underneath, the thing actually happening beneath the surface of the skin. She thought of the ledger hidden under floorboards in a boarding-house room, forty-one names in her careful hand, each one a discrepancy, each one a contradiction, each one a widow's question that would never be asked because no widow would ever be told there was anything to ask.

A clean record. Yes. She understood now, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was how it was done. Not with burnings in the square, not with midnight arrests. With receptions and cream-colored cards and fifty-year closures and kind, symmetrical smiles. History was not erased. It was *curated*. The lie did not need to be written; it needed only for the truth to be filed somewhere no living person could reach it.

* * *

She moved through the rooms slowly, learning the geography of power the way she'd once learned the geography of a new ward — which corners mattered, which doors were guarded, which faces answered to which names. The archive itself was not in the hall; she gathered that the physical records were already crated in a warehouse on the east quay, awaiting transfer to the victors' capital. But the hall held the machinery around them: clerks with inventory lists, officers with manifests, and at the center of it all, near the fireplace, Colonel Vasseur, whose section — she learned by listening to two lieutenants who thought themselves discreet — held sole authority over the *investigative* files. Court-martial records. Supply inquiries. Anything touching fraud.

"The Brandt matter closes Friday week," one lieutenant said, low, over his wine. "Final finding goes to Duclos for inclusion in the commission report."

"And the finding?"

"'Inconclusive.'" The lieutenant pronounced the word with relish, savoring its weightlessness. "Insufficient documentation. Key witnesses deceased or unrecoverable. Records incomplete due to wartime conditions." He shrugged. "It's elegant, really. The fire at the garrison did half the work for us. Burn the evidence, then cite the absence of evidence. You can't call it a cover-up when the cover story is that there's nothing left to cover."

His companion laughed. "And the nurse? The one Vasseur's people have been asking about since the checkpoint?"

Paloma's blood went very still.

"What about her? If she surfaces, she surfaces. A nurse with a grievance and no documents is a woman with an opinion. Opinions don't go in commission reports." The lieutenant swirled his wine. "Besides, Vasseur says she'll come to us eventually. They always do. Everyone wants something — a pardon, a pass, a brother kept out of trouble. Sentiment is negotiable. That's the whole art of it."

They moved on. Paloma remained by the window, looking out at the harbor without seeing it, her reflection ghosted against the fog and the masts.

*Sentiment is negotiable.* So Vasseur had already named her, already priced her, already assigned her a category in his inventory. And the investigation into the supply fraud — the investigation that existed nowhere except as a future line in a sealed report — would close in nine days as *inconclusive*, and the word would sit in an archive like a headstone, and in fifty years some doctoral student would read it and write that the matter was never resolved, and that would be the end of Elias Brandt's dying confession, and the end of the ballast shipped in place of medicine, and the end of forty-one men whose names existed only in her handwriting and in God's memory, assuming God kept better records than the Commission.

She became aware that she was gripping her wineglass too hard and made herself loosen her fingers, one by one, deliberately, the way she'd taught orderlies to calm panicking patients. Breathe. Observe. Act only when acting will help.

Across the room, Vasseur was looking at her.

Not staring — he was too good for staring. His gaze simply rested on her for a moment among the hundred other moments of his survey of the room, and she could not tell whether recognition moved behind his eyes or whether she was merely one more gray dress in a room full of them. Then a historian claimed his elbow, and he turned, courteous and blank, and the moment dissolved.

But her heart was going hard now, and she knew it was time to go. She had what she'd come for. More than she'd come for. She set her untouched wine on a passing tray and began the slow orbit toward the doors, letting the crowd carry her, keeping her pace leisurely, a bored guest leaving a dull party early.

Near the entrance, Madame Duclos stood receiving farewells, and Paloma passed close enough to hear her say, yet again, to yet another departing dignitary, in that same kind and hollow voice:

"We owe the dead a truthful account. That is why we must be so careful about what we include."

* * *

The fog had thickened outside, swallowing the streetlamps, and Paloma walked home through it slowly, letting the cold wash the reception off her skin. The boarding house was dark when she reached it except for one window — Ada's, lamplit, waiting.

Marek was there too, sitting at the kitchen table with Ada, a pot of tea gone cold between them, and when Paloma came in they both looked up with the same question in their faces.

"It closes in nine days," she said. She untied the gloves and laid them on the table, still smelling faintly of someone else's lavender. "'Inconclusive.' Insufficient documentation. The fire did their work for them. And Vasseur knows about me — he's been asking after 'a nurse' since the checkpoint. They're not hunting me. They're *waiting* for me. Waiting for me to want something badly enough to trade."

Ada's jaw tightened. "Then we move faster."

"Marek." Paloma sat down heavily. "How fast can you print?"

He turned his teacup slowly on its saucer, thinking. "Fast enough. But paper alone won't do it. A broadsheet with no witnesses behind it is just a woman shouting in the fog — they'll bury me in libel suits and bury you in whatever charges they invent. If we're going to break this open before Duclos seals it, we need testimony. Sworn, witnessed, public testimony. Enough of it, from enough mouths, that sealing the record afterward looks like exactly what it is." He looked up, and in the lamplight his face had lost its printer's irony and become something harder and brighter. "We need to put the truth on the record *before* they finish building the false one. Do you understand what I'm telling you? We need a tribunal."

Paloma looked at the two of them — the printer with ink under his nails, the soldier who had lied to her for months — and felt the cold clarity of the reception settle into something else. Not fear. Not quite resolve. Something colder and more useful than either: the exact knowledge of how little time remained.

"Nine days," she said. "Then we have eight."
