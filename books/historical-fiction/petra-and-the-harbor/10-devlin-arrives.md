## 10. Devlin Arrives

The fog in Petra did not lift so much as thin, like gauze pulled slowly from a wound. Devlin Kavanagh stood at the harbor mouth and watched it happen, his sea bag over one shoulder, the ticket stub for a coastal steamer still crumpled in his fist. Three days on the water. Four days before that on trains that stopped running where the war's new borders had been drawn, and after that a cart, a mule track, a smuggler's path through hills where every shepherd looked like an intelligence officer and every farmhouse door opened onto either bread or a rifle barrel.

He had come three hundred miles to ask his sister to destroy a piece of paper.

He told himself it was more complicated than that. He was good at telling himself things were more complicated than they were; their uncle Tomas had taught him that much, all those years of port offices and stamped manifests and the family talent for finding the version of a story that could be lived with.

The harbor resolved out of the fog in pieces: mast tips first, then crane arms, then the long stone arc of the quay, black with wet. Petra smelled of tar and herring and printer's ink — he'd heard that last one from a fellow passenger, a typesetter who talked about the neutral city the way pilgrims talked about shrines. *Everything gets printed there,* the man had said. *Everything true, everything false, and no way to tell which is which until the ink dries.*

Devlin found a room above a chandlery, paid too much for it, and then went looking for his sister in a city of forty thousand strangers, carrying only her description, his own stubbornness, and the telegram he had not sent because telegrams left trails.

* * *

It took him two days.

He learned the geography of the fog district by walking it: the fish market where women gutted the morning catch and passed rumors along with the knives; the taverns with their windows papered over, full of men who spoke in the accents of four defeated nations and drank like men with nothing to lose but time; the print shops, seven of them, each one smelling of solvent and secrets. At the fifth shop — a narrow premises wedged between a rope-maker and a shuttered chapel, its window displaying pamphlets in six languages — a broad-shouldered man with ink-stained fingers looked at him for a long moment before he said anything.

"You have her chin," the man said. "And her habit of standing like she's waiting for bad news."

"Then you know her."

"I know her." The printer wiped his hands on a rag that was itself mostly ink by now. "Marek Sol. She's out — gathering testimony, if you want the honest word for it. She'll be back before dark." He studied Devlin the way a compositor studies type, sorting him letter by letter. "You're the brother. Devlin."

"And how would you know that?"

"Because she talks about you when she thinks no one is listening. And because you walk like Tomas Kavanagh, and I've seen his signature on enough manifests to know the man's shadow when I see it." Sol set down the rag. "Whatever you've come to say to her, understand something first. Every hour she spends protecting that letter is an hour she isn't protecting herself. If you're here to take it off her, you'd best be prepared to answer for what happens to the names inside it."

"I don't know anything about any letter," Devlin said, and heard how it sounded, and hated himself for it.

Sol almost smiled. "No," he said. "Of course you don't. Sit down anyway. There's coffee. It's terrible, but it's hot, and you look like a man who hasn't had either in days."

* * *

She came back at dusk, hood up, cheeks raw from the wind off the water, and stopped dead in the doorway of the print shop when she saw him.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Paloma crossed the room in four strides and hit him — not hard, a sister's blow, flat of her hand against his chest — and then her arms were around him and her face was buried in his shoulder and she was shaking, just slightly, the way a mast shakes when the wind drops after a storm.

"You idiot," she said into his coat. "You absolute idiot. Do you know what the roads are like? Do you know what they're doing to travelers at the border crossings?"

"I walked around them."

"You walked *around them.*" She pulled back and held him at arm's length, and he saw what the last months had done to her: she was thinner, sharper, her eyes carrying a watchfulness that had not been there when he'd waved her off to the garrison hospital a lifetime ago. "Uncle Tomas?"

"That's why I'm here." He glanced at Marek Sol, who was suddenly very interested in a drawer of type. "Not here."

"There's nowhere else," Paloma said quietly. "Whatever you have to say, say it where Marek can hear it. He's the only reason any of us might see spring."

Devlin looked at the printer. The printer looked back, unblinking, patient as furniture.

"Tomas has been arrested," Devlin said. "Nine days ago. They came to the house at night — not the port authority, army provosts, the ones who answer to Vasseur's people. They took him to the capital. Mother's seen him once, through glass, for ten minutes. He looked—" His voice caught, and he steadied it the way their father had taught them both to steady it, jaw set, breath slow. "He looked old, Paloma. He looked like a man who'd already been sentenced."

Paloma lowered herself onto a stool beside the press. Her hands were very still in her lap, and Devlin knew that stillness; it was the stillness of someone holding something down.

"They let you travel," she said. "They arrested him and let the rest of the family travel freely. Doesn't that strike you as strange?"

"The arrest isn't about him. That's the point." Devlin crouched so that he was level with her, the way you crouch to speak to a frightened child or a spooked animal, and immediately regretted the gesture when her eyes flashed. He pressed on anyway. "Paloma, listen to me. I spent a week in the capital before I came. I talked to Father Reilly, who knows a judge. I talked to a lawyer whose name Tomas gave me years ago for exactly this kind of trouble. What they both said, what everyone says, is the same thing: the investigation into the supply fraud is going to be closed as inconclusive. It's already decided. But there's one loose thread, one thing that keeps it open, and while it's open, Tomas stays in a cell and our family stays under suspicion and anyone connected to the case is a target." He reached out and took her hands. They were cold. "The thread is Brandt's letter. Whatever you're carrying — whatever Marek here has told you is worth printing — it is the single document keeping a lamp lit over our whole family's heads. Put it out. Burn it, bury it, drop it in the harbor. And within a month Tomas walks free, the investigation closes, and we go back to being nobody, which is the safest thing a family can be in a country that just won a war."

The press ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a church bell counted six, its sound arriving flattened by fog.

"Marek," Paloma said without turning her head. "Could you give us the room?"

"No," Marek said pleasantly, sorting type. "But I could pretend not to hear, if that helps."

* * *

They walked instead. Down to the quay, past the fish stalls closing for the night, out along the breakwater where the fog came in thick enough to drink. The lighthouse turned somewhere behind them, its beam arriving and departing like a slow thought.

"You didn't even ask me what's in the letter," Paloma said at last.

"I don't want to know. Knowing makes me complicit."

"You're my brother. You were born complicit." She stopped at the breakwater's end and faced the open water, where the fog and the sea were the same gray. "Do you want to know what Brandt told me before he died? Not what's written — what he said. He said the medicine crates that went to the Third Convoy were filled with ballast stones. Sandbags, sometimes. He said the requisitions were signed by officers whose names he wouldn't give me even dying, because giving them would get the letter burned and him buried faster than the fever was already doing. He said the scheme couldn't have run for two years without men at the ports signing clean manifests for dirty cargo." She turned to face him. "Tomas signed manifests, Devlin."

"He signed what he was made to sign—"

"Yes! Yes, I know. I went home, do you remember? Before the evacuation. I sat in his kitchen and he told me everything — the threats, the officer who came to his office, the way they made sure he understood what happened to port officials whose families had accidents. He's guilty the way a man held at gunpoint is guilty. I have never doubted that, not for one hour." Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Which is exactly why burning the letter won't save him."

"That's not what the lawyer says."

"The lawyer says what the lawyers are allowed to say." She stepped closer. "Think, Devlin. Think the way you used to think, before grief made you stupid. Why arrest Tomas now? After two years? Why this month, this week — right when the historians arrive in Petra, right when the archives are being sealed? If they wanted the matter closed quietly, they'd have closed it quietly, the way these things always close. Instead they arrest a sick old man who signed under duress, and they parade the arrest, and suddenly the family has a reason — a loving, desperate, honorable reason — to make the inconvenient documents disappear." She shook her head slowly. "They're not punishing him. They're using him. His cell is a lever, and you're the end of it, and they sent you here — whether you know it or not — to pull."

"I wasn't sent." But even as he said it, he heard the week replaying: the judge's clerk who had been so helpful about routes; the fellow passenger on the steamer who'd asked friendly questions about family; the ease of a journey that should have been hard. He had told himself it was luck. He had told himself a great many things.

"Maybe not knowingly," Paloma said, gentler now. "That's what makes it work. Love doesn't need to be recruited, Devlin. It volunteers."

The lighthouse beam swept across them and away. Out on the black water, a fishing boat's lantern bobbed, heading out with the evening tide toward waters where no nation's flag meant anything.

"So what's your plan?" Devlin asked, and heard the anger drain out of his own voice, replaced by something wearier. "Print it? Print a dead quartermaster's letter and a nurse's notebook and hope it changes what the victors have already decided to remember? They'll call it forgery. They'll call it enemy propaganda. They'll call it a hysterical woman's fantasy — I've read the papers, Paloma, I know what they write about nurses who ask questions."

"Some of that may happen."

"And Tomas rots either way!"

"No." She said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was worse than shouting. "If the record exists — printed, witnessed, sworn, distributed beyond anyone's reach to recall — then Tomas's arrest stops being leverage and starts being a liability. You can't quietly release a man whose case is in a thousand hands across four countries; releasing him proves the arrest was coercion. You can't keep him either, not with foreign journalists asking why a coerced manifest-signer is imprisoned while the officers who ran the scheme sit on decoration committees. The moment the truth is public, his safest road is the truth. Right now, with the truth sealed away, his fastest road out runs through our silence — and through whatever else they want from us next." She took his arm, and her grip was fierce. "Burning the letter buys him a month, maybe two. Then they come back for the ledger, and then for me, and then for you, because silence is never a payment, Devlin, it's a down payment. I have watched men die of missing medicine. Hundreds of them. I wrote down every name because the reports erased them, and I will not be the second erasure. Not even for Tomas. *Especially* not for Tomas — because he'd never forgive us, and you know it."

Devlin looked at his sister — this person he had carried on his shoulders across a beach when she was five, whom he had taught to swim, whom he had believed he would always, always protect — and understood that she had become someone the war had finished making while he hadn't been watching. She was not frightened. That was the terrible thing. She had walked all the way through fear and out the other side into country he didn't have a map for.

"If you're wrong," he said quietly, "it's his life."

"And if you're wrong, it's theirs." She touched his cheek, briefly, the old gesture. "I'd rather be wrong about Tomas with a clear conscience than wrong about four hundred dead men with a comfortable one."

* * *

They argued until midnight. They argued in the print shop while Marek Sol set type and said nothing; they argued on the stairs of the lodging house; they argued in whispers in the fog outside her door until the landlady thumped the wall. He brought out every argument the lawyer had given him, and some he invented himself, and she met them all with the ledger's arithmetic — numbers, dates, convoy numbers, names — until near the end she simply read him six names aloud, slowly, soldiers from their own county, boys he'd grown up throwing stones at gulls with, and he stopped arguing because there was nothing left to argue with.

He slept three hours on a cot in the print shop's back room and woke before dawn to the sound of voices below.

Through the floorboards he heard Marek's low rumble, and Paloma's voice answering — and a third voice, a woman's, familiar somehow, urgent and low and cracked at the edges. He lay still and listened, and caught fragments: *"...took it in the night... I swear to you I didn't..."* and then Paloma, sharp with something that sounded nothing like the calm she had shown him: *"Then tell me exactly what you told him, Ada. All of it. Starting from the checkpoint."*

Silence. A long, terrible silence, the kind that follows a confession.

Devlin sat up in the dark of the back room, his heart going hard against his ribs, and understood that whatever his sister had been guarding, the war around it had just changed shape again — and that the woman downstairs, whoever she was, was crying.

He got up quietly and went to the top of the stairs to listen.
