## 11. Ada's Bargain

The fog in Petra did not lift so much as it thinned, reluctantly, like a confession dragged out of a man who had rehearsed lying his whole life. Ada Reyes stood at the window of the rented room on Rope Lane and watched it happen, her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory she had not drunk. Below, the harbor worked itself awake — gulls screaming over fish guts, a winch groaning somewhere behind the salt-bleached warehouses, the bell of a neutral-flagged tramp steamer counting out the hour with the indifference of a thing that had never chosen a side.

Three days. They had been in Petra three days, and she had sent two reports.

The first had gone out from the customs house, scrawled in pencil on the back of a laundry list: *Arrived. Two women. Documents intact.* The second, slipped into the drop box behind the chandler's on Cooper Street, had been harder to write: *They have made contact with a printer. Sol. Letter still hidden.*

*Intact.* She had actually written that word. As if the ledger were cargo, crates of nails, bales of wool. As if it were not four hundred and eleven names in Paloma Kavanagh's small, square handwriting, names of men who had died of bad medicine while the army wrote *fever* and *dysentery* and closed its books.

Ada set the cup down because her hands had begun to shake, and a shaking cup was a sound, and sounds woke people, and Paloma slept badly enough these days without Ada's guilt clattering around the room.

Paloma slept curled near the wall, one arm under her head, her boots — the ones with the letter sewn into the heel — tucked beneath the thin blanket within reach even in sleep. That was the thing about Paloma that Ada had loved for six years and could no longer look at straight on: she trusted the way other people breathed. Automatically. Without deciding to.

*You're a coward,* Ada told herself. *No. You're a sister.* Both were true. That was the trap Vasseur had built, and he had built it well, and he had built it out of her, and there was no version of this where she came out clean.

* * *

It had started at the checkpoint. Of course it had. Everything since the checkpoint had been downstream of that morning.

The column of refugees had stretched half a mile along the coast road, and Colonel Vasseur's intelligence men had moved down it like a comb through lice, tipping flour out of sacks, slashing mattress ticking, making mothers cry and old men recite loyalty oaths they'd already recited twice. Ada had stood beside the cart with her papers ready, playing the bored corporal on escort duty, and it had almost worked. It had genuinely almost worked. The bread tin sat under the false bottom of the cart bed with the ledger inside it, and the lieutenant had put his boot on the cart wheel and looked directly at Ada and said, "Corpsman? You're escorting these people?"

"Medical escort," she'd said. "Fever cases back at the column's rear."

And then Vasseur himself had come up the line. She recognized him before anyone named him — the way he walked, unhurried, like a man who owned time itself, the way his eyes went over a cart the way an auditor reads a ledger, top line to bottom, nothing wasted. He had stopped. He had looked at her uniform, then her face, and something in her face must have answered a question he hadn't asked yet, because he smiled very slightly, the way a man smiles when he finds money in an old coat.

"Reyes," he had read off her tag. He hadn't needed to check. She understood later that he had already known; the garrison rosters were among the first things his section seized. "Corporal Reyes. You served at Harrow Point."

"Yes, sir."

"You knew the medical staff there." Not a question. A door being opened, politely.

She should have lied. She had lied to officers before; every soldier above the rank of innocence had. But Vasseur had looked at her with such patient, professional kindness, and he had said, quietly, so only she could hear: "I have a list, Corporal. Your sister is on it. Marta Reyes, arrested in March, awaiting transport to the penal colony at Veracruz. Transport leaves when the port reopens." A pause, exactly long enough. "Transports are subject to administrative review. Reviews are facilitated by cooperative witnesses."

Marta. Seventeen years old, arrested for printing leaflets, for nothing, for ink and paper, and Ada had spent the entire war knowing that somewhere behind the lines her sister was in a cell getting thinner, and had done nothing, because what could a corporal do? Nothing. A corporal was nothing. But a corporal who saw faces and heard conversations and knew where documents slept —

He hadn't asked for anything that day. That was the genius of it. He had simply handed her a card with a chandler's address and walked on, and the bread tin had stayed hidden, and she had told herself all the way into Petra that she would never use the card, never, that she would burn it.

She had used it within forty hours.

* * *

The second report had cost her more than the first. The first had been facts — arrival, documents, route. The second had named Marek Sol, and naming Sol meant naming the press, and naming the press meant that if Vasseur moved fast, the whole fragile scheme would die in a crate of unset type. She had written it anyway. She had stood in the alley behind the chandler's at dusk, sliding the paper into the slot, and she had thought: *one more report and I stop. One more and Marta's transfer gets approved and I stop.* Every informant in history had probably stood in some alley thinking *one more*, and the alley had probably smelled the same — tar and fish and rain coming.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape, the arithmetic that ate everything else: each report bought a piece of Marta. Vasseur had shown her the mechanism once — a memorandum, a signature, a transfer order moving up a queue. *Your cooperation is noted favorably.* Marta had been moved from the transport list. Moved to better rations. Each scrap of information Ada fed the colonel was, in the only currency that mattered to her, a meal her sister ate, a week her sister lived. What was a ledger against that? What were dead men's names against a living girl's skin?

She knew the answer. God help her, she knew the answer, and she wrote the reports anyway.

* * *

Footsteps on the stair. Ada turned from the window, spine straightening by reflex, the old soldier's trick of arranging your body into innocence.

But it was only Paloma, awake, hair loose, eyes shadowed. She looked at Ada standing fully dressed at dawn by the window and something flickered across her face — not suspicion, worse, concern.

"You didn't sleep," Paloma said.

"I slept."

"You're wearing yesterday's collar." Paloma crossed the room and took Ada's wrist, gently, the nurse's grip, checking pulse like they were back in the ward at Harrow Point and the world was only fevers and dressings. "Your heart's going like a drummer boy's. Sit down before you fall down. There's a fever going through the lodging house on the next street; Marek mentioned it. If you've caught something—"

"I haven't caught anything." Ada pulled her wrist free, too quickly, and saw the hurt move through Paloma's eyes and hated herself for it. "I'm fine, Paloma. Bad dreams. Everyone has them now."

That was true enough to pass. Everyone in Petra had bad dreams. The city was full of them — deserters sleeping in doorways with their hands twitching toward weapons that weren't there, widows who talked to empty chairs, printers who set type by lamplight with the expressions of men defusing ordnance. Bad dreams were the local currency.

Paloma let it go. She dressed, pinned her hair, checked the boot with the letter — Ada watched her do it, watched her fingers press the heel seam, and felt the familiar nausea roll through her gut, because Paloma performed this little ritual openly, trusting Ada to see it, trusting Ada with the knowledge of where the last real evidence slept. Trusting her like a sister.

*I have a sister,* Ada thought. *That's the whole disease right there.*

* * *

They spent the morning apart, which was routine now — Paloma at Marek Sol's shop, chasing witnesses, chasing paper, chasing the tribunal that was assembling itself out of tavern talk and stubbornness. Ada had errands. Ada always had errands. Errands were how a woman explained walking alone through a fog-bound city with a guilty conscience.

She did not go to the chandler's. She had promised herself that much: today she would not write, would not report, would go instead to the harbor authority office where the prisoner manifests were posted, and find Marta's name, and confirm with her own eyes that the machinery was turning. Proof that the bargain was paying. Proof that she wasn't selling the dead for nothing.

The manifest was posted outside the transport office under glass, in beautiful bureaucratic copperplate. Ada found the list for the *Corvina*, scheduled to sail on the spring tides. Her eye ran down the column of names — political, seditious, miscellaneous — and stopped.

*MARTA REYES — REMOVED PENDING REVIEW.*

Removed. Pending review. Which meant held. Which meant, in the language underneath the language, that Vasseur was keeping her. Keeping her the way a man keeps a debt instrument. Ada stood in front of the glass until a clerk asked if she required assistance, and she said no, thank you, ma'am, just looking for family, and walked away with her face arranged into blankness while inside her something gave way with a sound like a ship's rib cracking.

He had never intended to release Marta. Or rather — he would release her, eventually, at the end, when the ledger was in his hands and burned and the investigation sealed as inconclusive. Marta was not payment. Marta was collateral. And Ada had known this, some cold cellar of her mind had known it from the checkpoint onward, and had walked into the arrangement anyway, because hope will sign any contract put in front of it.

* * *

She found Paloma at Sol's shop in the late afternoon, and the scene there undid her further.

The print shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and cold metal. Marek Sol, a broad, slow-moving man with ink-stained knuckles and the gentle voice of someone who had spent thirty years talking to machines, was showing Paloma the forme he'd begun setting — the letter, Brandt's letter, transcribed in proof type, waiting. Around the walls hung proofs of the broadsheet-to-be, and Paloma stood reading them the way people stand at gravesides.

"They'll come," she was saying. "The gunner, Okafor — he can't speak but he writes a fair hand. The convoy driver, Lindqvist. The hospital steward, Perreira. Three sworn testimonies plus my ledger plus Brandt's letter. Marek says we can have five hundred copies off the press within a day of the tribunal."

"And after?" Ada asked, from the doorway.

Paloma turned, and her face in the lamplight was the face Ada had followed through blood and mud and burning wards for six years — tired past tiredness, lit from underneath by something that refused to die. "After, it doesn't matter what happens to me. Once five hundred copies exist in five hundred hands, they can't unprint it. That's the whole point of Petra. That's why this harbor exists." She smiled faintly. "You've carried it this far, Ada. We're nearly there. A few more days."

*A few more days.* Vasseur wanted the letter and the ledger before the tribunal convened. Vasseur's deadline and Paloma's deadline were converging on the same few days, and Ada stood between them like a bridge between two banks, and bridges are built to be walked over, and both sides intended to walk over her.

"I need to tell you something," Ada said.

Her own voice sounded far away. Paloma looked at her, waiting, patient, and the patience was the worst part — six years of friendship distilled into that waiting, the trust of a woman who had shared her water ration and her watch shifts and her dead, standing there offering Ada one more chance to be honest.

And Ada opened her mouth, and what came out was: "I need more lamp oil. For tonight. I'll take the watch again."

Paloma studied her a moment longer. Then she nodded, and squeezed Ada's arm, and said, "Get some sleep tonight. That's an order, Corporal," with the ghost of their old ward humor in it, and turned back to the proofs.

* * *

She tried to confess twice more that evening. Once on the walk home, when Paloma talked about Devlin — whether her brother would come, whether he could be made to understand — and the words rose in Ada's throat like floodwater and she swallowed them. Once at the lodging house, climbing the stairs, when Paloma stumbled on the dark landing and laughed and said, "Six years of night watches and I still trip over my own feet," and Ada had gripped her elbow and held on a half-second too long and said nothing, nothing, nothing.

Now it was midnight. Paloma slept. The tin — Ada had insisted they keep it here, in the room, under the floorboard by the hearth, the way Paloma had kept it at the garrison, habit becoming ritual — lay beneath three boards and a scatter of coal dust. The letter stayed in the boot. Two hiding places, two treasures, and Ada was the only living soul who knew where both lay, except Vasseur, who knew one of them, because Ada had told him about the tin at the checkpoint and he had never forgotten a fact in his life.

Tomorrow. Vasseur's note — passed through the chandler's boy at dusk, folded inside a receipt for rope — had been explicit. *Tomorrow night, or the review concludes unfavorably.* The review. Marta. Tomorrow night.

Which meant tomorrow was the last day of the war, as far as Ada Reyes was concerned. The last day she could be both women. The friend and the informant, the loyalist and the traitor, the woman who had carried a wounded Paloma Kavanagh on her back for two miles under shellfire and the woman who had sold her, board by board, house by house, secret by secret.

She sat on the floor beside the hearth until the candle guttered, listening to Paloma breathe, doing the arithmetic one final time. Four hundred and eleven names against one living girl. And then the other sum, the one that wouldn't balance no matter how she arranged the numbers: what would those four hundred and eleven men say, if they could speak? What would the gunner write? What would Paloma do — not the Paloma of the ledger, the Paloma of the ward, the one whose rule had been *we count everyone or we count no one*?

She knew what Paloma would do. Paloma would forgive her. That was the cruelest item in the whole inventory. Paloma would forgive her, and forgiveness would be heavier than any punishment, and Ada would have to carry it the rest of her life alongside everything else.

"One more day," she whispered to the dark, to the sleeping room, to the tin under the floorboard, to the sister she was trying to save and the sister she was destroying, because the word *sister* had only ever meant one person to her and the word had finally broken. "Give me one more day, and then I'll tell you everything. I swear it. One more day."

Behind her, Paloma shifted in her sleep and murmured something — a name, maybe, one of the four hundred and eleven, or her brother's, or no one's — and rolled toward the wall.

Ada did not sleep. She sat by the dying candle and watched the door, and somewhere below, in the fog-bound streets, she heard footsteps that paused outside the lodging house, waited, and moved on. She told herself it was a drunk. Petra was full of drunks.

It was full of other things too.

By dawn, the space beneath the floorboard would be empty.
