## 12. The Tin Goes Missing

Paloma woke to the sound of gulls and knew, before she opened her eyes, that something was wrong.

It was not a sound exactly. It was an absence — the particular silence of a room where another person's breathing should have been. For eleven days in the rented room above Marek Sol's print shop, Ada Reyes had slept in the narrow bed by the window, her breath slow and even, her boots placed beneath it with the parade-ground precision that had survived every hardship the war had visited on them. Paloma could set her waking by it. She had done so through fever tents and bombardments, through the long march to Petra, through nights when sleep came only because she trusted the sound of Ada breathing three feet away.

This morning the bed was empty. The blanket was folded. And the bread tin was gone.

She sat up so fast the room tilted. The floor was cold under her bare feet as she crossed to the wardrobe, pulling open the door, though some part of her already knew what she would find. The shelf where the tin had sat — behind a stack of mended shirts, wedged against the wall, invisible unless you knew to look — held only dust and the faint ring-shaped stain of rust where the tin's base had rested for a week and a half.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the empty room.

The word came out small. She hated how small it sounded.

* * *

She did not remember dressing. She remembered pieces: her fingers fumbling the buttons at her wrist twice; the cold iron banister under her palm as she went down the stairs; the smell of ink and hot metal rising from the press room below, where Marek Sol was already at work, sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow.

He looked up when she appeared in the doorway and whatever he saw in her face made him set down his composing stick very carefully, the way a man sets down a live shell.

"The tin," Paloma said.

"Paloma—"

"It's gone." Her voice cracked on the second word and she steadied herself against the doorframe, hating the weakness, hating the way her hands would not stay still. "Someone took it in the night."

Marek wiped his hands slowly on a rag, buying time with the gesture. He was a heavyset man with ink worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that he might have been born with it, and he had a habit of looking slightly past a person when he thought, as if the answer might be found printed somewhere just over their shoulder.

"When did you last see it?" he asked.

"Last night. Before I slept. I checked it — I always check it." She heard herself and stopped. *I always check it.* Eleven nights of checking, and the one morning she had not woken to do it, it was gone. "Ada was awake when I checked. She asked me if I'd counted the pages again and I said yes and she laughed at me. She laughed, Marek. She was lying there laughing at me for counting them, and she knew."

"You don't know that."

"Don't I?" Paloma pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. Behind her eyelids the ledger lay open in memory: two hundred and thirty-one names in her careful field-hand, each one dated, each one cross-referenced to a shipping number, each one a man the army had decided had never existed. Two hundred and thirty-one men whose mothers would grow old waiting for letters that would never come, because the letters had never been sent. "Who else knew where it was? You didn't. Devlin doesn't know it exists. The landlady has never been above the second stair. There is exactly one other person alive who has seen me put my hands on that tin, and she slept three feet from it for eleven nights."

Marek was quiet for a moment. Then, gently: "And she's the same woman who carried it through Vasseur's checkpoint with a bayonet six inches from her face."

The words were meant to steady her. They did not. They landed somewhere in her chest and turned over, because that was the vertigo of it — that the woman who had shielded the tin with her own body at the roadblock outside Marren was also the woman who had asked too many questions about wax seals, who had wanted to know what was written inside the letter, who had held the tin on her lap through the whole night watch out of Marren while Paloma pretended to sleep and watched her through her lashes, watching the way her thumb moved absently over the lid, back and forth, back and forth, like a woman touching something she was trying to decide whether to keep.

"I have to find her," Paloma said.

"She went out early," Marek said. "Before light. I heard the street door. I assumed she'd gone to the fish market — she said yesterday she'd buy us something better than salt cod for once."

"Before light," Paloma repeated. The fog was thick off the harbor most mornings; the fish stalls did not open until the bells rang seven. "To buy fish. In the dark."

Marek said nothing. His silence was its own answer, and she was grateful to him for not saying the thing they were both thinking.

* * *

Petra in fog was a city of half-people. Shapes detached themselves from the white and resolved into fishermen, then dissolved again. Rope creaked somewhere unseen. The harbor smelled of tar and brine and, beneath it, the sweetish rot of fish heads dumped beyond the breakwater. Paloma walked fast, arms crossed against the damp, scanning the waterfront with an urgency she tried to keep off her face, because she understood now — had understood since the checkpoint, since the letter first burned a hole through her pack — that in Petra a woman who looked frightened was a woman marked. Half this city lived on secrets, and the other half made their living selling them.

She checked the fish stalls first. Empty nets, idle knives, a boy scaling mackerel who shook his head at her question. She checked the chapel on Tanner's Row where Ada sometimes went to sit — not to pray, Ada never prayed, but to be alone, which was its own kind of prayer for her. Empty. She stood a long time at the corner of the customs house, watching the victors' officers come and go through its doors, their gray-blue uniforms crisp even in the fog, wondering with a sickness in her stomach whether any of them had seen Ada walk in that morning carrying a dented bread tin.

By the time the fog began to thin, she had circled back toward the print shop, and that was where she found her — coming up Harrow Lane at a half-run, cheeks flushed, a paper parcel of actual butter clutched in one hand like a trophy.

"Paloma!" Ada's face opened with relief and then closed again as she read what was in Paloma's face. "What is it? What's happened?"

"The tin is gone."

For one full second — Paloma would count it later, in the long hours of reexamining everything, and the second would feel longer than any minute of her life — Ada's expression did not change at all. Not confusion, not shock. Nothing. A blankness, smooth as still water.

Then the color drained from her face, all at once, like water through a broken hull.

"Gone," she said. "Gone how?"

"That's what I'm asking you."

"Me?" Ada took a step back. The butter parcel crumpled in her fist. "Paloma, I've been at the market. I left before you woke, I couldn't sleep, I went walking and then the stalls opened and—"

"You left before light."

"I couldn't sleep! Since we got here I can't sleep, you know I can't—" Ada stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone flat and controlled, the voice she used for triage, for holding a screaming man still while the surgeon worked. "You think I took it."

"I think someone took it. And there are only two people in this city who knew it existed under that roof, and one of them is me."

"And the other one spent the whole march to Petra telling you we'd get caught, telling you to bury it, burn it, throw it in the harbor — and did anyway, because it was yours and you asked me to carry it." Ada's eyes were bright now, overbright, and Paloma recognized with a lurch that she was not only angry. She was wounded. Genuinely, deeply wounded. "Say it plainly, Paloma. Say you think I sold it."

"I don't want to think anything. I want the tin back."

They stood in Harrow Lane with the fog lifting around them and a cart driver cursing at them to move, and neither of them moved, and Paloma understood that whatever happened next, something between them had already cracked in a way that might never set right.

* * *

They searched.

Not the room — the room took ten minutes and yielded nothing but the rust ring on the shelf, proof of absence. They searched the city. Or rather, Paloma searched the city, and Ada searched beside her, silent, stiff, hurt, and Paloma could not stop watching her sideways, cataloguing her, and could not stop despising herself for doing it.

They went to the tavern on Coopers' Quay where Marek said witnesses sometimes gathered, where a one-eyed pilot named Doss kept a table near the fire and traded gossip for gin. Doss had heard nothing about raids. But the potboy, leaning close to wipe the table, said — without being asked, without looking up — that there had been soldiers in the lower town after midnight. Gray-blue coats. Going door to door along the ropewalk, quiet as thieves, knocking soft, asking questions about papers.

Paloma's heart lurched. "Which house?"

"Didn't catch the numbers." The boy shrugged, wiped, moved on.

At the harbormaster's office, the clerk — a bored young man with chilblains — confirmed it: the harbor authority had granted a search warrant at midnight. Signed by Colonel Vasseur himself, acting on intelligence of "seditious documents smuggled into the port." The warrant covered the Harrow Lane district. Every lodging house. Every print shop.

Every print shop.

"They were here," Paloma whispered. "Last night. While we slept, they were in this building."

"Marek would have told us."

"Would he? Would he risk it?" She thought of Marek's careful hands, his careful face, a printer who had survived twenty years of wars and occupations by knowing exactly how much truth a press could hold. Maybe he had hidden them. Maybe he had simply stayed in his room and listened to boots on his stairs and let it happen, the way sensible men let things happen, and she could not even blame him for it.

But Ada — Ada had changed. Somewhere between the chapel and the harbormaster's office the stiffness had gone out of her shoulders, replaced by something worse: a terrible, urgent earnestness. She kept stopping Paloma in the street, gripping her sleeve.

"Think about it. Think about the timing. Vasseur gets a warrant at midnight, searches the whole lane, and takes one bread tin out of one room and nothing else? No papers seized from Marek, no arrests, nobody dragged out in the fog? That's not a raid, Paloma. That's a retrieval. Someone gave him exactly where to look."

"And who would give him that?" Paloma asked, and heard her own voice go cold and strange.

"I don't know." Ada's grip tightened. "I swear to you, on my mother, on my sister, on every— I was asleep. I woke when the stairs creaked, I heard them moving below, I thought it was rats or drunks, I lay there listening and I must have drifted off again, I don't remember—"

"You heard them," Paloma said quietly. "You heard them come up the stairs. Past our door."

Ada let go of her sleeve.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Down at the quay a bell rang eight, and the sound rolled across the water, flattened by the last of the fog, and Paloma watched Ada stand very straight in the middle of the wet street with her jaw tight and her hands opening and closing at her sides, and felt the ground tilt beneath everything she had believed for nine years. Nine years since the training hospital at Corvane. Nine years of shared rations and shared watches and Ada's shoulder under her hand during the bombardment of the third winter, when Paloma had cried and Ada had said, flatly, "Not yet. Cry later. We're working." Nine years, weighed against one bread tin.

Or weighed against whatever else Ada might be carrying that Paloma knew nothing about.

"If you took it," Paloma said, and her voice barely obeyed her, "and you give it back now, today, before it reaches anyone's desk that matters — I will say nothing. To anyone. Ever. That is the last time I offer."

"I didn't take it." Ada's voice broke on the words, actually broke, and Paloma had known this woman through plagues and amputations and had never once heard her voice break. "God help me, Paloma, I didn't take it. But I think I know why you'll never believe that again, no matter what I say."

* * *

She went back alone in the late afternoon. Ada had walked off down the quay without a destination, without her butter, without looking back, and Paloma had let her go because following her felt unbearable and letting her go felt like surrender and she could not tell which was worse.

In the print shop, Marek was setting type by lamplight though it was barely dusk, the leaden letters gleaming like rows of tiny coffins in their tray.

"Nothing?" he asked.

"Nothing." She lowered herself onto the bench by the door, and the exhaustion arrived all at once, like a tide coming in. "Two hundred and thirty-one names, Marek. Dates, regiments, shipping numbers. Three months of Brandt's ledgers copied out in my own hand. Everything that proves the convoy was poisoned, everything that proves the army wrote those men out of existence — gone into Vasseur's desk drawer, or into his stove, whichever he prefers."

"Not everything."

She looked up.

Marek set down his composing stick and came to sit across from her, and leaned forward on his knees, and studied her face with those eyes that always seemed to be reading something just past her shoulder.

"The letter," he said. "Brandt's letter. Where is it now, Paloma?"

Her hand moved before her mind did — down, quick, to her left boot — and stopped. And she saw that he had watched the whole motion, and that he had asked the question precisely so that he could watch it.

"The raiders didn't search your boots," Marek said mildly. "Men in a hurry, hunting a tin, following a tip about a tin — they don't kneel down and unlace a sleeping woman's boots. Which means the letter is still where it has been since Marren. Which means Vasseur has taken a great deal of weight off your conscience and none off your cause. The ledger proved the scale. The letter names the men. Names, Paloma. A ledger is evidence. A name is a weapon."

She stared at him. The lamplight flickered between them.

"But it's only one document," she said slowly. "One dying man's accusation. Without the ledger behind it—"

"Then we make more evidence." Marek's heavy face had changed; something lit in it now, something fierce and old. "There are men drinking in the taverns of this city tonight who saw what came off those ships. Survivors of the supply convoys. Deserters who handled the crates. This harbor is full of witnesses, if anyone thinks to ask them. If anyone thinks to write down what they say, and swear them to it, and print it beside the letter for every soul in Petra to read—" He paused. "A tribunal, Paloma. Not the kind with judges. The kind with witnesses."

Outside, the fog had come in again off the water, swallowing the masts, swallowing the quay, and somewhere out in that white blindness Ada Reyes was walking with her guilt or her innocence or whatever it was she carried instead, and Colonel Vasseur was reading two hundred and thirty-one names by candlelight, deciding what to burn first.

Paloma pressed her boot against the floorboard and felt the small hard rectangle of wax-sealed paper above her ankle, and understood that she had lost almost everything except the one thing they wanted least — because they did not yet know it existed.

Vasseur had the ledger. He thought he had everything.

Which meant that tomorrow, when he came to make his offer, he would make it believing he had already won.
