## 13. Marek's Press

The press had a voice, and Marek Sol had learned to hear it the way other men heard weather.

It was an old Heidelberg, older than the war, older than his father, who had bought it secondhand from a newspaper in Vellmark that had gone under for printing the wrong truth at the right time. The frame groaned when the flywheel turned past a certain speed, a low complaint like a ship taking swell, and the platen came down with a sound that Marek had always thought resembled a door closing — firm, final, the sound of ink committing itself to paper where no one, not any colonel or historian or harbor magistrate, could take it back.

He was alone in the shop on Salt Lane when Paloma Kavanagh came to him again, three days after her first visit, and this time she did not stand in the doorway deciding whether to enter.

The fog outside had thickened into that particular Petra soup that swallowed lamplight whole; through the front window, the gas bracket over the street burned as nothing but a smudge of amber, and beyond it the harbor was a rumor of masts. Inside, the shop smelled of oil, lead, and the dried seaweed stink that got into everything in Petra no matter how high you built your shelves.

"You couldn't sleep either," he said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes had the look of a woman who had been lying on a cot listening to every sound the night made and assigning each one a meaning it didn't deserve.

"Colonel Vasseur's men walked the length of the waterfront twice between midnight and dawn," she said. "I watched them from the window of my room. They weren't looking for anything. They were being *seen* looking." She set something on the composing stone with more care than it deserved — a strip of paper, folded small. "I copied out what I could remember of Brandt's letter. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case I lose the original." She looked at the great black machine filling half the shop, at the cases of type ranged along the wall like books on a shelf in a language only he could read. "You told me you printed broadsheets during the grain riots. Before the war."

"I did."

"And they hanged two printers for it in Corvane."

"They did." Marek wiped his hands on his apron, though there wasn't a spot of ink on them yet tonight. "One of them was my brother-in-law. The other was a boy of nineteen who'd only been setting type for a year and printed what the older man told him to print. That's the part nobody remembers about martyrs — that they usually drag someone young and frightened down with them." He watched her absorb that without flinching. She was not a flinching sort of woman; he'd understood that about her within ten minutes of their first conversation, when she'd laid a sealed wax-stamped letter on his counter and asked him, flatly, whether he was a coward. "So before you say whatever you've come here to say, understand that I already know the price list."

"Then you know why I'm asking anyway."

* * *

She unrolled her plan across his composing stone the way other people unrolled contraband maps — which, in a sense, it was.

Vasseur controlled the archives. Vasseur controlled the garrison liaison office, the harbor authority's paperwork, and by now, if Ada Reyes's reports had flowed the way Paloma feared they had, a fair portion of Vasseur's own intelligence picture of Petra. Within weeks, perhaps days, the investigation into the supply fraud would be closed as inconclusive, sealed, stamped, shelved behind a door that required a minister's signature to open. Every document that mattered would be in a room Marek could never reach.

"But," Paloma said, tapping the copied strip of paper, "he cannot control what is *already printed*. Not here. This is neutral ground. Your press has a license from the harbor authority — I checked. A licensed printer publishing a sworn statement isn't sedition against either belligerent. It's news."

"It's a hanging offense dressed in a legal collar," Marek said, "and both governments will tear the collar off the moment it embarrasses them. Neutral means nothing once the ink dries. Neutrality is a promise ports make until they're threatened." He turned the copied text toward the lamp and read it again, though he knew it by heart now: the quartermaster's dying confession, the shipping numbers, the word *ballast* where the word *quinine* should have been, and beneath all of it, in Brandt's failing hand, the name that had brought Paloma across a war zone — *the Kavanaghs signed what they were made to sign.*

"You want me to publish this alone?" he said. "A dead man's accusation? Vasseur will bury it in a week. No signature, no witnesses, one sheet of paper — he'll call it a forgery, and worse, everyone will believe him, because that's what forgeries are. Convenient."

"That's why I need more than the letter." She pulled the bread tin from her satchel then — the tin, the ledger, the whole weight of her secret months — and set it beside the copied page. "I need names. Living ones. Sworn testimony. Witnesses who can stand in public and say *I carried those crates, I saw the ballast stones, I buried the men.*" Her jaw tightened. "There are survivors. I know there are. Some of the convoy guards lived. Some of the garrison orderlies. They wash up in neutral ports the way everything does. Petra is full of men the war spat out."

Marek looked at the tin, at the ledger inside it with its columns of careful, furious handwriting — the names the officers had erased — and felt the old feeling rise in him, the one that had gotten his brother-in-law hanged and had gotten himself exiled from Corvane with ink still under his fingernails. The feeling was not fear, exactly. Fear was simple; fear said run. This was the other thing, the thing that sat in a printer's chest and whispered that type existed for exactly this, that a press idle while truth starved was just so much scrap iron.

"You're describing a tribunal," he said slowly. "Except you can't hold one. You have no court, no magistrate, no legal standing anywhere on this coast."

"No."

"So we build one out of what we have." He began to pace, because his mind worked best in motion, had always worked best in motion — his father used to say Marek thought on his feet because he'd never learned to think sitting down. "Listen. A testimony has weight when three things are true of it. One: it's given freely, before witnesses who can vouch it was given freely. Two: it's recorded — written, dated, signed. Three: it's published fast enough that silencing the witness afterward doesn't silence the words." He stopped at the window, watching the fog eat the streetlamp. "We can do all three. Here. Now. In a tavern, aboard a ship, anywhere in this city where people gather. We swear them in — not with lawyers, there are no lawyers we can trust — but publicly. Before each other. Before the crowd. If a man stands up in a room full of forty witnesses and says his piece and signs it, and I print it within the day with all forty names listed beneath—"

"Then killing him buys Vasseur nothing," Paloma finished. "And proves everything."

"*Exactly.*" Marek turned back to her, and he could feel the grin pulling at his face, the reckless one, the one his wife had hated. "That's the beauty of it. We're not asking anyone's permission because there's no one whose permission matters. The law won't come with us — fine. The law has spent this whole war on the wrong side of that ledger anyway." He nodded at the tin. "We make our own law out of witnesses and ink. An improvised tribunal. Justice outside the law, because the law has resigned."

Paloma stood very still. Outside, somewhere down Salt Lane, a drunk was singing something in three languages badly, and a foghorn spoke from the outer anchorage, long and low, like the harbor clearing its throat.

"There's a problem," she said at last. "Witnesses don't line up to be shot. The moment the first man testifies, Vasseur's people will move on the rest. Intimidation, arrest on old charges, money — whatever works. I've seen how he operates. He doesn't need to kill many. He needs to frighten the next ten."

"Then we don't testify one at a time." Marek was already pulling a fresh sheet toward him, sketching as he talked, the printer's old habit — layout first, always layout first. "We gather them quietly, separately, in the taverns, over days. Nobody knows who else we've spoken to. Then we bring them together all at once, all in one place, all in one afternoon — and we print the same night. One blow. Forty testimonies, your letter, the ledger's names, all of it landing on the streets of Petra in a single edition before Vasseur wakes up. After that—" he tapped the press, and the old frame seemed almost to lean toward her, "—after that, the words belong to everyone. You can't arrest a city that's already read it."

* * *

They worked through the small hours, and Marek found, to his surprise, that he was enjoying himself — that grim, galvanized enjoyment that arrives precisely when a thing becomes dangerous enough to matter.

He drafted the form of the testimony on proof sheets: a preamble stating that the signatory spoke freely, of sound mind, knowing the risk; lines for the account itself; lines for the date, the place, the signature; and beneath, space to print the names of the assembled witnesses who had heard it given. Simple. Almost liturgical. His father had set type for a hymnal once, and Marek remembered the old man saying that the difference between a prayer and a contract was only the faith of the people reading it.

"We'll need a place," Paloma said, bent over the draft. "Neutral ground. Somewhere the harbor authority won't simply hand over to Vasseur because he outranks everyone in the room. And it should mean something. Symbol matters — you taught me that much tonight."

"A ship," Marek said.

She looked up.

"The harbor's full of them. Decommissioned hulls the port authority hasn't scrapped yet — moored off the coal quay, rotting politely. Neither navy owns them anymore. They're not territory, they're *wrecks*, and nobody fights over wrecks." He smiled thinly. "Besides. Think of the symbolism you wanted. Men who died of bad medicine on ships that sailed under false manifests — their testimony taken aboard a dead ship in a free harbor. The dead ship bearing witness for the dead."

"The *Meridian*," Paloma said quietly. "She's moored off the coal quay. I've seen her — triple-masted, stripped. My brother served on a sister hull early in the war." Something moved across her face, grief and resolve tangled together, and did not leave.

"The Meridian, then." Marek wrote it down, and the act of writing it made it real, the way type always made things real. "Now. Witnesses. Who do we have?"

"One for certain. Sergeant Hollis — convoy guard, took fever, survived it. He's been drinking himself gray at the Anchor & Chain and telling anyone who'll listen that the medicine crates were light. Half the tavern thinks he's mad. He isn't. I've spoken to him twice." She counted on her fingers, and Marek watched her turn from the woman who had crept into his shop at midnight into something harder and older — a nurse triaging a battlefield, sorting the living from the reachable. "An orderly named Fenn who worked the garrison stores. Possibly. He fled the garrison before the evacuation and I don't know where he landed. And there's a gunner from the escort battery — I'm told he can't speak. Fever took his throat or his nerve, depending on who tells it. But he *writes*. If he saw what happened to the convoy—"

"A mute man's testimony," Marek said, "in his own hand, witnessed by forty strangers. Vasseur can't buy him and can't intimidate him into recanting what he never says aloud." He shook his head slowly. "That might be the strongest voice in the whole tribunal."

"He disappeared from the lodging house where he'd been staying. Two nights ago."

Marek's pen stopped.

"Disappeared, or was taken?"

"I don't know yet." Paloma met his eyes evenly, and he gave her credit for that — she didn't soften it, didn't dress it up. "Which is why we work fast. Faster than he does. Every day we spend gathering witnesses is a day Vasseur spends erasing them. It's a race, Marek. He has marines and money and a government's patience. I have a bread tin and a printer."

"A printer with a press, a license, and a grudge," Marek corrected. "Don't undersell the inventory."

But after she left — slipping out into the fog with the tin back under her arm, refusing for the hundredth time to let the ledger out of her sight — Marek stood alone in his shop and let the grin fall away, and looked honestly at what they had decided to do.

He pulled the lever on the old Heidelberg just to hear it, one cycle with no paper in the jaws, the platen closing on empty air with its soft, decisive clap. A door shutting. Or opening. With presses it had always been hard to tell the difference, and men had died on the wrong side of that ambiguity.

Two hundred and eleven names in her ledger. One letter naming her family among the guilty. Three witnesses, maybe four, hunted by a colonel with a government behind him. Against all of it: a decommissioned hulk, a borrowed ritual of swearing-in, and a press old enough to remember the last time Corvane hanged a truth-teller.

It was madness. It was also, Marek thought, reaching for his coat and his hat and heading out into the fog toward the Anchor & Chain, where Sergeant Hollis drank, the only kind of justice that had ever actually worked — the kind nobody authorized, built overnight out of desperation and type metal, and defended afterward by the simple fact that too many people had already read it.

At the corner of Salt Lane, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step behind him. Marek didn't turn around, didn't break stride. But he noted the footsteps, and noted that whoever walked behind him walked like a soldier, soft-heeled and even, and that they turned the same way he turned, twice, toward the harbor and the taverns and the long race for witnesses that had just begun.

He hoped, with the whole of his ink-stained heart, that it was Ada Reyes.

He feared, in some quieter chamber underneath, that it was not.
