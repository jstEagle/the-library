## 14. Witnesses in Taverns

The Salt Row taverns of Petra woke late and smelled of last night's smoke, and it was there, in the gray hour when the fog still owned the streets, that Paloma began her search for the dead men's living shadows.

She had a list. Marek Sol had copied it out in his cramped, ink-stained hand the night before, pressing the paper into her palm as though handing over something more dangerous than gunpowder. *Three names. Three survivors of the Meridian convoy — the one that carried ballast crates marked as medicine.* He had gotten the names from a deserters' boarding house on Rope Lane, from a man who remembered faces better than he remembered his own mother's name.

*Corporal Ferro. Gunner Ilya Senn. Private Ansel Dore.*

Three names out of four hundred. It was not enough, and Paloma knew it was not enough, but she had spent two years learning that arithmetic — how a ledger began with one line, then another, until the lines themselves became an argument no one could ignore.

She dressed plainly, in Ada's spare coat with its frayed cuffs, and pinned her hair under a woolen cap the way fishwives did. The letter rode against her ankle inside her boot, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her own body. She touched it through the leather every time she crossed a street, a small superstitious gesture now, half prayer and half inventory: *still there, still there, still mine to carry.*

* * *

The Anchor & Chain sat at the crooked end of Salt Row where the cobblestones gave up and became mud. Its windows were fogged with breath and cheap gin, and inside, the air was thick enough to chew. Deserters from three armies drank side by side without looking at one another — a truce of exhaustion, not of friendship.

Paloma found Corporal Ferro at the corner table, exactly where the boarding-house man said he would be: a heavyset soldier with a butcher's forearms and a face like a dropped plate, drinking small beer because, he told anyone who asked, strong liquor made him remember things.

"I don't want to remember," he said when she sat down across from him uninvited. "Lady, whoever you are, I pay good money to forget."

"I'm not selling anything." She kept her voice low and level, the voice she used with dying men. "I'm a nurse. Garrison hospital at Cape Verren. Two years."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not warmth, but recognition. Every man who had passed through a field hospital wore the same look when they heard the word *nurse*, the same flinch of memory: the smell of carbolic, the screams that stopped.

"The convoy," she said. "March supply run. Crates marked quinine and laudanum that came up full of river stones and sand."

Ferro set his glass down very carefully, the way a man sets down something he might otherwise throw. "You know about that."

"I know four hundred men died waiting for medicine that was never in the crates."

"I know what I saw." His hands flattened on the table. They were trembling, she noticed; the tremor of a man who had learned to hold very still around officers. "We loaded those crates ourselves at the depot. Sealed, stenciled, waxed. Then at the river crossing we lost a wagon in the mud and cracked one open to salvage what we could." He swallowed. "Stones. Cut to size, packed neat as eggs. Somebody had taken the trouble to make them *heavy*, so the manifests would balance. You understand? Somebody thought about the weight. Somebody sat at a desk somewhere and calculated how much four hundred lives weighed in stone."

"Who signed the manifests?"

He looked at her then, fully, and what she saw in his face frightened her more than any threat could have: not fear of speaking, but the terrible fatigue of a man who had already spoken and been unanswered. "I told a captain. He wrote it down. Three weeks later the captain was transferred and my company was broken up and scattered so far none of us could find each other again." He laughed, a dry, awful sound. "That's not an answer to your question, nurse. That's me telling you I don't have one. I saw crates. I saw signatures on paper I wasn't allowed to read. If you want names, you need a man who read them."

"Gunner Senn," she said. "They say he was at the depot office."

Ferro's face closed like a door. "Senn doesn't talk."

"So I've heard."

"Not since the fever took his voice at Cape Verren. But he writes." Ferro leaned across the table, and now the tremor was gone, replaced by something hard and deliberate. "He's been writing letters for two years. To the ministry, to the war office, to anybody with a seal. Nobody answers. You know why nobody answers?" He tapped the table once, sharply. "Because a mute man's testimony is easy to lose. No voice to remember. Just paper, and paper burns."

Paloma thought of army clerks feeding field records into iron stoves. She thought of her ledger, hidden under floorboards, measured always in what could be carried.

"Where do I find him?"

* * *

Ilya Senn lived above a sailmaker's loft on Rope Lane, in a room barely wide enough for a cot and a writing desk, and the desk was the first thing Paloma understood about him. It was immaculate. Pens aligned. Blotter squared to the edge. A stack of copies — dozens of them, each page numbered in a careful, patient hand — letters written and rewritten and sent into silence.

He was younger than she expected, mid-twenties perhaps, with a long face and eyes that seemed always to be finishing a sentence. When Ferro introduced them with a jerk of his thumb — "Nurse, from Cape Verren. The hospital" — Senn went very still, then gestured: *sit.*

He wrote fast, in that same careful hand, and turned the page toward her.

*You are the one asking about the manifests.*

"Yes," Paloma said aloud, and then, remembering, added, "I can read your answers faster than most people speak them. Take whatever time—"

He waved this off impatiently and wrote:

*I waited two years for someone to come ask. Don't tell me to take my time.*

She almost smiled. There was a temper under all that patience, banked like a coal.

*What do you want to know that I haven't already written to six ministries?*

"What happened at the depot. What you saw. Not what you reported — what you *saw*. Testimony is different from correspondence. It has to be sworn, spoken before witnesses, entered into a record that can't be quietly filed away." She hesitated, then decided he deserved the whole truth. "There's going to be a tribunal. Improvised, here in Petra, aboard a ship. Not sanctioned by any government. Run by a printer named Marek Sol. Sworn statements, published in full, alongside documents naming the men who profited."

Senn read her face while she spoke, the way mute people learn to, hunting for the lie or the hesitation. He found neither, or found something better — found the thing in her that had carried a ledger of the dead under a floorboard for two years.

He wrote for a long time. The pen scratched like a small animal in the walls.

*I was clerk-assistant at the depot the spring of the convoy. My job was countersigning weights. The ballast substitution required someone inside the weighing office — the stones had to pass inspection. I noticed the discrepancy in March: inbound shipments of medical stores logged at weights that matched outbound records but never matched the actual crates. I reported it to Quartermaster Elias Brandt.*

Paloma's breath caught. "Brandt. He was the quartermaster?"

Senn looked at her strangely, then wrote:

*Brandt was the one who first showed ME the discrepancy. He came to my table at night, after hours, and put two shipping ledgers side by side and said, "Read them and tell me I'm mad." He'd suspected for months. He couldn't prove who above him was signing off the swaps, because the authorization orders came down unsigned — cipher traffic, military channel. He started keeping his own record. A private ledger of every shipment, every weight, every crate he opened.*

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Paloma gripped the edge of the desk.

*A private ledger,* she repeated silently. Like hers. The dying quartermaster had been keeping his own count of the dead all along.

*When the fever swept Cape Verren that autumn, Brandt knew he wouldn't live. He sealed everything he knew in a letter and gave it to a nurse he trusted — a woman who kept records of the men the reports left out. Do you know her?*

Paloma looked at this thin, silent man who had spent two years shouting onto deaf paper, and she reached into her boot, unwrapped the oilcloth, and laid Brandt's letter on the desk between them.

Senn went white. He did not touch it. He looked at it the way men look at graves.

Then he pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began to write — not answers now, but testimony, dated and sworn in advance by its very precision, the account of a weighing clerk who had counted stones instead of medicine while men drowned in their own fevers. He filled three pages without stopping. At the bottom he signed his name, rank, and regiment, and beneath it wrote:

*I have been a ghost's voice for two years. Put me in your record. I will appear in person if the printer will have me. A mute man on a witness stand frightens them more than a loud one — they cannot say I misspoke.*

"He'll have you," Paloma said. "I swear it."

* * *

Private Ansel Dore was harder. The boarding-house man said Dore drank at the Gull's Rest, two streets over, but Dore had grown wary since winter — moved lodging four times, drank alone, sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door. Paloma found him at midday in the Gull's Rest, sober for once, eating bread and salt herring like a man fueling a machine rather than taking a meal.

He was maybe forty, gray at the temples, with the flat watchfulness of a soldier too long behind lines. When she sat down and said the words *Cape Verren* and *the convoy*, he didn't flare or deny. He just looked at her for a long moment and said, "Who sent you?"

"A printer. Marek Sol. And a dead quartermaster, in his way."

Dore pushed his plate aside. "Sol's name is on the victors' watch lists already. You know that? Vasseur's people have been through every tavern on Salt Row twice this month asking after 'agitators.' And now a nurse walks in asking about the poison convoy." He shook his head slowly. "Either you're brave or you're bait. I've survived this long by assuming everyone is bait."

"I watched men die of what was in those crates' absence," Paloma said. "Four hundred names. I wrote every one of them down. If I were bait, Private, I'd hardly lead with the evidence."

Something in her voice — the flat grief of it, the absence of performance — decided him. Or perhaps it was only that a man can stay afraid for so long before the fear rots into recklessness. He glanced at the door, then leaned in.

"I drove the rear wagon. I was there when Ferro's wagon mired and we cracked the crate. Stones. I carried one of the stones to the divisional provost myself — walked three days with it in my pack like an idiot pilgrim, thinking *this will settle it, this rock, they can't argue with a rock.*" He laughed without humor. "The provost weighed it, wrote a receipt, and told me to return to my unit. A week later my tent was searched. The stone was gone. The receipt was gone. And a major I'd never met called me in and explained, very kindly, that men who spread fever-panic in wartime faced courts-martial, and that my widowed mother in Corvane had recently been approved for a pension she'd otherwise not qualify for. Very kind. Very clear." He turned his cup in a slow circle. "So yes, nurse. I saw the stones. I'll swear to it. I'll even stand up on your printer's ship and say it loud. But understand what you're asking us to stand against. These aren't thieves who got greedy. Thieves run. This goes up high enough that it *stays* — that's why nothing sticks, that's why every inquiry drowns. Whoever they are, they're not afraid of tribunals. They own the people who decide which tribunals happen."

"That's changing," Paloma said. "Tomorrow, aboard the *Meridian*. Whether the harbor authority likes it or not."

Dore studied her, and slowly, grudgingly, some of the flat wariness drained out of his face, leaving something almost young underneath. "The *Meridian*. The old hulk at the breakers' quay?" He snorted. "A tribunal on a condemned ship. God, it's perfect. They can't burn a record that's already public." He stood, dug coins out for his meal. "Midnight, then. I'll be there. And nurse—" he paused, hat in hand, suddenly awkward, "—those four hundred names. My brother is one of them. Sergeant Tomas Dore, Third Sappers. The official report says he fell at the river crossing 'of camp fever.'" His jaw worked. "He died of sand in a medicine crate. Write his name properly."

"It's already written," Paloma said quietly. "It's been written for two years."

* * *

She should have gone straight back to the *Meridian*. That was the plan, and plans were the only armor she had left. But Dore mentioned that Senn had moved rooms again — fear made the mute man restless — and Paloma wanted to confirm the hour, wanted to be certain the fragile machinery Marek was building wouldn't lose its strongest gear to a landlord's suspicion or a neighbor's loose tongue.

Rope Lane at dusk was all shadow and rigging-rope, gulls screaming off the fog. The sailmaker's loft was dark. She climbed the outside stair anyway, heart counting itself calm the way she'd learned to on night rounds, and knocked softly at Senn's door.

No answer.

She knocked again, and the door swung inward at her touch — unlatched. Her skin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the fog.

Inside, the room was wrong. The immaculate desk was swept clean, pens gone, blotter gone. The stack of letter-copies — two years of patient, furious correspondence — was missing entirely. A chair lay on its side. On the floor near the cot, a boot heel had scuffed a dark arc through the dust, the mark of a body dragged or carried, and beside it, small and deliberate, someone had left a single object: a smooth river stone, cut to size, packed-flat on one face.

Neat as an egg. Heavy as a lie.

Paloma stood in the doorway a long moment, listening to the fog, to her own blood. *They cannot say I misspoke,* Senn had written, so proud of the fright a silent witness would give them. They hadn't let him frighten anyone. They had simply erased him — the way they erased everything, cleanly, with paperwork and patience.

She did not touch the stone. Evidence, Marek would say; but she understood it for what it truly was — a message, addressed to her, and she refused to receive it. She backed out, latched nothing, and went down the stairs fast and quiet, pulling the woolen cap low.

By the time she reached the corner of Rope Lane she was running, boots striking wet cobbles, one thought hammering with every stride: *two witnesses left, one night, and Vasseur's men are already inside the city — tomorrow the tribunal opens whether the record is fat or starving, and everything now depends on getting Dore and Ferro aboard the Meridian before midnight.*

Behind her, the fog closed over Rope Lane like water over a sinking thing.
