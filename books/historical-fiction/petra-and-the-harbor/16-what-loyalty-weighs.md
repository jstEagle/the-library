## 16. What Loyalty Weighs

Vasseur had left the room the way tide leaves a beach — slowly, and taking something with him.

Paloma sat alone in the back parlor of the Gilded Eel, the tavern where Marek Sol had first promised her type and paper, and stared at the space on the table where the colonel's gloved hand had rested. He had set down the terms with the neatness of a man arranging cutlery: the letter, surrendered by morning; her uncle Tomas walking free within the week; Devlin's name kept out of every report, every inquiry, every future history of the war. In exchange, nothing but silence. Silence was the cheapest commodity in the world, he had said, and the most generously compensated.

*Sentiment*, he had called her ledger. A nurse's habit of counting what could not be counted, like a child tallying stars.

She pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood grain printed itself into her skin. The letter was in her boot, against her calf, where it had lived since the night the tin went missing. She could feel its edges through the leather, patient as a splinter. All she had to do was walk to the harbor authority office and hand it over. Tomas would come home. Devlin would be safe. The war would end the way wars always ended, with the victors writing the arithmetic, and she would go back to bandages and bedpans and the particular mercy of not knowing things.

It would be so easy. That was the obscenity of it. Not that Vasseur offered — men like him always offered — but that the offer was *reasonable*. That was the word her mind kept circling, a moth against glass. Reasonable. Sensible. The kind of choice a sensible woman made, and no one, not even the dead, could call her a fool for it.

Except the dead could. That was the whole point of the ledger.

She heard the door behind her and knew Devlin's tread before she saw him — heavier on the left foot since the shrapnel at Cape Verren, a hitch he'd never learned to hide. He came in carrying two glasses of the tavern's thin cider and set them down without asking whether she wanted one. He had been doing that since he'd arrived in Petra: deciding things for her, small things, as though deciding enough small things might add up to deciding the large one.

"He spoke to me too," Devlin said. "Before he found you. On the quay."

"I know. He told me."

"Then you know it's not a trick. Tomas will walk out of that prison. They've already drafted the release order — Vasseur showed me. It just needs a signature that won't come if you keep making noise."

Paloma turned the cider glass in her hands. The cider was the color of weak tea and smelled of orchards a long way from any war. "And after Tomas walks out, what happens to the men in the ledger?"

"Paloma—"

"Because that's the part Vasseur doesn't say out loud. The investigation closes. *Inconclusive.* The manifests go into a sealed archive that no one will open for fifty years, if ever. And the families — Devlin, there are families. Mothers who got a printed card saying *lost at sea* when their sons drowned in a harbor with ballast in their veins instead of medicine." Her voice stayed level. She was surprised by that. She had expected it to crack. "You want me to trade their names for Tomas's freedom."

"I want my sister to stop standing in front of a firing squad and calling it principle." Devlin dragged a chair around and sat facing her, knees almost touching hers. He had their mother's eyes, dark and warm and stubborn as wet clay. "Tomas signed those manifests under threat. He told you so himself. He's not the villain here — he's a hostage they've kept for ten years, and now they're offering the ransom and you're refusing to pay it."

"Because the ransom isn't theirs to pay." She heard how it sounded, cold and doctrinal, and hated it, and could not find softer words. "The ransom is the dead."

"The dead are dead." It came out of him before he could stop it, and she watched him flinch at his own words, watch the color climb his neck. But he didn't take it back. That was the thing about Devlin that had always frightened her a little: he would rather bleed than retract. "They're dead, Paloma, and no tribunal on any ship is going to un-dead them, and you are *alive*, and Tomas is alive, and I am asking you — not Vasseur, not the army, *I* am asking you — to keep us that way."

The parlor was very quiet. Through the floorboards she could hear the tavern below, the murmur of deserters and dockworkers and the two foreign journalists Marek had begun feeding scraps of the story, all of them unaware that upstairs the whole enterprise was balanced on the head of a pin.

Paloma looked at her brother for a long time. Then she reached down and pulled the letter from her boot.

Devlin's breath caught. She laid it on the table between them, the wax seal unbroken, Brandt's dying fingerprints still ghosted into the paper's grime. She did not hand it to him. She put one finger on top of it, the way a customs officer puts a hand on a crate.

"You think I don't understand what I'm refusing," she said. "You think I'm drunk on righteousness. So I'm going to show you what it actually is. Not the letter — you've heard about the letter. The ledger."

"Paloma, no—"

"You'll stand there and listen, or you'll leave, but you'll do one or the other." She was already moving, lifting the loose plank behind the parlor's cold stove where Marek had taught her to hide things — he had hidden pamphlets there during the last border war, he'd said, and a woman, though he'd never elaborated — and drawing out the ledger wrapped in oilcloth. It was heavier than a book had any right to be. She set it beside the letter and untied the cord with fingers that did not shake, though she felt the shaking in her chest, behind the sternum, like a second heartbeat.

She opened it to the first page and began to read.

"Okafor, Emeka. Private. Garrison hospital, bed nine. Died of the flux on the fourteenth of March. Official report: deserted at Cape Verren." She looked up. "He was nineteen. His mother sent him socks. I have the socks."

Devlin said nothing.

"Brandt, Elias. Quartermaster. Died of fever on the third of June. Official report: killed in action." Her voice found a rhythm now, the cadence of the ward at night, when she had walked the rows with a lamp and spoken each name to herself so the dark couldn't have them whole. "He was the one who kept the real numbers. He was the one who couldn't live with them."

"Paloma." Devlin's voice had gone rough. "You don't have to—"

"Halloran, Seamus. Gunner's mate. Died of the flux, second of April. Official report: lost at sea." She turned the page. The ink had faded unevenly; some entries were in her careful nurse's hand, some scrawled by candlelight when the garrison was shelled and she'd written lying flat on the floor. "I was there when he died. I held the bowl while his body purged itself. There was no medicine to give him because the medicine had been poured out and replaced with seawater, and the men who poured it out signed their names in ledgers just like this one, neat as scripture, and every one of those signatures has a family that still sits down to supper."

She kept reading. Name after name. She did not perform them; she did not weep or let her voice tremble for effect. That was the terrible discipline of it — she read them the way she'd recorded them, plainly, each one given its full weight and no more, and the plainness was what did the work. The room filled with the dead the way a ward fills at night, not all at once but bed by bed, until the living felt like the visitors.

Devlin stood with his hands braced on the chair back. Somewhere around the fortieth name his knuckles went white. Somewhere around the sixtieth he sat down without seeming to decide to. By the time she reached the entries from the last convoy — the poisoned-supply convoy, the one that had started everything, the one whose survivors were even now gathering in Petra's taverns because Marek had sent word through the dockside grapevine — he had his head in his hands.

"Okafor, Emeka," she said again, because she had begun at the beginning and she would end at the end. "Private. Nineteen. Socks."

She closed the ledger.

The silence afterward had texture, like fog. Devlin didn't move for a long moment. When he finally lifted his head, his eyes were wet and he made no effort to hide it, and something in Paloma's chest unclenched for the first time since Vasseur had walked out of the room.

"You knew some of them," he said hoarsely.

"I nursed eleven of them. I held nine of their hands at the end." She sat down across from him. "I'm not drunk on righteousness, Dev. I'm an accountant. Someone kept books on the ballast and the seawater and the money, and someone has to keep books on the other column, the one the officers tore out. That's all the ledger is. The other column."

"And if you give Vasseur the letter — if you take his bargain — Tomas lives and those men stay *lost at sea* forever. Their mothers die believing their sons were deserters." He said it slowly, testing each word, and she watched him arrive at it the way a man arrives at a coastline in fog, not because he wants to but because the ground runs out. "That's the ransom."

"That's the ransom."

Devlin looked at the letter on the table for a long time. Then he pushed it across the wood, back toward her, and stood.

"I came here to make you burn it," he said. "I'm not going to pretend I've stopped being afraid. I'm afraid right now. I'm going to be afraid tomorrow and the day after, when Vasseur realizes his offer expired." He rubbed his face with both hands. "But I'm not going to ask you again. And when the tribunal happens — Marek's ship, the Meridian, whenever it is — I'll be there. In the crowd. Where you can see me."

"Dev—"

"Don't." He almost smiled. It was their mother's smile, the one that meant *I have said the true thing and now I am embarrassed by it.* "Let me have this. I've been wrong for a week and I'd like to be right about one thing." He put on his coat, and at the door he paused, his hand on the frame. "The socks. Did they fit him?"

"They were too big," Paloma said. "He wore them anyway."

Devlin nodded once, and was gone into the fog.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the parlor with the ledger open on her knees and the letter in her lap and the lamp turned low, and she read the names again, not aloud this time, only moving her lips, the way she'd done in the ward. Somewhere past two in the morning she heard the tavern empty, heard Marek's tread on the stairs and his knock, soft, and she told him through the door that she was fine, and after a moment his footsteps went away, and she was alone with the dead again, and it was not a bad way to pass the small hours. It was, in fact, the only honest company left to her.

The fog came into the streets of Petra before dawn, as it always did, rolling off the harbor in slow grey banks until the lamps burned as halos and the masts of the ships were suggestions rather than facts. Paloma heard the footsteps on the stair at half past three and knew they were wrong — too quick, too uneven, the tread of someone running who was trying not to.

She was on her feet with the lamp before the knock came, and when she opened the door Ada Reyes nearly fell into the room.

Paloma's first thought, absurdly, was that her friend had been wounded — Ada was grey-faced, her coat torn at the shoulder, a smear of soot or ash along one cheekbone, and she was breathing in the shallow gulps of someone who has run a long way on a short fuse. Her second thought, arriving a half-second later and infinitely colder, was that Ada had no business being free at all. Vasseur owned her. Vasseur had owned her since the checkpoint, since her sister, since whatever bargain had been struck in whatever room Paloma would never see. If Ada was here, it was because Vasseur had sent her.

"Before you bar the door," Ada gasped, "look at what I'm holding."

She was holding a bread tin.

It was dented now — dented in a way Paloma remembered from a checkpoint on the road to Petra, a soldier's rifle butt, a moment when the whole world had balanced on a tin of bread — and the seal of wax Marek had pressed around the lid was cracked, and there was a dark stain along one corner that Paloma did not let herself examine too closely. But it was the tin. It was the ledger.

"You—" Paloma's voice failed and started again. "Vasseur's office is in the harbor authority. It's guarded. It's—"

"Guarded by four men, two of whom drink, one of whom owes me money, and one of whom is asleep at his post because I put laudanum in his supper." Ada leaned against the doorframe and laughed, a small broken sound with no humor in it. "I've been telling myself for three days that I'd do it. Since the night of the raid, when I stood in that room and let them take it and said nothing. I told myself I'd go back for it, and every day I found a reason, and today Vasseur gave me the reason himself. He told me to fetch the tin because he wants to burn it tomorrow, before the tribunal can convene. He said—" she stopped, steadied herself against the frame, "—he said the record should be *inconclusive*, and then he smiled at me, Paloma. He smiled like we were colleagues."

She pushed off from the doorframe and crossed the room on unsteady legs and set the tin on the table beside the letter, beside the ledger's oilcloth, and stood looking down at it as though she couldn't believe her own hands.

"My sister walks out of that prison in three days whether I help him or not," Ada said quietly. "That was the bargain, and he'll honor it — men like him honor the bargains that cost them nothing. But I sat in that office tonight and I looked at the tin and I thought about what's inside it. You never told me. You never read it to me. I had to guess, all this way, all this time, and I guessed it was just names." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and wrecked and more honest than Paloma had ever seen them. "It's not just names, is it. It's the other column."

"It's the other column," Paloma said.

Ada nodded slowly, and then, because she was Ada, because she had carried stretchers through shellfire and jokes through the worst winter of the war, she said: "Then we'd better get it to Marek's press before dawn, because I left the laudanum man's relief schedule in my coat pocket at the office, and when they find it they'll know exactly who walked out with their evidence."

Paloma was already wrapping the ledger in its oilcloth. Her hands were steady now, steadier than they had been in weeks. Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor in long grey ribbons, and somewhere out on the water the decommissioned hulk of the Meridian rode at anchor, waiting for morning, waiting for witnesses, waiting for whatever the day would bring.

Ada picked up the tin. Paloma took the letter from the table and put it back in her boot.

"Tell me on the way," Paloma said, "how bad it's going to be."

Ada managed something that was almost a grin. "Bad," she said, and opened the door onto the fog.
