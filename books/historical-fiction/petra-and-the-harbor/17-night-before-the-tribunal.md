## 17. Night Before the Tribunal

Ada Reyes had spent six years learning to read a ship by its sounds, and the Meridian spoke a language of surrender. Her hull groaned not with purpose but with age, timbers settling against each other like old men rearranging themselves in their sleep. The hulk had been stripped of everything that made her dangerous — her guns long since pulled for harbor defense, her rigging cut down to two bare masts, her hold converted into a floating warehouse for condemned rope and rusted chain. She sat at anchor in the outer roadstead where Petra's harbor authority moored the ships nobody wanted to look at, and it was exactly this — the official indifference of the thing — that Marek Sol had counted on.

"She's listed on the rolls as storage," he'd said when he first proposed it. "Nobody inspects storage."

Now Ada climbed the accommodation ladder in the dark with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light and felt every rung announce her weight. Below deck, in what had once been the wardroom, lamplight leaked yellow through the door seams. She could hear voices inside, low ones, the careful murmur of people who had learned all over again how to whisper.

She paused at the top of the ladder and let herself do the thing she'd been doing for three days now: she took an inventory.

The gunner, Oskar Vell, mute since the convoy, who could write his testimony in a hand so precise it looked engraved. He was aboard already; she could hear his boots on the deck above, pacing his watch. The two convoy survivors, Halloran and the boy Piet, smuggled out of the Sailors' Rest before dawn by a fishwife who owed Marek money. Paloma, below right now, probably reading the ledger one more time, committing names to memory as though paper might be taken from her — as though it hadn't already been taken from her once. And Marek, setting type by lamplight because there wasn't time to print before morning, only to prepare, so that whatever happened aboard this ship could be set down in metal within hours of being spoken.

And herself. Ada Reyes, corporal, deserter by any honest accounting, traitor by the more complicated one. The woman who had carried a bread tin full of dead men's names through a checkpoint and then told Colonel Vasseur where to find it. Who had stolen it back from his office at a risk that still made her hands unsteady if she let herself think about the corridor, the sentry's back turned, the ten seconds when the whole world balanced on whether a clerk would look up from his ledger.

Her sister was free. That was the arithmetic. Lila had walked out of the holding barracks on Tuesday with nothing but a bruise on her cheekbone and a sentence suspended, and every night since, Ada lay awake doing the same sum: Lila's freedom weighed against a bread tin, a letter sealed in wax, three hundred and eleven names. The sum never came out even. It never came out anywhere. It just kept turning over like a coin that refused to land.

She went below.

* * *

The wardroom smelled of lamp oil, cold tea, and old varnish. Someone had nailed boards across the table to make a surface wide enough for type cases, and Marek Sol stood over them now, sleeves rolled to the elbow, moving among the little compartments of lead with the tenderness of a man handling seeds.

"Careful," he said without looking up, which was how he greeted everyone. "You're breathing on my e's."

"You have enough e's?"

"I have enough of nothing. I'm short on t's, I'm short on asterisks, and I am catastrophically short on capital K." He finally glanced up, ink-stained to the second knuckle. "So we'll spell your friend's name in lowercase. She can be humble for posterity."

Paloma sat at the far end of the room with the oilcloth bundle open in front of her, but she wasn't looking at the ledger. She was looking at the wall, or through it, toward the water. When Ada came in she straightened slightly — the way soldiers did, Ada thought, when someone they trusted entered a room, and the way civilians didn't understand.

"How is it out there?" Paloma asked.

"Quiet. One patrol boat on the harbor circuit, same as every night. If they meant to come tonight they'd have come loud." Ada hung her coat on a nail. "They'll come at dawn instead. With paper."

Marek snorted. "Vasseur doesn't strike me as a man who fights with paper."

"He fights with whoever owns the paper." Ada pulled a chair around backward and sat astride it. "I worked in his headquarters long enough to know. He filed a petition with the harbor authority this afternoon — Marek's boy saw the courier go in. He's asking them to declare the Meridian an unlawful assembly and clear it at first light. His argument is navigation. He says a crowd aboard a moored hulk obstructs the channel."

"A crowd," Paloma repeated softly. "Six witnesses and a printer."

"To him you're a mob. Mobs are easier to disperse than testimonies." Ada turned the chair so its back faced the porthole, blocking the grey line of water. "Here's what matters. The harbor authority meets at seven. The tribunal starts at eight. We have one hour where we exist legally, and after that we exist only as fast as word travels."

"And the harbor master?" Marek asked.

"Herr Dunmore. Fat, cautious, and terrified of both armies equally, which makes him neutral in the way a fence post is neutral." Ada allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "He also owes his dock lease to a shipping concern that Marek has been printing bills of lading for since the war started, none of which were entirely accurate."

"I contain multitudes," Marek said, returning to his type.

* * *

They came aboard one at a time through the last hours of dark, ferried by the fishwife's son in a punt too small to draw attention. Ada stood watch at the rail between arrivals, counting heads the way she'd once counted rifles, and each face that came up out of the fog tightened something in her chest that she couldn't afford to examine yet.

Halloran arrived first, a big man gone soft around the middle, who shook the ladder so hard coming up that Oskar Vell had to reach down and steady him. Halloran had been a teamster on the supply convoy — the one that rolled out of Brandt's depot with crates marked as medicine and weighted instead with river stones and sand. He'd survived because he'd been riding the rear wagon, half a mile back, when the front of the column reached the garrison hospital and the wounded began drinking what was supposed to save them.

"I don't talk good in front of people," he said to Ada, gripping the rail. "That's why I came early. To practice standing somewhere."

"There'll be a rail right there," Ada said. "Hold it like this one."

The boy Piet came next, seventeen maybe, with a burn scar down one side of his neck like a melted candle. He wouldn't go below until he'd checked the punt twice with his eyes, watching the fog behind them. Then Oskar Vell took him gently by the sleeve and led him down, the gunner's silence so complete that the boy seemed to find it restful.

By four o'clock they were all aboard, and Marek called a kind of order to the wardroom, and Ada watched Paloma stand up in the lamplight and do the thing she had apparently done in hospitals for years: make frightened people feel organized.

"Tomorrow we speak in this sequence," Paloma said. "Halloran, because you saw the wagons loaded and you can describe the crates. Piet, because you drank from the canteens and lived, and a living body is evidence no clerk can file away. Then Oskar's testimony will be read aloud — he wrote it, it's sworn, and it names faces. Then I read the ledger. Not explain it. Read it. Names, units, dates of death, and the words 'omitted from official record' after each one. Three hundred and eleven times."

Halloran shifted. "Three hundred and eleven. That'll take till noon."

"It'll take as long as it takes," Paloma said, and there was something in her voice that closed the subject like a door.

Ada had heard that voice before, in the hospital tents, when a doctor tried to move a dying man out of the priority queue and Paloma Kavanagh informed him, pleasantly, that he would not. She had loved that voice for years. She had betrayed that voice for her sister. Both facts sat in her chest tonight like swallowed stones, and she had stopped trying to decide which one was heavier, because they changed places depending on the hour.

* * *

Near dawn, with the others sleeping in hammocks slung along the old officers' berths, Ada found Marek alone at his type case, working by a single shaded lamp.

"You should sleep," he said, still setting letters.

"So should you."

"I'll sleep when the forms are locked." He blew lead dust off a finished line and held it up to the light, squinting at it reversed. "Read that."

*THE UNDERSIGNED TESTIFY THAT THE SUPPLY CONVOY DEPARTING BRANDT'S DEPOT ON THE 14TH OF MARCH CARRIED NO MEDICINE.*

"It's beautiful," Ada said.

"It's backwards. Everything worth printing is." He slotted the line into a galley with the delicacy of a man laying an infant in a cradle. "You know what I've printed for twenty years? Prices. Grain prices, freight prices, the price of a barrel of tar. Nobody ever risked anything to read them. Tomorrow I print the first thing in my life that somebody might burn the shop down over." He looked up at her, and his eyes were tired and bright at once. "Worth it. You want to know the strange part? I'm not afraid of Vasseur. I'm afraid of typos."

Ada laughed — a short, surprised sound — and then found she couldn't stop it from turning into something else, something closer to the truth, and she pressed her hand over her mouth until it passed.

Marek watched her with printer's patience. "You going to tell me what that was?" he asked when she lowered her hand.

"No."

"All right. But I'll say this, and then I'll mind my own business, which will be a novelty for both of us." He wiped his hands slowly on his apron. "Whatever you did, and whatever it cost — tomorrow you're going to stand on this deck while those names get read out, and you're going to hear whether the sound of them balances anything. Sometimes it does. Usually it doesn't. But it's the only scale we've got."

Ada looked at the galley of locked type, at the little reversed world of it, all those letters waiting to be thrown forward into sense. "What are *you* willing to lose?" she asked. "For this."

"My press. My lease. Possibly my teeth." He shrugged. "I'm fifty-eight. I've been renting my whole life. This is the first thing I've ever owned." He nodded toward the wardroom, where Paloma slept. "She asked me that question two nights ago. What are you willing to lose. You know what she answered, when I turned it back on her? She said, *everything except the names.*" He shook his head slowly. "I've been thinking about that ever since. It's a good answer. It's the answer of somebody who's already lost the thing she was most afraid of losing, and found out she could keep walking anyway."

* * *

At five o'clock Ada went up on deck, where Oskar Vell stood his watch at the rail, and together they looked east, where the fog was thinning over the water in the first colorless suggestion of light.

Out past the breakwater, the harbor town was waking. Smoke rose from bakery chimneys. Somewhere a bell rang the half hour. And down at the harbor authority offices, in a room with a good view of the channel, Colonel Vasseur was — Ada had no doubt — dressing carefully, gathering his papers, preparing to arrive at seven o'clock with the law folded neatly under his arm.

"They'll come with marines," she said to Oskar, though he couldn't answer. "Not many. Twelve, maybe. Enough to look inevitable."

Oskar took out his notebook — he carried it everywhere, Ada had noticed, the way other men carried tobacco — and wrote for a moment, then turned it toward her lantern.

*I COUNTED SHIPS MY WHOLE SERVICE. YOU LEARN TO TELL WHICH ONES WILL SINK AND WHICH ONES ONLY LEAN.*

"That's either very comforting or very ominous," Ada said.

He wrote again: *BOTH.*

Below them, faintly, they could hear Halloran practicing his testimony in his sleep, murmuring crate counts to no one. The Meridian leaned gently on the tide, patient as a gravestone, and the fog lifted off the water in long grey ribbons, and Ada stood her watch and thought about scales, about coins that would not land, about everything she was willing to lose and the one thing she'd already lost twice.

Then she saw it: a launch putting out from the military quay, running lights burning against regulations, cutting across the dark water toward the harbor authority pier. Even at this distance she knew the silhouette of the man standing in the bow with his hands clasped behind him, watching the hulk at anchor the way a man watches a debt.

Dawn was an hour away. The tribunal began in three.

"Oskar," Ada said quietly, taking one last look at the launch, "wake them all. It's starting early."
