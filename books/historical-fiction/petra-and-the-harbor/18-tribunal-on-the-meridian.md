## 18. Tribunal on the Meridian

Dawn came to Petra the way it always did — not as light but as a slow thinning of fog, the harbor emerging in pieces: first the masts, then the cranes, then the grey faces of the warehouses along the quay. Paloma stood at the rail of the *Meridian* and watched the water turn from ink to pewter and thought of all the mornings she had stood watch like this over men who would not see another one.

The decommissioned frigate sat low in the water, her guns long since struck out of the naval register, her decks given over to salt rot and gull droppings and now, improbably, to justice. Marek Sol had chosen her because she was neutral ground in the only way that mattered — registered under no flag that Vasseur could invoke, moored beyond the customs line where the harbor authority's writ went soft at the edges. A ship without a country. It seemed fitting. So many of the names in her ledger had died for countries that would not write them down.

Below her, on the quarterdeck, Marek was directing a small army of volunteers. A tarpaulin stretched between two stanchions made a roof against the mist. Crates turned on end became a table for the witnesses. Someone had found an actual chair — a high-backed thing salvaged from a hotel being stripped for scrap — and set it at the head of the arrangement for the man who would preside: old Captain Ferrant, retired thirty years, deaf in one ear, famous in Petra for having once refused a bribe so large that the story had become a children's rhyme.

Paloma touched the pocket where Brandt's letter rode against her ribs. She had not slept. She had not wanted to. Sleep felt like a door left unguarded.

Ada appeared beside her with two tin cups of chicory and handed one over without a word. They drank together, watching the docks.

"They're coming already," Ada said.

They were. Even at this hour, even through the fog, the quay was gathering people. Fishermen who should have been hauling nets stood instead in knots near the bollards, their faces turned toward the ship. Women with market baskets paused on their way to the fish stalls. Two boys had climbed onto a warehouse roof for the view. Word had moved through Petra overnight the way word did there — by rumor, by chalk marks on doors, by printers' apprentices slipping handbills under shutters — and the city, which had swallowed every kind of fugitive for three years and asked nothing of any of them, had decided to come and listen.

"Vasseur will have seen them too," Ada said quietly.

"I know."

"You're not afraid."

It was not a question, so Paloma did not answer it. She was afraid. Her hands were steady only because she was holding the cup. But fear, she had learned in the wards, was just the body's way of marking what mattered. She had felt it before every amputation, every letter home to a mother whose son had lasted four hours past the writing of it. Fear meant you still believed something could be lost. This morning, for once, she let herself believe the opposite: that something could be won, and that it would be harder than anyone in power had imagined to take it back.

* * *

The tribunal began when Captain Ferrant mounted the quarterdeck at eight o'clock, rapped his walking stick twice on a crate, and announced in a voice that carried across the water better than any bell: "This is not a court. Courts belong to governments, and governments are parties to this war. This is a record. We are here to make one."

There was a bench for him to sit on; he ignored it and stayed standing, which somehow settled everything.

Marek had drawn up the order of testimony in the small hours, and he read it now from a card: the gunner first, because silence spoken early gives courage to the voices that follow.

Silas Vann came forward slowly, favoring the leg the ballast crates had crushed when the convoy's hold collapsed around him. He was a big man gone gaunt, with a gunner's forearms and a face burned brown by weather that had nothing to do with his service anymore. He could not speak — the fever that followed the crush had taken his voice and never given it back — but he had spent three nights with one of Marek's compositors teaching himself to write quickly enough to be read aloud, and now he climbed to the witness crate, unfolded a sheet of paper covered in a laborer's careful block letters, and held it up so the crowd on the dock could see it too.

Ferrant read it in his carrying voice, line by line.

*"I served eleven years as a gunner on supply convoys out of Harrow Point. In the spring of the last year of the war I loaded crates marked as surgical stores, quinine, and morphine into the holds of six ships. The crates were the correct weight. They were not the correct contents. I know because one broke."*

He stopped reading. He looked out at the water, and the whole harbor waited with him.

*"Inside were river stones, packed in straw. Every crate on every ship. I helped load stones while men in the hospitals at Harrow Point were dying of fevers we were told the medicine was coming to treat. When I reported it, I was flogged for theft and discharged. The stones were my evidence, and they took the stones, and then they took my voice, though I tell you honestly I do not think the two things were connected except by God's habit of letting wicked men finish their work."*

A sound went up from the docks — not a cheer, nothing so clean. A low murmur, hundreds of voices at once, like the sea deciding something.

Then Silas Vann raised his hand, and Ferrant nodded permission, and the mute man pointed one thick finger at Paloma Kavanagh, standing at the rail, and held up a second sheet of paper he had kept folded inside his coat until this moment.

Ferrant read: *"Ask the nurse. She counted the dead. Ask her how many."*

* * *

She had promised Marek she would not do it this way. The ledger was to be entered formally, page by page, after the survivors spoke — procedure, even improvised procedure, had its comforts. But Silas Vann was looking at her from the witness crate with the patience of a man who had waited a year to say one sentence, and the crowd on the dock was looking at her, and Captain Ferrant, deaf or not, missed nothing, and said mildly:

"The tribunal will hear Nurse Kavanagh whenever she chooses. Perhaps sooner rather than later."

So she came forward. She had dressed in her nurse's uniform, the grey one with the faded red cross at the collar, because it was the truest thing she owned. She carried the ledger in both hands, wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the crate-table before the whole visible harbor, and heard the murmur change key.

"This book," she said, and her voice surprised her by holding, "contains two hundred and sixty-one names."

She opened it to the first page.

"Official reports from the Harrow Point garrison hospital, from March of last year to the final evacuation, record one hundred and twelve deaths. I kept count of the rest. Not from ambition. From arithmetic. Men died in my ward whose families received letters saying they had deserted, or fallen ill on leave, or simply — vanished from the rolls, as if the sea had taken them without ever taking their names."

She lifted the book.

"I am going to read some of these names aloud. Not all of them — there isn't time, and the full pages go to print tonight. But I will read enough that no one on this dock can say afterward they didn't hear it. These men existed. They served. They died of wounds left untreated and fevers left untreated, because the supplies that were bought and paid for and signed for —" here she drew Brandt's letter from her pocket and laid it on the table beside the ledger, the wax seal catching the strengthening light — "were replaced, somewhere between the treasury and the hold, with stones."

She began to read.

"Tomas Okafor-Reyes—" no, that name was not hers to speak yet; she steadied herself. "Private Anselm Duarte, Fourth Coastal Rifles. Died of gangrene, June the ninth. Age nineteen. His report says: *deserted.*

"Gunner Petros Halim, Harbor Defense. Died of fever, June the twenty-third. His report says: *struck from rolls, cause unstated.*

"Sergeant Milo Ferenc, who carried three men out of the surf at Blackrock and got a medal for it and a fever from the same water. His report says: *nothing.*"

She read ten names, then fifteen. Somewhere past twenty, the dock had gone entirely silent — fishermen with their caps off without realizing they'd removed them, the market women standing among their baskets, the two boys on the warehouse roof leaning so far out they had to be holding each other. She read names from every regiment, every port town, names that belonged to faces she had washed and bandaged and held while they finished. Her voice cracked on the name of a boy called Wren who had recited poetry to her through his last night, and she did not apologize for the crack, because the crack was part of the testimony too.

When she reached the last name she had planned to read aloud — Corporal Edmun Shaw, *drowned during transport, body unrecovered, listed officially as absent without leave* — Captain Ferrant rose, took the letter from the table, and held it high.

"Quartermaster Elias Brandt's confession," he declared, "sealed in his own wax, dictated in his own blood-fever, naming the chain of signatures that converted medicine to ballast and ballast to private profit. It is entered into the record of this tribunal, witnessed by every soul within the sound of my voice — which, thank God for this harbor's acoustics, appears to be several thousand."

He set the letter down and looked slowly across the water.

"Let the historians of the victors note the date and hour. Let them explain, if they can, why a record kept by a nurse in defiance of her orders contains more truth than their entire sealed archive."

The sound that rose from the docks then was not a murmur. It was a roar, rolling off the warehouses and back again, and Paloma stood in the middle of it gripping the edge of the crate-table and thought: *they cannot unhear this. Whatever comes next, they cannot make a harbor forget what it heard with its own ears.*

* * *

After the gunner came the others.

Tomasin Grell, a convoy driver with a ruined shoulder, testified to the loading schedules and the night wagons that ran between the treasury annex and the freight yard, always empty going out and heavy coming back. A widow named Corla Menn spoke for eleven minutes about the letter she had received telling her husband had deserted, and how she had believed it for a year, and how she had hated him for it, and how she would spend whatever remained of her life hating herself for that hatred — and the dock was so quiet you could hear the tide working against the pilings beneath her words.

And then, near noon, a commotion at the gangway, and Devlin came aboard.

Paloma's heart dropped before her mind caught up — she saw his face and thought *arrest, Vasseur, marines* — but Devlin was alone, hatless, breathing hard from running, and he pushed through to the witness area and stood there gripping his cap and staring at her.

"I wired the release papers myself," he said, loud enough for the front rows to hear. "Uncle Tomas walks free tomorrow. And I've spent three days telling you to burn that letter, and I'm done telling you." He turned to face the crowd — his own crowd now, his own town's people — and his voice shook and did not stop. "My family's name is on that letter. Signed under threat, extorted, used — but on it. And I came here to ask you to hear me anyway. Because if a Kavanagh can stand on this deck and say *it happened, it happened exactly as she wrote it,* then maybe nobody else gets to hide behind their name either."

He looked at his sister.

"Read the next name, Paloma."

She could not speak for a moment. Then she opened the ledger, and read the next name, and the next, and her brother stood beside her like a wall stands beside a house.

* * *

It was past two o'clock when Ada Reyes, posted at the seaward rail as lookout, went rigid, and Paloma followed her gaze across the water.

Two longboats were pulling away from the naval anchorage beyond the breakwater. In the bow of the nearest one, unmistakable even at distance by the way he sat — straight-backed, unhurried, hands folded on his knee as though the boat were his office — sat Colonel Vasseur. Behind the boats, at the breakwater itself, the grey shapes of marines were forming up on the mole.

Ada's hand found Paloma's arm. "How many can he bring ashore?"

"Not enough," Paloma said, and discovered she meant it. "Not nearly enough. Look at the docks."

The crowd had not thinned since morning. It had grown. And it had heard everything.

Captain Ferrant rapped his stick twice on the crate, and the tribunal of the *Meridian* prepared to receive its first visitor.
