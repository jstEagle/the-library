## 21. What Can Be Carried

The knock came at the hour when Petra's fog lay thickest against the windows, when the harbor lamps were nothing but blurred coins of gold and the whole city seemed to hold its breath between one day and the next. Paloma was awake. She had been awake most nights since the tribunal, sleeping in snatches, her body still tuned to the garrison habit of listening for footsteps that meant trouble.

These footsteps stopped at her door.

"Marek," she said through the wood, though she already knew it wasn't him. Marek Sol walked like a man who had spent twenty years hunched over a composing stone—soft-footed, apologetic even in his tread. These steps were measured, deliberate, the walk of a soldier who wanted to be heard.

"Fraulein Kavanagh." Colonel Vasseur's voice was quiet, almost courteous. "I'd ask you to open the door. I have a warrant."

She stood very still in the narrow room above the print shop, her hand resting on the table where she'd left her boots by the bed. In the left boot, folded small enough to disappear, Brandt's letter had ridden for weeks. It was not there now. It lay in Marek's archive, pressed flat between sheets of acid-free paper, catalogued under a number that meant nothing to anyone but him. The letter was beyond her reach, which meant it was beyond Vasseur's.

That thought steadied her.

"The warrant is for what?" she asked.

"Desertion from a military hospital corps. Theft of official documents. Aiding the dissemination of material harmful to the war effort." He recited them the way a clerk reads a grocery list. "The charges are old. Some of them are older than the armistice. But charges are charges, and I find that old ones serve as well as new."

"You mean they're fabricated."

"I mean," Vasseur said, and she could hear the smile in it, thin and bloodless, "that history has been written. The broadsheet printed your names, your ledger, your testimony—I read every word of it, Fraulein, over breakfast, with great interest. And now the record stands, exactly as you wished. So there is no further need of you. You've become an appendix. And appendices," he said, "can be removed without altering the text."

Paloma crossed the room silently and looked out through the gap in the shutter. Below, in the fog-drowned street, two marines stood beneath the lamp bracket, their breath steaming, their rifles held with parade-ground patience. Vasseur himself was a shadow at her door, his cap dark with moisture, his face invisible.

"Your uncle will be released this week," he continued. "Your brother's commission will be restored with full honors. Those things are settled; even I cannot unpromise them now. You have my word on that, and whatever you think of me, my word on paper is worth more than my word in person, so you may have it in writing if you like." A pause. "But you will come with me. Quietly, before the city wakes. There is a transport leaving for the capital, and there is a cell with your name on it, and in six months there will be a trial so brief that no journalist will bother to attend, because by then no one will remember why any of this mattered."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then these two gentlemen come up, and we do it less quietly, and the outcome is identical except for the state of your furniture."

Paloma leaned her forehead against the cold shutter slat. Through it she could smell the harbor: brine, tar, fish blood, coal smoke—the smell of a city that lived by what arrived on its tide and left on it too. Somewhere out in that gray blindness, boats were moving. Fishing boats went out with the morning tide. That was the rhythm of Petra, older than any war, indifferent to any colonel.

"Give me five minutes," she said. "To dress."

"Three," said Vasseur. "And I'll be counting."

* * *

She used none of them to pack.

There was almost nothing to pack. She had learned months ago, on the night the evacuation orders came, to measure her life in what could be carried—and what could be carried had shrunk, over the years of war, to nearly nothing. A spare shift. A hairbrush with three teeth missing. The little tin cup from the hospital ward, dented, that had measured out water for dying men whose names she alone remembered. She looked at it sitting on the sill and found, to her surprise, that she could leave it. Its work was done. Everything's work, soon, would be done.

What mattered was downstairs, in the cellar behind the press.

She opened her door. Vasseur stood back, courteous as a dance partner, the marines flanking the stairwell below. She walked past them all with her chin level and her hands loose at her sides, and if her heart was hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, none of it showed in her face. Nursing teaches you that. You learn to be calm in the presence of death—not because you feel calm, but because the person bleeding needs your hands steady more than they need your fear.

At the bottom of the stairs, instead of turning toward the street door, she turned toward the cellar.

"The archive is down there," she said. "You'll want to seal it. That's procedure, isn't it?"

Vasseur hesitated only a heartbeat. Curiosity was the crack in him; she'd seen it at their private meeting, the scholar's appetite under the officer's armor. He followed her down into the damp and lamplit dark, and there was Marek Sol, standing among his shelves with a candle in his hand, as though he'd been waiting for them all night—which, Paloma understood in that instant, he had.

"Colonel," Marek said pleasantly. "You're up early."

"Printers keep irregular hours. I should have guessed." Vasseur's eyes moved along the shelves—ledgers, bundles, boxes labeled in Marek's careful hand. Somewhere among them, flat and anonymous, lay the Kavanagh papers. "This constitutes evidence in an ongoing matter. It will need to be confiscated."

"It constitutes a private archive," Marek said, "assembled in a neutral port, under neutral law, by a licensed printer, and registered yesterday with the harbor authority as a commercial holding. Here is the registration." He produced a paper from his waistcoat with the unhurried grace of a magician producing a dove. "You are welcome to apply to the harbor magistrate for an order of seizure. He is, I believe, available after ten o'clock. Though I should mention—he subscribed to the broadsheet. Bought six copies. Sent one to his mother."

For a long moment nobody moved. The candle flame leaned in some draft from the sea, and shadows swung across the shelves like slow birds.

"You have arranged everything," Vasseur said at last, and there was something almost admiring in it, and something very tired. "The tribunal. The journalists. Now this." He turned to Paloma. "Do you know what I was going to do, Fraulein? Before all this? Years ago, at the checkpoint on the Petra road—you never knew this—I had orders to detain every traveler carrying documents. I searched forty carts that day. Forty." His gaze rested on her, unreadable. "I missed a bread tin. I have wondered, since, whether missing it was carelessness or instinct. Whether some part of me already knew how it would end, and chose not to see it."

"You let us through," Paloma said slowly.

"I let a column of refugees through a checkpoint, because the alternative was a massacre on the road and a report I didn't want to write. What happened afterward happened afterward." He straightened his cap. "I am not a sentimental man. Your ledger—that was sentiment. Names, names, names, as though a name were a person. But I will grant you this much, nurse: sentiment won. This once, against me, with the odds it had—it won. I'd advise you not to expect it to win again. History is patient, and so am I."

He turned and climbed the stairs. At the top he paused.

"The warrant stands," he said. "It doesn't expire. Men like me don't expire either—we only rotate. One day a fishing boat won't be fast enough, or a border won't be close enough, and I'll be there, older, in a different coat, with the same piece of paper." The floorboards creaked as he shifted. "Until then. Give my regards to the tide."

His boots crossed the ceiling of the cellar, and the street door closed, and Paloma discovered she had been holding her breath long enough that her lungs burned.

* * *

Ada Reyes came at first light, moving fast through the alley behind the print shop, her collar up, her eyes doing the soldier's sweep of rooftops and corners that never quite leaves a person who has done sentry duty.

"They're watching the quay," she said, slipping inside. "Four men, plain clothes, bad at pretending to fish. The harbor master's launch is anchored mid-channel—if anyone runs for open water they can signal it. But—" she allowed herself half a smile, tired and crooked, "—they're watching the big boats. Nobody watches the little ones."

"What did you find?"

"The *Gull*. Herring boat, out of the northern villages. Her master owes me—his boy was in my sister's ward at the prison infirmary, and I sat with him three nights when the fever peaked. He sails with the morning tide, seven o'clock, and he can put you ashore at a cove sixty miles up the coast where there's a road and no questions. After that you're on your own." Ada's jaw tightened. "It isn't far enough. It should be another country, another ocean. But it's what there is."

Paloma looked at her friend—at the shadows under her eyes, at the raw places on her knuckles from the night she'd broken into Vasseur's office and stolen back a bread tin full of dead men's names at risk of a prison cell herself—and felt the old vertigo rise and settle, rise and settle, like weather.

"I reported on you," Ada said abruptly. "Since the checkpoint. Since before the checkpoint—I was writing to them from the garrison, Paloma, months before. And then I stole the tin back, and I told myself that squared us, and it doesn't. Nothing squares it. I handed a colonel the shape of your days for weeks, and you're standing here about to run for your life because of what I helped set in motion, and I will carry that until I die, and I want you to know that I know it." Her voice cracked on the last words and she mastered it the way she'd mastered everything her whole life, by force. "So say whatever you're going to say. Don't spare me. I'm past being spared."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. Out in the street, the city was waking—a cart rattling, a gull screaming somewhere above the fog, the first smells of bread from the bakery two doors down seeping under the door.

"When you gave them information," Paloma said, "did you ever give them a name from the ledger?"

Ada blinked. "No. Never. They asked about you—your movements, your habits, who you spoke to. I never—"

"Then here is what I say." Paloma took both of her friend's hands. They were cold and callused and shaking slightly. "You traded what you had to save your sister, and any one of us would have done the same, and the ones who wouldn't are lying. You stole the tin back. You found the boat. You're here at dawn with four men watching the quay and you walked straight past them because you're better at this than they are." She squeezed. "I don't forgive you, because there's nothing to forgive. I trust you. That's harder, and I'm doing it anyway."

Ada turned away sharply, swiped at her face with the heel of her hand, and when she turned back she was a corporal again, crisp and dry-eyed. "Seven o'clock," she said. "Berth fourteen. Wear the gray shawl. And Paloma—" her voice dropped, "—don't look back at the dock. Masters hate passengers who look back. Says it's bad luck for the catch."

* * *

The last hours went to the ledger.

Marek had it out on the big sorting table in the back of the shop, and beside it the finished broadsheets, stacked in neat hundreds: the ledger reproduced page by page in small clear type, Brandt's letter facsimiled beside it, the sworn testimonies set off in boxes like scripture. Four hundred copies had gone out on yesterday's coaches. Two foreign journalists had carried duplicates in their dispatch cases, bound for capitals Vasseur couldn't touch. The record was loose in the world now, multiplying, unownable. That had been the whole design, and it had worked, and Paloma touched the printed pages with fingertips that trembled slightly and could not stop.

"I've catalogued the original under the number we discussed," Marek said. "Third shelf, behind the Dutch shipping registers, in the false-spined box. If anyone comes asking, the Kavanagh papers were destroyed in a fire last spring—there's a scorched dummy volume to show them, and I've already rehearsed the story twice." He adjusted his spectacles. "In fifty years, some doctoral student will find the real box and make a career of it. That's immortality, Fraulein. Not monuments—misfiled boxes."

"Will you stay? When they come for you too?"

"Oh, they won't come for me." Marek smiled, folding his ink-stained hands. "I'm neutral, licensed, and indispensable—half the harbor authority owes me printing favors. Old men with presses are the safest creatures alive. It's young women with memories they arrest." His smile gentled. "Go north. Cross the border when you can. And when you're safe, write to me through the bookseller in the cove town—code the letter as a book order. 'Send two volumes of the maritime almanac' means all's well. 'Send three' means trouble. I'll know."

They wrapped the ledger together. Oilcloth, then canvas, then cord, Marek's fingers quick and certain, hers slower, lingering. Inside the wrapping, between the pages of names, he had tucked a copy of the finished broadsheet—so that the original and its firstborn child would wait together in the dark, however long the waiting lasted.

"There," he said, and set it on the shelf, and slid the Dutch registers in front of it, and stepped back, and it was done. Five hundred and eleven names. She knew the number the way she knew her own heartbeat. She would not see the book again—she understood that with a clarity that felt less like grief than like setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had forgotten their shape without it—but the names were not in the book anymore. They were in the type, and the paper, and the dispatch cases, and the doctoral students yet unborn, and the mouths of families about to receive letters beginning *We write concerning your husband, your son, your brother, whose name was omitted from the reports...*

"You could come with me," Paloma said. "North. There's room in the world for printers everywhere."

"And abandon my press? My type? My fifty years of misfiled boxes?" Marek shook his head, laughing softly. "Every army has its deserters, Fraulein. I deserted long ago—from the country that taught me that archives burn. I'll stay here and keep the fire from spreading to this one." He bowed, formally, absurdly, in his apron spotted with thirty years of ink. "It has been the honor of my working life."

* * *

The fog was lifting off the harbor in long ragged banners when she came down to berth fourteen, and the sun came through it in pale shafts that turned the water to hammered pewter. The *Gull* sat low and salt-crusted at her moorings, nets drying in tangled gold along the rail, her master—a squat, weathered man with kind creased eyes—looking at her papers exactly long enough to see nothing in them at all.

Ada was already aboard, stowing a canvas bag in the wheelhouse. She'd insisted on coming as far as the cove; there had been no arguing, and Paloma hadn't tried. Some debts are paid in miles.

They cast off at seven o'clock precisely, with the tide, as the master had promised, and the little boat slid out past the breakwater into the swell, and Petra began to fall away behind them—the red roofs, the masts, the fog-banners burning away to reveal the whole bright ordinary morning of the world.

Paloma kept her eyes forward, as instructed. But at the mouth of the harbor, where the channel bent and the city showed itself one last time in profile, she broke the rule. Just once. She looked back.

On the quay, small with distance, stood a figure in a printer's apron, one hand raised—not waving, just raised, the way a man salutes a ship. Beside him, impossibly, unmistakable even at that distance by the set of her shoulders, stood a second figure in uniform grays. Devlin. He had wired that he was coming; he was late; he was here. Their eyes could not possibly have met across that stretch of shining water, and yet she lifted her hand anyway and held it up until the point bent away and the harbor closed behind them like a book.

"He made it," Ada said quietly beside her. "I wired him the berth myself. Thought you shouldn't leave without—" She shrugged. "He'll take Uncle Tomas home when they release him. He'll stand at the memorial when they read the names. Somebody from the family ought to be there, hearing it."

"Somebody from the family," Paloma repeated softly, and found that the words fit some ache in her exactly.

She settled onto the hatch cover as the *Gull* leaned into the long swells of the open coast. The wind smelled of nothing but salt and cold and distance. In the canvas bag at her feet: one shift, one brush, one tin cup after all—Ada had smuggled it aboard without asking, and Paloma had pretended not to notice, because measuring your life in what can be carried apparently includes being carried sometimes by the people who love you.

Everything else was behind her, on a third shelf, behind Dutch shipping registers, in a false-spined box, waiting for the future.

Five hundred and eleven names, and she did not need the book to recite them. She had begun learning them aloud to herself in the hospital ward, name by name, when sleep wouldn't come; she knew them the way sailors know stars—not all at once, but well enough to steer by. She faced the gray line of the northern horizon and began, silently, at the beginning, as the herring boat ran on with the morning tide toward whatever waited in the cove, and the road beyond it, and the years beyond the road.

The first name was Anselm Pryce, age nineteen, and he went with her, and all the rest of them after him, out over the bright cold water.
