## 22. Petra and the Harbor

The fog came off the water in the mornings the way it always had, folding itself around the masts and the fish stalls and the crooked chimneys of Petra as if the harbor were a thing being remembered rather than seen. Paloma Kavanagh — who had not been Paloma Kavanagh in four years, who was now Sister Marta to the patients of a charity ward in a town two hundred miles up the coast, in a country whose language she spoke with an accent no one could place and no one asked about — still woke some mornings expecting the smell of carbolic and salt blood, expecting the garrison bell, expecting the weight of a ledger against her ribs.

The expectation faded faster each year. What remained was quieter: the habit of counting. She counted what could be carried. It was how she had measured every day of the war, and it was how she measured this one — a basin of clean water, a bundle of linen, three names on her morning rounds. Enough. It was enough.

* * *

The book arrived in the town's only bookshop on a Tuesday in autumn, in a crate from the south, and Paloma would have passed it by entirely if the shopkeeper — a chatty widow named Frau Ostmann who liked to read aloud to her customers whether they wished it or not — had not been standing at the door with it open in her hands.

"You were at the harbor city during the war years, weren't you?" Frau Ostmann said, falling into step beside Paloma on the cobbled street. "You'll know this one, then. Everyone who was anywhere near the water knows this one."

The cover was plain gray cloth, unadorned but for a stamped anchor and the title: *Petra and the Harbor: A Record of the Last Year of the War*. Beneath it, the historian's name — Dr. Ilse Brandtner, of the University at Vellmar — and beneath that, in smaller type, the phrase that stopped Paloma's breath in her throat:

*Including, for the first time in print, the complete text of the Kavanagh papers.*

She did not trust her voice. She bought the book without haggling, which Frau Ostmann found so unlike her that she remarked upon it twice.

That night, in the small room above the ward where she slept, Paloma lit her lamp and opened it.

* * *

She had expected to feel fear. That was the first surprise — that there was none. Four years of another name, another life, another woman's posture in another woman's clothes, and when she saw her own name in print — *the Kavanagh papers, assembled and preserved by an unnamed nurse of the coastal garrison* — she felt only a strange, floating lightness, like the moment after a fever breaks.

Dr. Brandtner wrote carefully. Paloma could see the care in every line, the historian's discipline laid over what was plainly a personal obsession. She quoted Marek Sol's original broadsheet at length — the whole of it, the letter Elias Brandt had sealed in wax with his dying hands, the sworn testimony of the gunner whose voice the poison had taken but whose handwriting filled three pages of the record, the survivors' accounts gathered in taverns while Colonel Vasseur's men searched the streets outside. She reproduced the shipping numbers, the manifests, the arithmetic of ballast sold as medicine. She gave the number of the dead — not the official number, which had been eleven, but the true one, which was two hundred and forty-one.

And then, on page ninety, she printed the ledger.

Every name. In order. In full.

Paloma read them the way she had once written them, by lamplight, one at a time, letting each one be a man again for the length of his line. Aldous Wren, private, drowned in transit. Ferenc Malloy, gunner, fever, untreated. Tomas Ibarra, cook, dysentery. On and on, down through the pages, until the last entry — the one she had written with shaking hands on the night before the evacuation orders came, the name she had carried longest and closest —

*Devlin Kavanagh, orderly, fever, untreated.*

Her brother's name, set in type, in a book that would sit on shelves in cities she would never see.

She sat with the lamp burning low for a long time after that. Outside, the town settled into its evening noises — a dog, a cart, the church clock striking the hour with its slightly cracked second bell. Inside, a dead woman's name lived in print, and the woman who had buried that name sat very still and let herself grieve properly, at last, without hurry, because grief given shape and acknowledgment was the whole of what she had ever asked for anyone.

* * *

Brandtner's introduction addressed the question of authorship directly, and with a candor that made Paloma smile despite everything.

*"Who kept the ledger,"* the historian wrote, *"we do not know. The broadsheet of 19 March gives no name. The tribunal record aboard the Meridian gives none. The witnesses questioned afterward described a nurse — young, dark-haired, soft-spoken — who appeared among them in the final weeks, collected their testimony, read the names aloud on the deck of that ship, and then, when the victors moved to arrest her, vanished into the harbor crowds as completely as fog into water. Some said she died on the fishing boat north. Some said she took ship for the colonies. One witness swore he had seen her years later tending cholera victims in a river town, under a name he could not remember.*

*"I have chosen not to pursue her,"* Brandtner continued. *"Not from indifference — I pursued every other thread of this history down to its fraying end — but from a conviction I reached slowly and against my own instincts. The archive does not need her name. The archive has her work. There is a kind of authorship that consists in making oneself unnecessary to the record one leaves behind, and I suspect — I cannot prove, but I suspect — that she would prefer to be exactly what she is: a sentence in someone else's footnote, and two hundred and forty-one names that might otherwise have been nothing at all."*

Paloma closed the book and pressed her palm flat against the cover, the way she had once pressed her hand to the foreheads of dying men, as if to take a measure of something, or to give one.

*Sentiment,* Vasseur had called the ledger, that night in the rented room with its single candle, his voice gentle as a man offering absolution. *Names in a notebook. Grief dressed up as evidence.* He had offered her uncle's freedom and her brother's safety for the letter in her boot, and she had spent one long sleepless hour genuinely considering it, and she had never told anyone — not Ada, not Marek, not even Devlin — how close the bargain had come to closing. Let him keep that. Let him believe, wherever he was now — retired, decorated, his name attached to the official history's grudging final chapter on "isolated corruption in the supply services" — that he had understood what he was buying.

He hadn't. That was the victory, and it was bitter and small and it was hers, and it was enough.

* * *

Winter came early that year. The ward filled with coughs and fevers, ordinary miseries, the honest ailments of the poor — nothing like the war wards, nothing like rows of men poisoned by the very medicine meant to save them, and Paloma found, moving down the lines of beds with her basin and her linen, that she was grateful even for the ordinariness. Suffering without conspiracy behind it. Death that was only death.

In February, a letter came.

It was forwarded twice — first to the charity's old address in Petra, then through Marek Sol's printing office, where a clerk with good sense recognized the name on the return direction and sent it on. The hand was Devlin's; she would have known it anywhere, the careful upright letters of a man who had learned penmanship from a port official's manifest books.

*Sister,* it began, and she laughed aloud alone in her room, because he was the only person alive who would dare.

*I saw the memorial notice in the Vellmar papers. They are reading the names this spring, all of them, at the harbor. Two hundred and forty-one. They asked me — me, can you imagine — to speak, since I am the brother of the woman who kept the list and the man whose name ends it. I told them I would say very little. I will say that my sister taught me that loyalty is not silence. I will say that our uncle Tomas wept when the release order came, and that he lived four more years and spent every one of them telling anyone who would listen what he had signed and why, so that no one would ever sign it again. He is buried now beside Mother. His stone says PORT OFFICIAL AND WITNESS. He chose the words himself.*

*Ada sends her love. Her sister is married with two children and names everything after Ada, who pretends to find it tiresome. Ada still writes to me every month asking if I have heard from you, and every month I write back the same answer, and every month she writes back: Good. Tell her nothing. Tell her the harbor remembers, and leave it at that. She says you will understand why she means it as tenderness.*

*Marek has printed a second edition. He says the first sold out in six weeks, which for a book of shipping manifests and dead men's names is either a miracle or an indictment of the peace. He asks, as always, if you want your share of the proceeds sent somewhere. As always, I have told him the cholera fund in the river towns. He says the fund's treasurer reports anonymous donations arriving quarterly and refuses to speculate.*

*Come to the memorial. No one will know you. Four years, Sister — the harbor has forgotten faces faster than it forgot names.*

*I will look for you anyway. I always do. Every dark-haired woman near the water, I make a fool of myself. Ada says I should stop. I tell her I will stop when it stops being the truest thing I do.*

*Your brother,
Devlin*

Paloma read the letter three times. Then she folded it along its careful creases and put it inside the gray book, between the pages of the ledger, where Devlin's name stood in type at the end of the list — the living name tucked against the dead ones, which was, she thought, exactly where he would have wanted to be.

* * *

She went. Of course she went. She told herself she would decide on the road, and she knew from the moment she packed her bag — one bag, everything she owned, measured and weighed out of four years' habit — that the decision had been made the day she bought the book.

Petra in spring was fog and gulls and the smell of tar and bread, and it undid her a little, walking down from the coach station, because the body remembers places the mind has agreed to forget. The corner where the checkpoint had stood. The alley to Marek's press, where the type had gleamed like teeth in the lamplight. The dock where the Meridian lay rotting at her moorings, half-hulked now, her decks open to gulls — but a new plaque had been bolted to her gangway rail, and a knot of tourists stood before it reading aloud.

*On this ship, on 19 March, testimony was heard that restored the names of 241 soldiers erased from the record of the war.*

Paloma stood among the tourists and listened to a schoolboy sound out the plaque's last line — *let the record show that they lived* — and had to turn away toward the water and breathe until her eyes were dry.

The memorial was held at noon on the harbor square, before the customs house where her uncle Tomas had signed his coerced manifests and where, years later, he had walked in freely to give his own testimony, trembling and unbowed. Crowds filled the square and spilled onto the docks. Fishermen stood on their decks to see. And when the mayor finished speaking — the usual words, progress and healing and the price of freedom, delivered with the smoothness of a man reciting — a thin gray-haired woman in a plain coat stepped to the podium and announced that the names would now be read.

All of them.

They began with the first name on the ledger, Aldous Wren, and a hush went over the square that Paloma had heard only once before, on the deck of the Meridian, when she herself had stood where the reader stood now. The names came slowly, respectfully, a different voice for each stretch of the list — aldermen, priests, sailors, children too young to understand taking their turns — and the crowd answered each one with the same low refrain, a custom Paloma did not know and understood instantly, the way one understands any ritual built out of grief: *present.*

Aldous Wren. *Present.* Ferenc Malloy. *Present.* On through the alphabet of the lost, the fog lifting off the harbor behind them as though the water itself were listening, and Paloma stood at the edge of the crowd with her hood up and her hands folded, answering under her breath with everyone else, giving each name its due, keeping her count.

Then the voices neared the end of the list, and she felt Devlin before she saw him — some older recognition, bone-deep, the way one feels family across a room. He stood near the front with a woman whose arm was linked in his, and he was scanning the crowd the way he always scanned crowds, she knew, the way he had written he always would. Their eyes met across forty feet and four hundred strangers.

He did not move toward her. He did not call out. He looked at her for a long moment — long enough for her to nod, long enough for her to see that he understood, that Ada had trained him well in the grammar of tenderness that means *I see you, I know you, I will not unmake you* — and then he turned back to the podium just as the reader reached the final name.

"Devlin Kavanagh," the reader said. "Orderly. Fever, untreated. Brother of the keeper of this list."

"*Present,*" said the square, hundreds of voices, and Devlin Kavanagh stood straighter than Paloma had ever seen him stand, and answered with them, his voice breaking only on the second syllable, "*Present.*"

Paloma's hand rose to her mouth. Around her, strangers wept openly and were not ashamed. The fog burned off the harbor in long bright rags. Somewhere behind her, a bell began to ring — the harbor bell, cracked and sweet, ringing two hundred and forty-one times, once for each name, and she stayed for every stroke.

* * *

She left before the wreaths were thrown on the water. There was a boat going north on the evening tide — there was always a boat going somewhere on some tide; the harbor taught you that if it taught you anything — and she wanted to be on it, wanted the water under her and the town behind her, wanted to carry away what could be carried and no more.

At the foot of the gangplank, a woman fell into step beside her. Gray at the temples now, a scar Paloma didn't recognize along one jaw, but the walk was unmistakable, and the voice, when it came, was rough with feeling it refused to display.

"They'll ask me where I'm going," Ada Reyes said. "The harbormaster's clerk. He asks everyone."

"Tell him you're seeing off a friend."

"A friend." Ada tested the word, found it sound. They walked together to the plank's foot and stopped. Neither embraced. That was their way; it always had been. "Marek says the second edition has your uncle's testimony appended. Full text. He says Brandtner cried when he read it."

"Marek cries at proofs."

"Marek cries at invoices." Ada looked past her at the boat, the fishing crew stowing nets, the gray water beyond. "Vasseur died in the winter. Quietly. In his bed. Decorated."

"I know."

"And it doesn't — " Ada stopped. Started again, lower. "It doesn't spoil it? Any of it?"

Paloma considered the question honestly, because Ada deserved honesty and had paid for it dearly. "No," she said at last. "Because it was never a fight against him. It was a fight for the names. He was only ever in the way."

Ada nodded slowly, twice, the way she nodded when a wound was dressed properly and she had nothing to add. Then she pressed something into Paloma's hand — small, wrapped in waxed paper, warm from her pocket. A heel of bread. Of course. The old joke, the old sacrament: the tin, the hiding place, the thing carried.

"For the road," Ada said. "In case you need to hide something."

"I don't have anything left to hide."

"Yes, you do." Ada's eyes were bright and hard. "You have two hundred and forty-one reasons to stay alive and unknown. Same as always. Go on, Sister Marta. The tide won't wait, and neither will I — I've got a sister's grandchildren coming to dinner and a reputation for punctuality to maintain."

Paloma boarded. At the rail she turned, and Ada was still there, arms crossed, already turning away, already becoming one more figure in the harbor crowd — and Paloma understood that this was the last sight she would keep of her friend, and that it was a good one: Ada walking back into the city that remembered everything, shoulders straight, unhurried, free.

The boat slipped its moorings. The harbor opened ahead, wide and gray-gold in the late light, and behind it the town shrank to rooftops and masts and the customs house where the memorial banners still hung. Paloma unwrapped the bread and ate a little, and put the rest in her pocket, and stood watching the water.

Somewhere behind her, in a printer's archive, in a university library, in the gray book on Frau Ostmann's shelf and a thousand shelves like it, the names waited — patient, permanent, present. Somewhere ahead, in a river town that needed a nurse, a bed was empty and a basin waited to be filled. She measured it all the way she had always measured everything: what could be carried. A memory of bells. A heel of bread. The knowledge that the record outlived her, and that this — this, exactly this — had been the whole of the work, and it was finished, and it was enough.

The fog was rising off the water as the harbor fell astern, folding itself around the boat gently, like a thing remembering her, or forgetting her, or both — and Paloma Kavanagh, who was no one, who was Sister Marta, who was a sentence in a footnote and the reason for two hundred and forty-one names, stood at the rail until the harbor was gone, and then went below to sleep, unafraid, carrying nothing but what could be carried, which was everything that mattered.
