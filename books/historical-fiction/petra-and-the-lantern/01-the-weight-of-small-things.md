## 1. The Weight of Small Things

My father taught me to weigh things with my hands before I ever learned to weigh them with scales. He was a pearl-diver, and a diver's life depends on knowing, in the dark and the deep, exactly what a thing costs you — how much air it will eat from your lungs, how much strength it will take from your arms, whether the stone in your hand is an anchor or a tool. He would press objects into my palms when I was small: a river pebble, a brass button, a dried fish, a coin worn smooth as an egg. *Tell me*, he would say. *What can you carry? What will carry you?*

I was nine when the water took him, and eleven when I understood that everything I owned could fit inside a rice sack, and fourteen when I learned the trick that has kept me alive since: that a man who carries nothing draws no eyes, and a man who draws no eyes survives.

This is what I know about weight. Everything weighs something. The question is not whether you can escape the weighing — no one escapes it — but what you choose to hold, and what you let go, and whether you have counted honestly before the moment comes when counting is all there is left to do.

* * *

The Meridian Trading Company warehouse smelled of camphor and wet rope and the sweet rot of palm oil gone over in the heat, and at dawn, before the clerks arrived with their ink-stained fingers and their voices like clattering shutters, it belonged to me.

I had been there since the fourth hour, moving through the aisles with a lamp turned low so its light fell only where I needed it. The evacuation orders had come down from the factor's office the evening before — chalked on the slate by the gate in Mr. Calloway's own careful hand, the letters upright and tidy as gravestones: *ALL COMPANY GOODS TO BE MANIFESTED AND CRATED FOR RIVER TRANSPORT WITHIN THE WEEK.* Below it, smaller, almost apologetic: *BARGE DEPARTS AT NEXT SPRING TIDE. NO EXCEPTIONS.*

No exceptions. I had read those three words four times while the other workers crowded around the slate, and each time they meant something different. No exceptions for the goods, certainly. The company's bolts of Manchester cotton were going upriver if men had to bleed carrying them. But the word sat differently depending on where you stood. For the clerks, *no exceptions* was a promise. For me it was a question mark, and questions like that are best answered early, alone, with your hands busy.

So I worked.

There is a particular silence to a warehouse before the town wakes. The floodwater had already crept into the lower streets, and sound carried strangely across it — the slap of water against pilings came up through the floorboards like a pulse, slow and patient, the way my father said the river breathed when it meant to swallow something. Outside, the mist off the water lay thick enough to drink, and the first grey light came through the high louvres in long dusty blades that moved when I moved, so that walking the aisles felt like walking through the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal.

I began with the dry goods, because dry goods lie least. Rice in hundred-weight sacks, stacked six high; I tested each stack with my shoulder before trusting it, found one leaning column and rebuilt it, sack by sack, my hands reading the weave of the burlap the way a blind man reads a face. Some of the sacks were damp along their southern sides. I set those apart without being told to. By the time the second bell rang from the mission tower — thin, tinny, swallowed almost whole by the fog — I had segregated three days' worth of stock that would spoil if it traveled in a wet hold, and I had done it quietly enough that no one would ever know who had saved it.

That was the way of things. I did the work, and the work vanished into the company's account books, credited to no one, which suited everyone including, most days, me.

Invisibility is a trade like any other. It has its tools and its apprenticeship and its wages. The tool is stillness. Not the stillness of fear — fear makes men twitchy, and twitchy men get seen — but the stillness of a thing that belongs where it is. A crate among crates. A shadow among shadows. I had learned it first as a boy running errands through these same aisles, when the warehousemen would box my ears for being underfoot until I understood that being underfoot was a choice. You learn the sightlines of a room. You learn which floorboards speak. You learn to move when carts rumble past the open doors and stand like furniture when they stop. You learn, above all, to want nothing visibly, because wanting is the loudest noise a body makes.

I wanted many things. I had simply gotten very good at keeping my back to the room when I felt them.

* * *

By the time the sun burned the mist off the water and the clerks came clattering in — Okafor first, always first, his bicycle bell announcing him forty yards out like a small brass conscience — the near half of the warehouse was manifested, crated, stenciled, and ready, and I was up a ladder in the rafters sectioning rope, which was as good a place as any to be when the day's owners arrived.

"Oyelaran." Okafor's voice carried the particular flatness of a man acknowledging furniture. "The manifests for the cotton lots. Where are they?"

"Finished. On your desk, weighted." I did not look down. Looking down invited conversation, and conversation invited noticing, and noticing was the enemy. "Third lot had damp in the outer bindings. I broke the bale and re-dressed it. It'll need re-stenciling."

A pause. I heard him flipping pages. "Hm," he said, which was the closest Okafor ever came to gratitude, and then his footsteps receded toward the office, and I went on with the rope.

From up there the warehouse looked like what it was: the gutted belly of a town preparing to abandon itself. Crates everywhere, stenciled with the company's mark — a stylized meridian line crossing a globe — stacked in ranks like coffins waiting on names. And below them, the other cargo, the kind that never got stenciled: the workers themselves, arriving in ones and twos through the waterside door, wading the last flooded stretch with their trousers rolled, carrying their tools and their silences. Old Ekwueme the cooper. The twin brothers who ran the stamping press. A new boy, sixteen maybe, with the wide eyes of someone seeing flood season for the first time. They took up their stations the way men do when the work is the only solid thing left, and nobody spoke much, because everybody was doing the same arithmetic I was doing, and arithmetic like that does not improve with discussion.

Five berths on the last barge — that was the rumor moving down the lines even then, passed hand to hand like contraband tobacco. Five berths, and the company's African staff numbered nearer fifty. The rest would stay behind the closing bridge and wait out the flood in the lantern-lit core of the town, eating company rice at company prices, and some would be fine, and some would not, and history had already shown which ones tended to be which.

I sectioned rope and listened and said nothing. My family's name was not yet on any list I had seen. That was either very good or very bad, and I would not know which until the list itself appeared, and lists appear when the powerful finish deciding, never before.

* * *

Midmorning, Mr. Calloway came down into the warehouse himself, which he did perhaps once a month, and the temperature of the room changed the way it changes when weather arrives.

You could hear him before you saw him — not loud, never loud, but present, the polished tap of his boots on the boards, the rustle of his linen coat, the low continuous murmur in which he wrapped every instruction like a gift. He stopped at the damp-rice stacks first. He put his hand flat on a burlap flank the way a physician touches a patient.

"Damp here," he said, not accusing anyone. Simply noting, the way God notes rain.

"I separated them, sir," Okafor said quickly. "First thing this morning."

"Good man." A pause, the length of a heartbeat too long. "Who dressed the third cotton lot?"

Silence, of the kind that has a shape. In that silence I felt the familiar pull — the little tug at the base of my throat that says *speak*, that says *it was me*, that says *let yourself be seen just once*. I had felt it a thousand times in seven years and obeyed it perhaps twice, and both times had taught me why I didn't.

"It was handled, sir," Okafor said.

"Handled," Calloway repeated, mildly, and moved on, and I stayed up among the rafters with the rope coiling through my hands and my heart beating hard against nothing at all.

Here is the strange part, the part I have never managed to explain to anyone, least of all myself: I did not hate him. I should have. Seven years of my life signed away to fill the hole my father's debts left in the company's ledger, two years yet remaining, and every hour of it spent proving myself useful to the very machine that held the paper on my freedom — hate would have been the honest wage for all that. But Calloway was not cruel in the way the old factors had been cruel, with whips and slurs and open contempt. His cruelty — if it was cruelty — wore the face of courtesy. He remembered names. He asked after mothers. Once, finding me re-inking a faded manifest by lamplight after hours, he had stood watching for a full minute and then said, quietly, "Your father had hands like that. Careful hands." As though carefulness were a legacy he had a right to appraise.

It is harder to hate a man who sees you than a man who doesn't. But being *seen* by Calloway was not the same as being known, and I had learned the difference the way you learn a current — by being pulled by it. To him I was a well-made tool, and tools are seen precisely to the extent of their usefulness and no further. A hammer is admired for the head, never asked about the handle.

I finished the rope. I climbed down. I took up the next task, and the next, and made myself smooth and silent and essential, which is to say I made myself nothing, which is to say I survived another morning.

* * *

At noon I walked home through water.

The divers' quarter lay on the low side of the town, as it always had — the ground the company never wanted, the ground that drowned first — and the flood had taken the footpaths a week early this year. I rolled my trousers and waded calf-deep past the shuttered fronts of houses whose owners had already gone upriver or inland, past a woman standing hip-deep in her own doorway wringing out a cloth and staring at nothing, past children poling a broken door like a raft and laughing, because children laugh at floods the way they laugh at funerals — not from hardness but from not yet knowing what is being buried.

Our house stood where it had always stood, on stilts my father had driven deeper than the others, because a diver knows what water does to foundations. Adaeze Oyelaran built her house the way she built her days: to outlast.

She was on the veranda when I came up the ladder, sorting beans at a speed that looked leisurely and was not. My sister Ngozi sat behind her with a borrowed book held close to her face, though I noticed she was watching our mother between pages. Grandfather Tamuno was nowhere visible, which meant he was at the river, which meant his heart was heavy, which was most days now.

"You're late," my mother said, without looking up.

"The manifests ran long."

"Mm." She poured beans from one basin to another, letting the bad ones show themselves in the wind. "There's food. Eat it while it's warm, because warm is a condition this house may not afford much longer."

I ate. The stew was mostly greens and oil with a suggestion of fish, and it was good, because she could make anything good, and because I had learned years ago that refusing food wounded her worse than insulting it.

"Ngozi tells me there are five berths on the barge," my mother said presently, still sorting. The words came out level and unhurried, like stones laid one at a time on a wall.

"That's the talk."

"And the talk also says the company decides who fills them."

I set down my spoon. Through the open door I could see the river, brown and swollen and glittering, moving with terrible patience toward the bridge that would soon close, and beyond it everything — the capital, the schools, the world that existed on the far side of water.

"There'll be a list," I said. "Posted at the office. When it posts, we'll know."

"We'll know." She looked at me then, and her eyes were the same dark water as the river, holding the same patience and the same threat. "Jude. Your father's debt bought this house its stilts and your sister her school fees and you your trade, and I have never once regretted the price. But I will tell you now, and you will remember it: whatever that list says, whatever arithmetic those merchants do with our lives — this family does not travel in pieces. Whatever else happens, we do not travel in pieces."

Ngozi had lowered her book. Her eyes were bright and fixed on me with a hunger I recognized and feared, because it was the hunger I fed by staying silent — the hope of the capital, the examinations, the life on the far side of the bridge — and hope, in my experience, was the heaviest thing a person could carry, heavier than rice, heavier than rope, heavier than a drowned father's name.

"The list posts tomorrow," I said. "I'll see it first. I work in the counting house."

And my mother nodded, satisfied, and went back to her beans, and Ngozi went back to her book, and neither of them saw the splinter go in — the small cold knowledge settling under my skin as I smiled and cleared my bowl:

That tomorrow's list was being written tonight, by candlelight, in Mr. Calloway's office, by men who knew exactly what my family was worth.

And that I had spent seven years making certain no one at the company ever thought to wonder what *I* was worth at all.
