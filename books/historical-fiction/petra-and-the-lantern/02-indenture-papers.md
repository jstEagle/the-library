## 2. Indenture Papers

The counting house smelled of ink and camphor and the ghost of other men's debts.

Jude Oyelaran had learned to read that smell the way his father had once read the river — by pressure, by texture, by the small betrayals of surface tension. Camphor meant the ledgers had recently been aired against rot; the damp came up off the water even here, three streets inland, seeping into everything the way the company seeped into everything. Ink meant work. Work meant he was useful. And usefulness, Jude had discovered at fourteen, was the only country a bonded man could travel in without papers.

He sat at the low desk nearest the window — the one without a chair back, because chairs with backs went to men whose names appeared on documents as more than a mark — and squared a stack of waybills with the edge of his palm. Outside, the morning fog was lifting off the Meridian wharf in rags, and through it came the sounds he knew better than his own heartbeat: hawsers creaking, a winch taking up slack, the factor's clerks arguing about tonnage in the bored voices of men who had never once feared a number.

Two years remained. He did the arithmetic the way he did everything now, silently, automatically, the sum already waiting in him like a stone at the bottom of a well. Two years minus this morning, minus the hour he'd already spent hauling crates before dawn, minus the hour he would spend after supper copying freight manifests in a hand so neat that Mr. Calloway sometimes held it up to the light like a curiosity. Two years, four months, eleven days. He had stopped counting in weeks because weeks broke his heart in increments too small to be useful. Years were a size a man could carry.

* * *

His father had carried pearls up out of the dark for nineteen years, and in all that time he never learned to carry less.

Jude had been seven the first time he watched the boats go out — small outriggers riding low, the divers' bodies oiled black against the brown-green swell, each man trailing a rope and a stone weight and a basket, each man breathing the way the old ones taught: not a gulp but a *pouring*, air let down into the chest like water into a jar, slow, patient, hoarded. Tamuno had taught them all, though Tamuno's own diving days were behind him by then; the old man stood on the shore with his arms folded and his eyes half-shut, and when a diver surfaced too fast, gasping, Tamuno would click his tongue and say, *the water does not chase you, why do you chase it?*

Emeka Oyelaran chased nothing. That was what the other men said about him. He went down smooth and came up smooth, and the pearls he brought up were round and clean-skinned, and the buyers from Meridian Trading paid him fair — for a while.

Fair lasted until it didn't. Fair lasted until the season the red tide killed the beds off Point Ansa, and the company advanced Emeka credit against next year's catch, and next year's catch came up thin, and the year after that the monsoon shifted and the beds silted, and the advances kept coming because the company's clerk was a kind-faced man named Okon who said, *your family must eat, brother, we understand, we are reasonable people,* and every word of kindness was written down in a book with a leather spine, and every line of the book bore interest.

Jude was nine when he first saw the ledger. His father had spread it on the table one night by lamplight, not reading it — he read it often enough — just resting his hand flat on the open page, the way you'd rest a hand on the chest of something sick. The columns ran past where the light reached.

*How much is it?* Adaeze had asked from the doorway, her voice level in the way Jude would learn, much later, was the loudest thing about her.

Emeka didn't answer with a number. He said, *It is shaped like my life.* And then he laughed, short and terrible, and closed the book, and told Jude to bring him water.

That was the year Jude began measuring things. Not in money — money in their house was weather, it arrived and passed — but in weight. A bag of rice: heavy enough that a boy of ten could carry it twenty paces before setting it down. A pearl of good grade, wrapped in cloth: almost nothing, a breath in the palm, worth more than the boat, the nets, the roof. His father's whole trade, Jude realized early, was the exchange of weightless things for heavy ones, and the company had found the trick of reversing the exchange whenever the current suited them.

When the fever took Emeka — a bad lung, the divers' sickness, the coughing that started pink and ended dark — Jude was twelve, and Ngozi was six, and there were two younger ones besides, and the ledger did not die with its debtor. Debts, the company believed, outlived men. There was even a clause.

* * *

"You've the look of a man doing sums, boy."

Jude's pen stopped above the waybill. He hadn't heard Calloway come in — he never heard him, that was part of the man's craft, a big Englishman moving through his own domain as quiet as a heron — and now the shadow fell across the desk, smelling of pipe smoke and bay rum and the particular cold sweetness of madeira taken before nine in the morning.

"Waybills, sir." Jude turned the sheet so the figures faced up. "The Kasai consignment. Fourteen crates, marked and weighed."

"Mm." Calloway picked up the sheet, held it at arm's length — he refused spectacles on principle, considering them an admission of age — and studied Jude's hand the way he always did, with the frank pleasure of a collector. "Beautiful writing. You know what my old schoolmaster would have said? He'd have said you wasted it on a warehouse." He set the paper down. "But then, your schoolmaster wasn't paying for your schooling, was he. We were."

There it was. Jude felt it land the way he'd learned to feel rain coming — a coolness first, in the air between them, before any drop fell. Calloway was not cruel. That was the thing no one who hadn't worked under him ever understood, the thing that made the merchants in town call him a decent fellow and mean it. He was not cruel the way some factors were cruel, with kicks and docked rations and public shaming. He was kind the way a dam was kind: he held you, he contained you, and the holding was the whole point, and if you mistook the containment for care you drowned slower, that was all.

"Yes, sir," Jude said. "I'm grateful."

"And how much longer on the bond?" Calloway asked, though Jude knew — knew with a certainty that lived in his bones beside the arithmetic — that the factor knew to the day. To the hour, possibly. Men like Calloway kept time the way God supposedly did, everywhere at once, indifferent.

"Two years, sir. Four months."

Calloway smiled. He had a good smile, broad and pink and genuine-seeming, the smile of a man who enjoyed his food and his family and his accounts. "Four months. Well, well. Practically a free man already." He leaned one hip against the desk — against *Jude's* desk, taking possession of even that — and looked out the window at the fog burning off the wharf. Below, stevedores were swinging the Kasai crates onto a lighter, and Calloway watched them the way a farmer watches oxen. "You know, when your father died I told the board we should simply write the remainder off. Christian charity, good feeling in the quarter, all that." He tapped ash into a dish that wasn't there, remembered, pocketed the ash in a fold of paper instead. "Old Whitcombe voted it down. Said a debt forgiven teaches nothing. Said—" and here Calloway pitched his voice into a fair imitation of the late director, dry as biscuit, "*—said the boy signs or the boy sails, and either way Meridian recovers.*"

"I signed," Jude said.

"You signed." Calloway nodded, satisfied, as if Jude had performed a trick well. "And you've been the best of the indentured lot since. Don't think I haven't noticed. Numbers don't lie, boy, whatever else does." He pushed off from the desk, straightened his waistcoat. "Keep on as you have and those two years will pass quick as a wet season. There's a place for a steady man in this house when his time's served. Remember that."

He went out. The door didn't quite close behind him — nothing in the counting house ever quite closed, the damp swelled the wood — and through the gap came the ordinary noises of the morning resuming, clerks calling figures, the winch groaning, gulls fighting over fish guts on the pilings.

Jude sat very still and let the resentment come up in him, and did what he had trained himself to do with it, which was the same thing his grandfather had taught the divers to do with air.

He poured it down. Slowly. Patiently. Hoarded.

Because resentment spent was resentment gone, and a man who spent his anger on a Tuesday got nothing for it but a beating and a black mark in a leather-spined book. But resentment banked — banked like coals, covered over, fed just enough to keep its heat — that was capital. That was a pearl forming around the grit of him, layer over layer over layer, and someday, someday, he would bring it up into the light and find out what it was worth.

He dipped his pen and went on with the waybills.

* * *

The signing had happened in this same room, five years ago, on a morning so similar to this one that the memory needed no effort to return — it simply lived here, in the walls, in the smell of camphor, and walked out to meet him every time he crossed the threshold.

He had worn his dead father's shirt, taken in at the shoulders with stitches his mother refused to let anyone see her cry over. Adaeze had walked him to the counting house door and stopped there, at the property line, because she would not stand inside a Meridian building one minute longer than grief required. She had straightened his collar with two hard tugs, looked at him with eyes like river stones, and said only: *You will keep count. Whatever they tell you, whatever they write, you will keep your own count in your head, and one day your count will be the true one.*

Then she had kissed his forehead once, briefly, the way you seal a letter, and turned and walked back toward the divers' quarter with her spine so straight that the women watching from the doorways said afterward it looked like she was going to her own funeral and intended to complain about the arrangements.

Inside, the clerk Okon — still kind-faced, still reasonable — had laid the indenture papers on the desk and turned them to face Jude, and explained them slowly, in the gentle voice adults used when they wanted a child to feel that a trap was his own idea. Seven years of labor to Meridian Trading Company, in satisfaction of the debt of Emeka Oyelaran, deceased, plus lawful interest, the said minor to be housed, fed, and instructed, his wages forfeit to the account until discharge.

"Instructed," Okon repeated, tapping the clause. "You'll learn letters and cipher, boy. Better than most free boys get. Your father would be proud."

Jude had looked at the paper. He could read by then — Tamuno had drilled him in it, sitting on the sea wall with a scrap of sailcloth and a burnt stick, saying *a man who cannot read his own chains will believe they are bracelets* — and so he read every line, twice, while the clerks exchanged amused glances and Okon checked his watch. He read the clause about wages forfeit. He read the clause about extension for cause, meaning any illness, any idleness, any act of God that cost the company a day's labor could be added back onto the end of his bond, day for day, at the board's discretion. He read the clause that bound his future wages, upon discharge, to be first applied against any residual balance.

There would always be a residual balance. He understood that even then, at twelve, standing in a shirt that smelled faintly of his father. The interest was designed the way a weir is designed: water passes through, but the fish stay.

"Where do I make my mark?" he had asked.

Okon slid the paper around and offered the pen with real warmth, and Jude took it, and instead of an X he wrote his name — full, careful, in the round schoolroom letters Tamuno had taught him — and beneath it, in smaller characters that no one bothered to examine, the date. His own count. The beginning of his own ledger, kept where no clerk could reach it: the true record, running alongside theirs, of every hour owed and every hour served and every hour stolen.

Five years of entries now. He added to it every night, by the light of a stub of candle he bought with pennies earned carrying parcels on his one half-day, and the candle was the only thing in his life he permitted himself to burn for himself alone.

* * *

At noon he carried a folio of invoices up to Calloway's office and found the window open and the factor standing at it, looking down at the wharf, where a barge was being fitted with new planking — the big river barge, the one that ran passengers and cargo up to the capital when the bridge allowed it.

"Flood reports," Calloway said, without turning. He said it the way other men said *good morning*, and Jude felt the coolness move through the room again, ahead of the rain. "River's a hand higher than last week. Bassi's been telling anyone who'll listen the crossing won't hold past the rains. Bassi exaggerates." A pause. "Bassi has also been right for forty years."

"The barge looks sound, sir."

"The barge is sound. The barge is also small, and this town, when it panics, is large." Calloway turned from the window and took the folio, and his fingers paused on Jude's hand a moment — not affection, exactly, more the proprietary pat one gives a fine animal. "You'll help me draw up the evacuation lists when the time comes. Berth allocations. It's fiddly work, and I need a careful hand." He smiled his good smile. "Who knows — maybe there'll be a berth on that list for a clever young clerk who's nearly served his time. Stranger things have floated down this river."

"Thank you, sir," Jude said, and meant nothing by it, and knew Calloway meant nothing by it either, and understood that between two such fluent liars a perfect courtesy could pass like coin, both parties knowing the metal was base.

He went back down to his chairless desk. Outside, the fog had burned away entirely, and the river lay open and glittering and indifferent, going where it pleased, asking no one's leave, keeping no one's books.

Jude squared his papers, dipped his pen, and began again on the waybills — and underneath the scratch of the nib, underneath the camphor and the ink, he could feel it starting already, far upstream where none of them could see it yet: the rain, gathering itself over the hills, patient as interest, coming for the bridge, coming for the town, coming for the lists that were not yet written.

He wrote the date at the top of the page, in his own true hand, and kept his own count.
