## 3. Mother Counts the Rice

Adaeze Oyelaran counted the way her own mother had counted, in the dark before dawn, when the house was still honest with itself and no one was awake to see what poverty required of a woman's face.

She sat on the low stool by the cooking hearth with the rice sack open in her lap and measured nothing at all — that was the trick of it. Measuring cups lied. A cup heaped high was not a cup leveled flat, and a woman who told herself she was measuring was only telling herself a story. Adaeze did not measure. She *counted*. She took the grain between her fingers in small deliberate pinches and moved it from the company-stamped sack into the clay pot, and each pinch was a number, and each number was a day, and when she reached the bottom of the arithmetic she knew exactly how long her family could eat without lying to anyone.

Forty-one days.

She had known it would be thirty-something. It was always thirty-something now, ever since Tamuo died and the company's ledger swallowed his diving debts along with his body, since the sea off Meridian Point gave back nothing but his weight-belt and the company clerk gave back a bill. Forty-one days of rice, and beside the pot the beans in their jar, the dried fish wrapped in oilcloth, the palm oil down to its last two fingers' depth in the jug. She added them to the sum the way you add sorrows to sorrows: not because it helps but because the total must be known.

Outside, the divers' quarter was still blue-dark, but the river was already speaking. It spoke differently this season than any season Adaeze could remember — lower in its throat, thicker, as though it were chewing on something upstream and had not decided whether to swallow. The rains were coming early. The old men said so. The fish said so, moving strange. Even the mosquitoes said so, arriving in numbers that made no sense for the month, and Tamuno, her father-in-law, who had read water for sixty years the way priests read scripture, had stood at the quarter's edge three evenings running and said nothing at all, which frightened her more than any prophecy.

She covered the rice sack, tied it, and set her hand flat upon it for one breath. This too was inheritance: the gesture of a woman claiming her provisions against the world.

Then she rose, banked the embers, and began to make the morning lie — the porridge stretched with cassava grit so that it looked, in the bowls, like plenty.

* * *

Ngozi came to the hearth first, because Ngozi always came first, drawn by the smell of food the way an arrow is drawn to a target. She was fifteen and built out of her father's long bones and her mother's stubbornness, and she carried her slate under her arm even to breakfast, even to the privy, Adaeze suspected, because the girl had decided some years ago that education was the boat she would take out of this quarter and she intended to be found holding her ticket at all hours in case salvation left suddenly.

"Is it rice porridge?" Ngozi asked, sliding onto the bench.

"It is breakfast," Adaeze said.

Ngozi peered into the pot with the frank audacity of youth. "It is mostly grit."

"It is mostly love," Adaeze said, ladling it, "which is heavier than rice and keeps longer."

Jude came in from the warehouse shift-change smelling of camphor and rope tar, nineteen years old and already moving through doorways sideways, as if his whole strategy for survival were to present less surface to the world. He kissed the top of Ngozi's head, nodded to Adaeze, took his bowl, and ate standing, because Jude never fully arrived anywhere; he was always half-departed, always keeping one shoulder toward the exit. The little ones tumbled in after — Chidi, eight, all elbows and questions, and little Ama, six, who still slept with her fist closed around whatever she'd held last, this morning a river snail shell she refused to surrender even to wash her hands.

Four children. Four bowls. One pot. Adaeze served herself last and least, as her mother had done, and her grandmother before that, and she understood now what she had not understood as a girl watching those women eat air and call it supper: that this was not sacrifice. This was accounting. A mother who ate her full portion was spending tomorrow's strength today, and Adaeze Oyelaran did not spend what she could not replace.

"You're doing it again," Ngozi said quietly, not looking up from her bowl.

"Doing what?"

"Eating nothing. I can see your bowl."

"My bowl is my business." Adaeze touched her daughter's chin, tipped it up. "Eat. You have examinations."

"I don't care about—"

"You care about nothing more." She said it without heat, the way you state that the river is wet. "You will sit those examinations in the capital, and you will pass them, and you will walk into a school where the teachers are paid by people who are not us, and none of it happens on an empty stomach. Eat."

The word *capital* worked on the table like yeast. Chidi began asking whether the capital had rivers, whether it had bigger rivers than theirs, whether the boats there were taller than the factor's house; Ama demanded to know if the capital had snails; Jude ate and watched his sister glow, and something passed over his face that Adaeze had learned, in nineteen years, to read at a distance — not jealousy, never jealousy, Jude did not permit himself even that affordable vice — but a kind of careful sorrow, the look of a boy doing arithmetic he thought no one could see him doing.

She saw him. She always saw him. That was the cruelty of being the one who counted: you saw everything, and everything you saw had a price attached.

* * *

Midmorning, Adaeze walked to the market road with Ama on her hip and a basket on her arm, and there she met the thing she had been walking toward for a week without admitting it: Mrs. Okonkwo, standing outside the company store with her own basket, wearing the particular expression of a woman about to commit kindness.

"Adaeze." Mrs. Okonkwo's voice was gentle in the way that made Adaeze's spine straighten. "I bought too much gari again. My eyes are old. My husband will laugh at me if it spoils. Take half, I beg you, or I'll have to throw it to the goats, and you know these goats, they're already fat enough to be insulted—"

"No."

"It is not charity, it is—"

"It is charity wearing my friend's wrapper," Adaeze said, "and it is very well dressed, and the answer is no."

Mrs. Okonkwo's face folded. Around them the market road went on with its noise — fishwives arguing the price of bream down to slander, a boy beating a drum that had lost its skin and most of its dignity — but between the two women there opened that silence which is its own country, with its own weather.

"Adaeze." Her friend lowered her voice. "Everyone knows the rains are coming early. Everyone says the bridge may close. If the crossing shuts, prices—" She stopped, because they both knew. Prices. Everything would climb except wages, and wages at the company climbed never.

"Then I will pay the higher prices," Adaeze said, "for less food, and we will be hungry together with our dignity intact, which is more than the goats can say."

"You are proud."

"Yes." She shifted Ama on her hip. The child's cheek was hot against her collarbone; she filed the fever away in the ledger behind her eyes, between the palm oil and the forty-one days. "I am proud the way a roof is proud. It does nothing decorative. It keeps the rain off my children. Take your gari home to your husband, Okonkwo, and tell him your eyes are perfect and your heart is a troublemaker."

But Mrs. Okonkwo did not move, and her voice, when it came again, had shed its gentleness and put on something older and harder, the voice of women who had buried husbands the same year Adaeze had. "Your son works their warehouse from dark to dark. Your daughter is cleverer than the factor's own clerks and studies by a lamp that costs you oil you cannot spare. Your father-in-law dives at his age, Adaeze, at his *age*, because pride does not fill pots either. And you stand here and tell me dignity is a roof." She shook her head slowly. "A roof keeps off rain. It does not keep off water."

Adaeze said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not cost more than silence, and she was, above all things, a woman who understood cost.

Mrs. Okonkwo sighed, pressed the parcel of gari into Ama's arms despite everything — the child, feverish, accepted it with the solemnity of a treaty — and walked away before refusal could be organized. Adaeze stood a moment in the market roar, holding her daughter and her daughter's contraband, feeling the fraying start somewhere under her ribs, the sound a rope makes just before it decides.

*That evening,* the gari appeared in the family pot anyway, cooked through, disguised, unacknowledged. Dignity, Adaeze reflected, stirring, was a garment you mended in private and wore in public, and sometimes the thread that held it was a friend who loved you enough to let you pretend.

* * *

It was that same night, with the children asleep and the lamp turned so low it was nearly a rumor of light, that Tamuno came to her doorway and stood there dripping river-water onto her swept earth floor, and she knew before he spoke that the water had spoken first.

"The bridge won't last the season," he said. No greeting. Old men past a certain grief dispensed with greetings. "I've been telling Calloway's men for two weeks. They write it in their books and do nothing, because writing is cheaper than timber and they think the flood will flatter them by waiting."

"How long?"

"A month. Perhaps less." He filled her doorway, this relic of a generation of divers who had pulled pearl and profit from the riverbed until the river grew tired of the arrangement and began collecting divers instead. His hands, scarred white in patterns like coral, hung at his sides. "And the company will evacuate by barge, and the berths will be few, and the list will be written in that counting house by men whose arithmetic has never once included us."

"We are on the lists," Adaeze said. "We are always on the lists. That is the problem."

Tamuno almost smiled. Almost. "You sound like my son."

Her chest did the thing it did whenever Tamuo surfaced in conversation — a quick submersion, a held breath, a rising. Two years, and still he rose in her like a drowned thing returning to the light it remembered. "He sounded like himself," she said. "Everyone else sounds like the company."

The old man studied her a moment across the lamplight, and then he said the thing he had come to say, and he said it plainly, because he was too old for approaches. "When the order comes, and it comes soon — count your rice, Adaeze. Count it carefully. And count your children twice, because the company counts them once, and their counting has a way of losing one."

He left. The river went on speaking in the dark, lower in its throat than ever.

Adaeze sat a long time with the lamp turned down to almost nothing, doing sums no sack of rice could hold. Five mouths. One bridge dying. One barge, someday soon, with berths numbered like seats at a feast to which not everyone was invited. And her eldest son, who had spent nine years making himself useful and unseen, who moved through doorways sideways, who ate standing and slept lightly and never once in his childhood had asked her for anything — not a toy, not a story twice-told, not even, after his father died, permission to cry where she could hear him —

Her son would give away his place. She knew it the way she knew the rice ran out in forty-one days: by counting what was actually there instead of what should be. Jude had been practicing disappearance his whole life. Someday soon the company would hand him a stage for it, dressed up as duty, and he would step onto it smiling that thin sideways smile, and he would think she didn't see.

Adaeze pinched out the lamp and sat in the honest dark, and let the fraying sound grow a little louder, and began — as she did every night, as her mother had done, as her grandmother, as every woman in every flooded season since the first flood — to plan how to hold her family together with nothing but arithmetic and fury.

Across the compound, in the boys' room, Jude slept badly, dreaming of ledgers.

Three mornings later, Mr. Calloway nailed the evacuation notice to the warehouse door, and the whole town came to read its fate in ink.
