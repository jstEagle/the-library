## 4. The Factor's Daughter

The lantern market opened on the last dry Saturday of the season, the way it always did, as if the town itself understood that soon there would be no more evenings worth lighting.

Jude carried crates to it before dawn — lamp oil for the factor's stall, paper shades in their flat wooden boxes, a bolt of wire for the hanging frames. He moved through the dark square with a crate balanced on one shoulder and the other hand free to steady himself against walls he knew by touch: the corner of the dye house, warm even now from yesterday's vats; the loose brick by the well that no one ever fixed; the low beam over the chandler's door that had raised a lump on every Oyelaran head for two generations. His father had struck it once, diving days before his last dive, and laughed and said the beam was the river's cousin, testing whether a man kept his wits above water too.

By full light the square had transformed. Stalls bloomed along its edges like coral after a tide, strung with unlit lanterns waiting for dusk — red ones from the inland traders, pale green ones dyed with river sedge, white ones the old women bought for graves and would not explain to children who asked. The air smelled of hot oil and frying plantain and the particular sweetness of rice glue, which was the smell of a thousand small things being made to hold together. Jude set down his last crate in the shadow of the Meridian awning, received a nod from the clerk that was not thanks but acknowledgment of a task completed, and understood that he was dismissed until evening, when the crates would need carrying home again.

Dismissal was a kind of gift. He had learned to receive it gratefully.

He meant only to walk the edge of the market. He told himself that much on the way — that he would look at the lanterns the way he looked at everything beautiful in this town, quickly and without stopping, the way a man checks a purse he knows is empty. But the crowd was thick and the morning was bright and his feet, which were honest feet, took him into the middle of it, past the woman who sold smoked fish and past the boys selling sugarcane cut in lengths, and there, at the stall of the paper-lantern maker, arguing cheerfully with an old man about the quality of his bamboo ribs, stood Miriam Calloway.

She was out of uniform — that was the first strangeness, seeing her in a plain cotton dress the color of weak tea, her hair braided back without ribbon. School was out between terms; he had forgotten. The second strangeness was that she was winning the argument.

"—and I say the rib is split, because I can see it split, and you say it is character, and I say character is what you call your mistakes when you charge extra for them."

The old lantern-maker threw back his head and laughed, a sound like a door swinging on unoiled hinges, and Jude saw that this was a ritual between them, worn smooth by repetition. Miriam turned to pay and her eyes passed over him the way eyes pass over everything in this town that carries things — and then came back.

"You," she said.

He did the arithmetic instantly and found nothing in his favor. *You* was not how the factor's daughter addressed warehouse boys. He glanced behind him, but the space there held only a fishwife and her tray of silver-bellied catch.

"Me," he agreed, because denying it seemed worse.

"You're the one who reads." She said it plainly, without the softening people used when they spoke of such things among his kind — no wonder, no condescension dressed as kindness. She said it the way she might have said *you're the one who limps* or *you're the one with the crooked stall*, a fact filed away. "In the warehouse. Papa caught you once with a book and didn't stop you."

"He didn't see me," Jude said. "I made sure of it."

"Why?"

It was a real question. She waited for a real answer, and that was the trap of her, he would think later — she asked questions the way other people asked nothing at all, and expected the world to answer honestly, and half the time the world obliged.

"Because a man watching you read decides what the reading means," Jude said. "A book in my hands could be inventory. It could be theft. It could be ambition. He chooses, not me. If he doesn't see, the book stays mine."

Miriam looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted — not softened exactly; she did not seem a girl given to softening — but recalibrated, the way a clerk recalibrates a scale when the weight surprises it.

"That's the saddest clever thing anyone has said to me all year," she said.

"I have others."

"I believe it." She turned back to the lantern-maker's array, and Jude should have gone. Every part of him that had survived seven years in the Meridian warehouses — the part that measured exits, the part that read faces, the part that knew precisely how invisible he could afford to be — instructed him to go. He stayed. He stayed the way water stays in a cracked bowl, finding the low place and filling it, and the low place was that no one had spoken to him in three years as though his answers mattered.

She held up two lanterns. One was crimson, lacquered, expensive-looking, its paper stretched drum-tight over perfect bamboo. The other was a smaller thing, pale as a fish belly, slightly lopsided, one rib showing through the paper like a bone under skin.

"Which one am I?" she asked.

"That's a trick question."

"It's a test question. Different animal."

"The red one costs four times what the white one costs," Jude said. "So if I say the red one, I'm flattering your father's money. If I say the white one, I'm flattering your taste for humility, which rich girls wear like the red one anyway." He paused. "The white one has better light, though. Thin paper throws farther than thick."

Something happened to her expression then — a quick, involuntary movement around her mouth, quickly mastered — and he realized he had made her laugh internally and that she was declining to let it out, and that they were both, in this moment, managing their faces across a distance far wider than the width of a market stall.

"Thin paper throws farther," she repeated. She paid the old man for both. Then she pressed the white one into Jude's hands before he understood what was happening — pressed it flat against his chest with the heel of her palm, quick, almost rough, so that it looked from any distance like a merchant handing a delivery to a carrier.

"For carrying," she said loudly. "My father will want it hung at the stall."

And quietly, only for him: "Consider it a joke between classes."

"We're not in classes together."

"No," she said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

He carried it through the market like contraband, which in a sense it was.

Not the lantern — a lantern was nothing, a few coins of paper and bamboo, the kind of thing a factor's daughter scattered without noticing. What was contraband was the conversation still going on inside him, replaying itself in a loop he could not shut off. *Thin paper throws farther.* Where had that come from? He had not known he thought in such sentences. He had not known the sentences were in him, packed down under the inventory lists and the tide tables and the careful blankness of his face, waiting for someone to ask a question real enough to draw them up.

His father had been like that, his mother said. Quiet men were not empty men; they were full men with the lid on.

Jude walked the long way back toward the warehouse quarter, along the river path where the morning light lay on the water in broken gold, and stopped under the tamarind tree that leaned out over the bank. He unfolded the lantern and looked at it properly for the first time. It was badly made, truly — the rib visible through the paper, the paste showing in thumbprints at the seams, the whole thing listing slightly to starboard as though it had already been at sea a while. On its base, in pencil, in handwriting he recognized from the ledgers he was forbidden to read closely — the same quick slanted hand that signed the delivery notes — someone had written a single word:

*Petra.*

He turned it in the light. Petra. Not a word from any language spoken in this town, not trade-English, not the river tongue his grandfather prayed in, not the port pidgin. He turned it over twice more. There was nothing else. No explanation, no joke explained, just the name sitting on the paper like a bird that had landed and intended to stay.

He should burn it. That was the sensible thing, and Jude was a sensible creature; sensibility had kept him alive and fed and mostly unremarked upon since he was twelve years old. A gift from Miriam Calloway was a debt he could not carry, and debts were the one cargo he knew the weight of down to the ounce. Seven years of his life weighed exactly what his father's diving debts had weighed. He knew what it was to owe.

But it was not a debt, some quieter voice argued. It was a joke between classes, she had said, and jokes were the one currency that flowed uphill and downhill alike without changing value. And besides —

Besides, thin paper threw farther.

He folded it flat against his chest, inside his shirt, where it rode against his heartbeat like a second, lighter heart, and went back to work.

* * *

Work that day was the counting house, because the factor wanted the quarterly tallies begun early and the tally clerks wanted tea fetched and crates hauled and the tall boy — that was what Calloway called him, *the tall boy*, as though height were his whole name — wanted for anything heavy or high or tedious.

Jude had spent enough hours in the counting house to be furniture there. Furniture heard everything. He stacked ledgers in the corner and listened to the clerks complain about the rains coming early this year, about the river rising a hand's width overnight, about the bridge timbers that sang at night now instead of creaked, and old Osaretin the head clerk saying, not joking, that when the bridge began to sing it was tuning up for a funeral. They laughed, but thinly, the way men laugh at things that are probably true.

Calloway himself came through mid-afternoon, and Jude watched him the way he watched everything about the factor — sidelong, through the geometry of stacked crates, taking in details without being caught taking them. Calloway was not a cruel man, which everyone agreed was almost worse; cruelty you could brace against. Calloway was *fair* the way a scale is fair, weighing everything and loving nothing, and he had weighed Jude's father's drowning once, years ago, in this very room, and pronounced the account settled — meaning the debt survived the diver, meaning the son inherited it like a name or a nose.

"Papa says the river's up another finger since Tuesday," Miriam said from the doorway, and Jude's spine went rigid before his mind caught up.

She had come in with a basket — errands, apparently, a daughter's afternoon of errands — and she was speaking to her father, not to him, and there was no reason on earth for his body to react as though a lantern had been lit inside it. He kept his eyes on the ledger spines. He counted the ledgers. There were thirty-one. He counted them again in case the number had changed, which of course it had not.

"Mm," said Calloway, not looking up. "Tell Bassi the company will want the crossing assessed by week's end. If we lose the bridge we lose the month, and if we lose the month—" He waved a hand, completing the sentence with economics, which for Calloway was a complete language.

"And if we lose the month, we evacuate," Miriam finished. "Yes. I can count, Papa."

"You can count," her father agreed fondly, and something in his voice was warm in a way Jude had never once heard directed at himself, and hated noticing that he noticed.

Miriam crossed the counting house to the shelf where the stationery was kept. Her path took her within arm's reach of Jude and the crate he was stacking. She did not look at him. She selected a pen nib, tested it against her thumbnail, rejected it, chose another, and in the entire duration of this performance she said, in a voice pitched precisely for a radius of two feet and no farther:

"Lopsided things throw the best light. Ask anyone."

And was gone, basket on her hip, the door swinging shut behind her, leaving behind her a scent of some soap or oil that smelled faintly of lemongrass, and leaving Jude holding a ledger he had been holding motionless for the better part of a minute.

Old Osaretin looked up from his columns, looked at Jude, looked at the door, and looked back at his columns. He said nothing. He was an old man in a company town, and he had survived by knowing what not to see, and he extended Jude the same professional courtesy.

But that night, walking home along the river path with the last of the crates delivered and the first stars pricking through the heat-haze, Jude took out the lantern and unfolded it and read the word again by feel more than sight. *Petra.* He sounded it out. It felt like stone in the mouth — a hard, patient word, a word that would sit at the bottom of a river for a hundred years and still be itself when the water left.

He did not know what game she was playing, if it was a game. He did not know what she wanted, or what he wanted, and the not-knowing frightened him more than any knowing could have, because his entire survival had been built on knowing — on reading the current before it read him, on measuring every room for its exits, on keeping his wants so small and so deep that no one could tax them.

Now there was a want, and it was shaped like a badly made paper lantern, and it did not fit in any of his hiding places.

From the town behind him came the ordinary sounds of evening — pots, voices, a dog, the factor's household lamps going up one window at a time. From the river ahead came a sound that was less ordinary. He stopped at the bank and listened. The bridge timbers, half a mile downstream, black against the last of the light, were singing again — a long, high note held under the wind, the sound wood makes when it bears more than it was built to bear.

The river was higher tonight than it had been that morning. He could see it without measuring, the way his father's people had always seen it, in the body before the eye: the water fuller, faster, tasting of places upstream that it had no business reaching yet. The rains were weeks early. Everyone said so, and everyone said it lightly, the way people speak of weather, which is to say the way people speak of the future when they have not yet had to live in it.

Jude folded the lantern small, put it away against his heart, and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the bridge sing.

Two days later, the first notice appeared nailed to the customs post: *RAIN SEASON COMES EARLY. ALL PERSONS TAKE HEED.*
