## 5. Rain on the Bridge

Elder Bassi had lived seventy-one years on this bend of the river, and he had learned to read water the way priests read scripture — slowly, reverently, and with an eye always toward what it was threatening to say next.

So when the first true rain of the season came down in the third week of the planting moon — a full month early, hammering the tin roof of his house like a fist demanding entry — he did not do what his neighbors did, which was to stand in doorways and remark upon it with pleasure. He went out into it. He stood in his yard in his old man's undershirt until the water ran off his chin, and he listened to the river.

The river had a voice for every season. In dry months it muttered and gossiped over its stones, low and intimate, a sound you could sleep beside for years without hearing it at all. When the rains came properly, it found something like laughter — broad and rolling, full of itself, carrying whole branches and the occasional drowned goat past the town with a showman's flourish. But tonight, under that first premature rain, the water made a third sound. A sound Bassi had heard only four times in his long life, and had prayed never to hear again.

It sounded like a man clearing his throat before delivering bad news.

He stood there until his bones ached with cold, and then he went inside, lit his lamp against the gloom of mid-afternoon, and began to make a list.

Bassi was a maker of lists the way other men were makers of trouble. He had been the town's record-keeper for longer than anyone could remember — not by appointment but by accretion, the way lichen becomes part of a stone. Births, deaths, debts, disputes, the price of palm oil across forty seasons: all of it lived in his ledgers, in his cramped, patient hand. The Meridian Trading Company kept its own books, of course, in its own counting house with its iron shutters and its clerks who wrote numbers that moved when you looked away from them. But the company's ledgers recorded what the company wished to be true. Bassi's recorded what was.

And what was, as of tonight, was this: the rains had come a month early, the river was already at his second marker stone, and the bridge — the great timber bridge that was the town's single thread to the road, the market, the capital, the world — would not survive a season like the one that was coming.

He dipped his pen and wrote, at the top of a fresh page:

*Third week, planting moon. Rain out of season. River at the second stone.*

Then, after a moment, he added: *God have mercy on the poor.*

* * *

By morning the whole quarter knew something was wrong, though no one could yet say what. That was the nature of dread in a river town: it arrived ahead of its reasons, the way a smell of smoke arrives ahead of fire. Women drew their water earlier than usual and stood talking at the well with their buckets forgotten between their feet. The fishermen came in with half their catch and full silence, which told Bassi more than any report — fishermen talked; it was only when the fish themselves were strange that they stopped.

He walked down to the bridge after breakfast, moving slowly, leaning on his stick, giving himself the time of an old man because he had earned it and because slowness let him see things hurry concealed.

The bridge had been built before his father's birth, by engineers from across the sea whose names were carved into a plaque nobody could read anymore, worn to ghost-letters by sixty years of hands. It crossed the river at its narrowest point near the town, nine great spans of hardwood on masonry piers, wide enough for two carts, strong enough — everyone said — to hold anything a man might want to carry across it. Everyone said this the way people said the levee would hold or the company would deal fairly: because saying otherwise was unbearable, and because it had mostly been true so far.

But Bassi remembered the flood year of his boyhood, the one the old people still called simply the Bad Water, when the bridge had groaned for three days and nights like a creature being flayed, and when it finally held, it held changed — a hand-span lower, forever after, its back permanently bent. Every flood since had bent it a little more. He put his palm flat against the nearest pier now, feeling for what his fingers knew better than his mind: a faint tremor, a vibration too quick to be wind, coming up through the stone from the water worrying at the foundations below.

The river was brown. Not its ordinary tea-brown but a thick, muscular brown, the color of earth in motion, hauling the mountains' skin down to the sea. Debris rode its surface — leaves, branches, a fence post still wearing a hinge. The current sucked at the piers and curled away in eddies the size of compound courtyards.

"Second stone," said a voice behind him, "and climbing."

It was Okoro the ferryman, come up from his mooring with his arms folded against the drizzle. He nodded at the marker stones set into the bank downstream — three of them, placed by some forgotten prudent ancestor, each a step higher than the last.

"When I was a boy," Okoro said, "we saw the second stone once in ten years. My father saw it twice in his whole life." He spat, and the river took his spittle instantly, indifferently. "This year I think we'll see the third."

"The third means the lower quarter goes under," Bassi said. "The third means the divers' street, the tanneries, the warehouse row."

"I know what it means. I live above it, God forgive me."

They stood together in the rain without speaking, two old men watching a river gather its strength, and around them the town went about its morning with the studied normalcy of people who had decided, collectively and without discussion, not to look directly at the thing in the room.

* * *

Rumors are the true weather of a river town, and by midweek they had thickened faster than the clouds.

Bassi collected them at the market the way he collected everything — patiently, without judgment, writing nothing down yet but filing each account where it belonged. The rains had burst a lake in the highlands. The rains had washed out a mine. The rains were a punishment — though for what sin, the tellers disagreed, each proposing conveniently one that spared his own household. The company's agents upriver had sent word that the crossing would close within the month. The company's agents had sent no such word, and anyone claiming otherwise was a fool or a liar. The barge — Meridian's fat cargo barge, the *Prosperity*, which hauled palm oil down and salt and cloth up — would make one final run before the closing, and after that, nothing larger than a ferry would move on the water until the dry season.

That last rumor Bassi tested carefully, because it mattered most. He presented himself at the company offices under the pretext of settling a question about a deceased neighbor's account, and he watched young Clerk Mensah's face while the clerk confirmed it with the bright, untroubled voice of a man reciting approved language.

"One departure, Elder. Before the bridge closes, naturally. The company would never leave its people stranded." Mensah smiled. "Berths will be allocated according to company records. Priority to employees of standing."

"Of standing," Bassi repeated.

"Good standing," said Mensah, with the serene confidence of a parrot that has never considered the meaning of its words.

Walking home, Bassi turned that phrase over like a coin he suspected of being shaved. He had lived long enough among the company's papers to know that *standing*, in their arithmetic, was not a moral quality but a bookkeeping one — that a man's standing could be excellent while his soul was in arrears, and that the reverse was also common enough. He thought of Jude Oyelaran, the Oyelaran boy who worked the warehouse dawn shift, quiet as furniture, useful as a lamp; the boy whose father Tamuno had once been the finest diver on this stretch of the river and whose death the company had converted, with a stroke of ink, into a seven-year bond against his son's labor. Two more years on that bond, if the ledger spoke true. And the ledger — Bassi had learned — spoke only the truths that paid rent in it.

He wrote in his evening entry: *One barge. Berths by 'standing.' Remember: the company counts its own.*

* * *

On the fifth night of rain, the bridge spoke.

Bassi heard it from his house, a quarter-mile off — or believed he heard it, which in a town built on listening amounted to the same thing. A deep wooden groan, drawn out and released, drawn out and released, like an old man rising from prayer. By morning half the town claimed to have heard it, and the other half claimed those who claimed it were fools, and both halves went down to look.

They stood along the banks in the drizzle, hundreds of them, umbrellas and banana-leaf awnings and children on shoulders, and the bridge groaned for them on schedule, and a murmur went through the crowd that was almost worse than screaming — the low collective hum of a people revising their understanding of safety.

Bassi pushed through to the rail where the headmen gathered, and there he found Mr. Calloway himself, the factor, come down from the big house with his hat beaded with rain and his face arranged into calm. Calloway was a puzzle Bassi had never fully solved: a man capable of genuine warmth, genuinely intelligent, and yet sealed inside the company's logic the way a fly is sealed inside amber. He liked the town. He also owned pieces of it, and the liking and the owning had never once, in Bassi's observation, been permitted to meet.

"There is no cause for alarm," Calloway called out, pitching his voice to carry. "The bridge has stood through worse. The engineers' reports—"

"The bridge is singing," said Old Tamuno.

The crowd stilled. Tamuno stood at the rail's end, the last of the master divers, a spare dark stick of a man with white in his hair like foam that refused to wash out. His voice was not loud, but rivers teach men to project, and it reached every ear along the bank.

"A pier sings before it goes," Tamuno said. "Any diver knows it. You feel the water working the footing, and the wood answers. That sound is the sound of roots letting go." He looked at Calloway with the mild, terrible directness of very old men. "You can bring your engineers' papers if it comforts you. The water doesn't read."

Someone laughed, then stopped, uncertain whether laughter was permitted. Calloway's composure held — Bassi gave him credit for that — but something behind his eyes recalculated, quickly and coldly, and Bassi recognized the motion because he'd seen it in merchants' faces all his life: the shifting of an asset column.

"We will of course monitor the situation closely," Calloway said smoothly. "In the meantime, the company reminds everyone that the last barge departs on the appointed day. Those wishing passage should ensure their accounts are in order."

*Ensure their accounts are in order.* Bassi turned the sentence over all the way home. Not *those in need*. Not *families*. Accounts. As if salvation were a transaction with a queue, and the queue already sorted, and the sorting done in a room none of them would ever see.

* * *

That evening he visited Adaeze Oyelaran's compound, because a list-maker checks his sources.

She received him in the small room that served as kitchen and parlor both, where a pot of rice stretched with beans perfumed the damp air, and her children orbited the lamplight — Ngozi, fourteen and sharp-faced with ambition, reading a borrowed schoolbook even as she ground pepper; the little ones squabbling over a scrap of rope; and Jude, who sat mending a net with fingers so deft they seemed ashamed of how much attention they were drawing, and who looked up when Bassi entered with the wary courtesy of a boy long accustomed to being measured for his usefulness.

"You've heard the bridge groan," Bassi said, when the courtesies were done.

"Heard it. Felt it in my floor at night." Adaeze ladled him a bowl without asking whether he wanted one; refusing would have been an insult to them both. She was a handsome woman gone lean with work and widowhood, and she carried her pride the way other women carried water — daily, over distance, without complaint. "The company will do what the company does. We'll manage."

"The barge takes how many?"

A small silence. Ngozi's grinding hand slowed.

"Four berths per family, they're saying," Adaeze said. "Perhaps five, for those of good standing." Her voice did not change on the phrase, but something in the room did — a tightening, subtle as a sail taking wind.

"And there are five of you," Bassi said gently.

"There are five of us," she agreed. "There have always been five of us. We are not a problem to be solved, Elder Bassi. We are a family." She smiled, and the smile was armor polished to a shine. "We'll manage. We always manage."

Jude had not spoken. But Bassi, gathering his stick to leave, caught the boy's eye across the lamplight, and saw in it a thing that troubled him more than the groaning bridge: not fear, exactly. Calculation. A boy doing arithmetic he should have been decades too young for, arriving at sums he clearly did not like, and deciding — visibly, deliberately — to tell no one.

* * *

Rain again that night, harder. Bassi sat up late with his lamp and his ledgers, and he wrote until his hand ached.

*Sixth day of rain. River at two and a half stones. Bridge groaning at each span. Company announces one barge, berths by standing. The factor says no cause for alarm. Tamuno says the roots are letting go. I believe the diver.*

He paused, pen hovering. Then, beneath, in smaller letters — the private hand he used for the entries that might someday matter more than the public ones:

*I have seen this before. Not the flood — the counting. In the year of the Bad Water, the company held workers past the closing. Some were 'lost.' Their bonds were extended for the days they could not work, and the extension was charged to them. The paper survived the water better than the people did. If it comes again, someone must keep the true list. Not theirs. Ours.*

Outside, the river cleared its throat.

He snuffed the lamp and lay listening to the rain on the tin roof, and to the bridge groaning far off in the dark like a great patient animal being asked to bear one burden more than it could carry. Sleep came slow and shallow. In his last waking thought, unbidden, rose the image of the Oyelaran boy's eyes across the lamplight — the arithmetic in them, the silence around it — and Bassi understood, with the certainty of an old man whose list had never once been wrong, that whatever the flood was going to take from this town, it would not take it honestly.

The water would only be the first thief. The paper would be the second.

He resolved to watch the paper.
