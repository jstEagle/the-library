## 6. Orders Come

The notice went up on the morning the rain changed its mind.

Jude felt it before he saw it — a shift in the warehouse air, the way the other workers moved, quick and murmuring, toward the counting house wall where nothing had been posted since the tariff revision two seasons past. He was halfway up the ladder with a bale of indigo on his shoulder when the crowd began to gather, and for a moment he considered simply staying where he was, arms full, invisible as ever among the crates. That was his talent. That was his survival. But something in the quality of the murmur — not gossip, not grumbling, but that hushed arithmetic of families counting themselves — pulled him down the rungs before he had decided to go.

He wedged the indigo into its slot and crossed the yard with the unhurried gait of a man who had nowhere particular to be. Around him the company compound smelled of wet timber and lamp oil, of the river's green breath pushing up under everything, stronger each day now, as though the water itself were leaning in to listen.

The notice was a single sheet, pasted at eye level, already curling at one corner where the damp had found it. The company seal sat fat and red at the bottom like a spider that had fed well. Calloway's clerk had written it out in the round, careful hand reserved for announcements that were meant to look generous:

**BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY**

*Owing to the rising of the river and the imminent closure of the great bridge, the Company shall dispatch its final barge of the season from the lower wharf on the morning of the twenty-third day of this month. Passage will be provided to dependents of Company personnel according to allocation. Berths are limited. Claims must be entered at the counting house no later than the seventeenth.*

*Persons remaining after this date remain so at their own risk and charge.*

Jude read it twice, because the first reading was for the words and the second was for what lived underneath them.

*Dependents of Company personnel.* He turned the phrase over the way his father had once turned a pearl against the light, looking for the flaw. His mother was a dependent — Adaeze Oyelaran, widow of Tamuno's son, whose name appeared in the bond register under *household of*, which was the company's tidy way of saying that a dead diver's family still belonged, in some ledger's cold opinion, to Meridian. Ngozi was a dependent. Old Tamuno himself drew the pension they paid in cloth and salt rather than coin, which made him a dependent too, though he'd have bitten anyone who called him that. And Jude — Jude was not a dependent at all. Jude was an asset. Seven years bound, two remaining, entered not on any household list but in the bond register itself, in his own line, in his own ink: *Jude Oyelaran, indentured, term of seven years, commenced upon the decease of Ezenwa Oyelaran.*

So the notice did not name him. The notice named everyone but him, the way the world always had.

And yet — he read it a third time now, lips moving slightly, and heard behind him the yard holding its breath — there at the bottom, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*Allocated berths, as assessed by the Factor:*

*1. Adaeze Oyelaran — household head.*
*2. Ngozi Oyelaran — minor, schooling age.*
*3. Tamuno Oyelaran — pensioner, infirm.*
*4. Jude Oyelaran — bond-servant, retained.*

He stood very still. Rain dripped off the eaves of the counting house and struck the packed earth beside his foot, each drop a small dark star that bloomed and died. *Retained.* The word sat on the page like a hook sitting in a fish's mouth — patient, barbed, dressed to look like ordinary language. Not *excluded*. Not *forfeited*. *Retained*, as if his staying were a courtesy the company was doing him, as if his body were a tool too valuable to ship out of harm's reach, as if two more years of his labor were worth more than his lungs full of floodwater.

Four berths. Five names. And the fifth name struck from the list by a single word in small print that most men would skim past, because most men were meant to skim past it.

The relief came first, and he hated how fast it came, how animal it was. *They are going. Mother is going. Ngozi is going. They will be on the water before the bridge sings its last creak.* It rose in him warm and enormous, and for three whole heartbeats he let himself feel it, let himself imagine his mother's hands on the rail of the barge, dry for once, pointed away from the drowning quarter.

Then the splinter slid in, the way splinters do — thin, deep, working inward with every movement. Because he knew ledgers. He knew the company's hand. And he knew that a list with four names on it and five people in the house was not an oversight; the company did not overlook things. The company weighed everything, down to the last grain of rice in the store, down to the last breath in a diver's chest. If Jude Oyelaran was *retained*, then someone had decided it, written it, and signed beneath it in red.

Someone had done the arithmetic and concluded that he was worth more drowned than delivered.

* * *

He worked the rest of the morning as if the paper on the wall were just weather. That was the discipline of invisibility: you did not flinch, you did not linger, you became again the shape that moved crates. But inside him a new thing had begun to count. It counted the barge's tonnage against the number of souls the factor might have squeezed aboard if he'd wished. It counted the days until the twenty-third. It counted, most carefully, the distance between the word *retained* and the word *drowned*, and found it very short, and crossed it easily.

At the noon bell he carried his ration tin to the shade of the warehouse eaves and ate his maize porridge watching the river. It had come up another hand's width overnight. The brown water slid past carrying whole branches, a drowned cooking pot, a section of fence with a chicken coop still lashed to it — the river taking inventory of the town already, practicing for the real collection. Downstream, the bridge's stone piers stood hip-deep and patient, and the current combed long white beards of foam off them.

"You've read it, then."

Elder Bassi lowered himself onto the crate beside him without asking, the way old men do when the crate belongs to the town and not to anyone. His raffia mat was rolled under one arm; his eyes, milky at the rims, were sharp at the centers. "You've read it twice," he said. "I watched your lips."

"Everyone's read it," Jude said.

"Everyone has read the big letters." Bassi took a pinch of snuff, considered it, put it away un-taken. "Nobody reads the small ones until it's too late. In my father's time there was a flood year like this one coming. The company posted a notice then too. My uncle's name was on the list." He paused, letting the river finish a thought for him. "His name was on the list, and he was not on the barge. When the water went down, they found his entry in the bond book extended by four years. Extended," the old man said softly, "by a flood he did not survive to work off."

Jude kept his eyes on the water. "That was a different factor."

"It was the same company." Bassi tapped the ground between them with his staff, once, like a judge's gavel. "The company does not change its handwriting, boy. It only changes who holds the pen." He rose then, slow, joints announcing themselves, and looked down at Jude with those rimmed-milk eyes. "You read it twice. Good. Now go home and read it to your mother. Let her read it three times. She'll find what you found. Mothers always do."

He shuffled off across the yard, and Jude sat with his empty tin cooling in his hands and understood that the old man had seen the splinter without ever seeing the list — had known it would be there the way you know rain is coming by the smell.

* * *

The divers' quarter at dusk was a village of small fires and smaller comforts. Smoke lay flat along the rooftops because the wind had died, and the air tasted of wood ash and drying fish and the green rot at the river's edge. Word had outrun him, of course; word always did. By the time Jude reached the lean-to door, half the quarter's children were chanting the barge date in skipping rhythm, and old Ekwueme the net-mender was telling anyone who'd listen that his own name was third on the wharf-side list posted at the fish market, and wasn't Providence generous after all.

Inside, his mother had already heard everything.

Adaeze sat at the table with the lamp trimmed low — she never burned it high; light was money — and the notice lay flat before her, smoothed with both palms until it surrendered. She had borrowed a copy from the clerk's boy, or won it in some exchange Jude didn't want to imagine, and she was reading it a third time exactly as Bassi had prescribed, her lips moving, her finger traveling under each line like a woman walking a dangerous road in the dark.

Ngozi sat across from her, chin propped on her fists, glowing. Fifteen years old and lit from within, the way she'd been lit lately, ever since the mission school mistress had said the word *capital* in her hearing and a door opened somewhere behind the girl's eyes that nothing since had managed to shut. "Second berth," she was saying, for perhaps the tenth time. "Mother, do you see? *Schooling age.* They put me down as schooling age. There are schools across the water — real ones, with —"

"I see it." Adaeze did not look up.

"And Grandfather's third, which is right, he's earned rest, and you're first because you keep us, and —" Ngozi stopped. Jude watched her stop. Watched the glow flicker, watched her count — five, four — the way children learn arithmetic from funerals. Her eyes came up and found his across the lamplight, and he saw her arrive at the answer before he could arrange his face into anything innocent.

"Where's yours?" she asked.

"Ngozi." Their mother's voice was quiet, which in Adaeze meant loud.

"No. Where is his name?" Ngozi's finger came down on the fourth line and stayed there, pressing the paper flat as if it might confess. "*Retained*. What is *retained*?"

"A bond-servant stays with his master," Adaeze said. "It's in the terms. I read the terms when I buried his father. I read every paper they gave me to sign, and I signed none of them gladly, but I read them." She folded the notice at last, precisely, along lines it already remembered. "It's lawful."

"Lawful." Ngozi laughed, one short note with nothing in it. "The water is lawful too, I suppose. It rises lawfully. It drowns whoever the ledger says." She turned on Jude, and here it came, the worst part, the part he had braced for all the walk home: not anger yet, but hope, her hope turning on him like a lamp turned on a corner, demanding to illuminate him. "You can argue it. You're useful to them, everyone says so, even the clerks say so. Tell Calloway you'll work double through the closing — tell him anything. You know how to talk to those people. You've spent seven years learning how to be unseen; be *seen* for one hour and ask for your berth."

"I'll handle it," Jude said.

"That's what you say when you mean you'll carry it alone." She was standing now, and the lamp threw her shadow huge against the wall, taller than any of them, taller than the room. "You've been doing it since Father died. Carrying alone, saying *handled*. Well, I don't want a brother who drowns politely so my schooling goes uninterrupted. I want —"

"*Sit down.*" Adaeze's voice cracked across the room like a wet rope snapped taut, and Ngozi sat, and for a moment the only sound was the river in the distance, that constant low conversation it had been having with itself for weeks.

Adaeze looked at Jude for a long time. She had his father's patience and none of his softness, and when she spoke it was slowly, each word set down like a stone in a ford. "Your grandfather eats with us tonight. We will say nothing of lists at the table. Nothing." Her eyes flicked to Ngozi, daring her. "A family does not spend its fear before it must. And you—" this to Jude, softer now, the softness worse than the shouting— "you will eat everything on your plate. Whatever you've decided in that closed face of yours, whatever you think you'll sacrifice — you will not decide it hungry. Do you hear me?"

He heard her. He heard, underneath it, the thing she was really saying, the thing mothers say in code: *I know you. I know what you're already doing behind your eyes. Don't.*

Tamuno arrived as the smoke thickened, smelling of the river and of the palm wine he pretended he hadn't bought. He was ancient in the way of trees — bent but rooted, and with a sense, Jude sometimes thought, for weather inside human affairs the way other men had it for rain. He ate in silence, and when Ngozi, unable to help herself, began to speak brightly about the capital's schools, the old man raised one hand and she fell quiet mid-word.

"There were notices in my time too," Tamuno said, to the fire more than to anyone. "Every flood year, a paper on a wall, and every flood year, men stood reading it twice, three times, hunting for their own names the way a diver hunts a pearl bed. And I will tell you what I learned at the bottom of that river, which is the same thing I learned at the bottom of their ledgers." He set down his spoon. "The company writes the list before it reads the water. Remember that. Whatever the paper says, the paper was written first, and the water only agrees with it afterward."

No one spoke. Outside, far off, the bridge groaned — one long note, deep in its stones, like something turning over in its sleep.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Jude took the lamp down to the water's edge. This was permitted; he was, after all, *retained*, and a retained man may walk where he likes so long as he walks back. The river had climbed the bank another span during the evening. It breathed against the mud with a sound like slow applause, and out in the dark he could hear it working at the bridge, patient as a debt.

He stood where his father had taught him to stand, feet apart, weight settled, a boy's ghost in a man's stance, and he did what Bassi had told him and what his mother had forbidden and read the notice a final time in his memory, hunting the splinter, measuring it.

Four berths. Five names. *Retained.*

Relief, yes — real relief, warm as the lamp in his hand. His mother would cross. His sister would get her schools. His grandfather would see dry ground on the far side of the season. That much was certain, written, sealed in red wax.

But certainty, he had learned from ledgers, was only the first draft of a document. Things could be amended. Names could move. A berth allocated could become a berth reassessed, and he had watched Calloway's clerk make small erasures before with a knife and fresh paper, tidying the world.

And there was the other thing, the colder thing, the one he turned over now like a stone in his palm: that the company wanted him here. That his usefulness, the invisibility he had worn like armor for seven years, was precisely the chain they meant to hold him by. That when the water came, it would not be the river that decided whether Jude Oyelaran breathed — it would be an entry in a register, in a hand he was beginning to know better than his own.

The lamp guttered. He shielded it with his body, out of habit, out of seven years' training — protect the light, protect the flame, be the shelter and not the fire.

Out on the black water, something large turned — a log, a barrel, the bridge losing another argument — and the current carried it downstream toward the capital, toward dry ground, toward everyone the barges would carry.

Everyone, he thought, except the names the paper had written first.

Behind him, up the slope, a lantern kindled in the counting house window — late hours, the audit season coming, the factor working. And Jude stood a moment longer at the edge of the rising river, feeling the splinter work deeper, and understood that between this night and the twenty-third day of the month, he would have to learn what his father had known in the water and never spoken aloud:

that the difference between being saved and being kept is only a matter of who holds the list —

and that lists, unlike rivers, could be rewritten.
