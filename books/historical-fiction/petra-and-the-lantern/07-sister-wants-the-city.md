## 7. Sister Wants the City

Ngozi Oyelaran had a theory about doors.

Her mother said a door was wood and iron and nothing more, and that a girl who stared at doors would grow up wanting things she could not afford. But Ngozi knew better. She had watched the doors of this town her whole life — the plank doors of the divers' quarter that bowed in wet weather and never quite closed; the louvered shutters of the company houses on the hill that swung open each morning to let the river breeze through rooms she had only imagined; and above all the great double doors of the Meridian counting house, lacquered green as beetle wings, which opened twice a day to admit clerks with ink-stained cuffs and closed again like a mouth deciding what it would say.

A door, Ngozi believed, was a promise made of wood. It said: *there is something on the other side.* And whether you were permitted to cross was a different question entirely — but the promise itself cost nothing. Even a girl from the divers' quarter could stand before a door and know there was a beyond.

She stood before one now, though not the counting house's. This was the schoolmaster's door at the mission school, gray with salt air, its paint peeling in long curls like the bark of a fever tree. Behind it came chalk sounds, the scratch and tap of Mr. Ekwueme working sums for tomorrow's lesson, and beneath that, faintly, the smell of camphor and old paper that Ngozi loved more than any perfume sold at the lantern market.

She did not knock yet. Knocking meant asking, and asking meant admitting she was outside. Instead she pressed two fingers flat against the wood and let herself imagine — as she did every evening when the light went long and gold across the compound — the other side of all the doors between here and the capital.

The capital. Port Meridian proper, four days downriver by barge, though everyone spoke of it as if it were another country, because in every way that mattered it was. There were schools there where girls could sit examinations alongside boys. There was a teachers' college whose gate Ngozi had seen once, from the deck of a company packet boat she'd been allowed aboard to deliver her brother's mended coat — a gate of white pillars with a brass plate she had read three times until the letters burned themselves into her memory: *Meridian Normal Institute for the Instruction of Teachers.* Instruction. Teachers. A person could become someone who opened doors instead of standing before them.

"Ngozi." Her name, spoken through the door, warm with amusement. "I can hear you breathing. You breathe like your mother — loud and stubborn."

She laughed and pushed inside. Mr. Ekwueme's schoolroom was one long room with a cracked floor and windows fogged by river damp, twelve desks for sixty students who attended in shifts, and a blackboard worn gray in the center where ten years of lessons had eaten into the slate. He sat at his own desk now, a thin man with spectacles that magnified his eyes into something owl-like and kind, marking exercise books by lamplight even though it was barely dusk.

"You've come about the examination," he said, without looking up.

"I've come to tell you I'm leaving," she said. "And I wanted you to hear it from me."

That made him look up. He set down his pen carefully, aligning it with the edge of the blotter the way he did everything, precisely, as if precision were a form of prayer.

"Leaving," he repeated.

"The barge." She heard her own voice go bright and quick, the way it always did when the future opened under her like water. "The evacuation notice went up yesterday. The bridge is closing within the month — Elder Bassi says the flood will be the worst in twenty years. The company is taking people downriver before the crossing shuts. Four berths for my family." She held up four fingers, then folded one down deliberately, showing him the arithmetic she'd already done a dozen times. "Mama. Jude. Grandfather Tamuno. Me."

"Four berths," Mr. Ekwueme said slowly. "For five Oyelarans."

"We're five counting Mama's cousin Obi, and he's gone upcountry since the rains began, nobody knows where. So we're four now. It fits." She smiled. "It fits exactly. Like the sum was done for us."

Something crossed the schoolmaster's face — brief, quickly folded away, like a letter slipped back into an envelope — but Ngozi was too full of her own news to read it properly. Later, much later, she would take that flicker out and examine it and understand what it had been. Now she only kept talking, because talking about the capital was the closest thing to being there.

"There's a teachers' college in Port Meridian," she said. "The Normal Institute. Girls sit the entrance examination in the dry season. If I can find work — laundry, copying, anything — while I prepare—" She stopped herself, laughing at her own greed. "I'm getting ahead of myself. First the crossing. Then the city. Then everything else, in order, the way sums are done."

Mr. Ewueme took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which he did when he needed a moment to arrange his face.

"You know," he said, "when your father was alive, he used to stand at my window just like you stand at doors. Not coming in. Just looking at the room where letters lived." He set the spectacles back on his nose. "He couldn't read, your father. But he believed reading was a kind of diving — going down after something precious in the dark. That's why he paid for you two to come here, even in the bad years. Especially in the bad years."

Ngozi's throat tightened. Her father had been dead six years and she still caught herself turning toward him with questions, still felt the specific loneliness of a grief that arrived on schedule, like the floods.

"He'd have hated the idea of me leaving," she said quietly.

"No." Mr. Ekwueme shook his head. "He'd have carried your trunk to the barge himself, swimming behind it if he had to." He picked up his pen again. "Go home and tell your mother the school owes her nothing, and owes you everything. And Ngozi—" His voice followed her to the door. "Read the papers they give you before you sign anything. All of them. Every line. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, meaning it lightly, the way one promises to eat well or sleep enough. Papers were paper. What could be in them that mattered more than the city waiting on the far side of the water?

* * *

The divers' quarter smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet — that green, metallic anticipation hanging over the low roofs like a held breath. Ngozi walked home along the raised walkway above the mud, past the drying racks hung with nets, past Old Bassi's compound where a knot of elders sat speaking in the low, urgent tones that meant the talk was about water or money, which in this town amounted to the same thing. Past the shrine where her grandfather left offerings of kola and river-salt every new moon, asking the water's forgiveness for taking a living from its dead.

Home was the last house before the reed beds, built on stilts her grandfather had driven himself forty years ago, when the ground here was considered too honest for building and everyone said Tamuno Oyelaran was proud to try. The pride had held. The stilts had held. Only the family had thinned, the way families do — some to the water, some to the city, some to graves.

Her mother was in the yard counting rice.

Ngozi saw her from the walkway and slowed. Adaeze Oyelaran counted rice the way other women prayed: kneeling, head bent, hands moving with terrible gentleness among the grains spread on a cloth. There was a wooden bowl beside her, and into it went the portion for tonight, grain by grain, while the rest stayed heaped on the cloth like a small brown hill that never grew no matter how many times she counted it. Six years of war rations and widow's arithmetic had taught Adaeze to see abundance in what others called scarcity, but Ngozi knew the truth underneath the skill: the hill was smaller than last season. Everything was smaller than last season. The whole town was shrinking toward its lantern-lit core like a snail retreating into its shell, and prices climbed the way floodwater climbs — slowly, then all at once.

"Mama," she called, dropping down the ladder. "It fits."

Adaeze didn't look up. "What fits?"

"The berths. Four berths, four of us. We're on the list, Mama — I saw our names. Jude showed me the notice board himself." She crouched beside her mother and reached out to help with the counting, and Adaeze let her, which was its own rare gift. Together their hands moved through the grains. "Port Meridian, Mama. By the end of the month. And there's a teachers' college—"

"I know what there is in Port Meridian." Her mother's voice was even, neither warm nor cold, which was how Ngozi knew to listen harder. "I have known what there is in Port Meridian since before you were born. Your aunt went there to be a seamstress and came back with a husband who drank and a debt she passed to your cousin. The city is a door too, Ngozi. It also has another side."

"This isn't sewing work, Mama. It's school. An *examination*. If I pass—"

"If you pass, you will be a teacher." Adaeze finally looked up, and her eyes were the same dark bronze as Jude's, and as unreadable as river water at dusk. "And teachers are paid in respect and little else, and respect does not buy rice during flood season. I know this also."

But her hand had stopped moving among the grains, and Ngozi saw — the way daughters see, in the small betrayals of a mother's body — the thing her mother would never say aloud: that she was afraid, and that the fear was not for the journey but for the letting-go. Adaeze had buried a husband to this river and nearly lost a son to the company's ledgers, and now the river proposed to take her daughter too, dressed in the respectable clothing of opportunity.

"Mama." Ngozi covered her mother's hand with her own, rice grains pressing cool between their palms. "Say you want me to go."

For a long moment there was only the sound of the river moving beneath the stilts, that endless patient conversation of current against piling.

"I want you to go," Adaeze said at last, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "And I want you to write every week. And I want you to remember that whatever door opens for you in that city, you walked to it on your grandmother's knees and your father's drowned lungs, and none of it was given free." She resumed the counting. "Now fetch water. And send Jude to me when he comes. He's been strange as a cat all week."

* * *

Jude was strange as a cat all week. Ngozi noticed it the way you notice a change in the river's color — gradually, then completely.

He should have been celebrating. That was the thing she couldn't understand. For two years she had watched her brother carry the family's weight in silence, hunched under it like a porter under cargo, and now, finally, the load was lifting — the whole family going downriver together, out of the flood's reach, out of the quarter's narrowing world — and instead of relief Jude moved through the days like a man walking on ice that hadn't broken yet but might.

He worked longer hours at the warehouse. He came home late with lamp-oil on his fingers and ledger dust in his hair. When Ngozi talked about the capital — the institute, the examination, the boarding house run by deaconesses where girls like her could lodge for a fee and a reference — he listened with a fixed attention that wasn't attention at all. He looked at her the way you look at someone across a table when you're deciding whether to tell them something, and then he looked away, and the something went back wherever he kept such things.

On the third evening she cornered him by the water tanks, where he went each dusk to wash the warehouse off himself.

"You haven't asked me a single question," she said.

Jude wrung out his shirt without looking at her. "About what?"

"About any of it. The city. The school. Whether I'll need money for the examination fee. Whether the deaconesses will take a diver's daughter." She threw a pebble at his shoulder; it bounced off harmlessly, but it made him turn. "You're my brother. You're supposed to interrogate me. Threaten the boys. Demand references. That's the order of things."

"What boys?" he said, and almost smiled, and for a second he was the old Jude, the one who used to hold her hand crossing the flooded planks when they were small, both of them screaming and delighted.

"Jude." She stepped closer, lowering her voice although the yard was empty except for chickens and the listening river. "What's wrong? You've had that face all week. The face you make when you're carrying something and pretending it weighs nothing."

For a moment — one breath, maybe two — he wavered. She saw it happen, saw the truth rise in him like a bubble rising through dark water toward the surface, and she leaned toward it, ready.

"It's nothing," he said. The bubble broke somewhere below the light and was gone. "Tired. Calloway's got me doing inventory before the closure. Every crate in the warehouse, twice counted." He squeezed the water from his shirt and slung it over his shoulder. "Your berth is certain, Ngo. Mama's certain. Go tell the schoolmaster. Start saying goodbyes if you want. Just—" He paused, and something in the pause was so heavy that the chickens went quiet. "Just don't count on anything being exactly what the notice says. Companies do arithmetic differently than families."

"What does that mean?"

"It means nothing. It means I'm tired." He kissed the top of her head, rough and quick, the way he'd done since they were children, and went inside.

Ngozi stood alone by the tanks with the pebbles and the darkening water, and told herself the feeling in her chest was excitement. It was mostly excitement. The rest of it — that small cold splinter, the sense of a word unsaid — she folded away with the day's clothes and did not look at again.

There would be time, she thought. Whatever it was, there would be time to know it later. There was a city first. There were doors with promises in them, and her name — she had seen it herself, in her own brother's careful clerk's hand, posted on the board for the whole town to read — written fair and square on the list of those who would cross.

* * *

Saying goodbye took her four days, and she gave each farewell the seriousness of a ceremony, because Ngozi had decided long ago that a life lived carelessly was a life half-lived.

She said goodbye to the mission school last of all, saving it, and when she got there she found she couldn't do it in one visit. She came at dusk to straighten the desks, and again at dawn to sweep the floor Mr. Ekwueme would only dirty again by noon, and on the final evening she brought him a gift wrapped in banana leaf: her father's diving knife, the bone-handled one, the only object in the house older than her grandfather.

Mr. Ekwueme unwrapped it and went very still.

"I can't take this."

"You can. Papa would want it kept by someone who reads." She made her voice brisk so it wouldn't shake. "Sell it if you must. Buy chalk. Buy exercise books for girls whose fathers think chalk is wasted on them. Let it dive for something besides pearls."

He held the knife a long moment, thumb moving over the worn bone handle where a generation of Oyelaran fingers had polished it smooth. Then he set it on his desk, next to the lamp, where it lay gleaming like a relic on an altar, and he wrote something on a slip of paper and handed it to her.

"A reference," he said. "For the institute. My name carries further than you'd think — I sat my examinations with the deputy registrar's brother. And Ngozi—" He put his owl-eyed face close, and the lamplight filled his spectacles so she couldn't see his eyes, only her own small reflection doubled in the glass. "You'll write to me. Weekly. And you'll tell me everything, including the things you don't want anyone to know, especially those. That's the tuition I charge."

She laughed and promised, and hugged him, smelling camphor and chalk, and walked home through the blue hour with the reference pressed flat against her chest like a second heartbeat.

The town was contracting around her as she walked. She could feel it happening, the way you feel a storm change pressure in your ears. Lanterns were lit earlier each night. The market stalls had drawn inward from the drowned-prone edges toward the stone core around the factor's house, and the space they abandoned filled immediately with brown water that reflected the lights back up at the sky so generously it looked, for one vertiginous moment each evening, as if the town had multiplied — two towns, one above and one below, mirror twins separated by a skin of floodwater.

At the top of the walkway she stopped, because ahead of her the notice board was lit by its own small lantern, and a handful of townsfolk stood before it in the damp, reading.

Ngozi had read the notice four times already. She knew it by heart — the departure date, the tide requirements, the stern instruction that baggage was limited to what one person could carry, the list of names arranged by household. She could have recited her own family's line like scripture: *Oyelaran, Adaeze; Oyelaran, Tamuno (elder); Oyelaran, Jude; Oyelaran, Ngozi.* Four names. Four berths. It fit.

But the crowd was murmuring in that particular tone, and one of the fishwives was saying, loudly, "Then where's his name? Show me his name on the paper," and Ngozi's feet carried her forward before her mind agreed to move.

She reached the board. She found the Meridian Trading Company's seal, and the date, and the tide tables, and the list.

She found *Oyelaran, Adaeze.* She found *Oyelaran, Tamuno (elder).* She found *Oyelaran, Ngozi,* in her brother's neat, upright, unmistakable clerk's hand — the hand he used for the company's figures, the hand that had copied out her examination practice sheets for two years, the hand she trusted more than her own.

Below it, where a fourth name should have been, there was a blank space the width of one line.

No *Oyelaran, Jude.*

She read it twice, as the fishwife had demanded, as everyone demanded, as if repetition could alter ink. The blank space did not fill. The lantern above the board hissed softly in the river damp. Somewhere behind her, the floodwater mirrored the town back at itself, twin upon twin, and Ngozi Oyelaran stood very still with a teacher's reference against her heart and her brother's handwriting all around the hole where his name should be — and understood, all at once, with the terrible clarity of a sum finally done correctly, that he had known.

That he had known all week.
