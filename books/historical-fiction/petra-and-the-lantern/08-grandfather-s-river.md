## 8. Grandfather's River

Old Tamuno did not knock. He never had.

Jude heard him first as a change in the rhythm of the divers' quarter — the dogs that always announced strangers saying nothing, the neighbors' voices dipping low and then rising again in the particular hush reserved for the very old and the very stubborn. Then the door swung open on its rope hinge and there he stood, framed against the last grey light of evening, a shape like a tree that had learned to walk upright out of sheer refusal to fall.

"You are packing," Tamuno said. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation either, though Jude felt the weight of both trying to attach themselves to the words and failing.

"Orders came today," Jude said. "The barge. Four berths."

"I know what the orders said." Tamuno's eyes, still sharp as awls beneath lids gone soft as wet leather, moved across the small room — the bundle of clothes tied with cord, the tin plate wrapped in cloth, the single book Jude owned with its cracked spine. "I know also what they did not say. But we will come to that." He turned without waiting for an answer, because Tamuno had never once waited for an answer in his life. "Bring nothing. Come."

"Where?"

"The river."

Jude looked at his mother, who was grinding pepper at the stone in the corner, her back to them both, her shoulders set in the line they took when she was listening to everything and conceding nothing. Adaeze did not turn around. She only said, quietly, the way one passes a verdict down through generations: "Go with your grandfather, Jude. The rice will keep."

* * *

They walked down through the quarter as the lamps were being lit, past the cook-shops where smoke rose straight up into windless air, past the company store with its shutters drawn and its painted sign already flaking — MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY, ESTABLISHED BY GRACE AND ENTERPRISE — past the mouth of the alley where the flood-stain on the walls made a brown tideline like the memory of some older drowning. Tamuno walked with a stick but did not seem to lean on it; it touched the ground more as punctuation than support, a period at the end of each step. People greeted him as they passed — *Papa Tamuno*, *master*, *old father of the water* — and he answered each greeting with the same slight dip of the chin, a king acknowledging tribute he no longer needed.

The bridge loomed ahead of them, black against the bruised sky, its pilings already standing thigh-deep in the swollen brown water. Jude had crossed it ten thousand times and had never once looked at it properly. Tonight it seemed less a bridge than a jaw, the floodwater running between its teeth with a low continuous grinding sound, like something enormous chewing slowly and without appetite.

"Not the bridge," Tamuno said, reading him. "We go below it. We go where your father went."

They followed the bank downstream, along a path that existed mostly as habit, through reeds tall enough to whisper overhead, until the town fell behind them entirely and there was only the river, wide and dark and moving with the patient certainty of something that has never once been late for anything. Here the bank opened into a shelf of smooth clay, and here — Jude knew it before his feet told him, knew it in his lungs — was the diving place. The deep hole. His father's hole.

Tamuno sat down on the clay with the economy of a man folding himself away for the night, laid his stick across his knees, and looked out at the water.

"Sit," he said. "And take off your shirt. You will want to breathe properly."

"Papa—"

"Sit, boy. I am old. My evenings are numbered, and I do not intend to spend this one listening to you argue."

Jude sat. He removed his shirt. The air was warm and thick, river-wet, carrying the smell of mud and fish and the green rotting sweetness of the reeds. Somewhere far off, a drum was playing — festival practice, maybe, or grief, or both; in this town the two rhythms had always been close cousins.

For a long time Tamuno said nothing. He simply watched the water, and Jude watched him watching it, and understood that he was being taught something already, though no words had been spoken. This was how his grandfather had taught everything: first you watched, then you waited, then, if you were worthy, you were told.

At last the old man spoke.

"Do you know why your father dove?"

"To pay the debts," Jude said. It came out flat, automatic, the answer of a man reciting a lesson rather than feeling it. "To clear the bond. For us."

"That is why he *said* he dove." Tamuno's voice was mild, almost conversational, which is how Jude knew the blow was coming. "A man says many things about why he does what he does. Mostly to himself. Your father dove for the debt, yes — the debt the company held over our family like a foot on the neck. But underneath that, deeper than that, where the light doesn't reach — he dove because the water had him. The way it had me. The way," and here the old eyes slid sideways, catching Jude and holding him, "it is beginning to have you. You feel it even now. Don't you. Sitting here at the edge of the hole, your breath has already gone long and slow. Your body is doing arithmetic it learned before you were born. That is not choice, boy. That is inheritance."

The drumbeat carried faintly across the water. Jude found that his chest was indeed moving in the old rhythm — four counts in, seven held, six out — the diver's count, the count his father had breathed into the house itself, so that even Ngozi, who had never once put her head under floodwater, exhaled like someone letting go of something heavy.

"He died down there," Jude said. "In that hole."

"He died in the company's service, in the company's water, diving the company's beds, to pay a debt the company counted." Tamuno picked up a piece of broken shell from the clay beside him, turned it in his fingers. "You want to know the true thing? The thing nobody says at funerals because the dead cannot be embarrassed and the living cannot bear it? Your father was the best diver this river has seen in forty years. Better than me, God forgive me for saying it, and I was better than my father. And the company knew it. And the company made sure — oh, so gently, so reasonably, so kindly, with such warm words and such fair faces — that the debt was never quite cleared. Always another season. Always another advance against the next catch. Always interest, compounding like silt, settling layer upon layer until the original loan is buried so deep under its own weight that no living man remembers what was borrowed or why." He tossed the shell into the water. It made hardly any sound at all. "Debts like that do not die with men, Jude. They pass down. Like land. Like names. Like breath."

"I know," Jude said. "I carry it. Seven years bound. Two left."

"Two years left by their counting." Tamuno said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Their counting has been generous to them for three generations. Tell me — in all your years among their ledgers, sweeping their floors, carrying their crates, being so useful and so unseen — have you ever once been allowed to see the whole of an account? Beginning to end? Your father's bond, written entire?"

Jude started to say yes — he had handled the registers, transcribed entries, fetched and carried the books themselves a hundred times. And then he stopped, because the honest answer assembled itself in him with the slow cold click of a lock turning: he had seen pages. Fragments. Whatever page Calloway's hand happened to open. Never the account entire. Never the sum of his family's life laid out in one unbroken column.

"No," he said.

"Mm." Tamuno nodded, unsurprised, unsurpriseable. "When your father died, I went to the counting house myself. Old Calloway's father was factor then, may the river deal with him as he dealt with us. I asked to see the bond. All of it. He smiled at me — they always smile, it costs them nothing — and he said, *Papa Tamuno, the figures are complex, you would find them tiring.* Tiring! I had held my breath longer than his whole career. But I was old, and grieving, and my son was three days drowned, and I let it go. I let it go." The stick came up off his knees and struck the clay once, sharply, a sound like a door closing. "That is the shame of my life. Not that I could not read the ledger. That I did not demand to."

* * *

The moon came up over the far bank, thin and white as a fish scale, and the river changed under it — the darkness breaking into moving silver, the current suddenly visible everywhere, the whole surface alive with direction. Tamuno rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and stood at the edge of the shelf with his feet in the last inch of dry clay.

"Now," he said. "Before anything else — before berths, before barges, before whatever the company is planning this season with its lists and its notices — you will dive. Once. For me. For your father. Not for Meridian. Do you understand the difference?"

Jude stood too. His heart was going hard, but his breath — treacherous, faithful, inherited — had already begun to deepen. "There's nothing down there to bring up."

"There is everything down there to bring up." The old man pointed at the water with his chin. "Your father's last catch was never weighed. His last day's work was never entered in any book. It sits down there in the dark, unpaid, unrecorded, belonging to no one. Go and stand where he worked. Feel the bottom he felt. Come back up breathing, and you will know something about yourself that no ledger can give you and no ledger can take away."

"And if I don't come back up?" It came out quieter than Jude intended. "Like him."

"Then you will die free," Tamuno said simply, "which is more than the company ever intended for any of us. But you will come back up. You are my blood. Now — four counts in. Hold. And when the fear comes, and it will come, riding up your spine like cold water — you greet it. You do not fight it. Fear is just the body asking whether you are sure. Be sure."

Jude walked to the edge. The clay gave softly under his bare feet. Below him the water lay black and total, holding the moonlight only at its skin, and somewhere down inside that darkness was the bottom, and the beds, and the place where his father's lungs had filled, and the place where — if Tamuno was right, and Tamuno was always right about the river — something of his father remained unclaimed.

He thought of the notice nailed to the warehouse post that morning. FOUR BERTHS. He thought of Ngozi's face lighting at the word *capital*. He thought of his mother's hands counting rice grain by grain. He thought, unwelcome and unavoidable, of a paper lantern with Miriam Calloway's laugh caught inside it like a moth.

He breathed. Four counts. Held. Seven.

Then he bent his knees and went into the river like a coin returning to the purse that minted it.

* * *

The cold took him first, then the pressure, then the silence — that vast, upholstered silence of deep water, which is not an absence of sound but a presence of something else, something listening. Jude sank with his arms out, equalizing his ears the way his father had taught him at eight years old in the shallows, *pinch and swallow, pinch and swallow, the river hates a full head*. The moonlight lasted longer than he expected, a silver ceiling above him that thinned and shredded as he dropped, and then there was no light at all, only the black, and his own heartbeat, enormous, filling the world.

Down. Down. His fingers trailed through water colder than the surface promised. The bottom came up to meet him the way it comes up in dreams — gradually and then all at once — and when his feet found it, the mud closed over his ankles with a grip that was almost gentle, almost personal, the river holding him the way a creditor holds a debtor's hand: warmly, firmly, and with every intention of keeping him.

He stood in his father's workplace. He could not see it, but he knew it — the slope of the hole, the gravel bar where the shells gathered, the current running steady from the north channel. His chest began to burn. Three counts of fear rode up his spine exactly as promised, and he greeted them, and they settled, and in the settling there was a moment — brief, unmistakable, terrifying and tender at once — when he felt the bottom as his father must have felt it: not as a grave, not as a debt, but as ground. Solid. Knowable. A place a working man stands while he works.

*I'm here,* he thought, into the black, into the listening. *I came.*

The river said nothing, which was its way of saying everything.

His lungs demanded. He kicked off the mud and rose, and the silver ceiling grew, and broke, and he came up into the warm loud world gasping like a landed fish, and Tamuno's hand was already there, closing on his arm, hauling him onto the clay, and the old man's face above him was wet-eyed and furious and proud, all three at once, in proportions Jude would spend the rest of his life trying to name.

"You stayed down too long," Tamuno rasped.

"I stayed down long enough."

"Yes." The old hand tightened. "Yes, you did. Your father would have done the same. That is what frightens me about you, boy."

* * *

They sat drying in the moon-heat, shirts spread on the clay, and the talk turned — as it always turned, eventually, in Tamuno's presence — to accounts.

"When the barge goes," the old man said, staring at the water, "your mother will tell you to take her berth. Or the girl's. She will dress it up as sense — an old woman, a dead-end town, a young man with two years left on his bond and a strong back. She will make it sound like arithmetic. Do not let her. Arithmetic is how the company talks. It is how they got your father into the water and how they mean to keep you on the bank. Five souls, four places — that is not arithmetic, Jude. That is design. Somebody counted, and somebody chose, and I promise you their choosing did not cost *them* anything."

"How do you know the list was—" Jude stopped. He had not told anyone yet. He had not admitted it even to himself in daylight. At dawn tomorrow he would go back to the counting house, back to the ledgers, and look again, properly, and confirm what his eye had caught and refused for a full minute to believe: that his own name, present on the draft allocation posted at noon, had been struck from the fair copy by evening — struck cleanly, in Calloway's own tidy ink, as though it had never been written at all.

Tamuno did not answer immediately. He reached down, took a handful of river mud, and worked it slowly between his palms, shaping it without looking at it, the way he had shaped weights and plugs and offerings his whole long life.

"Because I have watched this company for seventy years," he said finally, "and it has never once done a kindness that was not also a chain. Because your grandmother was promised passage north in the year of the smallpox, and her name was on the list, and the barge left without her. Because I am old, and old men learn that the water is not the only thing in this town that drowns people." He let the mud drop from his hands back into the hands of the river. "Whatever you saw in those books, boy — and I can smell a secret on you the way I smell rain — you look again. Look twice. Look until you understand that a ledger is not a record. A ledger is a weapon that has been lying on a table for three generations, waiting for someone from our side of the river to pick it up."

The drum across the water had stopped. In its absence the river's voice seemed louder — that low, ceaseless, grinding patience.

"Come," Tamuno said at last, taking up his stick. "It is late, and your mother will pretend she was not awake the whole time, and I am tired in a way sleep does not fix." He paused at the top of the bank, a black shape against the lesser black of the sky, and looked back once at the water. "Two years left, you said. By their counting."

"By their counting."

"Then hear an old man's last lesson." The awl-bright eyes found Jude's and held them. "Learn to count better than they do. Learn their numbers so thoroughly that you can find the lie inside them — because I swear to you, on your father's name, on this river, there is a lie in those books, and it has been compounding longer than you have been alive. Find it. And when you find it—" the stick tapped the clay, once, a period, a door closing, "—remember whose hands are holding it. Ours. Not theirs. Never theirs again."

They walked back up through the reeds toward the lamplit town, the old man and the grandson, and behind them the river went on speaking in its language of debt and patience, and ahead of them the counting house sat dark on its street, full of sleeping paper, waiting for morning.
