## 10. A Lantern Between Us

Miriam Calloway had learned to read her father's house the way other people read weather. The creak of the third stair meant he was awake and worrying about freight. The smell of burnt sugar from the kitchen meant the cook was grieving again, quietly, in the only language she had. And a light burning past midnight in the counting house — that meant trouble, or ambition, or both, wearing each other's clothes.

She saw it from her window, that thin gold seam beneath the counting house door, and she told herself she was only walking to check on the ledgers because her father would want them accurate before the audit. She told herself a great many things on the short walk across the compound, her shawl pulled tight against the river damp, her slippers darkening with dew. The moon was a sliver over the water, and the frogs had gone quiet in the way they did before rain, and Miriam walked through all of it toward a door she had no honest reason to open.

She opened it anyway.

Jude Oyelaran sat at the long clerk's table with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, three lamps lit around him like small suns, his pen moving in the careful, unshowy hand she had first noticed months ago on a shipping manifest. He did not look up. "If you've come to tell me the second lamp is wasteful," he said, "Mr. Calloway already told me. Twice."

"I've come to tell you your ink is going to blot."

He looked up then, and she watched him do the thing he always did — the quick arithmetic of her presence, weighing what it cost him to be seen with her against whatever it was he felt when their eyes met. She hated that arithmetic. She hated more that she understood it perfectly, having done it herself since she was twelve years old, when she'd first understood that her father's name opened some doors and welded others shut.

"You shouldn't be here," Jude said. It was not unkind. It was the voice of a man reading a list he'd memorized.

"Neither should you. Yet here we both are, being improper." She hung her shawl on the back of a chair as if she owned the chair, as if she owned the room, as if she were not the factor's daughter trespassing in the one place her father guarded like a wound. "What is it tonight? The pearl consignments?"

"The berth allocations." Something crossed his face when he said it — fast, gone, like a fish under brown water. "For the evacuation."

"Ah." She took the chair opposite him, uninvited, and pulled a ledger toward herself just to have something to do with her hands. "My father has been chewing his pen over those for a week. The board wants the barge full but not overloaded, the manifest balanced but not conspicuous, everyone accounted for except—" She stopped. She had been raised in a trading house; she knew how sentences ended even when they weren't finished.

"Except whom?" Jude asked, mild as milk.

"Everyone," she said, too quickly, and reached for the book in front of her instead of his eyes.

* * *

They had met properly at the lantern market, though Miriam suspected — the way she suspected tides, the way she suspected her own heart — that they had been circling each other for years without either admitting it. She was the factor's daughter, educated past usefulness, fluent in three languages and none of them the language of her own wanting. He was the indentured boy who shelved crates and vanished into walls. She had bought him a paper lantern as a joke between classes — *so you can find your way out of my father's warehouse* — and he had laughed, actually laughed, and the sound had rearranged something in her chest that had never quite settled back into its old position.

Since then there had been a slow accumulation of moments: conversations by the loading dock disguised as inventory questions, a shared umbrella during an unexpected squall, the time he'd corrected her Latin pronunciation and she'd let him, which was itself a kind of confession, because Miriam Calloway did not let anyone correct her.

Her governess had once told her that love, in families like hers, was less a feeling than a negotiation. She hadn't understood until now.

"Do you know what I was reading today?" she said, to fill the silence that had begun to hum between them. "A novel. An English novel, smuggled in a crate of calico, which tells you everything about what my father thinks of novels. There's a heroine who runs away to the continent and becomes someone entirely new."

"And does she?"

"She drowns in the second act." Miriam smiled despite herself. "The author lacked imagination. Or honesty. One of the two."

"Both, probably." Jude set down his pen. In the lamplight his face had the stillness she'd come to think of as his true expression — not blankness, exactly, but a held breath, a man perpetually deciding how much of himself to spend. "In the stories my grandfather tells, nobody runs anywhere. They dive. They go down into the water and bring things up. That's the whole plot."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is. He says the water keeps what it's given and gives back what it owes, and a diver's whole life is learning the difference." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to the table's edge, gripping it, and Miriam thought: *he is talking about himself, he is always talking about himself, only sideways.*

"Jude." She said his name plainly, without title, without the softening irony she usually wrapped around dangerous words. "What did you see in the berth allocation?"

The lamps flickered. Somewhere outside, the river moved in its sleep, vast and indifferent.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because you're copying the same page twice. Because you haven't turned a page in twenty minutes. Because I grew up watching men read this company's paper, and I know what it looks like when the paper has bitten them."

He almost smiled. "You make it sound alive."

"It is alive. My father feeds it every day." She heard her own bitterness and was surprised by it, the way you're surprised by a stone in your shoe you've been walking on so long it's become part of your foot. "I'm not a fool, Jude. I know what Meridian is. I've known since I was small enough to hide under these tables. I used to listen to the clerks talk about bonds and berths the way farmers talk about weather — like forces of nature, like things that simply happen to people. And then I grew up and realized the weather had hands."

Jude was looking at her strangely now, the held-breath stillness cracking into something rawer. "You shouldn't say such things."

"To you? Of all people?" She leaned forward. "Who else is safe to say them to? The clerks who depend on the company? My father, who *is* the company? The young men who call on me and discuss tonnage as courtship?" Her voice dropped. "Do you know what my future is, Jude Oyelaran? It's been drafted. Like a contract. There are terms. There is a party of the second part — a company heir, pleasant, ambitious, already balding at twenty-six. My father is pleased. The board is pleased. Everyone is pleased except the goods being transferred."

The silence stretched. Jude's pen lay where he'd abandoned it, a small dark bone on the pale page.

"You could refuse," he said at last.

"Yes," she agreed. "And the heroine could swim."

* * *

It was past the middle hour when she finally said the thing she had walked across the compound to say, though she had not known it until the words were already leaving her, the way a diver doesn't decide to surface but simply finds the air arriving.

"Let me speak to my father."

Jude went very still. Stiller than still — the stillness of a man hearing water rise behind a door.

"About the berths," she pressed on, because now that the door was open she could not stop the flood. "About the allocation. If your family's name has been moved, or struck, or — adjusted — I can speak to him. He listens to me more than he admits. He calls me his conscience, which is his way of saying I'm inconvenient, but he listens. I could—"

"No."

The word came out flat and final, and it stung more than she expected, because she had imagined gratitude, had rehearsed it, had built a small warm structure of it on the walk over and now watched Jude dismantle it with one syllable.

"You don't even know what I'd say."

"I know what it would cost." His voice stayed level, but his knuckles were white on the table's edge. "Miriam. Look at me. If you go to your father asking favors for an indentured clerk's family, what happens? He starts asking why. He starts watching. And everything you have — your standing, your name, the future you just described hating — it all gets weighed against me, and I lose. Not because I'm worth less. Because the scale belongs to him."

"That's coward's arithmetic."

"It's survivor's arithmetic. There's a difference, and the difference is who's still breathing in five years." He exhaled, and some of the anger went out of him, replaced by something that frightened her more: tenderness. "You offered to spend yourself for me. Do you understand that no one has ever offered me anything that cost them? People give me what's left over. What's discarded. You'd be giving me the coin out of your own purse."

"Then let me."

"No." But softer now, almost a plea. "Not yet. Promise me you'll wait. Promise me you won't go to him unless I ask."

"And when will you ask?"

He looked at the ledgers, at the lamplight, at the door behind her where the night waited with all its consequences. When he answered, his voice was so quiet she had to lean across the table to catch it.

"When I know what I'm willing to pay."

* * *

She left before the sky lightened, because leaving was the discipline her mother had taught her — leave while the leaving is sweet, before the world wakes and prices everything. At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, and looked back at him bent over the false and true pages of the company's books, this boy who measured his life in what could be carried, who had just refused the one gift that might have saved him because saving him might drown her.

"Jude," she said. "The heroine in my novel. Before she drowns, she says something. She says: *better the deep water than the shallow life.*"

He looked up. In the lamplight his eyes were the color of the river at dusk, holding everything and giving back nothing — or so she thought, until he said, "Your author stole that from a diver."

And Miriam walked home through the wet grass with her heart beating like oars against a current, knowing two things with terrible clarity: that she loved him, and that love, in her world, was a debt like any other — entered in a ledger, accruing interest, waiting for someone to be made to pay.

Behind her, the counting house lamp burned on. And somewhere in the town below, the river rose a finger's width in the dark, patient as paper, keeping its accounts.
