## 11. Calloway's Kindness

Mr. Calloway kept two ledgers, though only one of them was bound in leather.

The first sat on his desk in the counting house, its columns ruled in the company's own hand, every entry initialed and dated and true to the penny — or true enough that an auditor's finger, running down the page, would find nothing it wished to linger on. The second ledger lived behind his eyes. It had no columns. It had names, and beside each name a small private notation of what that name was worth: not in coin, for coin was simple and could be counted by anyone, but in usefulness, in pliability, in the quiet algebra of who owed whom and how much longer they could be made to remember it.

Jude Oyelaran occupied a full line in the second ledger. Perhaps more than a line.

Calloway thought about this on the morning the rains arrived in earnest, standing at the counting house window with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand, watching water sheet off the warehouse roofs and pool in the yard until the whole compound seemed to float like a ship at anchor. Below him, among the crates being shifted to higher ground, moved a slight figure with a barrow, directing three grown men half again his size with nothing but a lifted chin and a word placed exactly where it needed to be.

The boy was nineteen now. Calloway had watched him since he was eleven, when he'd come to the counting house door the week after his father drowned, still wearing the salt-stiff shirt he'd been diving in when the news came, holding his mother's letter of petition as though it were something alive that might die if he gripped it too hard. He hadn't cried. Calloway remembered that distinctly, because he had expected crying — expected it the way one expects weather — and instead the boy had stood there with red-rimmed eyes and a voice like a small dry stone and asked, *Who keeps my father's accounts?*

Not: *Why did he die?* Not even: *What do we owe?* But: *who keeps the accounts* — as if the answer to grief might be found in knowing where the numbers slept at night.

Calloway had shown him himself. And in the seven years since, he had never once regretted it.

He drank the cold coffee and thought: *There is no one in this town I trust more, and no one I have ever once considered treating honestly.*

The two thoughts coexisted in him without friction. That was the trick of it, the thing no priest had ever managed to make him feel guilty about. Kindness and ownership were not opposites. They were, properly administered, the same instrument — a good factor loved his people the way a good husbandman loved his stock, and the stock thrived, and the thronging of the thriving was itself a kind of gratitude, and everyone went on believing, because the belief was comfortable, that affection freely given could not also be a chain.

Calloway poured out the coffee and went down into the wet morning to be kind.

* * *

The warehouse smelled of pitch, damp canvas, and the green rot-smell of river mud tracked in on a hundred boots. The evacuation preparations had turned the great room into a country of stacked nations — rice from the interior, dyewood, coils of rope, crates stenciled MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY in letters that had begun, this season, to flake — and through this country Jude moved like a customs officer who knew every border by heart.

Calloway stood in the doorway a while before announcing himself. It was a habit he permitted himself, this small surveillance, and he told himself it was supervision, which was nearly true.

He watched Jude take a manifest from the clerk Osei without touching it — reading it upside down across the crate between them, which he could do, Calloway had discovered years ago, faster than most men read it right way up. He watched Jude catch an error — the third that morning, if Calloway's count held — a mislabeled bale of indigo that would have gone onto the barge as cotton and cost them a quarrel at the capital end. He watched him correct it without embarrassing Osei, murmuring something that made the older man laugh and shake his head, and the correction was entered, and the bale was re-stenciled, and the whole machinery ticked one tooth smoother because a bonded diver's son had put his shoulder to it.

*Two more years,* Calloway thought. *Two more years of that.*

It was not enough. He had known it for some time, in the place where he kept the second ledger, but the flood orders had brought the knowledge forward, made it urgent, given it a date. Two more years of Jude Oyelaran was a fortune walking out the gate. The boy knew the warehouses better than the warehouses knew themselves. He could smell water damage before it showed. He could look at a loading plan and see, without pencil or paper, that the weight would sit wrong and list her to port. He had taught himself figures off the company's own waste paper, and arithmetic off the tide tables, and patience off seven years of being the invisible hinge on which other men's profits swung.

A man like that did not stay bought. A man like that stayed *bound*, or he left. There was no third condition. Calloway had seen the third condition attempted twice in his career, and both times it had ended with a resignation letter written in beautiful copperplate and a warehouse inventory that took four months to untangle.

So: kindness. Administered properly, timed well.

"Oyelaran." He said it at normal volume, which in that room carried like a bell.

The barrow stopped. Three heads turned; Jude's came around last, unhurried, and there it was again — the dusk-colored eyes giving back nothing, or everything, Calloway had never decided which. He beckoned, and the boy set the barrow against a post with the care of a man setting down someone else's child, and crossed the wet boards to him.

"Sir."

"The Verity manifest. Finished?"

"Copied and checked. Osei's checking it again after me." A beat. "He'll find nothing."

Calloway smiled. It was the smile he used for board members and brides' fathers, warm and slightly rueful, a smile that said *I am a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable circumstances.* "I don't doubt it. Walk with me."

They walked. Out the side door, along the covered walkway between warehouse and counting house, where the rain came down past the eaves in grey ropes and the river beyond the palisade had climbed into the mangroves' knees and was climbing still. Jude matched his pace without appearing to think about it — another thing about the boy, another entry in the second ledger: he adapted to any man's rhythm within three steps and made the adaptation look like coincidence.

"You've heard the berth allocations are posted," Calloway said.

"I've heard."

"No complaints in your quarter? No grumbling?"

"I wouldn't hear it if there were, sir. They don't grumble at me. I'm company."

The words were flat as a plank laid over water, and Calloway, treading carefully onto it, felt it hold. *I'm company.* Said without bitterness — or with the bitterness so old and so thoroughly banked that it burned without light, like coal at the bottom of a stove. He had heard Adaeze Oyelaran's name in the town gossip: the woman who refused the company store's credit even in flood years, who paid cash and counted change aloud. Pride like that didn't vanish in the children. It went underground and grew sideways.

"Your family has berths," Calloway said. "All four. Your mother, your sister, your grandfather. Good berths — covered ones, amidships. I saw to it personally."

And here was the moment he had built the walk toward, the moment he had been composing since dawn, and he executed it as he had composed it: he stopped, under the eave where the rain made a curtain between them and the world, and he looked at Jude with all the warmth he genuinely felt, unfeigned, unforced, and he let the silence stretch just long enough for the boy to understand that something was being offered.

Then he took the paper from inside his coat.

It was a single sheet, folded once, company watermark in the corner, and it bore — in Calloway's own fair hand, the hand that ruled the columns of the first ledger — a certification of standing. *Be it noted that Jude Oyelaran, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, having served with diligence beyond expectation, is hereby recorded as in good standing, with consideration given to extension of term upon favorable terms, such extension reflecting the company's confidence and the bearer's continued merit.*

He watched the boy read it. He watched the dusk-eyes move down the page and stop — he knew exactly where they would stop — at the word *extension*. He watched the jaw tighten, a movement so small that anyone who did not know the face would have missed it entirely, and then relax, smoothed over like water closing above a dropped stone.

"You're offering me more time," Jude said.

"I'm offering you recognition," Calloway said, and meant it, and hated how easily both things fit inside the same sentence. "The audit comes in the dry season. When it passes, I intend to enter this formally. Good standing, extended term at reduced interest — you'd clear the bond a year early despite two extra years of service, if you follow the figures. Your mother would tell you the figures are honest. She counts better than my clerks."

"They're honest figures," Jude said. "On a dishonest frame."

Calloway let the rain fill the pause. He was not angry. Anger was for men who hadn't planned ahead. He had planned for this exact sentence the way he planned for the river's rise, and his reply had been waiting in him for days, patient as ballast.

"Is it dishonest," he said gently, "to want to keep the best man I have? Is it dishonest, boy, to stand in front of the board and argue — and I have argued, you may ask Osei, I argued in writing — that the company's bond system robs it of exactly the men it trains best? Seven years we spend making a man valuable, and the day he becomes valuable, the debt dies and he walks. Where is the sense in it? Where is the justice?" He spread his hands, and the rain applauded softly on the eave above them. "I cannot change the system. I am its servant as much as you are, whatever my coat says. But inside the system, inside what is allowed me — I can choose how to use what I'm given. And I am choosing to use it well by you. Extension at reduced interest. Good standing entered in the register under my own initials, which means when the audit reads it, it reads as *my* judgment, *my* protection. Do you understand what that's worth, in a house where auditors come looking for throats?"

He watched the boy watch him. He watched him do the thing he always did — the terrible, admirable, maddening thing — weighing it, actually weighing it, as though the offer might be real, as though somewhere beneath the paper there might be a floor that held.

"And if I decline?" Jude asked.

"Then nothing happens." Calloway shrugged, and the shrug was kind, and the shrug was the trap's soft lining. "Nothing at all. You serve your two years, you leave when the bond ends, the company loses a man it spent seven years building, and I write you a reference so glowing it will embarrass us both. I'm not a tyrant, Oyelaran. I'm asking. That's all this is. An ask, and a promise, and my word behind both."

Every word of it was true. That was the beauty of the instrument he had forged over thirty years in the trade: he almost never lied. Lies were for amateurs; lies left edges a auditor could catch a fingernail in. Truth, arranged in the right order, weighted at the right places, was stronger than any lie ever cast — truth that omitted, truth that framed, truth offered by a man whose warmth was real even while its purpose was a vice.

Because here was the thing Calloway did not say, the thing that lived only in the second ledger, in the space behind his eyes where the columns were invisible: the extension paper in Jude's pocket was worth nothing at all. Less than nothing. It was a promissory note drawn on a fund that did not exist. The audit would find what audits found; the register would show what the register showed; and if it became convenient — if the boy grew restless, if the family's absence across the water loosened whatever held him, if some rival factor whispered *you could be free* — then the certification of good standing would turn out to be a draft, unsigned, unentered, a courtesy shown in conversation and never in ink. And Jude would have two years added to his bond for the crime of having believed a kindness.

But that was the far shore. Today, the near shore only required that the boy take the paper, and believe — believe enough, doubt enough, but carry it — and understand, without anyone saying it, that the man who gave it could also withhold it.

Jude took the paper.

His fingers were careful with it, the way they were careful with everything, and Calloway felt the small hot satisfaction of a mechanism engaging, a ratchet clicking home one tooth.

"Thank you, sir," Jude said.

"Don't thank me. Earn it. Keep doing what you do." Calloway clapped him on the shoulder — the shoulder thin under the wet shirt, the collar frayed, the boy's whole economy of a body — and the clap was genuine, and the genuine clap was the cruelest part, and he knew it, and did it anyway. "Go on. The Verity won't load herself."

Jude went. Calloway stood under the eave and watched him cross the yard, the paper folded away inside his shirt, over the heart or near it — Calloway liked to imagine near it — and the barrow taken up again, the chin lifting, the word placed, the machinery ticking smoother.

*Two more years,* he thought. *Perhaps three, if the audit goes badly and I need a scapegrace with clean hands. Four, if the war talk from the capital turns real and the company needs men it can keep. The paper buys me all of it. The paper costs me nothing. The paper is the cheapest thing I have ever given anyone, and he will guard it like scripture.*

He felt no shame. He wanted to record that about himself, in the invisible ledger, in the margin of his own entry: *no shame*. Shame was a luxury of men whose instruments were crude. His father had been a Bristol chandler who cheated widows openly and died despised; Calloway had learned from that grave too. One cheated and was hated. The other was loved, and kept, and thanked. Given the choice between being his father and being what he was, he would choose what he was every morning until he died, and sleep soundly every night, and he did.

* * *

In the afternoon the rain eased to a fine persistent drizzle and Calloway worked through the correspondence, and it was while sealing a letter to the board — *re: seasonal disbursements, re: the audit, all in good order, all in good order* — that his daughter came into the counting house with rain in her hair and a book under her arm and her mother's mouth set in the particular line that meant she had been arguing with the world and winning.

"Papa."

"Mimi." He set down the seal. "You're dripping on the audit files."

"I'm dripping on your desk. The audit files are locked away like state secrets." She perched on the corner of the desk itself, which no clerk would dare and only she would think of, and looked at him with those eyes she'd gotten from her mother, the frank grey ones that had never learned the family art of giving back nothing. "The Oyelaran boy. Jude."

Calloway kept his face pleasant and his pen still. In the second ledger, behind his eyes, a new column opened itself without being asked, and began, silently, to rule lines.

"What about him?"

"He lent me a book. Well — he didn't lend it, I took it, he'd have been beaten for lending it, it's a company copy, Byron, can you imagine, Byron in a bonded diver's house." She was talking quickly, brightly, the way she talked when she was watching to see whether he was watching. "He knows it by heart. Papa, he *knows it by heart*, and he knows the tides, and he knows the manifests better than Osei, and today at the market everyone was talking about how he caught the indigo error before it shipped—"

"Today at the market," Calloway said mildly.

"—and I simply think it's wasteful," Miriam finished, with the magnificent obliviousness of the young, or the magnificent strategy of it; with her he could never be certain, and the uncertainty was itself a small alarm ringing in a room he kept locked. "Wasteful, and wrong, and when he's free in two years some other company will snap him up and we'll have trained him for our rivals. If you ask me you should free him now and hire him proper. Wages. A real wage. He'd stay for a real wage, men always do."

"He has a paper of good standing," Calloway said. "Extension at reduced terms. I gave it to him this morning."

Something crossed her face — quick, bright, gone — and Calloway, who had spent thirty years reading faces across bargaining tables, read it anyway: not disappointment. Alarm. Alarm, quickly buried, in the daughter who had just been arguing *for* the boy's freedom.

"Well," she said. "Good. That's — good. That's settled, then."

"That's settled," Calloway agreed.

She kissed the top of his head — she had done it since she was six, from the same corner of the desk, and he had never once told her not to — and went out through the rain with her book held under her coat, and the door closed, and the counting house was quiet except for the drizzle working at the eaves and the river, beyond the palisade, climbing.

Calloway sat for a long time with the seal cooling in its dish.

He was not a suspicious man by temperament; suspicion was inefficient, and he dealt in efficiencies. But he was a thorough man, and thoroughness, applied to the image of his daughter's face just now — the alarm in it, the *buried* alarm, buried by a girl who had never in nineteen years learned to bury anything — produced, slowly, the way sediment produces stone, a single unwelcome thought:

*That the boy had not taken the paper like scripture after all.*

*That the jaw had tightened not with gratitude struggling against pride, but with a reader finding a forgery.*

*That the second ledger ran both directions, and somewhere in this town, in a wet shirt with frayed cuffs, someone else might be keeping columns of their own.*

He dismissed it. He sealed the letter to the board — *all in good order* — and dismissed it, because dismissing it was efficient, and because the alternative thought was absurd, and because the rain was easing, and because tomorrow the Verity would begin to load, and there was work, and work was the surest anesthetic ever compounded.

But that night, alone, he took out the key to the bond register drawer and checked it — it hung where it always hung — and stood a moment in the dark counting house listening to the river rise, finger by finger, patient as paper, keeping its accounts.

And he found, to his mild surprise, that he had begun — without deciding to, the way sediment becomes stone — to wonder what, exactly, his daughter had seen in the Oyelaran boy's face at the market today, and why she had needed so many words to avoid telling him.
