## 12. The Berth Arithmetic

Adaeze Oyelaran had learned long ago that a table tells you everything about a family. Not the wood of it, not the grain, but the way bodies arrange themselves around it when something hard must be said. Her mother's table had been round, so that no one sat at the head and no one at the foot, and grief could travel in circles until it wore itself smooth. The table she had carried into this rented room in the divers' quarter was rectangular, salvaged from a trading post that had burned the year Tamuno's generation still dove without weights, and it made a head of whoever sat nearest the window. Tonight, without anyone choosing it, that place fell to Old Tamuno.

Five of them. She counted them the way she counted cups on the shelf, the way she counted days since the rains began — out of habit, out of dread, because counting was the one prayer she trusted.

Tamuno at the window end, his hands flat on the wood as if steadying a canoe. Ngozi beside him, sixteen and luminous, her schoolbooks stacked like a small city she intended to build. Kofi, eleven, too young for any of this and pretending not to know it, his knees drawn up under him. Jude across from her, his face the closed door he had worn since morning. And herself at the near end, where the lamp stood, so that its light would have to pass through her before it reached anyone else.

On the table between them lay the paper. Calloway's notice, copied fair by the company clerk in a hand so neat it looked kind. Four berths. Barge leaves on the third morning before the bridge closes. Names listed in order of priority, which was the company's word for worth.

Four berths. Five Oyelarans.

The arithmetic was simple enough for Kofi to do. He had done it already — she had seen him doing it all through supper, lips moving, eyes flicking from face to face like a boy counting who would be left standing when the music stopped. No one had said it aloud yet. That was the cruelty of tables: they held the silence as well as the food.

"Read it again," Tamuno said.

Jude read it again. His voice was steady, and Adaeze hated how steady, because she knew what it cost him. He read the names of the families above theirs, the factor's careful allocations, and then their own name — *Oyelaran* — with the number four written after it like a verdict. He did not read his own name, because his own name was not there. He turned the paper over as though the back might hold something the front had withheld.

"It says four," Kofi said, because someone had to, because children are the ones who say the thing everyone is bleeding around. "But we're five."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the shutters. Outside, the river spoke in its new voice — higher than it should be in this season, earlier than it should be, the sound of water climbing stairs that were built for dry feet.

"Yes," Adaeze said. "We are five."

She had rehearsed this evening in the hours after the market closed, standing over her rice pot, stirring what did not need stirring. She had rehearsed being calm. What she had not rehearsed was the way calm felt in the mouth — like a coin held under the tongue, cold and hard and saving nothing.

"The company has decided," she went on, "that my son is not a person this season. That is what the list means. They wrote four because they intend for us to leave him."

"Mother—" Jude began.

"Let me finish." She put her palm flat on the table, the way her own mother had done, the way women had been doing since the first flood. "I will not pretend this is weather. Weather takes whom it takes. This was written by a man with a pen, in a warm room, and he wrote it knowing exactly what he was doing. So before anyone in this house offers themselves up like a goat at market, understand: nobody here is worth less than anybody else. Not one. Not even the ones the company has decided not to count."

* * *

Ngozi had known before she came to the table. She had known since the afternoon, when she'd found the notice nailed to the company post and stood in front of it while other people's fingers moved along the lines and stopped, and looked at each other, and looked away. She'd watched Mrs. Ekwueme count her seven children against three berths and go gray in the face. She'd watched a man from the rope-walk tear the whole notice down and get shoved into the mud for it.

So she had done her own arithmetic on the walk home, and hers came out differently from her brother's, and that was the trouble with arithmetic — it depended on where you stood when you counted.

Her berth was certain. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. Not because the company valued her — the company valued nothing with her face — but because her name was attached to the scholarship, and the scholarship was attached to the mission school, and the mission school had a patroness in the capital whose letters arrived under seal. A berth had been promised in writing, in triplicate, in the language of institutions that keep their promises precisely because breaking them costs more than keeping them. Ngozi Oyelaran was going to the capital. She was going to sit in a real classroom with glass in the windows and learn chemistry, learn Latin, learn whatever they taught girls who were meant to become more than their mothers' kitchens.

And every step of that road ran over her brother's body.

She heard her mother saying *nobody here is worth less*, and she wanted to throw the words back — *then send me* — except that refusing to go would be its own violence, a different one, slower and quieter, the violence of a talent buried alive. She thought of the schoolmistress's face when Ngozi had recited the multiplication tables backward at age nine. She thought of the letter she had already begun composing in her head to the patroness. She thought of Jude teaching her letters on this same table, tracing characters in spilled rice because paper cost money, his big clumsy finger guiding her small sure one, and how he had said, *You'll write things I'll never read, little sister, and that's the best thing I'll ever make.*

He had planned this. She saw it now, looking at him across the table — the settled quality of him, the way his shoulders sat like a man already carrying his pack. He had seen the list first. He worked in the counting house; he had read it before any of them. And he had come home and said nothing, and let her spend the whole day dreaming about lecture halls, because he had already decided what the dream would cost and had agreed to pay it without consulting anyone.

That was the part that made her furious. Not the sacrifice — she understood sacrifice, she'd been raised on it, it was the family trade alongside diving and debt. It was the secrecy. It was being handed her future gift-wrapped in her brother's erasure, expected to smile and board the barge and become the person he'd spent himself to make.

"So it's decided, then," she said, and her own voice startled her with its sharpness. "Jude stays. We wave from the deck. I go to school on my brother's life. Is that the sum?"

"Ngozi," her mother warned.

"No. I want to hear him say it." She turned on Jude. "Say it. Say you're staying because the company wants an asset past the closing, and you've decided to be cooperative about your own cage."

"I'm staying because there are four berths and five of us," Jude said quietly, "and because I'm the one they'll take anyway whether we sail or sink."

"That's not a reason, that's surrender with good posture—"

"Enough." Tamuno's voice did not rise. It never rose. It came down instead, like a depth charge, and the table went still the way water goes still over something large moving underneath. "Both of you. Sit inside your anger a moment before you hand it to each other. Anger is good ballast. Thrown, it sinks the boat."

* * *

Old Tamuno had buried a son in this river and a wife in ground that would soon be under it, and he had made his peace with both graves in the only currency he owned, which was presence. He was seventy-one years old by the church register and older by the river's count, and his lungs held less air each year, and his hands ached in weather like a barometer of everything wrong. But his mind was clear. Clearer, he suspected, than anyone else's at this table, because grief had scoured him decades ago and left only the load-bearing parts.

He listened to them circle it. The mother offering to stay — absurd, and he loved her for the offer, and would not allow it, because children need their mother more than a grown man needs his grandfather, though he'd have died rather than phrase it so baldly. The girl raging because rage was the only honest response to a rigged ledger. The boy silent in the way of boys who have already grieved in private and now must perform it publicly. The small one, Kofi, watching everything with the terrible open eyes of childhood, cataloguing fear he didn't yet have vocabulary for.

They were arguing about who was most expendable. That was the company's arithmetic, and it had infected the table like bilgewater. He waited until the voices frayed, and then he put both hands flat on the wood and said:

"I will tell you how it will be, and then we can argue about something that matters."

They quieted. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"I am not getting on that barge."

"Grandfather—" Jude started.

"You will let me finish, boy. You've had your turn at martyrdom; it's my turn now, and I've had more practice." He let the ghost of a smile cross his face and vanish. "I am seventy-one. My son dove for pearls until the water took him, and his father before him, and his father before him, back to men who dove in rivers with other names. Every man in our line has given this water his breath. I am the last, and I am not finished giving. If I board that barge, what crosses? An old man with bad lungs and a head full of songs no one in the capital knows. If I stay, what remains? Someone who knows where the deep holes are. Someone who remembers which banks shift in flood season and which hold. When the water comes — and it will come higher than any of these merchants believe — the people who remain will need a diver. They will need me. And I will need a river to die in, because I refuse to die on land like a chicken with its neck wrung, far from the water that made us."

His daughter-in-law's eyes were shining and angry. He gentled his voice for her alone.

"Adaeze. You will go. You will take the children. All three." His gaze touched Kofi and stayed there a moment. "This is not negotiable, and I will tell you why, and then none of you may ever raise it again. My life is spent. Spent money buys nothing. But yours—" he nodded around the table, at the girl with her books, at the boy with his quiet fire, at the small one, at her — "yours is coin still in circulation. I will not have this family bankrupt itself to carry dead weight upstream. I am the anchor. Anchors do one thing well."

"And when the flood comes?" Adaeze asked, and her voice cracked on the second word, and he saw thirty years fall off her and there was the girl who had married his son, terrified and proud in equal measure. "Who pulls you out?"

"The same water that took your husband will decide that," Tamuno said. "I have an account with the river. It is old, and it is not settled, and I mean to be here when the final figure comes due."

* * *

The argument did not end there. Arguments like this never end; they just change shape, the way water changes shape around whatever holds it. Adaeze fought for another hour — fought for Tamuno's berth, fought for some arrangement where she stayed and sent the children, fought every configuration the numbers allowed and several they didn't. Jude sat mostly silent, and his silence was the loudest voice in the room, because everyone could see he was not fighting for his own place at all, and that refusal was worse than any argument.

It was past midnight when the shape of it finally set, like plaster, like a cast around a broken bone.

Ngozi goes — the scholarship, the future, the thing that cannot be replaced or argued with. Kofi goes — because he is eleven, and no one at this table would permit the conversation that would follow otherwise. Adaeze goes — because Tamuno invoked the authority of the oldest grief in the family and would not be moved. Tamuno stays — by his own decree, anchored to the river that owns him. And Jude—

And Jude, who was not on the list at all. Who had made himself absent so smoothly that the absence had nearly passed without comment, the way his absences always had. Invisible among crates. Useful and unseen.

Adaeze looked at her second son across the dying lamp and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the ears, that he had arranged this whole table. That his silence had been the frame and everyone else's love the picture hung inside it. He had not asked to be sacrificed. He had simply declined to be saved, and let the arithmetic do the rest.

"There has to be another way," she said, but her voice had no conviction left in it, only exhaustion wearing conviction's clothes.

"There might be," Jude said. And then, softer, almost to himself: "There has to be."

Kofi had fallen asleep against Ngozi's shoulder somewhere in the second hour, his cheek printed with the weave of her sleeve. She lifted him without waking, and his arms went around her neck with the blind trust of the small, and she carried him to the mat in the corner, and Adaeze watched her daughter do it and thought: *this is what I have made, and it is good, and it is not enough.*

One by one they withdrew to their sleeping places. The lamp burned low. Outside, the river climbed another hand's width up the pilings, patient as a creditor.

Adaeze stayed at the table last, as she always did, turning her wedding ring around her finger — the one object she owned that had never been collateral, never been weighed, never been entered in any company's book. In the back of the house, behind the loose brick where she kept the dried fish and the good thread, there was a tin. She had been putting coin in that tin for nine years, a few cowries at a time skimmed from marketing days, from mending work, from selling her mother's beads one string at a time so slowly no one noticed them going. Nine years of almost nothing, added together.

She did not know yet what she would do about the tin. She only knew that tomorrow the packing must begin — that a house of five lives lived long in one place does not fold itself into four berths' worth of luggage, that choices must be made about every bowl and cloth and photograph, that grief in this family had always been measured in objects left behind.

She pinched out the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the water rise.

In the corner of the room, in the dark, Jude was not asleep either. He was looking at the ceiling, and behind his eyes was the counting house, and the ledgers, and the particular weight of a pen in his father's hand — a memory he did not yet know he would need.

Three mornings remained before the barge. The river had already begun counting them down.
