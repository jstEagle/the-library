## 13. What Can Be Carried

The house had to be taught to let go.

That was how Adaeze thought of it, standing in the doorway at first light with her hands on her hips and the packing cloth folded over her arm like a soldier's banner. A house held things the way a river held silt — patiently, without opinion, until one day you looked down and could not see the bottom. Hers had held twenty-two years of living. It would not give them up in an afternoon.

She began where all honest work begins: with the smallest things.

The spoons went first. She counted them out of the clay pot by the cookfire — eleven, though she had bought twelve at her marriage, and the missing one had gone into the river with Chidi's funeral offerings, cast off the prow so he would have something familiar to hold in whatever water waited beyond this water. Eleven spoons wrapped in palm frond matting. Four spoons to a berth, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it, and then made herself keep thinking it, because arithmetic that is refused does not go away; it only waits, like debt, like floodwater, for the moment you are weakest.

*Five people,* she thought, tying the knot too tight. *Four berths. Do the sums now, in daylight, so they cannot ambush you at night.*

But that was not why she had come to pack today. Today she packed because if she sat still another morning she would begin to weep, and her children had never once seen her weep, and she intended to die — whenever dying came, however it came — before they did.

* * *

The house gave up its secrets room by room.

From beneath the sleeping platform she drew out the gourds: three of them, stoppered with wax and rag, holding rice, dried fish, and the pepper she had carried from her mother's compound as a bride and had stretched across two decades the way a good story stretches across a long night. She weighed each gourd in her hands. The rice was light. She shook it and heard it whisper against the shell like rain that had forgotten how to fall hard.

*Enough for nine days,* she calculated. *Ten if I cook the fish bones twice.*

Ngozi appeared in the doorway with her arms full of school books and her chin set at the angle that meant argument was coming whether anyone wanted it or not.

"Mama, take my trunk space for the food."

"The food rides loose."

"Then take my blankets. I'll sleep cold on the barge. It's only—"

"You will carry your books and your blankets," Adaeze said, "because you are going to a city where nobody will know your name, and when they ask who you are, you will say: I am Ngozi Oyelaran, daughter of Adaeze, and I arrived with my books." She took the trunk from the girl's arms and set it down between them. "Cold is temporary. Ignorance is a life sentence. Pack."

Ngozi's eyes went bright and furious, the way they always did when love caught her unprepared, and she turned back into her room too quickly for Adaeze to see what else crossed her face. But Adaeze saw enough. She had spent seventeen years learning to read her children the way old Tamuno read the river — by surface movement, by what the current dragged under.

Something was wrong with Jude. Something was wrong with the berth list. Her son had come home from the counting house the evening the notices were posted wearing a face she recognized from his father — the face Chidi used to wear coming up from deep water, when everything was fine, when the pearls were good, right up until the day it wasn't.

She had asked him once, plainly: *Jude. Is there something on that paper you have not told me?*

And he had said, looking at her with those eyes that were so much his father's and yet had learned a caution his father never needed: *Mama, everything on the paper is fine.*

Which is how she knew nothing was fine. Her children lied to her beautifully. It was, she supposed, a kind of inheritance too.

* * *

By midmorning the floor of the main room disappeared under sorted heaps, and the house began to look less like a home being emptied than a body being prepared — washed, anointed, arranged for mourning. Adaeze worked with the methodical fury that the women of her line called *sitting down properly* and the men called stubbornness and she called survival.

Tamuno sat in the corner through all of it, on his low stool, saying nothing, watching the piles grow with the expression of a man reading water he has no intention of entering. Twice she caught him touching the old diving weight that hung from the roof beam — the smooth stone his own father had cut, then Chidi's, then, by the silent law of their family, whoever dove next. He had taken it down yesterday and turned it over in his hands a long time, and then hung it back exactly where it had been, and Adaeze understood that the stone would leave this house over her dead body or his, whichever came second.

She did not ask him about his berth. He did not offer. Between them lay the whole subject, vast and clear, like the lagoon on a windless day, and neither of them stepped into it because both of them already knew what stood on the far shore. An old man does not spend his last season anywhere but beside the water that raised him. She had known it since the notices went up. She hated him for it and honored him for it in the same breath, which is, she had learned, what love becomes after fifty years of practice.

* * *

The clothing took until noon.

Her own wrappers she folded into a single bundle — six of them, and two too fine for a barge, and she put those aside anyway because a woman arrives in a new city dressed as who she intends to be, not as who the flood permitted her to be. Chidi's clothes she handled last. His diving shirt still hung on its peg by the door where she had left it the morning the company men came with their faces arranged for sympathy, and she had never moved it, because moving it would mean admitting the peg was empty, and the peg being empty would mean the door opened on a world with no Chidi in it, and she was not prepared, twenty months later, to live in such a world.

Now she took the shirt down.

It smelled of him still — salt and palm oil and the particular iron scent of deep water that clings to divers the way incense clings to priests. She pressed it to her face once, briefly, the way you touch a hot iron to confirm it is real, and then folded it into the bundle with her wrappers, flat and careful, because grief that is allowed to sprawl takes over the whole house.

*What can be carried.* That was the whole of it now, wasn't it? That was the question the company had reduced her life to, without ever saying it aloud, without ever once having to look her in the eye. Somewhere in the counting house a man with soft hands and a soft voice had drawn lines on paper, and every line said: *this may live, this must stay, this weighs too much, this is worth nothing.* Twenty-two years of her family's living, weighed by strangers against the draft of a barge.

Well. She had been weighing things her whole life too. And she would be the one to decide what survived her.

* * *

It was mid-afternoon when Jude came.

She heard him before she saw him — that was new, that was recent; her son had learned to walk loudly only since the notices went up, as if some part of him were trying to surrender the invisibility that had kept him alive this long. He came through the door with his shoulders carrying the warehouse still, dust in the creases of his knuckles, and he stopped when he saw the room, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she had done at dawn: the bundles, the empty pegs, the gourds, the silence where the cooking pots used to hang.

"It goes fast," he said. "When you start."

"It goes slow," she corrected him. "Fast is what it looks like afterward. Slow is what it is while you're doing it." She nodded at the heap of mats. "Take those to the corner. The wet-season ones stay. We'll sell the dry ones to Bassi's people — they'll need bedding for the families coming in from the lower quarter when the water rises."

He gathered the mats and then didn't move. He stood there holding rolled pandanus against his chest like a man holding a shield, and she knew that posture; she had married a diver, and divers' sons learn early that the most dangerous thing in the water is not the depth but the thing you can see approaching and cannot name.

"Mama," he said. "There's something I need—"

"Eat first." She pushed the bowl at him — the last of the yam porridge, made generous with the pepper, made generous on purpose, because whatever he had come to say deserved a mother's full strength behind it. "Whatever it is, it keeps better in a fed mouth."

He ate. She watched him eat and marveled, as she had marveled for nineteen years, at how completely this boy had assembled himself out of other people — Chidi's stillness, her own stubbornness, Tamuno's patience with water, and something else, something none of them had given him, some quality of watchfulness that belonged to Jude alone and that she suspected had been purchased at a price she had never seen paid.

Then he set down the bowl, and she braced herself, and instead of speaking he reached past her and lifted the lid of the wedding chest.

* * *

She had been saving it for last. Of course she had. Every woman packs toward her wedding chest the way a story packs toward its ending, and Adaeze had been circling it all day, doing the spoons and the rice and the shirts and the pots, letting the chest sit in the corner growing heavier with every hour, the way a truth grows heavier the longer you refuse to lift it.

Chidi's father had given her that chest. Ogbuefi, the pearl merchant's rival, the man who had laughed at the idea of his son marrying a pepper-farmer's daughter with sand in her hair — and then had shown up at the betrothal feast carrying this chest on his own shoulder, carved from a single log of iroko, its lid inlaid with a pattern of waves that his own father had cut, and had set it down at her feet and said, in front of everyone, in his great cracked voice: *"My son chose a woman who carries things well. Let her have a box worthy of what she carries."*

Inside it, twenty-two years of a life: her bridewealth beads. The cloth her mother had woven for her lying-in. Chidi's first good pearl, no bigger than a fingernail, imperfect, which he had given her instead of selling because he said imperfection was the only honest thing the water ever produced. The letters from Ngozi's first teacher, recommending her for the capital schools. The small tin where the coin lived — and here her hand paused, here her heart performed its private accounting — the small tin where eleven years of saved coin lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the beads, coin she had hidden even from Tamuno, coin scraped from the margins of a life the company owned outright, coin that existed for a purpose she had never spoken aloud because speaking it might summon someone to take it.

One bond. That tin could buy out one indenture. She had done the sums a hundred times in the dark. Not two. Never two.

"Leave the chest," Jude said quietly.

She looked up at him. He had crouched beside it, and his hand rested on the wave-carved lid the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal, and his face had gone very still — the diver's stillness, the stillness before the breath goes down and does not come back for a long time.

"It comes," she said. "However we travel, whatever we carry, it comes. I did not raise my children above an empty chest."

"Mama—" He stopped. Started again, in the careful voice he used for the factor's ledgers, the voice that measured every word before spending it. "If it comes to choosing. If the space isn't there. If—" and here his voice failed him for the first time, just a hairline crack, and she saw the boy inside the ledger-keeper, the boy who had been carrying something for days and whose arms had finally begun to shake. "If the list changes. If there aren't berths for everyone the way the paper says. Then the chest stays, and the clothes stay, and you get on that barge with Ngozi and Grandfather and you don't argue, because I will not have my mother arguing with a company clerk over furniture."

The room went very quiet. Outside, a kingfisher struck the water and came up flashing.

Adaeze set down the beads she had been holding. She looked at her son — this invisible boy, this useful boy, this boy who had spent his whole childhood making himself small enough to survive — and she understood suddenly, completely, with the sick clarity of a woman who has read water all her life, what he was telling her without telling her.

"You're not on the list anymore," she said. "Are you."

It was not a question, and he did not insult her by pretending otherwise. He only lowered his eyes, and that was answer enough, and the answer hit her like the cold layer of water that waits beneath the warm — the depth you don't feel until you're through it, and then it holds you and will not give you back.

"When did you find out?"

"Mama—"

"*When.*"

"Nine days ago."

Nine days. Nine nights of him eating her food, carrying her mats, watching her count rice, knowing. Knowing, and folding himself around the knowledge like cloth around broken glass so that no one would bleed. She thought of all the mothers in the lower quarter who would never know until the barge pulled away. She thought of the factor's soft voice. She thought of the tin in the chest, and the sums, and the word *one*, and how her son was already, had perhaps always been, arranging himself into the shape of a sacrifice — quietly, competently, invisibly, the way he did everything.

"No," she said.

"Mama, listen—"

"I said *no*." She rose, and the whole room seemed to rise with her, every bundle and basket and folded shirt attending. "You will not decide alone what this family loses. You did that with the ledger, whatever it was you found. You did it with the list. You have spent your entire life deciding things alone because you believed no one would notice you deciding — and I am telling you now, Jude Oyelaran, son of Chidi, grandson of Tamuno, that I noticed. I have always noticed. Invisibility was your craft. It was never your nature."

His jaw worked. His eyes were wet and furious and young. "And what would you have me do?" he said, and his voice broke fully open this time. "Tell Ngozi she can't have her school? Tell Grandfather he has to leave the river? Tell you—" He stopped himself, but the sentence had already escaped, and they both stood looking at it lying there between them. *Tell you to leave me behind.*

Adaeze crossed the space between them and took her son's face in both hands, the way she had when he was small and fevered, and she made him look at her.

"We are not done," she said. "Do you hear me? This family is not a sum that ends where the company's paper says it ends. Whatever they've written, whatever they've stolen, whatever you're planning in that beautiful secretive head of yours — we are not finished. Now help me close this chest. We are taking everything we can carry, and then we are going to find out what Elder Bassi knows about companies that strand their workers, because that old man has been watching Meridian longer than either of us has been alive, and he has been trying to catch my eye for three days."

Jude went still. "Bassi? What has Bassi said?"

"He hasn't said anything," Adaeze said. "That's what frightens me. Old men who talk are harmless. Old men who wait—" She snapped the chest shut, and the wave-carved lid closed over the beads and the letters and the tin of secret coin like water closing over a diver. "Old men who wait know something. Go and find out what."
