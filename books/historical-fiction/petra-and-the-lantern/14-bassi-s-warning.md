## 14. Bassi's Warning

The rain had taught Elder Bassi to count in a new way. All his life he had counted like a trader — coppers and cowries, sacks and souls — but forty years of watching the water rise had given him a second arithmetic, one that measured not what a man owned but what a man would lose, and in what order, and how fast. When the river came up two fingers on the bridge pilings, you lost the low paths first. Another hand-span, the goat pens. A forearm, the ground floors of the divers' quarter, which was why the divers' houses stood on stilts older than anyone living, their legs black with water-stain like the legs of herons standing in a marsh that never ended.

Bassi sat under the awning of his compound on the morning after the evacuation notice went up, counting losses before they happened. He was old enough that the young called him Elder without irony and old enough that it no longer pleased him. His knees ached with the damp. His eyes were still good. From where he sat he could see the bridge — the great wooden spine of the town, its planks darkened, its rail posts drinking the floodwater with an appetite that would end, this season or some future season, in catastrophe — and he could see the company noticeboard beyond it, where Calloway's clerk had nailed the list of berths under glass so the rain could not unmake it.

A crowd had gathered there at dawn. By midmorning it had thinned to the ones who read slowly, or who hoped that reading twice might change what was written. Bassi knew better. Paper did not change because you stared at it. Paper changed only when someone with power wanted it to change, and then it changed all at once, like weather.

He saw the Oyelaran boy come out of the counting-house lane with his shoulders carrying that particular set — the set of a man who has just read something twice and understood it differently each time.

Jude Oyelaran walked the way his father had walked, Bassi thought, which was to say he walked as though trying to take up less room than a man is owed. Kazeem Oyelaran had been the finest diver on this stretch of the river, a man who could sit on the bottom of the channel for the length of three prayers and come up calm, and even so he had moved through the town like someone apologizing for the space his lungs required. Indenture did that. It taught a man to fold himself small enough to fit inside a ledger line.

Bassi lifted two fingers off the arm of his chair — the smallest gesture, the kind an elder uses when he does not wish to be seen summoning — and the boy's eyes caught it across the lane the way a fish catches light.

"You," Bassi said. "Come and sit where the rain isn't."

* * *

Jude came because elders were not refused, and because refusing would have required him to explain himself, and he had nothing left in him for explanations. He ducked under the awning into the smell of Bassi's compound — woodsmoke, dried fish, camphor rubbed into old joints — and took the stool offered to him at the edge of the dry circle, the guest's place, close enough to the rain-veil to leave again quickly if he needed to.

"You read the list," Bassi said. It was not a question. Nothing about Bassi was ever quite a question; everything arrived already knowing its own answer, wrapped in the grammar of courtesy.

"I read the list."

"Twice?"

Jude looked at him then. Rain drummed the awning above them, a sound like ten thousand small fingers asking to be let in.

"How do you know I read it twice?"

"Because everyone reads it twice." Bassi poured palm wine from a clay flask into a cup and pushed it toward the boy without ceremony. "Once hoping, once checking. The hopeful reading and the frightened reading. They give different numbers." He drank from his own cup. "What did they give you?"

Jude turned the cup in his hands without drinking. "Four berths," he said. "My mother, my sister, my grandfather, and—" He stopped. The name that should have come fourth was his own, and it was not there, and saying so aloud to an elder felt like handing over the last coin in his pocket.

"And the fourth berth belongs to a man named Okoro, who works the bale-yard and owes the company eleven months of wages he will never repay," Bassi said mildly. "I know the list. I helped carry the beam for this awning before your father was weaned, boy. Do you think I don't know every name on that board?"

"The company said allocations were made by—"

"By need, by service, by seniority." Bassi waved the official liturgy away like smoke. "They say many things. In sixty years I have watched them say them." He settled back in his chair, and the rain filled the pause for him, patient and total. "Sit. Drink. You will want your hands steady for what I have to tell you, and wine steadies better than courage. Courage shakes. Wine only warms."

Jude drank. The wine was sharp and slightly sour, the way it always was this early in the season, and it did warm him, in the chest, where fear lived.

"Now," Bassi said. "You know why the bridge will close."

"Water."

"Water. And when the water closes the bridge, the last barge goes downriver, and after the barge, the town shrinks to its lantern-lit core, and the company keeps a skeleton staff through the flood months because the warehouses must be watched and the records must be kept dry and someone must be there in the drowned quarter to hold back looters, or so they say." His eyes were half-closed, an old man's economy, but Jude felt the full weight of them anyway, behind the lids. "And every flood season, boy, the skeleton staff is drawn from the bonded. Never from free men. Free men can walk away into the bush and be lost. Bonded men cannot run without becoming thieves in the eyes of the law, hunted men, their bonds sold onward at a discount to whoever buys trouble cheap."

"My family is going on the barge," Jude said.

"Yes."

"So whatever they ask of me through the flood months, I can bear it. Four months alone here is nothing against seven years."

Bassi opened his eyes fully then, and what was in them made the boy go quiet.

"Alone," Bassi repeated. "Is that what the list says? That you stay alone?"

"It says the warehouse watch is four men. I've seen the roster being drafted. I'm on it."

"Four men." Bassi nodded slowly, and began, in the manner of elders, somewhere far away from the beginning. "The year of the high flood — before your birth, before perhaps your mother's birth — there was a warehouse watch of five. I remember because I was one of the five." He smiled without warmth. "Yes. Even I was bonded once, boy, for a debt my father incurred buying salt he could not sell. Three years, it was meant to be. The Meridian Company has been making three years into seven since before the flag changed hands, and it will go on doing it after you and I are both silt."

"What happened to the five?"

"Ah." Bassi refilled his own cup, unhurried, a man who had learned long ago that the telling of a hard thing must be paced like the pouring of a hard drink. "That season the water came up faster than the clerks' estimates. It does that sometimes; the hills send their whole grief down at once. The lower quarter drowned by a fathom and more, and the watch was moved to the upper floors of the main warehouse, and there we stayed six weeks, eating company rice, keeping company hours." He looked into his cup. "When the water fell, four of us signed papers extending our bonds. Flood relief, the documents called it. Room and board rendered against principal. Six weeks of rice and a wet blanket priced at eight months of labor. I signed because my wife was pregnant and a hunted man cannot attend a birth. Etim signed because his mother was ill and the company doctor was the only doctor. The other two—" 

Bassi stopped. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere down the lane a door slammed, and a woman shouted at children to come inside, come inside now.

"The other two," Jude said quietly.

"Were lost to the water," Bassi said. "So the record says. One was said to have gone out at night in a borrowed canoe against orders. The other — a diver, as it happens, a good one, a man whose bond had only four months left in it — was said to have slipped from the warehouse stair during the night watches and drowned in the flooded yard. No body recovered. The river keeps what it takes; that is the one clause the company has never disputed." He drank. "His name was Nsibidi. He had a laugh like a door hinge. I think of him every time I hear this rain."

Jude's hands had gone still around the cup. "You're telling me the company killed him."

"I am telling you," Bassi said, "what I am telling you, which is all I can prove, which is nothing, which is the point. Listen carefully now, because this is the part the young never believe until it is too late to believe it usefully." He leaned forward, and the camphor smell of him sharpened, and his voice dropped beneath the rain rather than rising over it, so that Jude had to bend in to catch it. "A bonded man who drowns in flood season costs the company nothing. Less than nothing. His bond is voided by death — written off, closed, struck from the register — and no wages need be paid, no passage provided, no family compensated beyond the standard condolence fee, which is four coppers, boy. I have seen the condolence paid. Four coppers and a printed card with a verse about the river being God's ledger." His mouth twisted. "But a bonded man who lives through the flood season on company rice, in company rooms, at company prices — that man's bond grows. Every season, it grows. Do you understand what I am showing you? The company loses money when you live and loses nothing when you drown, and you are surprised that the list put you on the warehouse watch and left your name off the barge."

Jude set the cup down. His hand was not entirely steady, and he hated that Bassi saw it.

"They wouldn't," he said. "Not Calloway. He's hard, but he's — he counts things. Killing workers is bad arithmetic. Everyone knows the flood watch dies sometimes, but not deliberately, not—"

"Not deliberately." Bassi laughed then, short and dry as a stick breaking. "No. Not deliberately. Deliberately is for amateurs. The company does not push men into the water, Jude Oyelaran. The company merely arranges the world so that men fall in, and then it files the paperwork of grief with great efficiency." He held up fingers, counting, the trader's gesture returning to the old man's hand like a bird to its roost. "One: it puts bonded men, and only bonded men, on the flood watch. Two: it stations them beside water that rises past the height of a man. Three: it forbids night movement without a lantern — and lantern oil is issued by the company, rationed thin. Four: when a man is found missing, it conducts a search of exactly one day, because the current carries anything downstream within a day. Five: it extends the survivors' bonds for the cost of the rescue attempt itself." He folded his hand closed. "Five steps, none of them murder, all of them murder. I have watched this play performed four times in my life. The actors change. The steps do not."

* * *

The rain eased toward noon, dropping from a roar to a murmur, and Bassi rose stiffly and shuffled to the edge of the awning to look at the bridge, and Jude rose with him, because it seemed the thing to do.

From here, in the softened light, the bridge looked almost gentle — a long dark animal lying down in the brown water, its spine glistening. But Bassi looked at it the way other men looked at graves.

"Your grandfather," Bassi said. "Tamuno. He remembers the year of Nsibidi. Ask him sometime why he stopped diving for the company and started diving only for himself. Ask him what he found in the flooded yard the week they declared your predecessor lost." He glanced sideways. "He has never told me. Old divers keep the river's secrets and each other's. But I have watched him look at that warehouse the way I look at this bridge, and old men do not look at buildings that way unless the buildings have eaten someone they loved."

"He doesn't speak of my father's death either."

"No. He wouldn't. Grief and guilt sleep in the same bed in this town, boy, and have for generations." Bassi turned from the bridge and faced Jude squarely, and for a moment the elder's composure cracked along some old seam, and what showed through was not wisdom but fury — cold, banked, decades deep. "Now hear the warning entire, because I am old and may not get another chance to say it. The list on that board is not an oversight. Your absence from it is not clerical error. Calloway — and yes, Calloway specifically; do not let the friendly face fool you, the friendliest face in that office is the working one — Calloway has done this arithmetic before. He held the Igbo boatmen through the flood two seasons ago and extended every bond among them. He held the salt-carriers the year before that. Each time, the story is the same: the flood makes travel impossible, the bonded must shelter under company protection, protection has costs, costs accrue to principal. The flood is not the company's enemy, boy. The flood is the company's partner. It does the work of the lock and the chain without the expense of iron."

"Then what do I do?" The question came out of Jude raw, unplanned, younger than he wanted to sound. "If the list is rigged and the roster is a trap and the water is their partner — what does a man do with that?"

Bassi studied him for a long moment. Rain dripped from the awning's edge between them, a slow bead-count falling into a puddle that reflected the grey sky.

"That," Bassi said finally, "is the question I have been waiting forty years for someone to ask while there was still time to act on the answer." He reached into the folds of his robe and drew out something small, wrapped in oilcloth, and pressed it into Jude's palm — a key, green with age, heavy, unremarkable except that it was very old and very well kept. "The old counting house. The one below the present one, the flooded cellar level, the one the clerks call the dead room because nothing down there is worth stealing." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Nothing worth stealing except paper. Registers. Forty years of them, water-stained and forgotten, because no clerk ever throws away a page that might someday cover him. What is done in this town is done twice, boy — once in the open and once in ink. And ink," he closed Jude's fingers around the key, "ink floats, if you know how to make it float. Ink comes back up out of the water. I have seen it happen once. I was young and afraid and I said nothing, and Nsibidi's mother died not knowing whether her son's bond was four months from freedom or four years, because no one would show her the page."

Jude stood holding the key, the rain whispering down around them, and felt the shape of it through the cloth — small, cold, and heavier than any object he had ever carried.

"Why give this to me?" he asked. "Why now? Why me?"

Bassi lowered himself back into his chair with the groan of old wood and older bones, and looked out at the brown water climbing the bridge pilings, finger by finger, exactly as it had climbed all his life.

"Because your name is off the barge," he said. "And mine is not coming back. And because a man who has just learned the water is hungry should know, at least, where the food is hidden."

Down the lane, the company bell rang the hour, and Jude Oyelaran stood in the rain-smell of the old man's compound, turning a key over and over in his closed fist, already walking in his mind toward the counting house, where tonight the lamps would burn late — and where, he did not yet know, he would not be the only one awake after midnight.
