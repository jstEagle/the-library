## 15. Night Swimming

The shallows behind the salt sheds were not water so much as a rumor of land — the flood had come up over the mudflats and turned them into a mirror three feet deep, holding the moon the way a cupped palm holds rain. Jude waded out past the last of the shed pilings with his trousers rolled to his knees and his boots slung around his neck by their laces, and he told himself, as he had been telling himself for two days now, that this was nothing. A man might walk in the flooded flats. A man might cool his feet. The night watchman on the far side of the sheds was snoring against a bollard, and no one else in Meridian Town had any reason to look at a warehouse clerk standing knee-deep in the dark.

But his heart knew what it was doing before his mind would name it. His heart was already slowing itself, settling like a bird folding its wings, the way it had learned to do — or the way his blood remembered learning, though Jude himself had been nine years old and forbidden to go near the boats when his father last dove for pearl, when the river closed over him like a ledger closing, and did not open again.

He stood there until the cold climbed past his calves, and then he sat down slowly into the water, all at once, the way you pull a splinter — better done quick than considered.

* * *

His father's breath had been famous in the divers' quarter before Jude could count past twenty. Tamuno spoke of it the way other men spoke of saints: Oyelaran could sit on the bottom like a stone that had decided to be patient, four minutes, four and a half, hauling oysters while other men's lungs burned and hauled them up empty-handed. *The water does not take the breath from you,* Tamuno had said once, in the old man's slow grinding voice, *you give it to the water, and the water gives it back. The men who drown are the men who fight to keep it.*

Jude had never asked what it was like, the giving. He had been a child, and children believe their fathers will surface forever.

Now he filled his lungs — not the shallow chestfuls of a frightened man but low and deep, down where the belly goes hard, then a little more on top of that, the way he'd seen the pearl crews do it on the sandbars in the old years, the last gulp held in the cheeks almost. And he bent forward and slid under.

The world went green-black and silent. The floodwater was warm on top and cold beneath, two seasons layered like cloth, and the moon came through it broken into shaking coins. He opened his eyes. It stung, the river silt, but he kept them open because his father's eyes had been open, everyone said so; Tamuno said Oyelaran could see an oyster bed at eight fathoms by feel alone, but that he still looked, because looking is how you tell the water you are not afraid of it.

Twenty counts. Thirty. At forty his chest began its first complaint, a small tightening, polite as a creditor. He thought: *I am my father's son, I have his ribs, his blood, whatever it was in him that let him love the bottom.* At fifty-five the politeness ended. His body wanted air the way Ngozi wanted the capital schools, wholly, with her whole future in the wanting. At sixty-two he broke the surface gasping, and the noise of it seemed enormous in the sleeping town, and he hung onto a piling and coughed and felt, underneath the burning, something he had not felt since Calloway posted the berth list:

Usefulness. Not the company's kind — not the useful-and-unseen kind, the kind that packed crates at dawn and was thanked with silence. This was his own usefulness, discovered in his own body, at the bottom of his own inheritance. Sixty-two counts. His father had given the water four minutes.

* * *

He came back the next night, and the next after that. He told Adaeze he was walking off his appetite; she looked at him the way mothers look at sons who are lying badly and lovingly, and said only, "Don't bring the river home in your lungs." She had lost a husband to it. She counted rice every evening and she counted risks too, and she knew which of her children was spending himself somewhere after dark.

The third night the flood had risen another handspan, and the shallows behind the salt sheds were shoulder-deep where they had been knee-deep, and the current had begun to move — slow, but moving, with that patient muscular drag the Niger wears like a habit. Jude swam out past the pilings and trod water above the drowned fence-line of the old Bassi fish weir, whose tops he could just touch with his toes, and he practiced the descent.

That was the true lesson, he was finding. Not the breath — the breath came easier each night, the body a faithful clerk that learned quickly when properly instructed. The lesson was the letting-go. Every time he sank, some animal part of him clawed upward, spent its air in panic, shortened everything. You had to go down *empty* of struggle, the way you walk into Calloway's counting house with your face arranged into nothing. Invisibility, it turned out, was a skill that worked underwater. He had been training for this his whole life without knowing it.

*One hundred and ten counts* on the fourth night, hanging in the green dark with his arms loose, listening to the river work. *One hundred and thirty-eight* on the fifth, and when he surfaced Tamuno was sitting on the bank.

* * *

The old man had come without a lantern, which meant he had walked the quarter-mile from the house in full dark, over ground he had fished and dived for seventy years, and Jude understood that his own secret nights had never been secret at all. Tamuno sat on an upturned salt cask with his stick across his knees, and he did not speak until Jude had swum to the shallows and stood, dripping, in front of him.

"You dive like a boy who has read about diving," Tamuno said.

"I'm learning."

"Learning." The old man tasted the word and found it undercooked. "Your father did not learn it. He was born with the water already in agreement with him. Me also. We did not choose it. It chose us, the way debt chooses a family." He tapped the cask beside him. "Sit."

Jude sat. The water ran off him and darkened the mud. Somewhere across the flats the bridge groaned under the flood, a long wooden complaint, the sound the whole town had begun to sleep inside of.

"Two weeks," Tamuno said. "Maybe less. Then the crossing closes, and the barge goes, and the company decides which Oyelarans are cargo and which are inventory." He said it without bitterness, the way he said most things, bitterness being a luxury he had apparently spent long ago. "And my grandson spends his nights teaching himself to swim to the far side."

"If they don't take me," Jude said. "If the list—" He stopped. He had told no one what he saw in the counting-house ledger, not even Ngozi, not even his mother. The knowledge sat in him like a swallowed stone. "I want to know if I can make the crossing. That's all."

Tamuno was quiet a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he used only for the dead and the very young.

"The crossing is four miles at flood. Not four miles of swimming. Four miles of *surviving* — driftwood, snakes, whirlpools at the cut-bank, and the cold coming up from underneath like hands. Your father could have made it. Maybe. On his best day, with the river in a good mood." He looked at Jude then, straight on, and the moonlight caught the clouded white of his left eye, the one the river had taken its tithe of decades ago. "You are asking the wrong question."

"What's the right one?"

"Why the company wants you here so badly that they would rig a list for it." Tamuno rose, joints cracking like small branches. "A clerk is replaceable. I have known that since before your father was born. But they did not strike your name from carelessness, boy. Nothing in that counting house is careless. Everything is written twice, and both times on purpose." He picked up his stick. "Learn your breath if it steadies you. But don't plan to swim away from a cage, Jude. Plan to open it. Water forgives. Paper does not."

He walked back toward the quarter, a crooked shadow among shadows, leaving Jude in the shallows with the river pulling gently, patiently, at his legs — the same patience, he realized, as a debt accruing.

* * *

On the seventh night, Jude went further than he had ever gone. The flood had reached the second step of the salt-shed stairs, and the flats had become a lake, and beyond the drowned fish weir the current ran strong enough to carry leaves past him at walking speed. He swam out into it deliberately, turned downstream, and let it take him.

For a hundred counts he simply moved with it, feeling how the water held a body that wasn't fighting it — how it bore him along like something valuable, like cargo, and the thought made him laugh underwater, a burst of bubbles, his first laugh in a week. Then he turned crosswise and swam against it, testing, and the river pushed back with the calm immovable strength of Mr. Calloway reviewing a contract, and Jude learned the third lesson of the crossing: you do not beat the current, you negotiate with it, angle by angle, inch by inch, the way his mother negotiated with the company store prices every market day and lost slowly enough to keep her dignity.

He came ashore half a mile downstream, on the cut-bank below the drying sheds, coughing and elated, and lay on the mud looking up at the stars with his lungs heaving and his heart hammering out the arithmetic: *Four miles. If I rest on the driftwood. If I leave from the north point where the eddy runs slack. If the flood holds steady instead of rising.* Three ifs, and each one thin as the paper of a lantern.

He could do it. Probably. Perhaps one swimmer in five could, and the river kept the other four — Tamuno's voice said so in his head, and the memory of his father's funeral said it louder.

Then he heard footsteps on the bank above him, light ones, and a lantern came around the drying sheds, and his whole body went cold in a way the river had not managed — because he knew the walk, the quick deliberate stride of someone raised on flagstone corridors, and when the lantern swung he saw Miriam Calloway standing at the top of the cut-bank in a plain gray cloak, bare-headed, looking down at him with an expression he had never seen on her face before.

Not surprise. Something more dangerous than surprise. Recognition — the look of one invisible person finding another.

"So it's true," she said. "Old Bassi's granddaughter said someone swims the flats at night. Half the town thinks it's a ghost." She came down the bank carefully, holding her skirts up, and stopped at the waterline where the moonlight reached. Her eyes went from his soaked clothes to the far shore, measuring, the way he measured crates. "You're practicing the crossing."

It was not a question, and he found he had no answer that wasn't a confession, and the river pulled at his ankles like a hand offering to settle the matter for him. Above them, faint through the trees, the bridge groaned again — closer now, he thought, to its breaking — and Miriam wrapped her cloak tighter and said quietly,

"Then we need to talk. Because I've seen my father's papers too."
