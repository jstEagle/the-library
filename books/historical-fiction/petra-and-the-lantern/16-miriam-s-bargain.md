## 16. Miriam's Bargain

The rain had been falling for three days without conviction — not the true flood-rain that came down like judgment, but a thin, persistent grey drizzle that made the whole factor's house feel wrapped in wet wool. Miriam Calloway stood at her window on the second floor and watched it silver the surface of the compound's rain barrel, and she thought about arithmetic.

She had always been good at sums. Her father had seen to that, drilling her at the counting table when other fathers taught their daughters embroidery, saying *a woman who can read a ledger will never be robbed blind*, and she had believed he meant it as a gift. Now, watching the water rise by increments too small to see and too steady to doubt, she found herself doing a different kind of sum entirely.

One barge. Four berths for the Oyelaran family of five. One name struck from the register with two clean strokes of a clerk's pen — she had seen the list lying open on his desk three nights ago, had read it upside down while pretending to look at the shipping manifest beneath it, and she had seen the way the ink sat slightly wetter, slightly newer, where Jude Oyelaran's name should have been.

*Struck.* Not omitted. Struck. Someone had written him in and then thought better of it, or written him in so that striking him would look like mercy — like a difficult decision made in the family's favor rather than a theft committed in advance.

She had turned that page over in her mind the way she used to turn over river stones as a child, looking for what lived underneath. And underneath, cold and pale and certain, was this: the company meant to keep him. The bridge would close, the water would rise, the barge would go, and Jude Oyelaran would still be standing on Meridian soil when the flood season swallowed the lower quarter — because a diver's son who could read ledgers was worth more than a berth, and because debts did not drown.

Miriam pressed her forehead briefly against the cool glass. Below, in the courtyard, a servant was covering the firewood with oiled canvas, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this every year of his life and expected to do it every year hereafter. That was the thing about this town, she thought. Everyone here had learned to live inside the flood the way a snail lives inside its shell — carrying the disaster with them, pre-fitted, annual.

She had never learned that. She had only learned to want things quietly.

* * *

Her father took his supper at eight, alone at the long table, because her mother had been dead nine years and the house had never relearned how to set more than one place. Miriam joined him that evening without announcing it first. She wanted to catch him before the port wine loosened him into generosity and before the ledger-work stiffened him into stone, and that window, she knew from long study, was narrow.

"Father."

"Miri." He glanced up, and something warmed in his face before discipline smoothed it over. He was not a cruel man — that was the trap of him, the thing that had taken her nineteen years to understand. He was a kind man who had arranged his kindness around his interests the way a merchant arranges goods around weight and measure. "You're wet. Sit by the fire."

"I've been walking." She hung her shawl and did not sit by the fire. She sat across from him, at the place where the candlelight fell evenly, so he could not read her face in shadow and she could not hide in it either. Fair terms. She had learned that word recently, from a boy who measured life by what could be carried, and it had lodged in her like a splinter.

"You've been walking in the rain," her father said, in the tone he used for inventory discrepancies. "In the lower quarter."

The rain drummed softly on the shutters. Somewhere below, the river moved in the dark, patient as a creditor.

"The market lanterns are being taken down early this year," she said. "Elder Bassi says the bridge won't hold past the next spring tide. Half the quarter is already packing."

"So I'm told." He buttered bread he did not eat. Her father ate the way he signed documents — precisely, and only after reading everything twice. "The barge leaves on the twenty-third. The notice went up this morning. You saw it."

"I saw it." She folded her hands. "I also saw the berth allocation."

For one heartbeat — no longer — the knife in his hand stopped. Then it resumed, smooth as clockwork, and she understood that whatever came next had been prepared for, perhaps rehearsed against the dark glass of his own window, perhaps simply lived with so long that it needed no rehearsal. Her father did not improvise. Improvisation was for people without ledgers.

"And what," he said mildly, "did the allocation show you?"

"It showed five names in a household of five, and then it showed four." She kept her voice level, kept it the temperature of the room. "It showed Jude Oyelaran's name struck through in fresh ink. It showed the annotation — *retained, bond outstanding, skilled labor* — in your own hand, Father. Your initials. The little flourish you make on the C that no clerk in this house has ever been able to copy."

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the shutter and righted themselves. Her father set down the knife, aligned it parallel to the table's edge, and looked at her — and she watched him decide, visibly, between the lie and the truth. She watched him choose the truth, not because honesty was in him but because he had assessed her, in that long look, as someone who had already checked the figures.

"You read my correspondence too?" he asked.

"I read everything in this house. You taught me to."

A silence. Rain. River. The tick of the mantel clock that her mother had brought from the capital, still keeping perfect time in a town that ran on tides.

"Yes," he said at last. "His name is off the list. His bond runs another two years, and the company requires a literate clerk through the closing. When the water goes down, we'll reassess." He said it the way he might say *the shipment arrives in March* — flat, factual, already filed. "It isn't cruelty, Miri. It's arithmetic. The man is the best hand in my counting house and the only one who can be trusted with the flood inventories. If I put him on that barge, I lose two years of his bond for nothing, and the family loses nothing they wouldn't lose anyway — they'd be in the capital, poor, and he'd be indentured there instead of here. This way everyone keeps what's theirs."

"Everyone keeps what's yours," Miriam said.

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part — he didn't even flinch. "Yes," he admitted, and she felt the ground shift under her, because she had come armed for denial and he had handed her confession instead, and confession disarms differently. "That is what ownership means. I don't dress it up. I've never dressed it up for you, have I? You were raised in this trade with your eyes open."

"Were we talking about the trade?" She heard her own voice thin and pulled it back. "Because I thought we were talking about a person. A man whose father died in your company's water. Who has been paying his father's debt since he was old enough to hold a pen."

"He entered the bond freely—"

"He was twelve years old."

"—and the terms are the terms." Her father's voice hardened now, the warmth banking down to coals. "You think I invented indenture, girl? You think the Meridian Company invented it? Every cargo that leaves this river, every roof over every head in the divers' quarter, rests on bonds like his. The whole world is built on them. I manage it as fairly as it can be managed. Ask anyone in that quarter whether Calloway beats his hands, starves his hands, sells his hands downriver. Ask them."

"They'd tell me you don't," Miriam said. "And then they'd tell me about the berth list."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not guilt exactly, but the merchant's reflexive recalculation of a position gone unexpectedly expensive. She realized, watching it happen, that she had spent her whole life mistaking that flicker for conscience.

"What do you want, Miri?" he asked finally, and his voice gentled, and that frightened her more than the hardness had. "Say it plainly. You didn't come down here in the rain to quarrel bookkeeping with me. What is it you want?"

*To tear the page out,* she thought. *To write the name back in. To stand on the dock on the twenty-third and count five.*

What she said was: "I want to know why you told me. Just now. You could have lied. You almost never give anything away for free — so why this?"

He studied her across the candles for a long moment. Then he rose, went to the sideboard, and poured two glasses of the port he saved for board members and visiting inspectors, and set one in front of her, which he had never once done.

"Because I'd rather you hear it from me," he said, sitting again, "than watch you find out sideways and make a fool of us both. And because—" he turned the glass slowly under his fingers, watching the wine climb the sides— "because it occurs to me that you are nineteen years old, and in love, and about to do something foolish, and I would very much prefer to offer you an alternative before you offer yourself one."

The word *in love* dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Miriam did not move. She had grown up learning to keep her face still — it was the first lesson of the factor's daughter, earlier than letters, earlier than sums — but her heart was slamming against it like a bird against glass.

"There is nothing—"

"Miri." He said her name the way he pronounced a figure he'd caught someone fudging. "The lantern market. Twice in October. The paper lantern he carries home and hangs in the warehouse window where he thinks no one sees it. My own clerks talk less discreetly than you two imagine you're being." He took a slow sip. "I am not angry. Do you understand that? I am not even surprised. He's a remarkable young man, and you have taste, and I have always known you'd find this town small. I'm telling you the facts of your situation, the way I'd lay out any position before committing capital."

Miriam's hands were cold. She hid them in her lap and let him talk.

"In six weeks the board convenes in the capital, and Mr. Ashcombe's son will be presented to the company families. You've met Edmund. He's dull as ditchwater and heir to the northern offices, and if you take him, you will spend your life in the capital with a house, servants, a position above every woman on this river, and a husband who will never once ask where you go in the evenings." Her father's voice was warm now, genuinely warm, and that was the elegant horror of it — he meant every word as a gift. "Or you stay here. And if you stay here, you stay as the factor's daughter, and the factor's daughter does not walk in the lower quarter after dark carrying lanterns for bonded men. Nothing would happen to you, Miri. No scandal, no punishment, no scene. You would simply... remain. Comfortably. Entirely. Forever."

He spread his hands — the gesture of a man displaying wares, both options equally sound, both honestly priced.

"That's your choice. The barge goes on the twenty-third. There's a cabin kept for company family. I'll put your name on the manifest tomorrow if you say yes tonight." A pause, delicate as a scale settling. "Or you stay, and you lose nothing at all. Think of it, Miri. Either door opens. Neither one costs you anything except the other."

* * *

She walked back up the stairs slowly, holding the rail, because the house swayed faintly when she breathed and she did not trust her legs to know it wasn't the building moving.

In her room the drizzle had thickened toward real rain, and the river sound had risen to fill the whole dark — that deep, ceaseless breathing under everything, the sound she had slept inside her entire life without once truly hearing until a boy at a lantern stall had told her that his grandfather called the water *the oldest creditor*, that it lends and lends and collects in the end, and she had laughed, and then stopped laughing, because she had looked at his face and seen that he was not speaking in metaphor.

Now she heard it. Now she lay on her bed with her shoes still on and listened to the oldest creditor breathing beneath the floorboards of her father's house, and she turned her father's offer over and over in the dark like a coin she was testing for false metal.

Either door opens. Neither costs you anything except the other.

But that was the lie, and she saw it now, laid out clean as a falsified column — the beautiful symmetry of it, the gift-wrapped fairness. Because a choice is only free when you can refuse both doors. And she could refuse neither. Take the capital and she left Jude to his struck-through name, to the rising water, to two more years of a bond that her father's initials had just quietly lengthened into a cage — she would ride away on the twenty-third with a dull husband and a fine house and know, every morning of her comfortable life, exactly what her comfort had been purchased with. Stay, and she stayed as her father's daughter, in her father's house, under her father's terms — free to walk nowhere, change nothing, save no one. Free the way a bird is free inside a gilded cage with the door painted on.

Either way, the ledger balanced. Either way, she paid.

She sat up. The candle had burned to a thumb's width; she lit a fresh one from it, and the new flame stood up straight and small in the damp air, absurdly brave.

On the desk lay the book she'd lent him — *The Pilgrim's Progress*, of all ridiculous things, bought from the mission stall because it was the only English volume in the quarter and she had wanted to see whether he'd laugh at it. He hadn't laughed. He'd read it in four nights and returned it with a strip of paper marking a passage, and the note in his careful clerk's hand had said only: *He carries his burden all the way to the river before it comes off. Nobody tells you that part when they give you the book.*

Nobody tells you that part, Miriam thought, staring at the flame. They hand you a life the way her father had handed her those two glasses of port — generously, warmly, both doors opening — and they never mention that the burden doesn't come off until the water.

Below, the river breathed. On the twenty-third, a barge would leave with four berths where there should have been five, and her father's neat initials drying on the margin like a signature on a wound.

She snuffed the candle and, in the dark, began — methodically, the way her father taught her to begin every enterprise, by reading everything twice — to plan how she would speak to him. Not as his daughter. As his equal. She would need leverage, and she knew where the ledgers slept.

Rain on the roof. Water in the dark. Sixteen days.

She would need every one of them.
