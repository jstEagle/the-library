## 17. The Forged Page

The counting house at night was a different country.

By day it belonged to Calloway and his clerks — the scratch of nibs, the rattle of abacus beads, the sour smell of ink and cheaper ambition. By day it was arithmetic and authority, everything squared and witnessed and signed. But after the last lamp in the clerks' row guttered out and the shutters were barred from within, as they were tonight because the audit loomed and Calloway wanted no one coming or going with loose hands, the room became something else entirely. It became a held breath.

Jude sat alone at the long table with forty ledgers around him like sleeping animals, and he did not move for a long time.

He had told himself, walking over from the divers' quarter through streets gone quiet with early flood-season dread, that this was only work. The audit was real; Bassi had heard of it from the ferryman, who had heard it from a clerk who drank too much palm wine and talked too freely. Every bond register, every allocation sheet, every scrap of paper bearing the Meridian Trading Company's seal would be turned over and read by men from the capital who did not know anyone's face. Someone had to make the books presentable. That someone was Jude Oyelaran, because Jude Oyelaran was useful and unseen, and unseen men were given the tasks that no one wished to be seen doing.

That was the story he carried through the door. It lasted until the door clicked shut behind him and the silence came down like river fog.

* * *

He began, honestly enough, with the honest pages. He checked totals against tallies, matched receipts to entries, smoothed a corner here and straightened a column there. The lamp burned low and steady, its oil sweet-smelling, better than what burned in the quarter. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings. Somewhere a dog gave up on barking and settled into the damp.

It was the third ledger — the bond register, bound in green leather gone soft as old skin at the corners — where his hands stopped obeying him.

He had not meant to open it to that page. Or perhaps he had. Perhaps some part of him had walked across the whole dark town carrying exactly this intention, dressed up in the clothing of duty, and now stood naked in the lamplight and would not pretend anymore.

The page was ruled in faded red. Down the left column ran the names of the bonded: divers, porters, boatmen, girls hired to sort shell. Beside each name, the terms. Beside the terms, the marks — not signatures, most of them, because most of them could not write. A cross. A thumbprint in ink gone brown. And beside one name, in a hand Jude knew better than his own heartbeat:

*Oyelaran, T. — diver — debt of 41 pounds, 6 shillings, discharged by bond transfer to son, J. Oyelaran.*

His father's mark sat beneath it like a bird's footprint. Two strokes crossing, then a downward hook — a diving line, Jude had always thought, plunging into water. His father had made the mark a thousand times: on fish sold at market, on the company chits, once, memorably, on a letter to Adaeze dictated to old Bassi but *marked* by himself at the bottom, as if the mark alone could carry what the borrowed words could not. *I am alive. I am working. I will come up.* The mark said all three things if you knew how to read it.

Jude looked at that mark for a long time.

Then he took out a clean sheet of foolscap, and before he could think better of it — before the part of him that measured every act by its weight could step onto the scale and pronounce judgment — he dipped the pen and made the mark.

Two strokes crossing. The downward hook.

It was wrong. Too tall, too proud, drawn with a living man's flourish when his father had written like a man conserving breath. Jude stared at it, feeling heat climb his neck as though the night watchman were already at the window. He blotted it, folded the sheet small, and pushed it deep into his pocket, which was a foolish thing to do and he knew it, because evidence kept was evidence waiting to be found.

But his hands were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about forgery: it begins long before the pen touches the page. It begins with love. With attention. With all those years of being invisible in rooms where important things happened, watching.

Jude had watched Calloway sign documents for seven years. He had stood at the factor's shoulder holding lamps and boxes and letters, useful and unseen, and while the great man read aloud in his rolling voice about cargoes and commissions, Jude's eyes had been busy. He knew that Calloway's *C* began with a heavy press of the nib that left a small blot like a full stop before the letter even started — impatience made visible. He knew the *w* in his initials sagged in the middle, tired, as though the man who wrote it had grown bored of his own name decades ago. He knew the final stroke of the *C* flicked upward at the end, a little flourish, the single vanity permitted to an otherwise economical hand. He knew Calloway pressed harder on Mondays than Fridays. He knew that after the second glass of wine at supper — audible through the factor's office wall on nights Jude worked late — the flourish grew extravagant, almost playful.

And he knew, now, sitting very still in the lamplight, that he could do it.

Not well. Not yet. But the knowledge was there, whole and terrible, the way you suddenly know a river's depth by the cold climbing your legs. He could feel his hand remembering. He could feel the shape of another man's name living inside his own muscles, coiled there, patient.

Temptation, Jude thought, is not a whisper. It is a pair of hands. It waits until you have worked years without being seen, until you have learned every seam of a thing, and then one night it sets itself into your fingers and says: *now you may*.

* * *

He should have stopped. He understood that with perfect clarity, the way his father must have understood, on some dive, the exact moment when the air in his chest turned from provision to poison. There is a depth past which the body wants things the mind has not agreed to. Jude was below that depth now.

He cleared a space among the ledgers. He laid out three sheets of foolscap — practice sheets, deniable, the kind a diligent clerk might use for drafts. He trimmed a fresh nib to match the wear pattern of Calloway's own pen, which hung in the rack by the window and which Jude had studied the way other men studied scripture. He mixed ink: the company's standard black cut with a drop of the brown iron-gall that Calloway favored for private correspondence, a shade distinguishable only if you cared, and no auditor would care unless he already suspected.

Then he practiced.

The first dozen attempts were grotesque. His *C* bloomed too wide, apologetic. The blot before the letter — that impatient full stop — came out either absent or enormous, a wound instead of a period. He tore the failures into pieces no larger than a fingernail and fed them to the lamp, watching each curl and go, and started again.

By the thirtieth attempt something loosened. Not his hand — his *understanding*. Forgery, he discovered, was not drawing. It was forgetting. You had to forget your own name. You had to forget that you were a bonded man with two years left on a debt inherited from a drowned father, forget that your mother counted rice grains, that your sister dreamed of schools she could not see from the quarter, that a girl had bought you a paper lantern as a joke between classes and changed the temperature of your whole life. You had to become, for the length of four letters, a bored middle-aged merchant pressing his pen on a Monday morning. You had to wear his boredom like a coat.

*T.C.* The blot. The sagging *w*. The flick of vanity.

On the fortieth attempt, Jude set down the pen and looked at what he had made, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair, because it was good. It was not perfect — a forger's vanity, to believe in perfection — but it would pass under a tired eye, and auditors were tired men reading their hundredth ledger, and tired eyes saw what they expected to see. That was the whole architecture of his invisibility, inverted. For seven years people had seen nothing when they looked at him because they expected nothing. Now the same blindness could be turned around, made to serve him. The cage had a door, and the door had never been locked. It had only ever been unwatched.

His father's mark came easier. Of course it did — it lived in his blood. He practiced it smaller, humbler, worn down the way a workingman wore his letters, until the mark on the foolscap and the mark on the green register might have been brothers separated by years of weather. Two strokes. The diving line. *I am alive. I am working. I will come up.*

He caught himself smiling at that, alone in the dead hour, and the smile frightened him more than anything else the night had produced.

* * *

The lamp had burned low. Through the shutter slats the sky was still dark but thinking about grey, and the river's muttering had taken on the heavier voice it used before dawn. Jude gathered the practice sheets — all but one, which he had missed, or which some steady-handed part of him had chosen to keep — and fed them to the flame.

While the last scraps curled, he opened the green register again and laid his hand flat over his father's mark, feeling the paper's faint tooth through his skin.

He did not do it. That was the fact he carried out of the counting house like a stone in his shoe. The register lay open, the blank space beside his own name waiting with the patience of all official paper, and he did not touch it. He closed the book. He squared it with its neighbors. He cleaned the pen and racked it, trimmed the lamp to a night-safe flame, and sat back down to be, for the remaining hours, only a diligent clerk finishing his work.

But knowing is a thing that cannot be unknown. The hands remembered now. They would remember tomorrow, and the day after, and on every night the ledgers lay open and the watchman's boots went by on the far side of the shutters. Sooner or later — and Jude suspected he already knew which it would be — the temptation would stop asking and simply place itself into his fingers again, and this time he would not be practicing on foolscap.

He thought of Miriam saying, in the lantern-light weeks ago, *you could be more than useful*. He thought of Ngozi's face when she spoke of the capital's schools, hope so bright it was nearly arrogance, hope he had said nothing to dim. He thought of his mother measuring rice with her eyes and refusing charity with her spine. He thought of Tamuno by the black river, saying debts outlive men, and for the first time Jude had understood it not as warning but as instruction — that debts could be killed, that paper could drown just as men drown, that anything the water could take the water could take *for* someone.

He blew out the lamp as the first grey came through the slats.

When the clerks arrived at the seventh bell, yawning and complaining of the damp, they found the counting house in perfect order, the registers squared, the tallies checked, everything done that a useful, unseen man might be asked to do. Mr. Calloway passed through on his morning round, paused at Jude's table, and glanced down at the neat columns with something that in a better man might have been warmth.

"Good work, Oyelaran," he said. "The auditors will want everything exact."

"Yes, sir," Jude said, and bent to his pen.

And under the table, in his pocket, folded flat against his thigh, rode the single surviving practice sheet — his father's mark, and beneath it, in a bored merchant's Monday-morning hand, the initials *T.C.* — dry as bone, light as a lantern, heavy as the river in flood.
