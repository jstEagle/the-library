## 18. Flood Season Opens

The bridge closed on a Tuesday, at an hour when the mist still owned the river.

Elder Bassi was there to see it, because he had made himself the kind of man who witnessed things. He stood at the northern abutment with his stick planted between two stones and watched the company's men drive the last of the barricade stakes into the mud. The water had already climbed past the third marker post, brown and muscular, carrying branches and a drowned goat and, for one uncanny moment, a whole unlit lantern that turned over and over in the current like something trying to decide whether to rise or sink.

"Closed," said the foreman, not unkindly. It was not a proclamation; it was a weather report.

Bassi nodded as though he had expected nothing else, which was true. He had watched this river for seventy years and it had never once been argued with successfully. But watching the planks go up — rough-cut company timber, stamped with the Meridian mark — he felt the town shrink inside him like a map being folded. Everything beyond the bridge was now a rumor. The road to the capital, the markets on the far bank, the letters that came with the dry-season carriers: all of it sealed behind brown water until the world exhaled again.

He turned back toward the lantern-lit core of the town, where the lamps were being lit earlier each evening now, as if light itself could be stockpiled against the coming dark.

* * *

The market knew before the proclamation did. Markets always know first.

By Wednesday, Mama Ekwueme had raised the price of dried fish by a third, and by Thursday she had learned that every other trader had done the same, so her raise bought her nothing but the same profit at a louder number. She stood behind her stall and watched customers count coins twice, three times, their lips moving, their eyes going hard in a way that had nothing to do with fish.

"You rob us," said a woman Bassi did not know, a newcomer from the weaving quarter, her voice pitched high enough for the whole lane to hear.

"I feed you," Mama Ekwueme answered, level as a scale. "The barge brings no more goods after next week. What I have is what there is. Would you rather I sell it cheap to the first ten and let the rest starve?"

"I'd rather you remember we are neighbors."

"We were neighbors," Mama Ekwueme said, and the word landed in the lane like a stone dropped down a well — everyone heard it falling, and no one heard it land. "Flood season makes us arithmetic."

The woman left without buying. Three others stayed and haggled harder than they ever had in their lives, and Mama Ekwueme let them win small victories because she was not cruel, only clear-eyed, and clarity in flood season passes for cruelty everywhere.

At the grain stall, old Okon measured out rice with a hand that trembled — not from age, but from the arithmetic too. His son had gone upriver on the last crossing before the barricade went up, carrying a promissory note and a mule, and would not come back until the water fell. Which meant Okon would sit alone through the high water with his scales and his silence, weighing out other people's survival gram by gram.

* * *

In the divers' quarter, Adaeze counted rice.

She did it the way she did everything now: slowly, visibly, letting her children see the counting. The sack from the autumn stood open on its wooden stand, and beside it the smaller gourd of beans, and beside that the clay pot of oil with its neck wrapped in cloth to keep the weevils guessing. She put her hand into the rice to the wrist, then to the elbow, feeling for what was actually there beneath what she hoped was there.

"Ngozi," she called. "Bring me the small pot."

Ngozi brought it, moving through the room the way she had moved all week — quickly, brightly, touching things as if to memorize them. She had begun saying goodbyes. Adaeze had watched her daughter walk the length of the quarter on Tuesday evening just to stand in front of Old Nneka's doorway, and again on Thursday to help carry water for the widow Amara, and she understood what the walking was. Ngozi was gathering the quarter into herself, folding it flat like cloth, so she could carry it to the city whole.

Adaeze measured rice into the small pot until it reached a line only she could see — a line drawn not on the pot but in her mother's mind, inherited from her own mother, who had learned it from a famine year Adaeze had been told about but never asked about twice. The line said: this much, stretched, keeps five people alive until the water falls. Except now it needed to keep four, and even that arithmetic sat wrong in her chest, because the four included Tamuno, who ate little but refused to be counted as little, and did not include Jude, and the absence of Jude in the count was a hole shaped exactly like her firstborn son.

She covered the pot. She did not cry. Crying was a luxury priced higher than oil.

"Mother." Ngozi stood in the doorway with the light behind her. "When does the barge go?"

"Nine days."

"And you've packed?"

"A mother packs nine days early or not at all," Adaeze said. "There's no middle way."

Ngozi smiled — that quick, ambitious smile, the one that had already crossed the bridge ahead of her body — and went out to help the widow Amara again, and Adaeze stood alone in the dimness listening to the rain begin on the roof, patient as a creditor.

* * *

The rain came down the river valley in stages, and Elder Bassi, walking his evening rounds, named them for anyone who would listen.

First the smell — green rot and cold stone, arriving a day ahead of the clouds. Then the sound on the hills, a hushing like the whole world turning over in bed. Then the water itself, hours later, brown and certain. The young men laughed at him for reading weather like scripture, but the young men had not seen the flood of thirty years ago, when the lower quarter went under to the window sills and three families were never entirely accounted for afterward.

"The river doesn't hate us," he told the boys gathered under the eaves of the meeting house, because telling them was his work now, the way other men's work was hauling or hammering. "It doesn't love us either. That's what the company has never understood. Calloway thinks the water can be scheduled. Berths! Allocations! As if you could allot the rain."

The boys laughed, but Bassi saw that some of them wrote it down inside themselves, the way he had once written down the words of the elder before him, who had seen the flood of the century before that.

"And the barge?" one asked. "Will it hold?"

"The barge will float," Bassi said. "Whether it holds everyone is a question of paper, not wood. And paper sinks faster than any boat I've ever seen."

* * *

Jude worked.

That was the whole of his answer to the closing world, and he gave it daily: he rose in the dark and walked to the warehouse through streets running with water, and he loaded, tallied, stacked, and carried, and he was useful and unseen, and the invisibility that had once fit him like a coat now pulled tight across the shoulders like a garment outgrown. Nine days. He counted them the way his mother counted rice.

Calloway had posted the final schedule on the counting house door in his neat factor's hand: **LAST BARGE DEPARTS AT FIRST LIGHT, NINE DAYS HENCE. NO CROSSINGS AFTERWARD UNTIL THE WATER FALLS. COMPANY PERSONNEL REQUIRED FOR FLOOD WATCH WILL BE ACCOMMODATED.**

Accommodated. Jude had read the word four times. It was such a soft word for such a hard thing. Accommodated meant kept. Meant held. Meant *his name was not on the berth list and everyone would go on speaking gently around that fact until the water made it permanent.*

He stacked crates and thought about ledgers. He carried sacks and thought about ink. In the evenings his hands smelled of rope and lamp oil, and at night, when the quarter slept, he lay awake doing sums that had nothing to do with cargo — how long a page takes to dry, how deep an impression a pen leaves, whether a dead man's mark could be learned the way Tamuno had promised him a diver's breath could be learned: slowly, in the dark, alone.

* * *

Tamuno spent the evenings where he always spent them, on the low bench outside his door, facing the river he could no longer see for the rain.

Neighbors passed him with bundles on their heads, carrying their lives toward higher ground or toward the barge landing, and they called greetings to him, and he returned them, and both sides of the exchange understood that the greetings were a kind of packing too — that the quarter was saying goodbye to itself in advance, street by street, doorway by doorway.

Old Nneka stopped, breathless under her load of pots. "You're certain, Tamuno? You'll stay?"

"The river took my son," Tamuno said. "You think I'll leave before asking it why?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He looked at her load, at the way the largest pot rode on top like a dark moon. "What do you take and what do you leave? That's the whole of wisdom in flood season. Everything else is commentary."

Nneka shifted her burden and looked out at the grey curtain of rain. "I'm leaving my husband's chair," she said suddenly, as if surprised to hear it. "Forty years he sat in it. There's no room on the barge for a chair, and there's no room in my heart to watch it drown, so I'm leaving it and calling it a gift to the water."

"He'd understand."

"No," she said, almost cheerfully. "He'd be furious. He loved that chair. That's why it's a gift." She went on into the rain, and Tamuno sat with her laughter a while, the last warm thing the day would give him.

* * *

Prices rose. Tempers shortened. These were not separate events.

At the well on Friday there was shoving — actual shoving, women of the weaving quarter and the divers' quarter pushing at each other over the order of the buckets, over nothing, over the fact that the well was the same well it had always been while everything else got smaller. The headman's wife restored order with a voice like a dropped plank, but the look that passed between the two quarters afterward did not wash out with the rain.

At the company store, the clerk announced that credit would end at the close of business Saturday — cash only until the barge came — and a man named Obinna, who owed eleven months of credit, went quiet in a way that made the other customers drift toward the door. He was not a violent man. But the flood season had a way of finding the seam in a person and pulling.

Bassi heard about it and walked to Obinna's house and sat with him for two hours over palm wine neither of them drank, talking about anything else, which is sometimes the whole of what an elder can do: be a place where a man's anger can cool without catching fire.

"They count on us scattering," Bassi said finally. "High water and short tempers. A scattered town forgets. Remember that, Obinna. Whatever happens these nine days — remember. Write it on your heart if you have no paper."

Obinna looked at him a long moment. "Why do you say it like that? Like you're expecting something to happen."

"Because I'm old," Bassi said, "and old men don't expect. They recognize."

* * *

On the seventh night before the barge, the town lit its lanterns early.

It was not festival; the festivals were cancelled, folded away with everything else that required open space and good news. It was simply that the days had grown short and the rain heavy, and the lantern-lit core of the town — that shrinking circle of streets around the meeting house, the store, the church, the counting house — burned brighter each evening as the darkness pressed closer to it, the way a fire burns brighter as its fuel runs low.

From the ridge above the quarter, if a person climbed there — and Jude climbed there, on the seventh night, needing to see the shape of the thing — the town looked like a handful of embers cupped in two wet hands. Beyond the lantern ring, the lower quarter was already going dark house by house as families moved uphill or packed for the barge, and the river ran through all of it, wide and loud and indifferent, wearing the reflected lights on its back like stolen jewelry.

Jude stood in the rain without sheltering from it and counted windows. Somewhere among those lights was Miriam, behind her father's shutters, and somewhere among them was Ngozi, rehearsing her goodbyes, and somewhere among them his mother was measuring rice against a line only she could see. Eight days. Seven after tonight.

The barge date approached the way the flood approached — in stages, first the smell of it, then the sound, then the thing itself, brown and certain, carrying everything before it.

He turned to go down. Below him, at the edge of the lantern light, a figure stood at the landing where the barge would tie up, holding a lantern of her own — and even at that distance, even in the rain, he knew the set of her shoulders, the way she held her light slightly forward, as if offering it to the dark.

Miriam. Out alone, seven nights before departure, standing at the water like a question.

Jude started down the slope, and with every step the world narrowed — to the lantern, to the landing, to the space between them where everything unsaid waited, getting larger as everything else grew small.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him — the particular care of his footsteps on the wet boards, a care she had catalogued without meaning to.

"You shouldn't be here," they said, together, to each other, and the symmetry of it broke something loose, and Miriam laughed — a short, startled sound the rain swallowed immediately.

"My father's at the board supper," she said. "Until late. I wanted—" She stopped, lifted the lantern a little. Its light caught the brown water sliding past the pilings, fast now, impatient. "Everyone's packing. The whole town is one long goodbye. And I keep thinking: in eight days, that water will be the widest road any of us owns, and it goes nowhere any of us chose."

"It goes down," Jude said. "Eventually. That's the river's only promise."

"Your family will be on the barge."

It was not a question, and the pause before his answer was long enough that the lantern flame bent in the wind, and Miriam turned and looked at him properly then, the way she had learned to — reading not his face but the space around his face, the places where truth stood just offstage.

"Jude. Will they?"

"Yes," he said. "They'll be on it."

"And you?"

Above them the rain thickened; below them the river rose; and somewhere behind the shutters of the counting house a ledger lay open in the dark with his name missing from it, waiting, patient as water, for what he had already decided he could do.

"Someone has to mind the flood watch," Jude said.

And Miriam Calloway, who had grown up translating her father's gentle phrases into their hard meanings since she was six years old, went very still, and the lantern shook once in her hand, and the light between them trembled like the last lamp in a town going dark.
