## 19. Ngozi Finds Out

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a bundle of school circulars that Elder Bassi's granddaughter carried up from the company office. Ngozi Oyelaran read it standing in the doorway of the divers' quarter house, one hand braced against the doorpost as though the paper might otherwise blow away with her future still attached to it.

*Miss Ngozi Oyelaran is confirmed for passage aboard the Meridian relief barge, departing the last practicable day before the seasonal closure of the crossing. She is advised to present herself and her effects at the company wharf no later than the morning of departure. Berth twelve.*

Berth twelve. She read it three times, and each time the words arranged themselves more beautifully — *confirmed*, *passage*, *advised*, all the soft bureaucratic language of a door opening. She pressed the letter flat against her chest and felt her own heart knocking against it like someone asking to be let in.

She was going. She was actually going. The capital, with its schools whose names she had copied into the back of her arithmetic book until the ink wore thin. The library Bassi had told her about, four rooms of books open to anyone who could sign their name. The examinations in the spring. All of it, no longer a dream she kept folded small so it wouldn't tear, but a thing with a berth number.

Her mother was at the wash basins behind the house, knuckles red in the grey water. Ngozi ran to her without deciding to run, the letter held out in both hands like an offering, and Adaeze dried her hands on her wrapper before she would take it, because some papers deserved clean hands.

She read it slowly. Ngozi watched her mother's face for the flinch — the small contraction that always came now when the family's hopes were counted against the family's means — but it didn't come. Instead something in Adaeze's shoulders eased, just slightly, the way a rope eases when weight is taken from it.

"Then you'll go," her mother said simply.

"I'll go." Saying it aloud made it solid, made it real enough to be frightening. "Mama — I'll go."

"You'll go," Adaeze agreed, and then, because she was her mother: "And you'll write every week, even when there's nothing to say. Especially when there's nothing to say."

That evening the house filled. Grandfather Tamuno came from the river smelling of mud and woodsmoke, and Jude came late from the counting house with ink still ghosting the side of his right hand, and they sat around the lamp while Ngozi read the letter aloud twice, once for the words and once for the pleasure of it. Grandfather listened with his eyes closed, which meant he was pleased; he only closed his eyes to people he trusted not to stop talking.

"Berth twelve," he said at the end. "Your father would have counted them himself, the berths. He never trusted anyone else's arithmetic."

"Whose berths are the others?" Ngozi asked, and looked around the lamplight at her family — Mama, Grandfather, Jude — four faces, and the question hung there the way questions do when everyone has already answered them silently and differently.

"Mine's eleven," Tamuno said. "They put me next to you so I can keep an eye on your manners."

"And mine?" Adaeze asked lightly, too lightly.

"Ten, if the list holds," Jude said. His voice was even. He was very good at even; Ngozi had noticed it all her life, the way her brother could pour his voice flat as oil over anything that moved underneath. "Mother's ten, Grandfather eleven, you twelve."

He said nothing about thirteen. He said nothing about any other number. He sat there in the lamplight with his hands loose in his lap and let her believe the list was whole, and Ngozi, drunk on berth twelve, did not hear the absence where his name should have been.

Later — much later, when the lamp had been trimmed low and Adaeze had gone to bed and Tamuno had gone back down to the river where he slept half the time now among the boats — Ngozi found Jude awake on the step outside, watching the rain come down fine as sifted flour through the dark.

"Say something true," she said, sitting beside him. "Just once. About all this."

"What kind of true?"

"Any kind. You've been walking around for two weeks like a man carrying water in both hands and trying not to spill. Say what's in the buckets."

Jude was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for him. Then: "I'm glad it's you going. That's true. If it could only be one of us, I'm glad it's you."

"But it isn't only one of us. There are five of us and—" She stopped. Something in his stillness snagged her attention the way a fishhook snags cloth, gently at first, then not gently at all. "How many berths did Calloway post?"

"Three were posted first. Then amended."

"Amended to what, Jude?"

"To four." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the dark his face was mostly eyes. "You. Mama. Grandfather. And me."

It was smooth. It was so smooth that later — after everything — she would understand how much work the lie had cost him, how many nights of practice it must have taken to make his face do that. But she believed it, then, because wanting to believe is its own kind of sight, blinding. She squeezed his arm hard enough to hurt and said, "Four. We all go together," and went to bed happy, and the happiness had less than a week left to live.

* * *

The end came, as ends often do, through someone else's carelessness.

Ngozi had taken to helping at Elder Bassi's veranda school in the last weeks, reading aloud to the smallest children while their mothers traded and argued below. On the morning it happened she was copying out a passage for the older girls — handwriting practice, sentences about the seasons — when Bassi's granddaughter arrived with the day's company notices, fresh from the board outside the counting house, and dumped them on the table for Bassi to sort.

Bassi sorted slowly, one eye gone cloudy with age, reading each notice aloud in his cracked voice out of habit, because in the quarter the notices were law and law should be heard by all. Evacuation schedule. Ration prices. Ferry restrictions. And then:

*"Final passenger manifest, Meridian relief barge, amended second time. Berths as follows: ten, Adaeze Oyelaran. Eleven, Tamuno Oyelaran. Twelve, Ngozi Oyelaran. Manifest closes upon departure; no further amendments."*

Ngozi's pen kept moving for a moment after her ears understood, laying down letters to nobody, and then stopped.

"No further amendments," Bassi read again, unnecessarily, and looked up, and found her looking at him.

"Elder," she said, and her voice came out strange, level, cold, "read the names again."

He read them again. He watched her while he did it, the way old men watch young people break, with patience and a certain sorrow, as though he'd seen this particular weather before.

"Where is my brother?" she asked. "There were four. Jude said four. He stood in our yard and told me four."

"The boy said four?" Bassi set the notice down. "Or did the boy let you say four and stand next to it?"

The distinction landed like a stone dropped into still water, and the rings spread outward through everything she had believed for the past week — every relieved dinner, every goodbye already begun, every night she had lain awake composing her first letter home to a family that included him. She thought of Jude on the step in the rain, saying *I'm glad it's you going* with those flat, poured-smooth eyes, and she understood, all at once and completely, that she had been holding a bucket of nothing the entire time.

She left the school mid-lesson. The smallest children called after her, confused, and she didn't turn around.

* * *

She found him at the warehouse, of course, because Jude was always where the work was, useful and unseen, invisible among the crates exactly as he preferred. She came through the loading doors with the rain steaming off her shoulders and the amended notice crushed in her fist, and she saw him across the floor directing two loaders with quiet hand signals, efficient, competent, erased, and the sight of it — the sheer practiced ease of his own disappearance — made her furious in a way she had never been furious before. Not hot fury. Cold fury. Fury with structure.

"Jude."

He turned. He saw her face, and Ngozi watched — she would remember this always, how clearly she watched — watched her brother understand in a single breath what she knew and how she knew it. Watched him decide, in that same breath, not to pretend.

"Ngosi—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked across the warehouse like a whip over water. The loaders melted away; they were good at vanishing too, everyone in this town was. "Don't say my name like that. Don't soften it. You tell me right now why the final manifest has three names from this family and yours isn't one of them."

"It's complicated."

"No." She walked toward him, and he stood his ground, which she gave him credit for even through the anger. "Nothing is complicated. Things are simple and we call them complicated so we don't have to say them out loud. Here's the simple version: there are four berths. Mama. Grandfather. Me. And you are not on the list, and you have known since the list was posted, and you let me spend a week being happy."

"Ngozi—"

"A *week*, Jude!" The crushed notice came up between them, shaking. "I read the letter to the whole house! I started saying goodbyes! I wrote you in my head — every letter I'm going to send from the capital, you're in every one of them, and the whole time you were standing there letting me build a house on ground you knew was empty!"

"If you'd known—"

"*If I'd known?*" She laughed, and it was an awful sound, nothing like laughter. "If I'd known I would have refused to go! That's what you were afraid of, isn't it? That's why you lied. Because I would have torn up berth twelve and stayed, and then your sacrifice — your beautiful, noble, secret sacrifice — would have been wasted, and you couldn't allow that, could you? You couldn't allow me to have a say in my own life!"

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Someone has to go who can use it. Someone has to go who can become something. You have the examinations. You have—"

"I don't want to become something at the price of my brother!"

"Yes, you do!" It burst out of him louder than he'd ever spoken to her, loud enough to echo off the rafters, and then immediately quieter, terrible and gentle: "Yes, you do, Ngozi. You want it more than you've ever wanted anything. I've watched you copy school names into your books since you were six years old. And I am not going to be the reason you don't. I would rather you hate me from a classroom in the capital than love me in a flooded town with nothing ahead of either of us."

"So you decided for me."

"Yes." No flinch. He had decided that too. "I decided for you. Because you would have done the same for me, and one of us had to be selfish enough to make it stick."

For a moment the warehouse was very quiet. Rain drummed on the roof, on the crates, on the world. Ngozi became aware distantly that her hands were trembling and that she was crying, furiously, without permission, tears she hated because they proved how much she loved him, and love was the thing being used against her.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" she said finally. Her voice had gone quiet too, and that was worse than shouting; shouting spends itself, but quiet accumulates. "Not the lie. Not even that I have to leave you. The worst part is that you did it *kindly*. You did it gently, and patiently, and you let me keep my hope right up until the last minute because you thought hope was a gift. Hope isn't a gift, Jude. Hope is trust. You spent mine like it was yours to spend."

"I know."

"You don't know. You think protection is love. Sometimes protection is just control wearing love's clothes." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, once. "Mama taught us better. She taught us what dignity costs — well, here's the bill, brother. This is what it costs. It costs telling the truth to the people who love you even when the truth will wreck your plans."

"Ngozi." He reached for her arm and she stepped back, and the step back hit him harder than any shout; she saw it land, saw it go in under the ribs.

"I'll go," she said. "You should know that. I'll get on the barge, I'll take berth twelve, I'll pass the examinations, I will do every single thing you decided I should do. And I will do it knowing that you chose this for me instead of with me. Every good thing I ever accomplish from that desk in that city, some part of me will remember that my brother bought it with a lie and never even asked."

She turned and walked out through the loading doors into the rain, and this time he didn't call after her, and that silence followed her all the way home like a debt.

* * *

Adaeze found her daughter an hour later sitting in the dark of the inner room, dry-eyed now, staring at the wall where the family's few books were stacked. She didn't ask what had happened; mothers in the divers' quarter rarely needed to. She sat down beside her, took her daughter's hands, and unwound the crushed notice from her fist finger by finger, smoothing it out on her knee.

"He struck his own name off," Ngozi said dully. "Before I ever saw the first list. He's known the whole time."

"I know."

"You *know*?" Ngozi turned. "Since when?"

"Since the beginning." Adaeze smoothed the creases flat with a slow, deliberate hand. "Since the night he first read it and went quiet in that particular way of his. A mother learns her children's silences before their words." She was quiet a moment. "I didn't speak of it because I was waiting to see whether he would find the courage to tell you himself."

"And when he doesn't? When he just keeps lying until the barge pulls away?"

"Then that is his failure," Adaeze said, "and a heavy one, and he will carry it. But hear me, Ngozi." She took her daughter's chin and turned her face so their eyes met, and there was iron in it, the same iron that had refused charity at the company store for twenty years. "You will not carry it for him. You will not make yourself smaller to punish him, or refuse your own life to spite him. Do you understand me? Anger is a fire you may warm your hands at, child, but you do not move into it."

"He decided my whole future without asking me."

"Yes. And someday he will answer for that — to you, to me, maybe to himself most of all. But you will board that barge with your head up, not as his sacrifice and not as his victim. As Ngozi Oyelaran, who earned her own berth." She released her chin and rose, folding the notice into a neat square, setting it on the shelf between the books as though filing it among them, evidence and inheritance at once. "Now come and eat. Fury keeps better on a full stomach. That much your grandfather taught all of us."

But when Ngozi followed her mother out into the main room, she stopped in the doorway. Through the window, far down the slope where the roofs gave way to the river mist, she could see a single figure standing at the edge of the water — her grandfather, Tamuno, motionless in the failing light, looking out at the flooded channel as though listening to something beneath it. And behind her, faintly, from the direction of the counting house, she thought she heard the night watchman's bell ring the hour — the hour when her brother would be alone with the ledgers, alone with the lists, alone with whatever he was planning that none of them had agreed to.
