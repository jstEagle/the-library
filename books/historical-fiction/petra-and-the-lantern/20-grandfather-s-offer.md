## 20. Grandfather's Offer

The rain had found a rhythm by the third day of the family council — not the sudden violence of the early storms but a steady, patient drumming on the corrugated roof of the divers' quarter, as though the sky itself were counting down to the barge's departure. Jude lay awake beneath it, listening to the water run through the street gutters in long silver threads, and thought about arithmetic.

It was all arithmetic now. Four berths. Five bodies. One name struck from the company's list with a clerk's casual pen-stroke, as if Jude Oyelaran were a miscounted crate of indigo, an error to be corrected in the next inventory. He had told no one but Ngozi, and Ngozi's fury still hung in the house like smoke that would not clear. She spoke to him only when the words could not be helped, and even then her eyes said what her mouth withheld: *You let me hope. You watched me say my goodbyes and you said nothing.*

So he lay in the dark and did sums instead of sleeping. His mother's rice, stretched with cassava until it barely deserved the name. The coin Adaeze kept somewhere he had never been permitted to find. The weight of his grandfather's silence in the room below, heavier than any chest they would leave behind.

When the drumming softened toward the grey hour before dawn, he heard the old man rise. Tamuno moved the way he always moved — slowly, deliberately, each motion carrying the memory of forty years spent holding breath against current. Jude heard the door open, heard the rain take his grandfather's footsteps, and after a while he rose too and followed.

* * *

The river at first light was the color of hammered tin. Tamuno stood at the edge of the flooded shallows where the bank became rumor, his feet bare in the mud, his thin shirt already dark with spray. He did not turn when Jude came up beside him. Old men of the diving families never turned; they felt a body's weight arrive on the ground before they saw it, the way they felt a storm in their knees two days before the clouds gathered.

"You didn't sleep," Tamuno said.

"Neither did you."

"I finished sleeping years ago." The old man smiled without humor. "Everything since has just been lying down."

They stood together while the river worked at its morning business — pushing branches past the pilings, turning over its brown water like a man restless in bed. Somewhere upstream the bridge groaned, a sound like a great animal shifting in pain. The flood had come up another hand's width overnight. Jude could see it in the way the mangrove roots had disappeared, in how the mooring posts stood shorter than they had last week, wearing the water like rising boots.

"Ngozi isn't speaking to you," Tamuno observed.

"She'll speak again."

"When?"

Jude didn't answer. The old man nodded as if the silence were itself an answer, which perhaps it was.

Then Tamuno turned, and Jude saw that his grandfather's eyes — clouded at the edges now, the whites gone the yellow of old ivory — were fixed on him with a clarity that made the skin of his arms prickle.

"I have been doing sums too," Tamuno said.

* * *

They walked upriver along the levee path, past the drying racks where no fish hung anymore because no one fished these waters in flood season, past the shrine where the diving families had left offerings for generations — cowrie shells gone green with age, a rusted bell, the small carved figure of a woman with a fish's tail that Jude's father had once sworn was older than the town itself. The water lapped at the shrine's base now. By the time the barge left, it would be drowned entirely, and whatever gods lived there would have to swim.

Tamuno stopped where the path widened, where the old men used to sit in the evenings and argue about tides. He sat down on the flat stone there, groaning slightly, and patted the stone beside him.

"Your father sat here," he said. "The night before his last dive. Did you know that?"

"No."

"He sat exactly where you're sitting and he told me the company had offered him a bonus for going down twice in one day. Storm coming. Water dark. I told him no diver worth his salt takes the second dive when the river goes quiet like that. Quiet water is a lie, I told him. The river holds its breath before it bites." Tamuno looked out at the water, which was indeed very quiet, very smooth, moving with a slowness that seemed almost tender. "He laughed at me. Said the debt wouldn't wait for good weather. Said Meridian's clerks don't count storms, only pearls."

Jude's hands had gone cold inside themselves. He had heard fragments of this story his whole life — the storm, the dark water, the rope that came up slack — but never from Tamuno directly, never in this voice, which was low and level and therefore more terrible than weeping.

"The ledger says his death closed his account," Jude said carefully. "That's why they took me instead. Seven years of my labor against what he owed."

"That is what the ledger says." Tamuno's mouth twisted. "I have known that ledger longer than you've been alive, boy. I knew the clerks who wrote in it before Calloway, and the ones before them. Do you know what I learned in sixty years of watching that book?" He turned to face Jude fully. "The ledger never balances. It only rolls forward. Your father's debt rolled onto you. My debts rolled onto your father. And before me—" he gestured at the drowned shrine, the flooded racks, the whole drowning world— "before me it rolled onto men whose names aren't written anywhere at all."

The rain had stopped. In its absence the air was thick enough to chew, and the mosquitoes came up off the water in soft grey columns.

"So," Tamuno said. "Here are my sums. I am seventy-one years old. My lungs are half silt. My eyes are going. I cannot dive, I cannot haul, I can barely mend a net without my fingers cramping. What does the company get from giving my berth to an old man? A corpse delivered to the capital, that's what — and corpses cost money to bury on dry land." He raised a hand before Jude could speak. "But you. You are twenty-two years old. You are strong, you are clever, you read figures better than the clerks who cheat us. You have a whole life that hasn't happened yet. Give me one reason — one — why the arithmetic should not go my way."

"There are four berths," Jude said quietly. "Four people. If you give me yours, someone else stays."

"Your mother will not stay. She'd fight the factor himself with a cooking pot before she'd let that barge leave without her children."

"Then Ngozi—"

"Ngozi goes. That girl has wanted the capital's schools since she could talk, and if you rob her of that to make a point about fairness, I will haunt you personally, and I am told I make a very persistent ghost." Tamuno's voice cracked then, just slightly, the level tone splintering to show the grief underneath. "Which leaves you and me, boy. One berth between us. And I am telling you, with every year of judgment left in this worn-out body: the berth is mine to give, and I give it to you."

* * *

Jude stood up. He couldn't help it; sitting felt suddenly like drowning. He walked a few steps along the levee, his back to his grandfather, looking at the water that had taken his father and now meant to take everything else.

"You think I haven't done this sum?" he said. "I do it every hour. I wake up doing it. But you're wrong about the arithmetic, Grandfather, and I'll tell you why." He turned. "Because the moment I take your place, I stop being a son and become a debtor again. Don't you see? That's what the company wants. That's what the whole rigged list wants. They struck my name so that I would owe them — owe you, owe my mother, owe Ngozi — owe everyone who crosses that water because I stayed behind. A debt like that never closes. It rolls forward. Onto my children, if I ever have them. I won't do it. I won't be the next page in their ledger."

"And so instead you'll drown in the flood?" Tamuno's voice rose for the first time, cracking across the water. "Is that your grand refusal? To die nobly in a town that emptied itself and forgot you? Boy, pride is not a boat. It will not carry you across."

"I'm not planning to drown."

"Then what? The swimming?" The old man's eyes narrowed. "You think I haven't seen you in the shallows at night, practicing your father's breath like a boy sneaking palm wine? I taught that breath to your father and his brothers. I know what it looks like when someone learns it wrong." He struggled to his feet, joints popping like green firewood, and came to stand close, so close Jude could smell the river on him, the camphor he rubbed into his chest, the faint sweetness of the kola he chewed to keep his head clear. "Listen to me. I gave your father my blessing for his last dive because he was grown and the choice was his, and I have regretted it every single morning for six years. I will not give you mine. Not for this."

"It isn't yours to give or withhold."

"Everything I have is yours to inherit," Tamuno said softly, "which means everything I refuse is also yours to inherit. That is what inheritance is, boy. It is not the chest. It is not the coin. It is the refusals." He put one hand — spotted, trembling slightly, still strong at the palm from sixty years of rope — on Jude's shoulder. "Refuse this. Refuse to trade your life for mine. An old man's life is spent coin. Yours is not."

"Then come with us," Jude said, and his voice broke despite himself. "Take the berth. Get on the barge. Let me find my own way across or my own way to stay — but don't sit here waiting for the water like a man settling an account."

For a long moment Tamuno said nothing. The river moved. Far off, the bridge groaned again, and something downstream — a loose canoe, a section of fence — knocked hollowly against the flood.

"I have buried a wife in this town," the old man said at last. "I have buried a son in this river. Every stone on this levee knows my name. When I cross that water, I cross as cargo, Jude. Cargo for my grandchildren's pity, an old relic set on a shelf in a city that smells of coal smoke. Here, at least, when the water comes for me, it comes for a man it already knows." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Jude had ever seen. "You want to save me. But some things are not savings. Some things are just what's left."

* * *

Adaeze was waiting for them when they came back down the levee path.

She stood in the doorway of the house with her arms folded, and Jude knew from the set of her shoulders that she had heard — or enough of it. Word traveled through the divers' quarter faster than floodwater; the walls were thin and the neighbors thinner. Behind her, through the doorway, he could see the house half-gutted already, the packing done in stages, the wedding chest standing alone against the wall like a widow at a funeral.

"You woke the whole street," she said. Not angry. Weary, in the way that had become her permanent condition, the weariness of a woman who had been stretching too little across too many for too long.

"Mother," Tamuno began.

"Don't." She came down the two steps into the mud, unmindful of it, and stood between them the way she had once stood between Jude and the company's debt collectors, the way she stood between her children and every hard thing the world proposed. "I heard it all. The berth. The offer. The refusing." She looked at Tamuno. "And I heard my father-in-law call his life spent coin, as if he were a sack of rotting grain he could hand off to whoever would carry it."

"Adaeze—"

"You do not get to decide what you are worth," she said, and her voice did not rise but it filled the whole street, and a curtain twitched across the lane, and she did not care. "Not you. Not the company. Not the ledger. I have watched men decide what they are worth my entire life — my father decided, and went down for pearls and never came up. My husband decided, and the river took him. Now you decide, and you expect me to stand on a barge and wave goodbye to the last elder of my family while the flood eats his house?" Her chin trembled once, and she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, at cost. "No. No, old man. I buried enough of this family. I will not bury you too."

"Then take the berth yourself," Tamuno said gently. "Let the children—"

"My children go. Both of them." She turned to Jude, and her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely certain. "Both of them," she repeated, and Jude understood that she had made her own sums in the night, and that her answer to the arithmetic was different from everyone's, and final. "Ngozi goes to school. Jude goes wherever he is going — and he *is* going somewhere, because I know my son, and when he says he isn't planning to drown, he means it, God help me, he has a plan, and I am almost afraid to ask what it is." She held up a hand against his protest. "Don't tell me. Whatever it is, I don't want to know it before the barge leaves. If I know it, I'll try to stop it, and I am too tired to fail at stopping you."

"Mother—"

"But hear me, both of you." She stepped closer, and now her voice dropped to the register she used for the things that mattered most, the low fierce murmur that had carried her children through hunger and grief and the long slow theft of their lives by the company's ledgers. "Nobody in this family buys their passage with somebody else's life. Not with a berth. Not with a bond. Not with a sacrifice dressed up as love. We have paid this company enough in blood. We will not pay each other."

The street was silent except for the water. Down at the landing, Jude could see the barge tied up, dark and patient, its four berths like four empty mouths.

Tamuno looked at his daughter-in-law for a long time. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, surprised out of him, the first laugh Jude had heard from him since the orders were posted.

"Your mother," he said to Jude, shaking his head, "was a stubborn woman. But she was a candle beside this one." He reached out and touched Adaeze's cheek, rough palm against smooth skin, an old man's blessing given without ceremony. "Three berths, then. Three travelers. And the fourth berth sails empty, and let the company explain *that* to their precious manifest."

"Three," Adaeze agreed. "And one old man who is going to eat his supper tonight whether he likes it or not, and sleep under a roof I have not yet finished packing."

She turned and went inside, and the door did not slam, because Adaeze never slammed doors; she simply closed them with a finality that was worse.

* * *

Jude stayed on the levee until the sun cleared the treeline and laid a long gold road across the floodwater, all the way to the horizon, as if the river were showing them the way and mocking them for not taking it.

Three berths. Three travelers. The arithmetic refused to balance no matter how he arranged it, and he understood now that it never would — that the company had built the imbalance into the world itself, into bridges and barges and ledgers, so that families would do their cruelty for them, so that love itself would become the instrument of the debt.

Unless the debt simply ceased to exist.

He thought of the counting house at night. Of the bond register with its columns of names and dates, of his own name entered there in a clerk's careful hand seven years ago, *indentured, term of seven, against the debts of Ekon Oyelaran, deceased.* Of the way paper burned, and the way paper could be made to say anything, if the hand that wrote it was steady enough.

His father's mark was a simple thing — a circle broken by three lines, the sign of the diving families, pressed into wax on contracts he had signed without reading. Jude had seen it a hundred times. He had practiced it absently in spilled flour, in fogged glass, in the mud of the riverbank, all his life, the way children copy the shapes of the world around them.

He had never once thought of it as forgery.

Until now.

Downstream, at the company wharf, he could see Mr. Calloway standing on the barge deck with a lantern even in daylight, checking something against a paper in his hand, and Jude felt the cold settle into him again — the cold clarity of chapter upon chapter of other men's numbers, all of it waiting, all of it watching.

In three days, the auditors' boat would arrive from the capital.

Whatever Jude was going to do, he would have to do it fast.
