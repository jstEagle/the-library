## 21. Calloway's Audit

The letter from the capital arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside the ordinary dispatch of invoices and price circulars, as if the company had chosen to deliver its judgment the way a snake delivers its venom — hidden in an ordinary bite.

*By order of the Board of Directors, Meridian Trading Company, all outstation ledgers will be subject to full internal audit commencing the third week of the flood season. Factors are reminded that discrepancies discovered during audit shall be charged against the responsible officer's bond and standing.*

Calloway read it twice at his desk with the shutters half open, letting in the smell of the river — mud and diesel and the sweet rot of things the water had already begun to claim — and then he sat very still for a long moment, listening to the counting house breathe around him.

It was not that he had anything to hide.

That was what he told himself first, and it was almost true, which was the most dangerous kind of true. He had been factor at this station for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned that no ledger in any trading house between here and the capital was clean — that cleanliness itself was a kind of fiction the head office told itself so it could sleep. There were always small kindnesses done to oneself. A crate weighed generously. A commission rounded up. A berth allocated here, a berth quietly withheld there, according to needs the head office did not need to know about.

Everyone did it. The auditors knew everyone did it. The audit was not a search for sin; it was a tax on it, a ritual, a shaking of the tree so the fruit at the top could be collected. You paid your tribute — a few pounds written off as spoilage, a clerk reprimanded for arithmetic, a show of penance — and the inspectors signed their pages and went back downriver, and the river went on being the river.

But.

He set the letter down and looked at the ledger room door across the hall, its iron-bound oak, its lock that he himself had ordered changed two years ago when old Mr. Fenwick retired. And he thought about the allocations.

* * *

The berth list for the evacuation barge had been his own work, drafted in his own hand one hot evening in June when the rains were still a rumor and the bridge still stood. He remembered it clearly because he had enjoyed it, in the way a man enjoys any problem with an elegant solution. Five berths requested by the Oyelaran household; four available after the board's reduction; and Jude Oyelaran — the best clerk he had, the only man in the counting house who could reconcile a season's accounts without error, who could read three hands of script including his dead father's, who asked no questions and wanted nothing — Jude Oyelaran's indenture ran two years more.

Two years. In two years the new wharf would be finished, the pearl concessions renegotiated, the station's books finally in the state they should have been in for a decade. He could not lose the boy now. It was not cruelty. It was arithmetic.

And so he had struck the name. Not crossed out — never crossed out, that was the mark of an amateur — but simply never entered. The family listed at four: mother, sister, grandfather, and the younger boy, Tomas, whose bond to the company was nominal, a formality of inheritance that even the strictest auditor would wave through. Jude remained on the station rolls. Jude would ride out the flood at the warehouse, drawing wages, keeping the books, exactly where the company needed him. When the waters fell and the bridge reopened, the family would return or send for him, whichever the season allowed, and no harm done to anyone except perhaps some sentiment.

He had even told himself it was a mercy of a kind. The boy had no place on that barge anyway — berths were for dependents and debt-holders of standing, and a bond-man in his fifth year was neither, whatever the local customs said. Better to make the decision cleanly, aboveboard in his own mind, than let the family tear itself apart over arithmetic at Adaeze Oyelaran's table. He had seen such tables before. He knew how love behaved when it was made to count heads.

So: no forgery. No falsification. An omission, an allocation, a judgment call within his authority as factor. If an auditor asked — and none ever had, in eleven years, because berth lists were operational documents, not financial ones — he had his reasons ready, reasonable as rain.

He repeated all of this to himself on the Tuesday afternoon, sitting with the board's letter open on his desk, and he believed it the way a man believes a rope bridge holds: fully, right up until the moment he put his weight on it.

Because here was the thing about omissions. They lived in the same house as forgeries. They slept in the same bed. And an audit, he thought, turning the letter over as though the reverse might contain softer words, an audit was a lamp carried slowly through every room of the house.

* * *

He spent the next morning tightening the counting house, and he did it the way he did everything — thoroughly, pleasantly, and without once raising his voice.

First the keys. The ledger room lock received a new barrel, brought upriver on the last mail packet, and Calloway personally distributed the three copies: one for himself, one for the senior bookkeeper Mr. Adeyemi, and one sealed in the strongbox "against emergency," which meant against everyone. He announced this at morning assembly with a smile, standing on the low step of the warehouse porch with the gray light of the coming rains behind him.

"Gentlemen," he said, "the board is sending inspectors. This reflects well on our station — they do not audit stations they distrust; they audit stations they intend to promote." A ripple of careful laughter. "From today, all entries are to be made in ink, countersigned daily, and no page removed, inserted, or amended without my initials in the margin. I want our books to be the cleanest thing between here and the capital. Let them come looking for dust and find polished brass."

He watched them as he spoke. He always watched them; it was the habit of a lifetime, the factor's craft beneath the factor's manners. Adeyemi nodding too quickly. The junior clerks exchanging glances. Old Samson at the door of the bond registry shuffling his feet like a man standing on hot ground.

And Jude Oyelaran, third from the left, taking notes in his small precise hand, his face smooth as poured wax.

Calloway looked at him a moment longer than the others — not because he suspected anything, but because he liked him, and liking a man meant watching him, the way you watched weather you depended on. There was something restful about Jude. Eleven years in this town had taught Calloway that everyone wanted something, and most people wanted loudly; but the boy wanted nothing visible, moved through the warehouse like a current through water, useful and silent. His father had been the same, they said — Tamuno's son, the diver who died owing the company money, though Calloway had inherited that debt rather than created it and had never lost sleep over it either way.

"Oyelaran," he said now, and the boy's eyes came up, dark and level. "You'll prepare the reconciliation summaries for the inspectors. All seven years of the concession accounts. I want them bound and indexed."

"Yes, sir."

"You know those books better than anyone living. Even better than I do." He smiled to take any sting from the truth. "That's not flattery. That's a warning. If there's an error anywhere in those volumes, you'll be the one who finds it before they do."

Something passed behind the boy's eyes — quick, unreadable, gone. "Yes, sir," he said again, and bent to his notes, and Calloway turned back to the assembly and thought no more of it.

That was the tragedy of the man, though of course he did not know it yet, would not know it for many chapters of his life: that he saw everything except what was directly in front of him, because he had spent so long training himself to see people as instruments that he had lost the art of seeing them as faces. He noticed that Jude was reliable the way he noticed that the roof did not leak. It did not occur to him to ask what the roof thought about it.

* * *

*

In the afternoon he took inventory of himself, which was the harder accounting.

He cleared his desk of everything personal — not that there was much: a photograph of Miriam at eight years old on the steps of the capital school, a brass letter-opener bought in a port market twenty years ago, a bottle of the good rum he allowed himself one finger of on Sundays. The photograph he put in the drawer. The rum he considered, then left; hiding it would look worse than showing it.

Then he began the audit of his own small economies, going through them the way a man goes through his pockets before a border crossing.

The spoilage figures — honest, or honest enough; the river really did eat cargo, and he had never inflated it beyond plausibility. The commission on the pearl concessions — ten percent where the standard was seven, justified in writing as "local hazard allowance," a phrase he had inherited from Fenwick along with the desk chair. Three hundred and forty pounds over eleven years, spread so thin across so many seasons that it would take an inspector with a grudge and a month of leisure to assemble it into a crime. The advance he had drawn against next year's salary. The two cases of company brandy entered as "sampling loss."

Small sins. Respectable sins. Sins with receipts.

And then, last, the berth allocation, sitting in its folder in the operational cabinet like a stone at the bottom of a clear stream. He took it out and read his own handwriting and felt — for the first time since June — the faint cold breath of doubt move across the back of his neck.

Here was the trouble. Here was the flaw in the elegant solution, and he saw it now with terrible clarity, the way you see a crack in glass only when you hold it up to light: the board's directive reducing five berths to four was dated the ninth of June. His allocation striking Jude Oyelaran was dated the seventh.

Two days before he had the authority.

He stared at the dates. He remembered — he genuinely remembered — believing he had waited for the directive. But memory was a servant, not a witness; it told you what you needed. Perhaps the board's letter had come by word of mouth first, ahead of the paper. Perhaps. Or perhaps he had simply known what the answer would be and moved early, the way a confident merchant ships goods before the contract is signed — and had backdated, or mis-dated, or merely been careless, without ever noticing the difference between those three things.

An omission, he had called it. But an omission with a wrong date beside it began to look like design. And design, in a ledger, had another name.

He put the folder back and locked the cabinet and stood a while in the dim of the records room, among the shelves of bound years, smelling the paper and the river damp that got into everything no matter what you paid for locks. The rain had started outside, the first real rain of the season, drumming on the roof tiles like fingers practicing.

Three weeks, the letter had said. Inspectors from the capital, two of them, with the new standardized forms — the forms that asked, for the first time in company history, not only about money but about personnel. Bond registers. Berth allocations. Discharge and extension records. He had read the sample schedule twice and felt the questions like hands patting down his coat.

If they pulled the bond register — and they would, it was item fourteen on the schedule — they would see Jude Oyelaran, bond commenced, term seven years, two years remaining, status: active. Then they might pull the berth list to cross-check household allocations, because item fifteen concerned dependents, and there they would find a family of five reduced to four, and the missing name would be the same name sitting in front of them in the bond register, active, retained, employed.

It was defensible. He could defend it. Retention of skilled labor during flood closure was sound practice; he had precedents; he would write a memorandum tonight, dated properly, explaining the rationale as if it had always existed.

But a memorandum written after the fact was a confession wearing a clean shirt, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

* * *

*

That evening he dined at home, in the factor's house above the compound, under the slow ceiling fan, and watched his daughter across the table and understood nothing at all.

Miriam was quiet these days. He attributed it to the season, to the confinement of the closing town, to the natural melancholy of a girl of nineteen stuck in a river port when her school friends were all in the capital. He had, in fact, been composing in his head the conversation in which he offered to send her back to her aunt's house in the city before the bridge closed — an act of generosity he had already rewarded himself for twice.

"You're staring at your fish," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking all week. The whole house can hear it. It sounds like the rain."

He laughed despite himself. She had her mother's tongue, dead these nine years, and every time Miriam used it he felt the peculiar grief of being scolded by a ghost. "The board is sending auditors," he said. It was harmless to tell her; she cared for ledgers the way she cared for weather in another country. "I'm making sure everything is in order."

"Is it?"

"Of course it is."

She looked at him then — a look he did not understand, level and dark, so like the clerk Oyelaran's look that afternoon that had he been a more perceptive man he might have found the resemblance interesting rather than invisible — and she said, "You always say that. Of course it is. Before you even check."

"That's what thirty years in the trade teaches you. Where the errors live."

"And where do they live, Papa?"

"In the margins," he said, rising, gathering his napkin, pleased with the phrase. "Nobody audits the margins. That's where I keep mine tidy."

She did not laugh. He noticed that she did not laugh, and filed it away in the drawer where he kept everything about his daughter that he did not wish to examine, a drawer that had grown heavy over the years and whose lock, like so many locks in his life, he trusted far more than he should have.

* * *

*

He worked late that night, alone in the counting house, lamplit against the rain.

This was the hour he loved best and had never told anyone so — the counting house at night, the river muttering beyond the shutters, the great ledgers open like sleeping animals, warm-smelling and heavy. Other men feared numbers. He had built his whole life on the opposite faith: that numbers were the only honest things in the world, that a column of figures never lied to you, never flattered you, never left you. People lied. Weather lied. The river lied most beautifully of all, giving pearls with one hand and taking divers with the other. But four plus four was four plus four in Lagos, in London, at the bottom of the sea.

He worked down the checklist he had drafted for the clerks, initialing entries, squaring columns. He wrote the memorandum defending the berth retention — dated it carefully, correctly, honestly *now*, because he had decided that honesty about dishonesty was the last integrity available to him — and sealed it into the operational file. He checked the strongbox seal on the spare key. He tested the ledger-room lock twice, and stood a moment in its doorway looking at the shelf where the bond register lived, its spine worn pale where hands had pulled it year after year.

Item fourteen, he thought. Bond registers.

And a thought came to him then, unbidden, unwelcome — the kind of thought that arrives in a quiet room at night and cannot be sent away. It was this: that in eleven years he had never once audited the bond register himself. Not truly. Oh, he had signed its annual certifications, had recited its totals to the district office — one hundred twelve bonds active, aggregate indebtedness so-and-so — but the individual pages, the terms and discharges and deaths and inheritances, the human arithmetic underneath the total? Fenwick had kept those books before him, and Fenwick's predecessor before Fenwick, and the register was a palimpsest of thirty years of other men's hands, other men's margins, other men's small economies. Divers' debts rolled into sons' bonds. Extensions granted for favors. Discharges sold privately for cash that appeared nowhere. He knew such things happened — he had permitted some, profited marginally from others, forgotten most.

If the inspectors pulled that register and read it line by line, they would find a riverbed of silt thirty years deep. Not his silt, mostly. But silt does not care whose body it settled on. It clouds the water all the same.

He closed the register gently, as one closes a coffin, and stood in the lamplight feeling the walls of his orderly world draw in around him by inches — not collapsing, nothing so dramatic, merely tightening, the way a knot tightens, patiently, elegantly, from both ends.

Somewhere below, in the town, the rain went on falling on the lantern market stalls, on the flooded shallows, on the divers' quarter where a family was counting heads at a table he preferred not to imagine. Somewhere a night watchman made his rounds past the warehouse, boots ringing on wet boards. The river rose a finger's width by morning, it always did in this season, patient as debt.

Calloway blew out his lamp and stood a moment in the dark, and the last thing he thought, before climbing the stairs to his bed, was a small satisfied thought, and it was this:

Whatever anyone was planning in this town — whatever small sins slept in whatever margins — no one would dare touch the books now. Not with the auditors' boat three weeks out. The risk was ruin. Only a madman or a desperate man would try, and he had made certain, with locks and initials and seals, that even a desperate man would fail.

He slept well.

Down the hill, in the dark, in the counting house he had just locked, a lamp had not quite gone out — a thread of oil still burning in a clerk's private corner, a page of practiced signatures drying under a blotter, the letters *J.O.* written in a dead diver's hand, over and over, getting truer each time.

The rain covered the sound of the pen.
