## 22. The Lantern Market Burns

The lantern market opened at dusk, as it always did on the last dry night of the season, when the river dropped low enough between its banks to seem almost tame and the whole town agreed, for one evening, to forget what was coming.

Jude had not wanted to come. The barge left in nine days; the counting house ledger sat in his head like a stone he had swallowed; and every coin he earned now felt like it belonged to a future that no longer included him. But his mother had pressed two cowries into his palm and told him to buy oil before the prices rose again, and Ngozi had said, with the particular brightness she wore these days like borrowed cloth, *You should see the lights one more time, Jude. You never look at anything.*

So he came. He moved through the crowd the way he moved through everything — useful and unseen — carrying a jug of palm oil in one hand and keeping his shoulder turned to the stalls so the press of bodies could not take it from him. The market had spilled down toward the water as it always did, a hundred wooden frames strung along the embankment road, each hung with paper lanterns the color of embers, of eggshell, of unripe mango. Merchants sold them by the armful. Children ran beneath them trailing sparks from punk sticks. Somewhere near the fish sheds a drummer was working up to something, and the smell of fried plantain and lamp oil and river mud lay over everything like a second air.

He saw her before he let himself admit he was looking.

Miriam Calloway stood at the lantern-seller's stall near the old customs post, and she was laughing at something the seller's daughter had said, her head tipped back, her hair coming loose from its plait in the heat. She wore plain cotton tonight — her mother's doing, perhaps, or her own small rebellion — but even dressed as a merchant's daughter instead of a factor's, there was no mistaking her. She held herself the way people hold themselves when they have been watched their whole lives: perfectly, and without knowing they were doing it.

She should not have been here alone. Neither should he be standing where she might turn and find him. Every rule the town kept without writing down said so. But the crowd was thick and slow-moving, and Jude found, to his shame and his wonder both, that his feet had already decided.

*One word,* he told himself. *One word and then you go back to your side of the market.*

"You're holding that jug like it owes you money," Miriam said, without turning around.

His chest went tight and warm and afraid all at once. "It does," he managed. "Everything in this town owes someone money."

She turned then, and the light from the stall caught her face, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all. Around them the market roared on — the drummers, the haggling, a child shrieking with delight as a spark chased his heels — and none of it touched the small cleared space where the two of them stood.

"I thought you'd be packing," she said.

"Tomorrow." He shifted the jug to his other hand. "Tonight my mother ordered me into the crowd. She says a man who forgets how festivals smell has already left."

"She's wise." Miriam looked past him, out over the water, where the last daylight was going copper behind the far mangroves. "Nine days," she said quietly.

"I know."

"My father talks about the manifest at dinner. Berths, tonnage, the tide tables. As if we were all cargo being weighed for export." Her voice was level, but Jude heard the splinter in it, the same one he had carried since the night he read the list. "He asked me yesterday if I was excited to travel."

"What did you say?"

"I said I hadn't decided." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Which is apparently a scandalous answer from a girl who is supposed to be grateful."

A spark drifted down between them, orange dying to black before it reached the ground. Jude looked at it rather than at her, because looking at her directly had become, over these months, a thing his body did not entirely permit.

"There will be lanterns on the barge too," he said. "The company hangs them off the stern rail. My father used to say—" He stopped.

"Say what?"

"That a lantern on dark water is just a way of telling the river where you are." His mouth was dry. "I haven't decided yet whether that's a comfort or an invitation."

Miriam laughed — a real laugh this time, startled out of her — and several heads turned, and she did not care, and watching her not care was the most dangerous thing Jude had ever seen.

* * *

They walked.

Neither of them suggested it aloud; it simply happened, the way water finds the low place. They drifted together along the edge of the embankment road while the market thickened around them, talking about nothing that mattered and everything underneath it. She told him about the schoolmistress in the capital who supposedly owned forty books and lent them out to girls, which Ngozi would have traded a year of her life to hear. He told her about the night dives his grandfather still made him practice, the breath held low in the belly, the river's cold fingers finding every seam of a boy's ribs. She listened the way almost no one had ever listened to him — completely, as though what he carried were worth the weight of carrying.

"You dive," she said, "and I read. Between us we might survive one interesting life."

"You'll survive several," he said. "Yours has berths in it."

"And yours?" The question landed soft and terrible between them. She knew. Of course she knew — she had read the list over his shoulder in the counting house, or close enough to it; she had gone pale and said nothing, and neither of them had named it since. "Jude. What happens to you in nine days?"

"The company hasn't finalized the allocations," he said, which was the company's phrase, which was a lie wearing a lanyard.

She stopped walking. In the lantern light her face was very clear to him, clearer than he wanted anything to be.

"You're going to tell me it's fine," she said, "in that voice you use with the clerks. And then you're going to walk me back to the customs post, and bow, and disappear, and I'm going to spend nine days watching my father write numbers that decide your life, and neither of us is going to say one true thing to each other before the water closes the bridge."

The drummers struck up louder somewhere behind them. A vendor cried the price of grilled fish. The ordinary world went on insisting on itself.

"One true thing," Jude said at last. His heart was hammering against the inside of his ribs like a diver signaling from below. "That night at the market — the first time — when you bought me the lantern. I've kept it. I never lit it. I don't know why I'm telling you this except that you asked."

Miriam looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the rim of the oil jug in his hands — only that, only the clay, cool under her fingertip, and it undid him more thoroughly than any touch of skin could have.

"Keep it unlit," she said. "For now."

And that was when the world ended.

* * *

Later, everyone would argue about the first flame. The lantern-seller swore it was a punk stick dropped by a running child. The drummer claimed a gust off the river had driven a hanging lantern into the awning of the dried-fish stall. Elder Bassi, who missed nothing, said quietly that the wind had changed an hour before anyone noticed, and that the merchants had stacked their oil crates too close to the paper walls because the flood season always scattered them anyway, and what did it matter, the water takes everything eventually.

What Jude knew was this: there was a sound like a great indrawn breath, the whole market inhaling at once, and then the awning above the fish stalls was burning — not catching, but *burning*, the flame moving through the dry canvas faster than a man can run, and the paper lanterns overhead began to go up one after another like a string of small suns being counted off by some enormous patient hand.

Then the screaming started.

The crowd did not scatter; crowds never do, not at first. It *surged* — three hundred bodies all deciding at once that the river road was the way out, and the river road was narrow, and the stalls were on both sides of it, and the fire was jumping the road on the backs of the sparks. A crate of lamp oil went up near the customs post with a sound like thunder tearing cloth, and after that the smoke came low and black and hungry, rolling along the ground the way floodwater rolls, filling lungs, erasing the world to a few feet of orange glare and coughing dark.

Jude lost sight of Miriam in the space of a single heartbeat.

"MIRIAM!" The name tore out of him before any caution in him could catch it, before he remembered who she was, who he was, who was watching. The smoke swallowed the word.

He went toward the fire. Everyone else was going the other way, shoving past him, and he shoved back against the current of them, using his shoulders the way his grandfather had taught him to use his body in a heavy current — sideways, patient, giving ground to gain it. The heat hit him in waves, then all at once, a wall he had to lean into. Through the smoke he could see the lantern frames collapsing, the strings of them falling burning into the crowd like rain made of ruin.

He found her by accident, the way divers find things — not by looking straight on but by feeling for what does not belong. A shape crouched low against the customs post wall, a girl dragging another girl — the seller's daughter, he realized, the one Miriam had laughed with — whose ankle was pinned under a fallen frame, its paper long since burned away to a skeleton of black wire.

"Her foot!" Miriam screamed, or tried to; it came out as a rasp. Her plait was smoking at the ends. "Jude — her foot is caught—"

He didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. He dropped the oil jug — his mother's errand, his mother's cowries, gone into the mud forever — and got his hands under the frame, and lifted. The wire bit into his palms and the heat came off it in a breath that scorched the hair on his forearms, and the girl screamed and came free, and Miriam dragged her up onto her good leg, and then the three of them were stumbling together through smoke so thick it was less air than absence, following the pull of cooler air the way Tamuno had taught him to follow the current's cold seams toward open water.

They broke out of it near the fish sheds, where the crowd had thinned and the bucket lines were forming — men passing leather buckets hand over hand from the river, their faces lit orange from below like masks from some older, crueler festival. Behind them half the embankment road was a single roaring sheet of flame, and the paper lanterns that had not yet caught were drifting loose off their strings, dozens of them, rising on the heat like souls deciding whether to stay.

Miriam put the seller's daughter into the arms of the bucket men, who carried her off wailing toward the herb-women. Then she turned to Jude, and they looked at each other across four feet of firelight and ash, and everything they had spent months not saying stood naked between them in front of two hundred witnesses.

There was soot on her cheekbone. There was blood on his palms, black in the firelight, from the wire. Her hands found his face — both hands, no pretense, no market-day distance — and held it, and he heard himself make a sound he had never made before, low and broken, and her forehead came down against his, and they stayed like that, breathing each other's smoke, while the town burned behind them and the bucket line passed and passed and passed.

"Your hands," she whispered.

"Forget my hands."

"I won't." Her voice cracked clean through. "I'm done pretending I will."

And that was the moment Elder Bassi, standing at the edge of the bucket line with a wet cloth over his mouth and his old eyes missing absolutely nothing, lowered the cloth and looked at them — at the factor's daughter with her hands on the indentured clerk's ruined palms, at the pair of them framed in firelight like a scene from a story no one in this town would have dared to invent — and slowly, deliberately, turned away.

But others did not turn away. Others stared. Old Mama Ekwueme, who sold pepper and gossip in equal measure, had stopped mid-bucket-pass with her mouth open. Two of Calloway's own warehousemen stood by the third shed, and one of them elbowed the other, and the other nodded, once, slow as a verdict.

By morning, the fire would have a dozen causes and the market would be half ash and the seller's daughter would live with a scarred ankle and a debt to the Oyelaran boy that her family would speak of carefully, in lowered voices, for years. But none of that was the fire that mattered.

The fire that mattered was already spreading — quiet, invisible, unstoppable — through the factor's house on the hill, through the counting house, through the quarter, through every kitchen and veranda where this town kept its true ledgers.

Jude felt it start even as Miriam's hands fell away from his face, even as the two of them stepped apart into the ordinary distance the world demanded, even as she mouthed, one last time, *nine days* — and he understood, with the cold clarity of deep water closing over his head:

Some fires you escape.

Some fires announce you.

By dawn, everyone in Petra's Reach would know exactly where the factor's daughter had spent the burning of the lantern market — and whose name she had called through the smoke.

And Mr. Calloway would be waiting to hear it.
