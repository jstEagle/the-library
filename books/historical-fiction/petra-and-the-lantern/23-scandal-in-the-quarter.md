## 23. Scandal in the Quarter

The house knew before Miriam did.

She understood this the way one understands weather — not by seeing the storm arrive but by feeling it in the pressure of the air, in the way the servants moved through the corridors of the factor's residence with their eyes down and their steps quickened, carrying trays they did not need to carry past rooms they did not need to pass. By seven that morning the household had already decided something about her, and she had not yet been told what.

She sat at her dressing table while Marta brushed out her hair, and she watched her own face in the mirror the way one watches a stranger across a market square — with curiosity, and a little wariness. There were shadows under her eyes. She had slept badly, dreaming of smoke and of a hand gripping hers through heat, and waking had not entirely dispersed the dream; the smell of char still seemed to live somewhere at the back of her throat.

"You'll want the blue today," Marta said. Not asked — said. That was the second wrongness. Marta always asked.

"Why the blue?"

"Madam Okafor is coming at ten." Marta's hands did not pause in their work. "And the mistress wishes you dressed for receiving."

Miriam met her own eyes in the glass. *The mistress.* Her stepmother had been called worse things behind her back and better ones to her face, but hearing the title applied now, on this morning, made the word sound like a verdict being read aloud.

"My father?"

"Gone to the counting house early. He said—" Marta hesitated for the first time, and the hesitation told Miriam more than any completed sentence would have. "He said you would understand everything by noon."

* * *

She dressed in the blue because there was no dignity in dressing otherwise, and because she had learned long ago that in this house, obedience bought information. The gown was deep river-blue, the color her father had once said matched her mother's eyes, and wearing it was a small act of archaeology: every time she put it on she dug up a dead woman and carried her around the house on her shoulders. Today the weight of it felt deliberate, as though the household had chosen the color for her the way one chooses mourning clothes.

Her stepmother received Mrs. Okafor in the front parlor, the good room with the louvered shutters and the fan that a boy turned from the corner when the heat pressed too hard against the glass. Miriam was summoned to pour. This too was theater — her stepmother poured beautifully herself, and always had — but theater required an audience on both sides of the teapot, and so Miriam stood at the sideboard and measured leaves and waited to learn what she had done.

Mrs. Okafor was the widow of a shipping magnate and the aunt-by-marriage of a young man named Edmund Voss, who was heir to a third of the Meridian Trading Company's capital accounts and all of its ambitions. Miriam had met him four times. He was handsome in the way that ledger paper is handsome: ruled, orderly, promising a future already calculated. He spoke of the capital as though it were a possession he was merely waiting to inherit, which in fairness it nearly was.

"How well you look," Mrs. Okafor said, taking her cup. "The country air agrees with you."

"The river air," Miriam corrected gently. "There is nothing of the country here."

"No," Mrs. Okafor agreed, and smiled with all the warmth of a bill of exchange. "Nothing of the country."

Her stepmother laughed, a bright, rehearsed sound, and said something about how the town might be provincial but the company's business was anything but, and Miriam understood then, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, what the morning's machinery was for.

They were going to announce the engagement before luncheon.

* * *

Afterward she found her stepmother alone in the corridor between the parlor and the stair, arranging flowers that someone else had cut, moving stems half an inch left and right as though the fate of households hung upon such distances.

"You might have warned me," Miriam said.

"I might have," her stepmother agreed, without looking up. A frangipani bloom went into the vase, came out, went back in rotated. "But warnings give people time to argue, and arguments leave marks. I prefer announcements. An announcement is already true by the time anyone hears it."

"He hasn't asked me."

"He asked your father three weeks ago."

Three weeks. Three weeks ago she had been — what? Reading in the garden. Sitting with her books. Walking home from the lantern market with a paper lantern swinging from her finger and a diver's son laughing beside her about the ridiculous painted fish on it, saying it looked like his father's stories of the deep-water pearl-fish, all mouth and no substance. Three weeks ago her father had been selling her future over brandy while she traded jokes across a stall counter with a boy whose debt the family fortune held like a hook holds a fish.

"And Papa accepted on my behalf."

"It is done properly, in these matters. Fathers accept; daughters consent afterward. It has been the order of things since before either of us was born, and the order of things has kept this family respectable through two floods, one scandal, and your father's entire career."

"One scandal?" Miriam said. "Whose?"

Her stepmother's hands stilled over the flowers. When she looked up, there was something almost human in her face — fatigue, perhaps, or its cousin, fear. "Yours," she said quietly. "Or nearly. Do you think I don't hear what the quarter says? A fire in the market, and the factor's daughter pulled from the smoke by a warehouse clerk. By *that* clerk. You were seen, Miriam. You were seen by people who count everything, and what they cannot count they invent, and what they invent they sell. Your father woke on Thursday to find the story in three drawing rooms instead of two. On Friday it was in eight. Do you know what stops a story like that? Nothing. Nothing but a bigger story. So we are giving them a bigger story." She set down the flower stem at last and folded her hands. "Edmund Voss arrives on Saturday. The engagement will be announced at the reception. You will be radiant."

"And if I am not radiant? If I refuse?"

Her stepmother considered her for a long moment, the way one considers a sum that has come out wrong. "Then I will spend the rest of my life managing what you have made," she said, "and you will spend the rest of yours discovering how narrow a woman's world becomes when she makes it narrow herself. I am not your enemy, child. I am simply the only person in this house telling you the truth."

* * *

The truth, Miriam thought that afternoon, standing at the window of her room with the river laid out below her like a sentence awaiting completion, was that she had known this was coming. Not the shape of it — not Edmund Voss specifically, not the reception, not the blue dress — but the principle. She had grown up inside the principle the way a bird grows up inside an aviary: aware always of the bars, never quite believing they would close.

Her whole life had been a lesson in what she was for. She was for the company. Her father had lost her mother to the fever the same year he'd gained his factorship, and he had grieved, genuinely, deeply — she remembered him weeping at the grave, remembered his hand shaking on her small shoulder — and then he had turned that grief into industry, and the industry into a philosophy, and the philosophy into a life in which everything, including his daughter, was an asset to be maintained, insured, and eventually invested. He loved her. She had never doubted it. But his love was a ledger entry, entered honestly, kept accurately, and balanced against other entries: the company, the name, the future. She had watched him weigh her against columns her whole childhood. She had simply believed, until recently, that she would always come out ahead.

Now she knew what the other column contained. A boy named Jude Oyelaran, who quoted poetry he'd read by smuggled candlelight and dove like his father taught him and looked at her as though she were a person rather than a position. A boy whose name she had checked, more than once, against lists she had no right to be reading — she knew where her father kept the berth allocations, knew the weight of the drawer, knew the particular quality of the lamplight in the counting house after dark. She had seen the list. She had seen that his name was not on it. She had said nothing to her father, and everything to Jude, and offered — God help her, she had actually offered — to intercede, as though love could be administered like a recommendation letter.

He had refused her. Politely. Painfully. As if the offer itself were the wound.

And now the house was preparing to marry her to a third of a trading empire, and the whole quarter would watch her say yes, and the yes would bury the smoke and the fire and the hand in hers, and everything would be tidy again, counted, ruled, balanced.

Except.

Except that she had spent last night awake, and in the waking she had discovered something she could not un-discover: that she did not want to survive this. Survival was the family trade. Her father survived; her stepmother survived magnificently; Edmund Voss would no doubt survive with a portfolio. But she had pulled a boy out of burning smoke and felt, for eleven seconds, more alive than in twenty years of being preserved — and she could not go back to preservation knowing that. It would be like returning to a cage after learning the shape of sky.

* * *

Saturday came gray and windless, the kind of day when the river lay flat as poured tin and the heat sat on the town like a creditor. The reception filled the factor's residence by four o'clock: company men in white linen gone translucent with sweat, their wives fanning themselves with programs, the lesser clerks hovering near the food, the greater clerks hovering near Edmund Voss, who wore cream and gold and looked, Miriam thought, exactly like what he was — money waiting to be spent by committee.

Her father greeted guests at the door with the easy warmth he reserved for people who mattered. When Miriam descended the stairs in white — her stepmother had insisted; white photographed the moment, whatever that meant, white erased the blue — a hush ran through the parlor like a draft finding a crack under a door.

Edmund took her hand and raised it to his lips. His palm was dry. She noticed this with strange clarity, the way one notices details in a dream: dry palm, wedding-band tan line from some previous understanding broken off in the capital, a faint smell of bay rum and ink.

"Miss Calloway," he said. "I hope you'll forgive the abruptness of all this. Your father and I felt there was no sense wasting a fine season."

*Wasting a fine season.* As though seasons were berths, allocated by the company, first come first served.

"I'm sure," Miriam said, "we shall all be very efficient."

Someone laughed — too loudly — and covered it, and the reception resumed its hum, and her father led them both to the center of the room where the light from the shuttered windows fell in slats across the floor, and Miriam understood that this was it, this was the moment, the announcement that would quench the fireside gossip and seal her into the company's ledgers as securely as any indentured clerk.

Her father raised his glass. The room quieted. Somewhere outside, far off toward the bridge, a barge horn sounded, low and mournful, and Miriam thought absurdly of lanterns going out along the water.

"Friends," her father began, "it is my great pleasure—"

"Papa."

The word left her before she had decided to release it. It was not loud. It barely carried past the first row. But the room was tuned to silence just then, waiting for the announcement, and so the small word dropped into the quiet like a stone into a well, and everyone heard the depth of it.

Her father lowered his glass a fraction. His face did not change — he was too good a factor for that — but she saw his eyes do the arithmetic, rapid and cold: daughter, audience, investors, rumor, cost.

"Miriam, dear, we're—"

"I have something to say first." Her voice steadied as it went on, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake apart. "You've all heard the story of the lantern market. Some of you have been kind enough to tell it to each other all week. So let me tell you the version I know." She turned, slowly, so the whole room caught her profile, then her face. "There was a fire. And a man — not a clerk, a man — went into the smoke after strangers who weren't his to save. And I went in after *him*. Because I would rather stand next to that man in a burning market than sit in the finest paroon in the capital with anyone else the company might choose for me."

Silence. The terrible, ringing silence of forty mouths opening and closing.

Edmund Voss's expression did not change either, and somehow that was worse than outrage. He simply looked at her the way one looks at a shipment arrived damaged — assessing salvage value.

"Mr. Voss," Miriam said to him directly, "I am sorry. Truly. You deserve a wife who chooses you in daylight, not one handed to you in the dark to smother a rumor. I won't be that wife."

"Miriam." Her father's voice now, low, pitched for her alone, though the whole room strained to hear. "Think carefully about what you are doing."

"I have thought of nothing else for three weeks, Papa. Since you sold me."

The words landed harder than she intended — she saw them land, saw the flinch he mastered a half-second too late — and for one suspended moment she wanted to take them back, wanted to believe there was still a version of this evening where she said yes and lived a small, upholstered, suffocating life and never learned whether she could have been braver.

Then she thought of the berth list, and the absent name, and the boy teaching himself his drowned father's breath in the flood shallows because no barge would carry him — and the wanting to take it back passed through her and out the other side, leaving something harder underneath.

"I withdraw my consent," she said clearly, to the room, to her father, to the company, to the whole listening quarter beyond the shutters. "Whatever comes of it."

She set down her untouched glass, walked the length of the parlor through the parted crowd, and climbed the stairs without running, which was the hardest thing she had ever done.

Behind her, in the ringing quiet, she heard her father begin, smoothly, terribly, to laugh — a warm host's laugh, covering everything — and heard him say, "Forgive my daughter, friends; the season's heat affects us all—" and knew the machine was already turning, already deciding what story would replace the story she had told.

In her room she closed the door and stood with her back against it, heart hammering, white dress catching the last gray light. Downstairs, the reception resumed. It sounded, from here, like water rising.

She had chosen publicly. Now she would learn what the choosing cost — and she suspected, with a clarity that felt almost calm, that it would cost exactly as much as it was worth.

* * *

It was Marta who brought the news at dusk, breathless, having run the length of the gallery stairs.

"The factor's asking for you. No—" Marta caught herself, swallowed. "Not the factor. A messenger came from the company board, an hour ago. They've convened tonight. Your father's gone down to the office. And Miss—" Marta's eyes were wide, and frightened, and alive. "Miss, the boy's name is on everyone's lips now too. The warehouse clerks are saying the fire wasn't chance. They're saying he saved you, and they're saying the board means to make an example of *someone*, and your father—" She stopped, wrung her apron. "Your father sent word. He says come down tonight. He says you'll choose after all, one way or the other, before the barge sails."

Miriam looked past her, out the window, to where the first lamps of the town were being lit along the drowning street — small flames in paper skins, going up one by one against the coming flood.

"Tell him," she said, reaching for her shawl, "I'm already choosing."
