## 24. Jude's Reckoning

The quarter smelled of woodsmoke and wet ash for two days after the market burned, and Jude walked through it with his head down and his ears burning hotter than any stall had.

They saw him. That was the wound that would not close — not the smoke in his lungs, not the burn along the back of his hand where a flaming awning had kissed him as he pulled Miriam Calloway clear of the collapsing walkway. The burn he could bear; pain was an old countryman of his. What he could not bear was the seeing. Old Mama Ekwueme at her cassava pile, her mouth making a small round O. Two of Calloway's warehouse clerks by the well, their laughter cutting off when he passed, which was worse than the laughter itself. A boy no older than ten pointing with a fish bone and saying, loud as a church bell, *that's the one who carried the factor's daughter out of the fire.*

Carried. As if she were cargo. As if he had stolen something.

By the third morning the story had grown a tail: that the lantern Jude held when they found them behind the burnt stalls had been lit before the fire began. That the fire itself was only the sky agreeing with what everyone already suspected. Rumor needed no proof; rumor was water, and it found every crack in the town.

Jude carried crates. It was what he did, what he had always done, what the company paid him barely enough to keep doing — and he carried them now with a new knowledge sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. He had spent seven years learning to be unseen, and in one night of smoke he had become the most seen man in the lower quarter.

*Let them look,* something said in him, low and unfamiliar. *Let them look and see nothing worth keeping.*

He did not believe it yet. But he wrote it down in himself the way he wrote everything, in the careful clerk's hand his father had never lived to see him learn, and waited to see what it would grow into.

* * *

His mother knew before he spoke.

Adaeze had a way of reading him that had nothing to do with love and everything to do with arithmetic — she had counted five children through famine years, and counting teaches you to weigh things exactly. When he came in that evening she looked up from the rice pot, and her eyes went over him once, top to bottom, the way she checked a market purchase for rot.

"You have decided something," she said.

"I have thought about something."

"Thinking is how you decide slowly." She stirred. "Sit. Eat."

He sat. He ate. Around them the house was already half-gutted for the journey — Ngozi's books tied in oilcloth bundles, Tamuno's diving weights racked by the door like a choir waiting for its cue, the walls showing pale rectangles where pictures had hung. His mother's wedding chest stood alone against the far wall, the last thing unpacked, the last thing that would be packed. She had not let anyone touch it.

"Ngozi is at Bassi's," Adaeze said. "She is telling Elder Bassi's wife every detail of the capital's schools, and Elder Bassi's wife is pretending not to have heard it forty times. Your grandfather is on the river. So." She set down the spoon. "Tell me."

And because it was his mother, and because lying to her had always cost more than truth, he told her — not all of it, but the shape of it. That the berth list he had read in the counting house was not the list that mattered. That there was another page, in Calloway's own hand, with the true allocation. That his name was on no page at all except the bond register, where it would sit for two more years while the river ate the bridge and the company kept its useful invisible boy right where it wanted him.

"They will lose the whole quarter to the flood," Jude said. "The warehouses go under to the second beam. They know it. Everyone knows it. And they would rather write off the stock than write off me."

Adaeze was quiet for a long time. The rice pot breathed its steam between them.

"And Miriam Calloway," his mother said at last, very evenly, "is part of this."

"Miriam is—" He stopped. Started again. "Miriam offered to speak to her father. To buy my place. Her father has now announced her engagement to some heir of the company in Lagos, and she is standing in that house like a bird in a covered cage, and every tongue in this town is wagging about what I did or didn't do behind the burnt stalls, and none of it — none of it, Mama — changes what I am to Meridian Trading. I am a line in a book."

"A line in a book," Adaeze repeated. Something moved behind her face, old and banked, the same coal-fire Jude had carried since he was nine years old watching men carry his father's gear up from the water without ever carrying his father.

"Yes," Jude said. "A line in a book."

He watched his mother understand him one step before he understood himself. That was her gift and her curse; she always arrived first at the ugly places.

"No," she said.

"Mama—"

"*No.*" Not loud. Adaeze almost never raised her voice; she saved it the way she saved rice, against hunger. "I know what you are holding behind your teeth, Jude Oyelaran, because you get that face from your father, God drown me for saying it kindly. You are holding the ledger."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. Neither of them touched it.

"If they will not put my name on the barge," Jude said, each word placed like a foot on wet stone, "then I will take my name out of their book entirely. Not scratched — that they can un-scratch. Rewritten. Discharged. Paid. A dead uncle, a settled debt, a closed account. Let them audit it and find a bond that no longer exists. Let Calloway stand in front of his board and explain why the indenture papers of a warehouse clerk say what they say — when his own berth allocations, the ones *he* forged, sit three shelves away in the same room."

"You would hang yourself beside him."

"He built the rope, Mama. I am only learning to tie knots." He heard how it sounded and hated it and did not take it back. "You and Ngozi and Grandfather go on the barge. All four berths, used or unused, it doesn't matter — you go. And whatever happens after, no one can be traded for me. No one can be sold to cover me. If Calloway comes for the family to punish me, he finds nothing to seize. My debt dies the day the ink dries. That is the whole design. That is the entire architecture of it."

His mother looked at him across the steam and the lamplight, and for a moment he saw her young — saw her the way she must have been on the day she packed that wedding chest, some girl from upriver marrying a diver with salt still in his hair, believing that love was a thing you could carry.

"Do you know what happens to men caught forging in the counting houses?" she asked. "In the capital they flog them. Here, in company towns, they do worse. They extend. They add years. They make examples. Your father died owing these people, Jude. I have buried enough to them. I will not visit you in their custody."

"Then help me not be caught."

"That is not a plan. That is a prayer with ink on it."

"It is the only plan left." His voice cracked on it, and he let it crack; there was no one else alive he would allow to hear that sound. "Every other road is closed. The bridge closes in days. The barge leaves without me. Miriam cannot save me — her father has made sure of that, and every hour she tries, she sinks deeper into her own cage. Grandfather would give his berth but I will not take it, and even if I did, the bond follows me across the water. There is no road out of this that runs through honesty. Honesty got my father drowned. Honesty got me bound at nine years old. So I am going to lie, Mama. Once. Carefully. In a dead man's hand."

Silence. The pot cooled. Somewhere outside, the flood-water lapped at the lowest step of the quarter — a soft, patient sound, like something licking its lips.

Adaeze rose, took the lid off the pot, and served him a second portion without being asked. In her language, in the grammar of her table, this was either surrender or benediction, and he could not tell which, and perhaps neither could she.

"Eat," she said. "Then sleep. Decide again in the morning with a clear head. If the plan is still standing at dawn, then it was never the anger talking."

It was still standing at dawn. But that was later. First there was the night, and the night had work for him.

* * *

He went to the counting house because it was expected of him — that was the beauty and horror of invisibility, that even shattered, it still functioned; a useful boy reports for duty. Calloway met him in the doorway with the particular warmth he reserved for assets.

"Oyelaran." The factor's eyes went over him the way eyes go over damaged goods, assessing depreciation. "The hand. From the fire."

"It will heal, sir."

"The town talks nonsense, you know." Calloway said it lightly, pouring tea he did not offer. "People love a story. A hero pulling a drowning girl from flames — very dramatic. Very foolish. I trust you understand that stories like that have consequences for the people in them. For both people."

"I carry crates, sir. That is all I do."

Something flickered in Calloway's face then — a small, quick disappointment, gone so fast Jude might have imagined it. Later, turning it over in his mind, he would recognize it for what it was: the factor had liked him. Had perhaps even hoped. And Jude's flat obedience, delivered just slightly too flatly, told the man that the asset had learned something the asset should not have learned.

"Good," Calloway said anyway. "Keep it that way. The audit arrives within the fortnight — a board inspector, very thorough, very humorless. I want the registers immaculate. Every bond current, every entry clean. You'll work evenings until it's done."

"Evenings, sir."

"There's a good fellow." And then, as Jude turned, the voice followed him, silk over a hook: "Your mother must be proud. Such a steady son. Families are such fragile things, aren't they? One hears of terrible accidents in flood season. Whole futures washed away."

Jude stood with his back to the factor and felt the fear arrive on schedule — cold, professional, thorough — and felt something rise underneath it that was not fear at all.

"Yes, sir," he said. "Fragile things."

That night he worked the registers by lamplight with the watchman's boots creaking overhead, and he copied, in perfect facsimile, the format of a discharge entry from twenty years ago — an indenture bought out by kin, witnessed, initialed, closed. He copied it onto scrap and burned the scrap in the lamp flame and watched the paper curl into a black moth and die.

Two years remained on his bond. Seven years total; five served. A dead uncle's payment, entered retroactively, dated before the audit. Calloway's own initials at the witness line — initials Jude had studied until he could draw them drunk, blind, underwater. *C.M.C.* with the middle stroke broken, the flaw in the pen, the habit of a hurried man.

He was not ready tonight. Tonight was only the rehearsal, the pianist running scales in an empty hall. But he sat back on his stool and understood, with a clarity that frightened him more than the watchman ever could, that the fear was gone. Not mastered — gone. Burned out of him in the market fire along with everything soft.

Calloway thought he was holding a leash. Calloway did not understand that the collar and the knife were cut from the same leather.

* * *

On his way home, past the black skeleton of the burnt market, he stopped at the river wall.

The water was high and brown and moving with intent. Across it, the bridge stood dark, officially condemned now, planks already being prised up at the near end. On the far bank, the company's flag hung limp and dripping. Everything the light touched belonged to Meridian: the wharf, the warehouses, the counting house behind him, the ledgers with their neat columns of lives.

Everything the water touched belonged to no one.

Jude took the paper lantern from inside his shirt — Miriam's lantern, the joke-lantern from the market, singed now at one corner, its bamboo ribs bent where he had shielded it from the flames. He did not light it. Not yet. Lighting it would be a goodbye, and he was not finished with living here.

Instead he held it and made the promise aloud, to the river, in the old divers' words his grandfather had taught him — the words Tamuno said before every descent, the words his father had been saying when the water closed over him. Words for going down into the deep place and coming back changed.

"When I come up out of this," he told the brown water, "no one carries my name but me."

The river said nothing. Rivers never promise. They only remember.

Behind him, up the slope, a lamp moved in the factor's house — and in its window, briefly, a silhouette he knew better than his own heartbeat. Then the curtain drew shut, deliberate, final, a door closing in a language they both spoke fluently.

Jude turned his back on it and walked home through the flooded street, past shuttered windows and whispering doorways, a man carrying nothing visible at all — which was, he was beginning to understand, exactly how the dangerous ones traveled.

At dawn, his mother set rice before him and searched his face.

"Well?" she said.

"It's still standing," Jude said, and reached for the ink.
