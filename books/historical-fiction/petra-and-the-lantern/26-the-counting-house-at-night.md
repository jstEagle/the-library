## 26. The Counting House at Night

The counting house slept the way rich things sleep: heavily, surrounded by locks, certain of its own worth.

Jude stood across the lane from it in the blue hour after midnight, his back pressed to the cool brick of the cooper's shed, and watched the windows for the fourth time that night. Rain had come and gone twice since the bridge closed, and the air held water the way a sponge holds it — not falling now but present, beading on his eyebrows, slicking the cobbles so that each step had to be placed like a word in an argument you could not afford to lose.

He had counted the ways in, over six nights of walking past. There was the front door with its iron-bound oak and its lock the size of a fist — no. There was the loading door on the river side, where the clerks rolled barrels of company rum down to the barge quay — barred from within since the audit notice went up. And there was the coal chute.

The chute was an insult of architecture, a slot cut into the foundation to feed the counting house's single stove, angled steeply enough that a man had to go down it feet first, in the dark, trusting the dark. It was also, Jude knew because he had hauled the coal himself every cold season for five years, unbarred. Nobody guarded a hole meant for coal. Nobody imagined a man choosing it.

*Useful and unseen.* His whole life had been training for this one ignoble entrance.

He touched the folded paper inside his shirt — three pages, copied out eleven times in his own hand until the letters came without thought: the layout of the bond register, the sequence of dates, the exact phrasing of a discharge entry from four years past, when a diver named Okon had been bought free by an uncle who did not exist either, though that forgery had been Calloway's own. He had found it in the dead files while hunting for something else. The company taught corruption the way other houses taught prayers: by daily repetition.

His father's mark he had practiced on slate until his wrist ached — the heavy downward stroke, the hesitation at the base where the old man's scarred thumb always dragged, the small hook like a fish turning. *Tamuno says grief lives in the hand,* his grandfather had told him once, watching him copy it. *You write your father better than you write yourself.* It had not been a compliment. It had been a warning, and Jude had stepped over it the way you step over a root in a path, because there was no other way through.

The watchman made his round at the half hour. Jude had timed him three nights running: Okafor, old and flat-footed, lantern swinging, who paused longest at the cookshop corner because the widow there left out bread ends and he pretended not to take them. Forty minutes for the full circuit, six of them spent within sight of the counting house front, none spent at all looking at the river side, because the river side was mud and crates and nothing worth stealing.

Tonight the mud would have to be worth something.

* * *

He crossed the lane at the moment Okafor's lantern passed behind the church wall. Four steps on wet cobbles, then the soft ground behind the cooper's shed, then the counting house wall against his shoulder, guiding him down its length like a blind man reading by touch. The coal chute's wooden flap gave with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking. He fed himself in feet first.

It was worse than he had imagined. The angle dropped him fast, the sides closing in until his shoulders scraped both walls at once, coal dust rising around him in a cloud that got into his mouth before he thought to clamp his teeth shut. Then the floor, hard and sudden, and he was kneeling in the black under the stove, breathing through his nose in shallow sips, listening to his own heart as if it were someone else's footsteps.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, a gecko clicked. The stove ticked as its metal cooled. Somewhere the river said what the river always said.

He waited a hundred breaths. Nothing changed. He took out the candle stub and lit it with hands that were steadier than he deserved, cupping the flame until the little room resolved: coal bins, the stove's iron skirt, a ladder of cleats nailed into the wall going up to a trapdoor. He climbed, eased the trapdoor up an inch, and looked out into the counting room proper.

Moonless, but the lamp-glow from the street came through the shutters in thin blades, and by those blades he read the room he had dusted a thousand times: the long trestle desks, the pigeonholes with their paper tags, the tall case where the ledgers lived behind glass that Calloway had ordered locked only last week, when the audit letter arrived from the capital with its wax seal fat as a slug.

The glass case was new. Everything else was memory.

He came up through the trapdoor and stood a moment letting his eyes drink the darkness deeper. The case lock was brass, small, made for a key that hung — he had seen it a hundred times, hanging — from the ring at Calloway's belt, or on Sundays from the hook inside the factor's own desk drawer, third one down, beneath the false-bottomed tray where he kept the good brandy he did not offer to visitors.

Jude went to the desk first. His fingers walked the drawers the way his mother's fingers walked rice, counting what was there by weight and resistance. First drawer: pens, sand shaker, sealing wax. Second: papers, ribbon, the audit letter itself, which he did not stop to read because reading cost light and light cost time. Third drawer: the false bottom lifted, and there lay a bottle, and beside the bottle a ring of keys, cold as river stones.

He chose by shape. He had spent years carrying keys for this house in his pockets, delivering them, fetching them, and a key's business was written in its teeth. The small one with the double-bitted end went into the case lock, met resistance, turned a quarter, stopped.

His breath stopped with it.

Not wrong key — wrong turn. He reversed, felt the pins give one at a time like knuckles loosening, and the lock opened with a click that seemed to him loud enough to wake the town, though it was really only loud enough to wake himself. Sweat ran cold down his spine. He wiped his palm on his trousers and pulled the register out.

The bond ledger. Three years of entries in three different clerkly hands, all of them pretending to be one tidy truth. He laid it open on the desk, weighted the pages with the sand shaker, and set his copied instructions beside it. The discharge entries followed a grammar. That was the thing he had understood finally, lying awake in the divers' quarter with the flood hissing at the doors: a ledger was not numbers. A ledger was sentences. And sentences could be learned.

*Discharged in full, the sum of forty-one pounds, seven shillings, paid by kin, viz. Ekon Bassey, uncle to the diver, being deceased these two years, account closed.*

He dipped the pen. His hand shook on the first stroke, and he let it shake, because his father's hand had shaken too at the end of a long dive, and perfection was a merchant's virtue, not a diver's. Down-stroke. Hesitation. Hook. He wrote the fictitious uncle's name in a hand he built from three dead men's hands — a clerk named Whitcombe whose letters he had sorted for years, plus the slant of Okon's real discharge entry, plus something of his own that no one would ever trace to anyone.

Name. Sum. Kinship. Date. The date was the trap; he knew it was the trap; he chose it anyway, ten days past, the night of the lantern market fire, because a discharge dated too conveniently clean would smell of ink, and ink needed the grime of ordinary days around it. He blotted the page, breathed on it, rubbed a fingertip of coal dust along the fresh line until it sat in its row of neighbors like a child among strangers learning their posture.

Then he heard the front door.

* * *

It was not Okafor. Okafor's steps dragged on the left; these came down sharp and even, two boots in agreement, and they were already inside the vestibule before Jude's mind finished refusing the information. He closed the register. The case lock would take time he did not have. He blew out the candle, and the dark came down on him like water, and he did the only thing the water had ever taught him: he went still and sank.

Under the desk. Knees to chest, cheek to the cool floorboard, the register still open in his lap because there had been no time to shelve it. Through the gap beneath the desk's apron he watched lamplight swing into the room and lay itself across the floorboards in a long gold bar, moving toward him the way dawn moves toward a diver working the shallows — inevitable, indifferent.

Calloway's voice, low, talking to himself the way tired men do: *—audit Thursday, everything cross-referenced, if Whitcombe's figures don't reconcile I'll know why.* The lamp set down on the high desk. A drawer opened. The brandy bottle gurgled once, twice, and stopped, and Jude understood with a lurch that the factor had come for exactly the bottle whose hiding place had given up the keys, and that God or the river was laughing at the symmetry.

Footsteps crossed the room. Stopped. Turned.

The glass case stood open. Jude saw it in his mind with perfect clarity, the little brass tongue of the lock showing, and he watched the lamplight drift toward it and felt the whole plan of his life narrow to the width of a boot heel. If Calloway looked — if he noticed the case ajar, the register gone from its slot, the smell of coal dust hanging in a room that had been swept Tuesday —

A woman screamed in the lane outside.

Not a cry of pain — a cry of performance, pitched high and breaking beautifully, the kind of scream that makes every man in earshot think first of fire. It was joined by a crash, a second scream, and then — unmistakably, impossibly — laughter, low and delighted, a laugh Jude knew the way he knew the tide tables.

Miriam.

*Oh, you idiot,* her voice said through the window, breathless, *oh, you've knocked the whole stall — help me, help me pick it up before the watchman comes and thinks we're thieves —*

*I am a thief,* said a young man's voice, laughing too, some apprentice or other from the chandlery, and more crashing, and a cat yowled indignantly, and the noise swelled into a whole small carnival directly beneath the counting house windows.

The lamplight hesitated. Calloway swore softly — *that girl* — and Jude heard the exact moment the factor weighed his daughter against his ledgers and found, for once, the scales tipping toward blood. Boots moved away, quick now, toward the shuttered window. A shutter cracked an inch. *Miriam? Miriam, is that you?*

*Father!* Bright, startled, guilty — she was very good. *Go back to bed, I'm sorry, we only — the festival lanterns, we were relighting one, and Silas is a menace —*

Through the gap under the desk, Jude moved. Register under his shirt, case closed — he risked the two seconds of the lock, fingers remembering the pins — keys back in the drawer, false bottom down, pen returned to its stand with its nib wiped. He slid the trapdoor as the boots turned back, and went down the coal chute faster than he had come up, taking the scrape of it on his shoulders like a fair price.

Outside, the night received him. He flattened against the wall, and through the window above he heard Calloway's voice drop into the tone Jude knew best of all his tones, the warm one, the one that wore kindness like a coat over a ledger: *Come inside, both of you. Now. We'll speak about lanterns.*

And Miriam's voice, one last time, pitched just loud enough to carry down the chute and into Jude's chest like a hand closing over his heart:

*Goodnight, Father. Mind the audit.*

* * *

He did not go home. Home, in the divers' quarter, meant his grandfather's hearing and his mother's silence and Ngozi's eyes asking questions he could not answer yet. Instead he went to the drying sheds above the oyster beds, where the company stored its empty barrels, and sat between two of them in the dark with the register against his ribs and his heart still going like someone hammering a boat together in a hurry.

She had come. He had never asked her to come. He had told her nothing — only that he would be late walking home, only a look across the market ruins two days ago, and she had read it whole, the way she read everything, and had chosen to spend her night screaming theatrically over a fallen lantern stall in the rain rather than sleep safe in the factor's house.

*Terror and trust,* his grandfather would say, if Tamuno ever learned of this night, *are rope twisted from the same fiber. You cannot pull on one without the other coming tight.* Jude put his head back against the barrel and laughed once, silently, at the absurdity of it — that the factor's daughter had saved the diver's son from the factor's own lamp, and that the whole salvation had cost her a scolding and cost him ten years off his life.

He should return the register tonight. Every hour it stayed out of its slot was an hour the wound could be discovered. But his legs would not agree to move yet, and so he sat, and by degrees his heartbeat slowed to something like the river's pace, and he allowed himself, for ninety seconds only, to imagine the morning: the register shelved, the forged line sleeping among honest lies, his name struck from the book of things owned.

Then he heard the boots again.

Okafor's drag-step, left foot first, coming along the quay on the half-hour round — but stopping, stopping at the counting house, and the lantern raised, and the old voice muttering with professional irritation: *Now who's been at this door. Coal dust all over the step, look at that, swept Tuesday and here's mess again. Rat, most likely. Big rat.*

The lantern light swung toward the drying sheds.

Jude was already moving, low, barrel to barrel, the register clutched to him like a child, and behind him he heard Okafor pause at the coal chute, lift the flap, hold the lantern into the dark throat of it — and Jude, thirty feet gone in shadow with the river ahead of him, heard the old man say comfortably to the night:

*Rat. Been eating well, too. Must be the company's rat.*

The river took Jude's feet. The current, his oldest creditor, carried him downstream toward the shallows where he could wade ashore unseen, and he held the ledger above the waterline the whole way, and laughed again — silently, helplessly — because the company's rat had stolen the company's truth, and the truth was heavier than pearls and dry as bone.

Tomorrow the auditors' barge would anchor in the roadstead. Tomorrow Calloway would walk his ledgers like a man walking a bridge he suspected was rotten. And somewhere between this night and Thursday, the register would tell its mended story, and the only question left — the question that rode with Jude all the way home through the black water, sitting beside him in the dark like a patient passenger — was whether Miriam's father would find the forged page, or whether the factor's own buried lie would reach up out of the ledger first and pull him under.
