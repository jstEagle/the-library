## 30. The Last Barge

Loading day came gray and windless, the river lying flat as poured tin, and the whole town seemed to hold its breath along the banks to watch it.

Jude was there before dawn. He had not slept — sleep had become a country he could no longer afford the fare for — and he stood at the edge of the Meridian dock with his arms wrapped around himself, watching the barge sit low in the water like something already grieving. The *Charity of Meridian*, they called her. He had always thought the name a joke told by men who never laughed.

Four berths. The number had been carved into him over the past weeks until he could feel its edges in the dark: four berths, five names, and then three, when the company's arithmetic found his name inconvenient. He had learned to carry that number the way his mother carried water — carefully, close to the body, knowing every step would spill some of it.

But this morning the arithmetic had changed again, and Jude did not trust it.

The board's letter had come the evening before, delivered by a clerk who would not meet his eye, who set the envelope on Adaeze's table as though it might bite and left without a word about tea or weather. Inside, on heavy stock that smelled of the capital's ink houses, was the confirmation: four passengers, all named. And beneath the fourth name — his own, in formal type, *Jude Oyelaran, bond discharged* — a line so small he'd had to hold the paper to the lamp:

*Passage granted per resolution of the Board, ref. inquiry of the 14th.*

An inquiry. The word sat in his stomach like a stone dropped in still water. He knew what inquiries were now. He had spent the last week learning what ledgers said when other people read them, what boards did with scandals too public to bury quietly and too useful to punish fully. His freedom had arrived, but it had come the way floodwater comes — carrying things with it, unasked.

They were buying his silence with a berth. Or perhaps they were burying Miriam's confession under the weight of a solved problem: see, nothing happened, everyone is compensated, the boy rides away where he cannot talk. Either way, the name in type was not a gift. It was a lid.

He turned the thought over one more time and put it down. There would be time on the water, or after it, to understand what he had agreed to by agreeing to nothing. This morning there was only loading day, and his family coming down the hill with everything they owned in the world, and the river waiting with its flat gray patience.

* * *

Adaeze came first.

She came down the divers' quarter road with her back straight and her chin level, the way she had walked to market for thirty years, and if her hands trembled on the rope handle of the bundle she carried, only Jude saw it, because only Jude knew to look. Behind her came Ngozi, chattering to Tamuno about the capital — about the school with the iron roof, about libraries she had heard described by a girl who had never seen one either — and Tamuno nodding along, letting her voice wash over him like warm rain.

They looked smaller than Jude remembered them being. That was the strangest thing about leaving; he had watched people depart from this dock his whole life, and he had never understood until now that departure shrinks you before you ever board. The barge made them smaller still. Everything made them smaller except each other.

"You're staring," Adaeze said, arriving beside him. She did not look at him. She looked at the barge, cataloguing it the way she catalogued everything — the rail, the tarpaulin, the crates being winched aboard, calculating in one glance how much of a person's life fit into such a space.

"I'm memorizing," Jude said.

"Memorizing is free." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "It's the only thing left in this town that is."

Behind them, the loading went on. Company clerks moved among the passengers with their lists, ticking names, and Jude felt each tick like a hand passing over his body searching for contraband. When the clerk reached their family group, he read out the four names slowly, and Jude noticed — because he noticed everything now, noticing had become his trade — that the man's voice flattened slightly on *Oyelaran*, as though the name tasted of ink that hadn't dried.

"All correct," the clerk said, and moved on, and did not once look at Jude's face.

Ngozi had gone quiet. She stood with her ticket-crumb of luggage clutched to her chest, watching the water, and when Jude glanced at her he saw she was not excited anymore. Excitement had been for the weeks of goodbyes, for the imagined school with the iron roof. Now that the barge was real and the water was real, she looked the way he felt: like someone standing at the edge of a sentence they weren't sure they wanted finished.

"It's not too late to be angry with me," Jude said softly.

"I'm saving it," Ngozi said. "For the capital. I hear anger travels well."

But her shoulder pressed against his arm, and stayed there, and that was its own kind of forgiveness, or its own kind of promise that forgiveness would come later, once there was ground steady enough to stand on while feeling it.

Tamuno said nothing at all. The old man had refused a seat in the covered cabin, had planted himself at the stern rail where he could watch the water, and he watched it now with an expression Jude had seen on him only twice before: once at his son's funeral, and once the night he took Jude to the river and spoke of debts that outlive men. It was the face of a man keeping an appointment.

"The river will be high past the bend," Tamuno said, to no one, to everyone. "Two days to the crossing. Three if the wind turns."

"Grandfather," Jude began, "if you'd rather—"

"I know what I'd rather." Tamuno's eyes stayed on the water. "I'd rather your father were standing here instead of me. But the river keeps its own ledger, boy. We are not asked which pages we'd prefer to be written on." He finally looked at Jude, and his old eyes held something Jude couldn't name — pride wearing grief's clothing, or grief wearing pride's. "Go. Stand with your mother. An old man wants to watch the water alone."

* * *

The town came to see them off in ones and twos, the way the poor do everything — informally, without announcement, but completely.

Elder Bassi arrived leaning on his stick and his granddaughter's arm, and he stood before Adaeze and took both her hands in his spotted ones. Whatever he said was too quiet for the dock to hear, but Adaeze bowed her head over their joined hands, and Bassi turned afterward to Jude and gripped his forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"Records survive floods," the old man murmured. "So do men who keep them. Don't let the capital make you soft, boy. Water finds its level. So does truth."

From the quarter came the women Adaeze had refused charity with for seven years, the same women whose rice she had stretched and shared anyway, and they brought gifts that shamed any merchant's cargo: a sack of groundnuts, a woven mat still smelling of the reeds, a clay pot sealed with wax and full, Jude knew, of palm oil pressed from fruit none of them could spare. Adaeze received each gift with both hands and a lowered head, and Jude understood suddenly that his mother was receiving not oil and nuts but thirty years of accounts settled — dignity paid out in kind across decades, balanced to the last grain, and now closing.

By mid-morning the sun had burned through and the light lay hard on the water, and the barge sat loaded, and there was nothing left to load but the living.

That was when Jude saw her.

Miriam Calloway stood at the far end of the dock, apart from the company officials, dressed plainly — plainly for her, which meant simply and beautifully, which meant she had chosen the dress the way one chooses a word in an argument. She had broken her engagement publicly. She had confessed to the board. She had gambled everything on scandal forcing silence, and by the look of her — thinner, paler, her chin up the way Adaeze's was up — the gamble had cost, but she had won the only prize worth having, which was to remain exactly what she was.

Their eyes met across the crowded dock, across the whole width of everything that separated them — debt and ownership, factor and diver, seven years of invisibility — and neither of them moved. There was no need. Moving was for people who doubted.

His mother saw. Of course she saw; Adaeze Oyelaran had spent a lifetime reading what people carried in their faces, and her son's face just now was an open ledger. She looked from Jude to Miriam and back, and something passed over her expression — not approval, not yet, but the pause of a woman revising an account she had thought closed.

"Board her," Adaeze said quietly. "All of you. The tide won't wait for feelings, and neither does your sister."

* * *

The boarding was slow and terrible in the way that ordinary partings are terrible — no single moment large enough to weep at, only hundreds of small ones.

Ngozi hugged him first, fierce and quick, her chin tucked against his shoulder. "Write to me," she ordered, in the voice she used for ordering. "Real letters. Not those two-line things you call letters."

"They'll be three lines."

"I'll know if you're lying. I always know."

Then Tamuno. The old man took Jude's hand in both of his, and his grip was still strong, diver's strength banked in old bone, and he leaned close so that his words went into Jude's ear alone, private as a grave.

"Your breath is yours now," Tamuno said. "Your father gave it, the river tested it, and no company owns what a man carries in his own chest. Remember that when the water asks."

And then Adaeze. She stood before him for a long moment without touching him, and Jude realized she was doing what she had done with the wedding chest — choosing what to carry, deciding what this moment would weigh. Then she reached up and took his face in her hands, both hands, the way she had when he was small and feverish, and looked at him with eyes that had refused charity for thirty years and were refusing tears now on the same principle.

"You freed them with a dead man's name," she whispered, so low only he could hear, and his blood went cold, because he had never told her, had never told anyone, and her thumb traced his cheekbone once, gently, sealing the secret shut between them forever. "A mother counts everything, Jude. Count on that. Now go and live like a man whose mother is watching — which means: like a man who has something to lose."

She kissed his forehead, released him, and walked up the gangplank without looking back, because looking back was for women who had anything left behind that they regretted, and she had spent her whole regret budget long ago.

The whistle sounded. Long, low, mournful, a sound like the bridge itself groaning.

Jude stepped back onto the dock. The ropes came off. The barge began to move, gathering speed with the unhurried certainty of something that had done this a hundred times, and his family stood at the stern rail growing smaller — Ngozi waving with both arms, Tamuno motionless as driftwood, Adaeze with one hand raised, palm out, a blessing and a boundary in a single gesture.

He watched until the barge rounded the bend. He watched after it stopped being visible. He stood on the emptying dock with the whistle's echo dying over the water and the whole shape of his life changed around him, freed and abandoned and saved and sold, all in the space of one morning, and he did not yet know which word was truest.

Then a presence at his elbow, and the smell of lantern paper and river wind, and Miriam was beside him, looking at the empty bend in the river where his family had gone.

"Well," she said. "Now there are two of us the board bought passage for and didn't get."

Jude laughed — a rough, startled sound, the first in weeks — and the laugh cracked something open in his chest, and what came out through the crack was not laughter at all.

Because downriver, faint but unmistakable, carried flat across the high water, came the sound of the whistle blowing again — short, sharp, repeated — the signal every riverman knew, and hoped never to hear:

Trouble at the crossing.
