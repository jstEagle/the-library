## 31. Goodbye on the Water

The barge came up the river at first light, riding low and heavy in the water like a beast that had already eaten too much and been asked to eat more. Jude heard it before he saw it — the slow churn of its paddle wheel, the groan of timber taking current — and the sound went through him the way a bell goes through a room, touching everything and settling nowhere.

He stood at the edge of the divers' quarter with his bundle on his shoulder and watched it come. Behind him the town was waking to its own emptiness. Doors stood open on rooms already stripped bare. A dog trotted past with something white in its mouth — a child's shirt, maybe, or a length of sailcloth — carrying it off as if it too had been given a berth.

*Four berths,* he thought. *Four names.*

And his name, by some arithmetic he still did not fully understand, written back onto the list in ink he hadn't touched.

He should have felt joy. He had rehearsed joy on the walk down from the counting house, where the morning before the board's clerk had read out the amended allocation in a flat voice, not meeting anyone's eyes: Oyelaran, Adaeze. Oyelaran, Ngozi. Oyelaran, Tamuno. Oyelaran, Jude. Four names spoken like coins counted out onto a table, and everyone standing there knowing they were payment — not for passage, but for silence.

Instead what he felt was the weight of the bundle on his shoulder, which contained everything he owned, and which weighed almost nothing at all.

* * *

They gathered at the landing an hour after dawn, because Calloway had posted the departure for nine and everyone in the quarter knew that company time was a tide you did not argue with. The barge sat against the pilings, its rail hung with wet rope, its deck crowded with crates stamped MERIDIAN in faded black. A handful of other families were already aboard, folded into corners with their goods, faces turned toward the water as though looking anywhere else might undo them.

Adaeze arrived last, walking slowly, one hand on Ngozi's arm and one on her father-in-law's. She wore her good cloth — indigo, the pattern worn soft at the folds — and she carried nothing except a small bundle wrapped in palm matting that she held against her chest with both hands, the way a woman carries a sleeping child.

Jude had watched her pack the house over three days. He had watched her hold each thing up to the light of the single window and ask it, silently, whether it had earned its passage. The mortar and pestle had gone to a neighbor. The cooking pots had gone to Elder Bassi's household, along with half the stores she'd stretched across seven years. The mats, the lamp, the small tin box of her mother's things — given away, all of it, distributed through the quarter like seeds from a split pod, so that even leaving, she planted something.

Only the chest had defeated her. He had found her sitting before it on the last evening, her hands flat on its lid, not opening it, not closing it. It was the one object she could not weigh, because it did not hold things. It held a marriage, a river, a man.

In the end the chest itself stayed behind, empty, its lid propped open to the damp air. Whatever had lived inside it now lived in the bundle against her breast.

"Jude." She said his name quietly, and he came to her through the crowd on the landing, past the factor's clerks with their lists, past the last of the neighbors come to see them off.

She looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were dry. He had learned, growing up under her roof, that his mother's tears were private currency, spent only in the dark and never in front of those who would need her strong tomorrow.

"You are thin," she said.

"I am fed."

"You are lying beautifully." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth, gone as fast as it came. Then she lifted the bundle from her chest and pressed it into his hands.

It was heavier than it looked. That was the first thing. Cold, too, dense as a fistful of river bottom, and when the palm matting fell open in his palms he saw what it was and his breath stopped in his throat.

His grandfather's diving weight. His father's before that. A smooth oval of dark stone, polished by generations of grip, grooved where the cord had run, warm-seeming even in the morning chill as though it remembered every body that had ever held it. Tamuno had worn it at his waist forty years. Kofi Oyelaran had worn it the day the river took him, and the river had given it back three tides later, set gently on the mud below the ferry crossing as if returning a borrowed tool.

Jude had thought it lost with everything else. He had assumed his mother had sold it, or buried it, or left it to the house.

"Mama," he said. "This is yours."

"It is mine to give." She closed his fingers over the stone with both her hands, folding them shut the way she used to fold his fists around hot food when he was small — firmly, without discussion. "You will carry it."

"I don't dive anymore."

"You will." There was no doubt in it. She looked at him the way she looked at rice she was counting — measuring exactly what was there, deciding exactly how far it would go. "Your grandfather is old. Your father's trade is in your blood and in this stone, and I am not putting my husband's bones on a barge to gather dust in a city where nobody knows what the water says. You stay near the river. You hear it. That is not a curse, Jude. I spent years thinking it was. It is not."

He wanted to say something — that he had forfeited himself once already, that freedom had arrived wrapped in another man's shame, that the stone felt less like an inheritance than a summons. But his mother's hands were still over his, and her grip said what her voice would not: *I cannot grieve here. Do not make me grieve here.*

That was the shape of it, he understood suddenly. This was not a gift given so much as a grief deferred — pressed into his hands the way she pressed coins into Ngozi's palm for the road, so that the sorrow would travel somewhere useful instead of spilling out on the landing where the factor's clerks could watch it fall. She was blessing him. And she was forbidding him, with the same motion, to mourn in front of her.

"Yes, Mama," he said.

"Good." She let go. Her hands went immediately to smoothing her cloth, restoring order to herself, and she turned toward the gangplank with her spine straight as a mast. "Now help your sister. She has argued with that clerk twice already about the weight of her trunk, and she will win, but I would rather she didn't embarrass us entirely."

* * *

Ngozi won, of course. Jude arrived at the foot of the gangplank in time to watch the clerk — a narrow young man named Pell who had once confiscated Jude's lunch for eating in the warehouse — surrender before his sister's fixed smile and wave the trunk aboard without recording it.

"The manifest allows forty pounds of personal goods per passenger," Pell said weakly.

"And the manifest also allowed four berths for this family until ten days ago," Ngozi said pleasantly, "so perhaps we shouldn't lean too hard on what the manifest allows." She hoisted the trunk herself before he could protest — she was stronger than she looked, years of hauling water and hauling books had made sure of it — and started up the planks.

At the top she stopped. Turned. Looked down at Jude with an expression he had not seen since the night she'd found her name circled on the true list and his name absent — fury then; this was something else, something that had passed through fury and come out the far side weathered but intact.

"Ten days ago I hated you," she said, conversationally, loudly enough that Pell pretended very hard to be deaf.

"I know."

"I want you to know that I know what you did. All of it. The ledger, the bond, the—" she glanced at the clerks, lowered her voice, "—the thing with Miriam Calloway, which we will discuss when there is no one within a mile of us. I know, and I hate that I love you for it, and I have decided that both of those things can be true on a barge."

"That seems efficient."

"Everything about me is efficient." She came back down two steps, which cost her dignity and which she paid anyway, and gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Write to me. Not through the company post — through Bassi, he has cousins in the capital who owe him money, it's a whole arrangement. If you do not write I will come back down this river during flood season myself, and I cannot swim, so understand what a threat that is."

"I'll write."

"You'd better." Her voice cracked on the second word, just barely, just once, and she covered it by turning quickly and climbing. At the top of the plank she did not look back again. Looking back was for people with time, and Ngozi had a city waiting.

* * *

Tamuno boarded like a king refusing a throne. The old man walked the gangplank without touching the rope rail, his bare feet finding the worn boards by instinct, and when he reached the deck he stood a moment facing upstream, into the current, his eyes half-closed as though listening for something in the water's voice.

Jude followed him aboard with the diving weight wrapped again in its matting, tucked under his arm like a child.

His grandfather did not turn around. "I dreamed about this boat," Tamuno said. "Three nights running. In the dream it sinks."

"Barges don't sink in flood season. They ride high on the freshet."

"In the dream it sinks," Tamuno repeated, unbothered, "and I swim, because I am still a diver in dreams, and I surface in the flooded market with a lantern in my teeth, burning underwater, lighting the way for fish." He smiled at last, showing the few teeth the river years had left him. "Dreams are stupid. That is why we trust them. Come here, boy."

Jude came. Tamuno took his chin in a hand rough as bark and turned his face to the light, studying him the way he'd once studied pearls — for luster, for flaws, for worth.

"You gave up your place once," the old man said. "For your mother's pride and your sister's books. And the water gave it back to you. Do you know why?"

"Because the board needed to bury a scandal."

"Because the water gives back what is truly owed." Tamuno tapped his chest, once, over the heart. "Not what is grabbed. What is owed. Your father understood that. He went down for a pearl that was not owed to him — greed dressed up as need, the company's need — and the water kept him. You went down into their ledgers for what was owed to your family, and the water—" he gestured at the barge, at the four names, at the impossible morning— "gave you back. Remember which kind of diver you are."

"I'm not a diver."

"You're holding your grandfather's weight under your arm and arguing with me." Tamuno released his chin and shuffled toward the stern, where a coil of shadow offered shade. "When you stop arguing, come find me. I have maybe two seasons of teaching left in these hands, and I would rather spend them than save them."

* * *

Adaeze stood at the rail. The loading was done; the clerks had retreated down the gangplank with their lists; the paddle wheel was beginning its slow first revolution, slapping the brown water like a tired heart learning to race.

Jude came to stand beside her. Below them, on the landing, the quarter had come out to see them off — Elder Bassi upright as a drumstick despite his hip, the neighbors, the children, the women who had shared Adaeze's fire and her silences for seven years. Someone had brought a drum. Of course someone had brought a drum. This was the divers' quarter; they sent their men down into the dark with songs, and they would send their women up the river with them too.

Adaeze did not wave. She stood very still, watching the shore come toward them as the barge pushed off — the strange sensation of leaving a place by having it come at you — and Jude saw her lips moving, not in prayer exactly, in inventory. Naming what she was leaving. Weighing what she had managed to carry.

"Mama," he said softly.

"Don't." One word, quiet as a knife laid flat on a table. Her hand found his on the rail and gripped it, and her grip was steady, and her face was dry, and her eyes stayed fixed on the shrinking shore. "If you speak kindly to me right now I will come apart on this deck in front of the factor's clerks, and I did not raise my children to watch that. Hold the stone. Think of your father. Let me count."

So he held the stone. Through his palm he could feel its old cold turning slowly to warmth, the way river stones did, the way all patient things did. He thought of his father's hands around this same oval, thirty years of mornings, the cord biting the same groove. He thought of the chest with its lid propped open in an empty house. He thought of Miriam standing somewhere in that shrinking town, in a household that buzzed like a struck hive, and made himself stop thinking of her, because his mother had asked him to think of his father and there was only room in him for one grief at a time.

The drum on the landing found its rhythm. Other drums answered it, deeper ones, from the quarter's heart. A song rose — the divers' launching song, the one sung for men going down, repurposed now for a family going away — and Adaeze's shoulders finally shook, once, twice, and she did not turn around and she did not make a sound, and Jude put his arm through hers and stood with her and let the river take them.

Ngozi waved until the landing was a smudge. Tamuno slept in the shade with his mouth open, dreaming, presumably, of sinking boats and burning lanterns. The barge rounded the first bend, and the town folded itself away behind the mangroves — and with it, folded away, a girl with ink-stained fingers and a factor with a friendly face and a counting house full of drowned truths, all of it receding like something seen through water.

Jude stood at the stern rail long after the bend had taken everything, the diving weight warm under his arm, his mother's blessing settled into him like ballast.

Then, far downstream, back the way they had come, he saw it: a thread of smoke rising above the tree line. Thin. Dark. Wrong-colored — not cookfire gray but something heavier, tinged with the oily black of burning pitch or painted canvas.

From the direction of the market.

The drum song was still in his ears. The barge was still pulling north, carrying everyone he loved toward safety. And the smoke stood up over his town like a question no one aboard had heard asked yet.

Jude's hand closed tight around his grandfather's stone, and he turned to the pilot, already knowing the answer, asking anyway:

"How fast can this thing turn around?"
