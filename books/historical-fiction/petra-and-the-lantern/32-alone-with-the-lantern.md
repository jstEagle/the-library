## 32. Alone with the Lantern

The barge did not look back.

Jude stood on the mud-slick steps of the old customs wharf and watched it go — a low, heavy shape riding deep in the brown water, its single funnel trailing smoke that the wind bent and shredded before it could become anything like a farewell. Four berths. Four travelers. His mother at the rail with her spine straight as an oar shaft, refusing to weep where the factor's men could count her tears alongside her cargo. Ngozi beside her, already turned toward the capital as though the river were a road and she had simply been waiting all her life for someone to open it. Tamuno last of all, small and folded into himself at the stern, one hand resting on the gunwale the way a man rests his hand on the shoulder of an old dog he must leave behind.

They had not looked back because looking back was a thing their family did not do. Adaeze had taught them that much, if nothing else: grief is permitted, but only forward-facing, the way you may mourn a harvest while your hands are already in the next furrow.

So Jude looked back for all of them. He stood on the wharf until the barge rounded the bend below the mission hill and the smoke dissolved and the river closed over the place where they had been, smooth and indifferent as a ledger page turned.

Then he was alone in a way he had never been alone before, and the town around him began, quietly, to empty.

* * *

He walked home through streets that were shedding themselves.

The rains had come early and hard that year, and the evacuation had come early to match them, so that the exodus happened in a gray drizzle rather than a proper downpour — as if even the sky could not be bothered to make the leaving dramatic. Carts stood abandoned outside shuttered doors, their axles already darkening with rust-bloom. A child's sandal lay in the middle of Rope-Walk Lane, half-filled with rainwater, no foot anywhere in sight to claim it. The company warehouse, where he had spent seven years being useful and unseen, stood with its great doors thrown open and its shelves stripped bare, so that when the wind moved through it, the building made a sound like a man whistling through missing teeth.

He had carried things out of that warehouse for seven years. Sacks of rice, bolts of cloth, crates of palm oil, coils of wire, the whole endless inventory of other people's comfort. He knew its weight by touch the way a musician knows pitch. He had once told himself — back when telling himself things still worked — that a man who could carry anything could survive anything. That lightness was a kind of armor.

Now the warehouse was empty and the armor had done nothing at all, and the town was teaching him the opposite lesson in every doorway: that everything can be left. Everything. The cooking pots gone from the hearths. The mats rolled up and gone from the floors. The names chalked on doors by families hoping to return, already running in the damp — *Okonkwo, Bassi, Erewa* — letters bleeding downward like small wounds.

At the mouth of Divers' Row, Elder Bassi sat on his stoop under an umbrella that had lost its fight with the wind, watching the street the way old men watch fires they have decided not to fight.

"They tell me four hundred went downriver yesterday," Bassi said, without greeting, because greetings were for strangers and Jude had been carrying the elder's kola nuts since he was six years old. "And three hundred the day before. By Friday there will be more ghosts than people." He sucked his teeth. "The lantern festival is cancelled. First time in sixty years. Did you know I lit the first lantern myself, the year the bridge opened? My hair was black then."

"What will you do?" Jude asked.

"Sit," said Bassi, with tremendous dignity. "Someone must remain who remembers what the town looked like when it was full. Otherwise the water will take that too, and no one will be able to say otherwise." He squinted up at Jude through the drizzle. "Your mother is on the water now?"

"She is."

"Then you are the last Oyelaran on this bank." Bassi said it not as sympathy but as fact, weighing it, setting it down. "Be careful what you do with the weight. Last ones always carry double."

* * *

The house on Divers' Row was not a house anymore. It was a memory of a house wearing the shape of a house.

His mother had packed the way she did everything — thoroughly, ruthlessly, with love expressed as discipline. The walls showed pale rectangles where the photographs had hung. The corner where the cooking stones sat was swept clean, the ashes carried out and scattered in the garden because Adaeze did not believe in leaving warmth behind for strangers or spirits to misuse. Only the wedding chest remained, squatting in the center of the main room like a fat brown toad, and even that was emptied now — its contents distributed among four berths' worth of luggage, except for the single item she had pressed into his hands at the rail that morning without a word.

Her father's diving weight.

It sat in his pocket now as he moved through the empty rooms, a smooth oval of dark stone worn glossy by decades of his grandfather's grip, cold at first and then warming against his thigh until it felt less like an object and more like an organ, something his body had decided it needed. Seven pounds of stone. His mother had held it out to him on the gangplank with both hands, the way you surrender a weapon, or a crown, and had said only: *"You are staying; stay rooted."*

He had almost laughed. Rooted. He, whose entire life had been an exercise in being unnoticeable, portable, packable. But he had taken it, and the weight of it had traveled up his arm and settled somewhere behind his breastbone, and it had not left.

He climbed the ladder to the loft where he and Ngozi had slept as children, head to foot, arguing across the divide of a shared blanket about whose turn it was to fetch water. Her sleeping mat was gone. In the dust where it had lain, she had traced something with a finger — he knelt and made it out in the failing light: not words, just a long looping line, like a river drawn by someone in a hurry to get downstream. He put his thumb over it, then took his thumb away. Some marks should be allowed to fade on their own schedule.

Downstairs again, he stood in the main room as the afternoon dimmed, and the silence of the house spoke in the voices of everyone who had ever filled it. His father's laugh, low and rolling, gone eleven years into the river. His grandfather's cough from the room that had been his. Ngozi reading aloud — she had read everything aloud, signs and labels and other people's letters, as if the world were a book she refused to let anyone else hold silently. His mother singing under her breath while she counted rice, that soft arithmetic hum, *one measure for the pot, one for tomorrow, one for dignity.*

Empty houses are not quiet. Empty houses are loud with everything that used to happen in them.

He went out before the noise could bury him.

* * *

Dusk came down over the river like a lid.

From the bluff above the crossing, Jude could see the whole shrinking anatomy of the town: the bridge, closed now, its far end gated and chained, standing in the swollen water like a beast that had waded too deep and thought better of it. The core streets clustered around the company compound, lamplit, stubborn. And beyond them the lower quarter — his quarter, Divers' Row, the market, the drying sheds — going dark house by house, lamp by lamp, as the last holdouts either gave up and boarded the final barges or banked their fires and prepared to argue with the flood in person.

Here and there a lantern still burned. He counted them the way he had once counted sacks: nine, then seven, then five. Little points of warm light in a landscape going to water, each one marking some stubborn human fire that had decided the river would have to negotiate.

Miriam's lantern was in his shirt, folded flat against his heart, where it had lived for weeks.

She had bought it at the autumn market, back when the world still had markets in it — a cheap paper thing, red-gold, painted with a carp, bought as a joke between two people pretending they were not falling in love. *For the scholar of the counting house,* she had said, pressing it into his hands with that crooked smile she thought her father didn't know about. It had survived the fire at the lantern market because he had survived the fire, because he had pulled her out of the smoke and then gone back for a piece of burning paper worth less than a day's wages, and everyone who saw him do it had understood something that neither of them had yet said aloud.

A lot had been understood since then. Understood, and paid for, and buried in scandal and ledgers and the cold arithmetic of boards and factors. Miriam was somewhere in the lamplit core tonight — disowned, disgraced, free, holding together a company office full of men who despised her for her honesty the way a fevered patient despises the physician. He had seen her for one minute at the wharf that morning. One minute, at a distance, across a crowd of departing people, and she had not waved and neither had he, because waving was a language the watchers could read. She had touched her collarbone instead — once, briefly, where a pendant would hang — and he had touched his own chest, where the folded lantern lay, and that had been the whole conversation, and it had been enough, and it had been nowhere near enough.

Tonight, he decided, the lantern would do what lanterns are for.

* * *

He went down to the water at the old diving beach, below the rotted pilings where his father had taught boys to hold their breath, and he sat on the same flat stone where Tamuno had sat with him a season ago and spoken of debts that outlive men. The river ran high and fast, tawny with upland clay, carrying whole trees past the town like messengers sent ahead of an army. Somewhere out in the current, the water was already deeper than a man is tall. In three days, maybe four, it would be walking up Divers' Row.

He unfolded the lantern carefully, the way his mother unpacked rice — aware that what is fragile deserves slowness.

The paper had softened with age and body-heat, gone from crisp to something like skin. The carp on its side had faded from red to the color of dawn. He shook it gently into its true shape and it opened like a lung taking breath, and for a moment he simply held it there, this worthless beautiful object, this joke that had become a covenant, and let himself feel the whole weight of what it stood for: the market evening, her laughter, the smoke and the screaming and her hand in his, the scandal, the broken engagement, the board's grudging mercy, the barge, the empty house, the stone in his pocket. All of it gathered into a cylinder of painted paper no bigger than a loaf of bread.

Mourning, he thought, was just carrying something too long. And release was finally letting the arms come down.

There was no candle to spare in the emptied town — or rather, there were candles, but they belonged to the company store now, and he was done taking things from the company. Instead he broke off a twist of dried reed-cattail from the bank, teased out the fluff, and packed it tight in the lantern's base with a smear of his own precious store of palm oil. An old diver's trick. Tamuno had shown him once: *the water gives light to those who give it fuel.* His grandfather had been talking about something else entirely, probably. Old men usually were.

He struck flint against the back of his knife, twice, three times, and on the fourth stroke the cattail caught.

Warm light bloomed inside the paper, and the carp swam.

That was the thing he had never noticed, holding it unlit — that the fish only existed in flame, that the painting was nothing until light came from within and gave it motion. The carp seemed to turn in the glow, finned and alive, circling the small bright prison of the lantern, and Jude found his throat closing around something that had been building since the barge rounded the bend.

He did not sob. His family did not do that either. But he sat on his father's stone with his father's weight in his pocket and his love's light in his hands, and he let his eyes run hot and free in the darkness where no one could count them, and he said the names out loud to the river, one by one, the way you pay out rope.

"Adaeze. Ngozi. Tamuno." A breath. "Father."

The river said nothing. Rivers are terrible correspondents. But it kept moving, which is its own kind of listening, and the current rose and fell against the beach with a sound like slow breathing, like something vast asleep that might, at any hour, wake and stretch.

"I don't know what I'm staying for," he told it. That was true, or mostly true. He knew the facts — the drowned records, the rigged bonds of forty years sleeping in the flooded lower archive, the truth sitting in the mud like a pearl in an oyster nobody had opened. He knew that someone had to dive for it, and that the water would permit exactly one kind of person to do the diving, and that he had spent the last month teaching his lungs his father's patience for precisely this reason. But knowing a task is not the same as knowing a purpose, and in the dark with the lantern trembling in his hands, the difference yawned wide as the channel.

Then he thought of Miriam touching her collarbone, and of Ngozi's finger-traced river on the loft floor, and of his mother's voice saying *stay rooted*, and he amended the sentence.

"I know what I'm staying *from*," he said. "Maybe that's enough to start."

He stood. The stone in his pocket swung gently against his leg — seven pounds, his anchor, his inheritance — and the lantern swung in his hand, its little heat pushing against the night air, and for one moment man and light and stone hung balanced at the edge of the water like the beam of some crude scale, weighing everything he had lost against everything that remained.

Then he opened his hands.

The lantern did not fall. The breeze off the current took it the way a parent takes a child stepping off a boat — confidently, immediately, without asking permission. It lifted, dipped, caught, and sailed out over the brown water, rising and falling on the wind's uneven breath, its carp turning and turning in the golden dark. It drifted low along the surface, close enough that its light laid a shivering gold path on the floodwater, a road of brightness leading downstream, away, toward the bend, toward the capital, toward his mother and sister and grandfather and everything he loved that had left without looking back.

He watched until it rounded the bend and became a spark and became a rumor and became nothing, and the river went on breathing in the dark, and the gold road faded, and the night took the whole of it.

His arms came down slowly. They felt lighter than they had in seven years.

* * *

He slept that night on the floor of the empty house, wrapped in his one remaining blanket, the diving weight beside his head like a sentinel. Rain came in the small hours and drummed on the roof, patient and enormous, and in his half-dreams the drumming resolved into footsteps, into paddles, into the sound of a hundred barges' worth of water marching on the town.

He woke before dawn to a silence that was wrong.

Not the silence of an empty house — he knew that one now, had learned its vocabulary. This was the silence of held breath. He lay still and listened and heard what was missing: the river's breathing. The steady lap-and-pull against the beach below Divers' Row had stopped.

He was down the slope in the gray half-light with the mud sucking at his feet, and the river answered him before he reached it.

It had risen through the night — not gradually, not the polite inch-by-inch of ordinary floods, but with the sudden appetite of a thing that had finished waiting. The old diving beach was gone. His father's flat stone was gone. The water stood in the shadows of the lower houses, black and glassy-still, so new that it had not yet learned to move, holding in its mirror-face the inverted image of Divers' Row — doors, windows, the pale rectangles of absent photographs — a second town drowned upside down in the sky beneath the earth.

And far upstream, beyond the closed bridge, beyond the hills, the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and a sound was coming down the valley that Jude felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears: a long, low, continuous roar, like a crowd, like a tide, like the future arriving all at once.

The flood was coming. Not tomorrow. Now.

Jude put his hand on the stone in his pocket, turned, and ran for the high ground where Elder Bassi sat keeping the town's memory — because whatever the water was about to give back, someone would need to be alive to write it down.
