## 33. The Flood Comes

The river did not rise the way a thing alive rises — slowly, with warning, with intention. It rose the way a debt rises: silently at first, then all at once, and then it was everywhere, and there was nothing left that was not owed to it.

Jude stood on the high step of the old customs house on the third morning of the flood and watched the lower quarter go under.

It happened by inches, then by feet. The water had come up through the night like a thief who knew the house, sliding along the gutted lanes of the divers' quarter where he had been born, filling the doorways one by one as though taking inventory. By dawn the first row of houses stood to their sills in brown water. By mid-morning the water had climbed past the window ledges, and the doors that people had propped open — propped open so the flood could pass through without taking the walls, an old diver's trick his grandfather had taught him, *let the water in or lose the house* — stood open like mouths swallowing.

The town had contracted to its lantern-lit core. Everyone said so now; it was the phrase of the season, repeated in the market and on the church steps and in the counting house until it lost its meaning. The lantern-lit core: the ridge streets, the factor's hill, the upper warehouses with their stone foundations. Everything below the second cistern line was given over. The company had moved its movable goods upriver weeks ago. What remained below were houses, memories, and paper.

Paper. That was what kept pulling at him. Not the houses — the houses were gone already, and everyone in them had climbed to higher ground weeks before, carrying what could be carried, leaving behind what could not, which was nearly everything, which was always everything. But the Meridian Company's records office sat in the lowest warehouse on the waterfront, and Calloway, in the last chaos of the evacuation, had ordered the registers locked inside rather than carried up — too heavy, too many, no barge space for archives when there was barely barge space for people. He had posted two men to watch the door until the water made even that absurd, and then the men had come away, and the door had been barred, and the whole drowned weight of twenty years of Meridian bookkeeping had been left to the river like a body.

Jude had thought about it every day since. He had thought about it while hauling sandbags at the ridge, while boiling drinking water, while sleeping — badly — on the floor of Elder Bassi's back room with six other displaced families. He thought about it now, watching a roof detach itself from a house he recognized and go sailing down the middle of what had been Oroyemi Street, turning slowly, like a great dark leaf.

"Stop staring," Bassi said beside him. The old man's voice was rough with three sleepless nights. "It won't bring anything back."

"I'm not looking for what's gone," Jude said. "I'm looking for what's coming."

Bassi followed his gaze down to the warehouse district, where only the rooftops showed now, black triangles in a brown sea, and the company flagpole still standing above the records office like a grave marker. The old man understood before Jude said another word. Jude saw it happen in his face — the slow narrowing, the way his hand came up and gripped the rail.

"No," Bassi said.

"The ledgers are down there."

"They're drowned. They're ruined. And if they weren't ruined, they'd be worthless, because paper is paper, boy—"

"Bond registers are written twice," Jude said. He had spent enough years in the counting house to know this the way he knew his own heartbeat. "Ink copy and carbon press. The carbons are waxed against damp — Calloway had it done two years ago after the monsoon warped a shipment manifest. Waxed paper floats, Elder. It doesn't dissolve. It waits."

Bassi looked at him for a long time. Below them the river pushed a dead goat against the customs house pilings, turned it, released it.

"You're talking about diving into floodwater," Bassi said finally. "In spate. In the season when the river takes strong men standing on dry land."

"I'm talking about diving," Jude said, "in my father's water."

* * *

His grandfather had taught him the breath in the flooded shallows months ago, back when it had seemed like desperation instead of inheritance. Now Tamuno sat him down in Bassi's kitchen — the only dry room left with a table — and would not let him touch rope or weight until the lesson was done again, properly, the old way.

"Floodwater is not the lagoon," the old man said. His hands, scarred and knuckled like tree roots, lay flat on the table between them. "Your father dove clear water. He could see his hands at ten fathoms. You will see nothing. Nothing, do you understand? The flood is mud and motion. It will push you sideways. It will pull you into the current seams and hold you there like a fist. So we do not dive with the eyes." He tapped Jude's chest, twice, hard. "We dive with this. Count everything. Trust nothing you feel until you have counted it twice."

They built the rig together out of what the flood had not yet claimed: a plumb line of braided palm fiber, forty fathoms of it, knotted every fathom so Jude could read depth with his fingers in the dark; a stone weight shaped from a broken grinding quern; a fish-oil lantern sealed in a gourd for the surface crew; a knife; a net bag that had once held oysters. Adaeze watched from the doorway with her arms folded, saying nothing, her face doing the arithmetic he had seen her do all his life — the cost of things, weighed against the worth.

When she finally spoke, it was only this: "You come up when your grandfather signals. First signal. Not the second. Not when you're almost finished. The first."

"Yes, Mama."

"And you take nothing for yourself down there. Whatever you find, you find for everyone."

He looked at her. She looked back, and he understood that she knew — that mothers always knew, that she had perhaps known since the night he'd come home smelling of lamp oil and ink, since the register had changed its mind about his bond, since the word *forgery* had entered their house without ever being spoken aloud. She knew what he intended to pull out of the water, or at least she knew it was more than paper.

"Whatever I find," he said, "I find for everyone."

* * *

They went down at slack tide on the fifth morning, when the river paused between surges like a man catching breath. The rain had stopped in the night for the first time in nine days, and a thin, wrong-colored light lay on the flood — the color of pewter, of old coins, of skin gone cold. Four boats went out: Tamuno's dugout holding Tamuno and the rope, Bassi in the second with the gourd lantern and a pole to sound the submerged lane, and two younger men — Okon, whose father had been a rigger, and small, quick Etim, who had lost his apprenticeship papers in the evacuation and with them his trade — to work the lines.

Jude stripped to his loincloth in the gray light and stood in the bow while the water slid beneath them, brown and vast and patient. Somewhere under that water was the street where he had learned to walk. Somewhere under it was the counting house where he had learned to read, and the ledger room where he had learned to lie, and the shelf where the carbon-pressed bond registers slept in their waxed jackets, waiting. He felt no fear, and the absence of fear frightened him more than fear would have.

"Say the count," Tamuno said.

"One breath, four counts down. Rest on the bottom, no movement, let the body learn the water before the hands ask anything of it. Work in tens. Come up on the third knot of air or the first pull of the line, whichever comes first."

"And if the line goes slack?"

"It means the anchor dragged, or my grandfather is telling me something I don't want to hear. Either way, I come up."

Tamuno nodded once. Then the old man took Jude's face in both his scarred hands, the way he had not done since Jude was a child, and pressed their foreheads together. "Your father went down for pearls," he said quietly. "You go down for truth. The water respects both. Go."

Jude spat over the side — for luck, for the river, for the dead — set the stone between his knees, tipped forward, and let the flood take him.

* * *

The world closed over him like a door.

It was warm — that was the first shock, warmer than any water he had ever entered, warm as bathwater, warm as blood, the flood having sat in the sun for days steeping in everything it had swallowed. Then it was dark, darker than the deepest pearl bed his father had ever described, a darkness with texture, thick with silt, and he was falling through it blind, counting — *one, two, three, four* — feeling the knots of the plumb line run through his fingers like beads on a rosary.

At the fourth knot he leveled out. The bottom was not far; the flood had raised the riverbed's ceiling until the drowned street lay only eight fathoms down. His feet found it: not mud exactly but a soft chaos of things — silt over planking, planking over silt, the ghost-shape of a road. He hung in the brown silence with his arms out, letting his body learn the water, as his grandfather had said, and the current moved around him in slow pulses, pushing, releasing, pushing, like the breathing of some enormous sleeping animal.

He began to swim.

He had memorized the route from above: down Oroyemi Street's submerged spine, past the second cistern — its rim he might feel with his hand — turn east at the corner of the cooperage, forty paces along the warehouse wall to the records office door. Forty paces underwater, blind, in current. He swam by touch, one hand trailing the wall, and counted paces in tens as taught, and the wall gave him brick, brick, timber, a gap where a window had been, brick again, and then — at thirty — his fingers found carved stone, a lintel, and beneath it, at shoulder height in the new world, a barred door.

The bar was on the outside. Of course it was; the watchers had barred it behind themselves when they abandoned the post. Jude worked the knife into the gap and levered, and the bar groaned and gave, and the door swung inward on darkness deeper than the flood's.

Inside, the water was stiller. Silt hung in it like smoke. He pulled himself through the doorway into the ledger room, which he knew by heart from the other side of the flood — the long shelves, the plan chests, the iron cabinet where Calloway kept the bond registers under lock. The lock was nothing; the key hung in the counting house upstairs, and the lock had rusted anyway, and when Jude put his knife to the hasp it crumbled like biscuit.

He found the registers by shape. His fingers walked the shelves, reading spines the way Tamuno read knots: *Manifests, manifests, freight, freight — here.* The bond registers, eleven of them, going back past Calloway's tenure to his predecessor's, to the founder's, each one fat with years of names. He pulled four — the earliest, the ones Bassi had said would matter, the ones covering the years of the 'lost' divers and the extended bonds — and fed them into the net bag, waxed covers slick under his fingers, whole, intact, waiting. Twenty years of names. Twenty years of debts that had outlived the men who owed them.

He was cinching the bag when his hand passed over the open plan chest at the end of the shelf, and something caught his finger — an edge, a corner, a page lying loose on top of the others. He lifted it into the brown lightless light and read it by touch alone, tracing the impressed lines the way a blind man reads, and the words assembled themselves slowly in his mind from memory and shape:

*Bond extension authorized — J. Oyelaran — term renewed seven years — consideration: none.*

Dated three days before the flood. Signed with initials he knew better than his own name.

Calloway had written a new bond for him. Had prepared it, dated it, initialled it — and left it here, in the drowning room, because there had been no time, or no need, or because a bond unsigned in the master register is only a wish. It didn't matter why. What mattered was that Jude was holding proof — physical, datable, initialed — that as of this month, the Meridian Company had meant to bind him for seven more years. Not the berth list alone. Not the rigged allocation alone. This. A fresh chain, forged ahead of time, waiting for his neck.

The current chose that moment to shove him, hard, sideways into the shelving. The building groaned around him — a deep, structural complaint, timbers shifting in the softened mud — and somewhere above, faint through fathoms of water, he felt the plumb line twitch.

First signal. Not the second.

Jude hugged the bag to his chest and kicked for the doorway, and the river's breathing quickened around him, and he swam up through the brown dark counting knots, *one, two, three*, lungs burning, the surface a rumor of gray far overhead — and broke into the light with the bag in his teeth and his grandfather's hands closing on his arms like the hands of God.

* * *

They did not speak on the water. There was no speaking; the surge had begun again, and Tamuno poled them hard for the ridge while Okon and Etim bailed and Bassi stared at the net bag as though it might contain a serpent. Only when they had grounded the boat below the customs house steps, only when Jude stood dripping on the stones with the flood roaring at his back, did the old elder reach out with both hands and ask, silently, permission.

Jude gave it. Bassi opened the bag right there on the steps, in front of whoever had gathered — and there were dozens gathered now, drawn by the sight of the boats, the whole remnant of the lower quarter standing in the rain-washed gray light — and drew out the first register. Water beaded off its waxed cover and ran away down the stone. The pages inside, when Bassi thumbed them, were stiff and clean and legible, every name intact, every date, every crossed-out term, every renewal marked *consideration: none* in a dead clerk's careful hand.

The crowd had gone very quiet. Rain ticked on the wet covers. Someone near the back — a woman's voice, cracked — read aloud a name that everyone within earshot recognized, because the man it belonged to had gone into the floodwater nineteen years ago repairing the bridge piles and had never been listed among the paid-off, and his widow was standing not ten feet away, older than grief.

Then another voice read another name. And another.

Jude stood among them, streaming river water onto the stones, holding in his other hand the page he had torn free of the plan chest — the unfinished bond, the fresh seven-year chain, dated and initialed by a living hand. Around him the crowd thickened, and the reading spread outward through it like fire through dry grass, name after name after name, decades of them, and he saw Bassi straighten slowly, the old man's face changing, gathering itself into something that had not been on it in all the months Jude had known him: purpose, cold and total.

Elder Bassi folded the register under his arm and turned to face them all.

"Not revenge," he said, in the voice he used for judgments. "Record. Every name. Every year. Every theft. We write it all down while the ink is still wet — and then we take it to the board inspector, who arrives on the morning tide tomorrow."

He looked at Jude then, across the heads of the crowd, and something passed between them that needed no language.

"And someone," Bassi said, "will tell me everything that happened in the counting house at night — everything, boy — before the inspector asks us first. Because if Calloway learns what we hold before that man steps off the barge, these books will burn, and us with them."

Behind them, the river went on rising.
