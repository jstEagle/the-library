## 34. Evidence in the Mud

The registers came up out of the water heavier than they had gone down.

Jude felt it in his arms as he carried them through the drowned streets at first light — that strange arithmetic of soaked paper, how a thing meant to be weightless became a burden only after it had been submerged. Each register was wrapped in oilcloth he had stitched himself in the dark hours, because a diver's son knows that what you save from water must be saved twice: once from the drowning, and once from the air that follows. Water ruins slowly. Air ruins fast. His father had taught him that about pearls; the ledger taught him it about truth.

He walked the long way to Elder Bassi's house, past the lower quarter where the flood had done its patient work. Doors stood open on empty rooms. A cooking pot floated in a doorway like a small black moon. Someone's laundry line still stood, shirts swelling and subsiding with the current, as though the absent owners might return to claim them from the river. The water was beginning its retreat — you could see it in the mud lines on the walls, a tide chart written in silt, each day's falling marked in brown bands like the rings of a tree — but the retreat left everything it touched changed. Marked. Testified to.

Jude counted the registers under his breath as he walked. Nine. Nine bound volumes, their spines rotted soft, their pages fused into bricks of pulp and ink. He had spent three dives on the counting house cellar, and his lungs had learned his father's patience down there in the green murk, waiting for the silt to settle before each lift. The company's own records, drowned by the company's own negligence — Calloway had ordered the cellar sealed against looters when the evacuation began, and the seal had become a tomb. The water had kept what the company thought it owned.

His arms ached. He let them ache. Some weights you carry because putting them down would be a kind of lie.

* * *

Elder Bassi's house sat on the high ground above the old fish market, one of the few in the town that had never taken water, not in forty years of floods. It had been built by Bassi's father with that single intention, walls of fired brick instead of mud daub, floor raised on a plinth of stone, and the old man liked to say that prudence was the only inheritance worth leaving. This morning his door was already open when Jude came up the hill, as if Bassi had smelled the news coming on the wind off the water.

"You look like a man carrying a corpse," Bassi said.

"Close enough." Jude set the registers on the table in the front room, all nine of them, dripping onto the polished wood. "It's the company's memory. I pulled it out of the cellar."

Bassi did not touch them at once. He was an old man who moved the way a heron moves — long stillnesses, then a single precise strike. He bent close, reading the spines, and Jude watched him understand what he was looking at: the bond registers, going back past Calloway's tenure, past Calloway's predecessor, back into the years when the Meridian Trading Company had first learned that a man's debt could outlive a man.

"The flood took the counting house cellar," Bassi said.

"The flood took what Calloway locked in the counting house cellar."

"Ah." Bassi straightened. In his mouth the syllable held a whole sermon. "And now the water has given it back."

"That's what I came to say to you." Jude wiped river mud from his hands onto his trousers, a habit, an apology for bringing the outside world into this clean room. "I don't know what to do with it. That's the truth. I've known since I surfaced last night. I know what's in these books — I've read enough of them by lamplight to know — but knowing what a thing is isn't the same as knowing what it's for."

Bassi pulled out a chair and sat, and gestured Jude into another, and for a while neither of them spoke. The room smelled of brick dust and dried fish and the particular camphor that old men rub into their joints. Through the window, downhill, the town lay in its brown shroud, and somewhere below, the river made its endless argument with the pilings of the dead bridge.

"Do you know why my father built this house so high?" Bassi said at last.

"So it wouldn't flood."

"So it wouldn't flood," Bassi agreed. "But also so that when the flood came — and it always comes — there would be a place above the water where people could gather and remember what the town was before. Every flood season of my childhood, this room filled with neighbors. We counted who was alive. We counted who was lost. We wrote both numbers down." He tapped the table with one knuckle. "Counting is the oldest prayer we have, boy. My people were divers before your people were divers. We went down into the dark and brought things up, and the whole meaning of the act was this: *it exists, we saw it, we name it.*"

Jude looked at the sodden registers. "These aren't pearls."

"No," Bassi said. "They're better. Pearls you can spend. This—" his hand hovered over the nearest volume without touching it, the way a man hovers over a sleeping snake, "—this is the debt itself, made visible. Do you know how many bonds were rigged in this town? How many fathers' debts were transferred to sons without consent? How many workers were held past their terms because the company 'lost' their discharge papers?"

"I know mine was."

"I know yours was." Bassi's eyes were pale and steady as winter light. "But yours is one thread. Pull it and you have a story. Leave it woven and you have cloth. The question is whether you want a story or a cloth."

* * *

By midday the word had traveled the way word travels in a half-drowned town — mouth to ear across the flooded lanes, by canoe where the streets had become canals, by children wading thigh-deep with messages they only partly understood. Bassi sent for them all: the ones who had stayed through the evacuation, the ones the barge could not hold or would not hold, the ones who had refused to go. They came up the hill to the house that never flooded, and they dripped on Bassi's floor, and they stared at the nine ruined books as if the books might stare back.

Jude knew every face. That was the strange mercy of being left behind: the town that remained had become small enough to hold in one glance. There was Mama Ekwueme, who kept the last functioning cookshop and fed whoever could not pay. There was the brothers Ojo, ferrymen, who had run supplies across the floodwater when the bridge closed and never raised their prices, which in that season was its own kind of heroism. There was old Sister Amara, who had buried two husbands and a son to the river and still came down to its edge every evening to talk to the water as if negotiating. There were the warehouse men, the net-menders, the cooper's widow, a dozen others — perhaps thirty souls in all, the sediment the flood had left behind.

They crowded into the front room and spilled onto the veranda, and Bassi stood among them with his hands folded, and told them what Jude had carried up the hill.

Then he said: "Open them."

So they opened the registers, there on the table and on the floor, peeling apart pages that had fused like leaves pressed too long between boards. It was slow, sacred, ugly work. The ink had run in places, bloomed into grey ghosts, but the company's clerks had used good iron-gall ink and heavy paper, because the company trusted paper more than it trusted people, and much of it survived. Mama Ekwueme read aloud where the writing defeated others. She had a schoolteacher's voice, round and carrying, and she read names the way you read a litany.

"Entry 214. Diver Obi Nwosu. Bond extended four years on finding of 'unsatisfactory recovery yield.' Witness: none. Finding signed: R. Calloway."

"Entry 891. Debt of deceased diver Etim Bassey transferred to daughter, age eleven. Transfer fee charged to the daughter's account."

"Entry 1103. Worker discharged in full. Discharge certificate issued. Certificate recalled same day, 'clerical error.' Bond continued six years."

She read until her voice gave out, and then the cooper's widow took up the reading, and then one of the Ojo brothers. Outside, the rain began again, softly, drumming on the veranda roof, and inside the only other sound was the wet whisper of turning pages and the low responses of the listeners — not amazement, Jude noticed. Not even shock. What rose around those tables was something older and quieter: recognition. Half the room heard their own family's names, or their neighbors', or names they had grown up hearing spoken carefully, the way you speak of the drowned.

Sister Amara found her son. Entry 1407. He had died diving for the company at nineteen, and she had always been told his death had cancelled his debts. The ledger said otherwise. The ledger said his death had *transferred* them — to his mother, to the woman now sitting on Bassi's floor with the page trembling in her hands.

For a long moment no one spoke. The rain filled the silence.

"He was nineteen," she said finally, not to anyone. "They let me believe the water had settled his account. They stood at the burial and let me believe it."

"They lie with paper," Bassi said gently, "because paper doesn't bleed and doesn't scream. But paper drowns." He nodded toward the windows, toward the flooded town below. "The company thought it had buried these books safe in its cellar. The river disagreed. Now the question is what we do with what the river has ruled on."

A voice from the veranda — Tambo Ojo, the younger ferryman, a hard young man whose hands were scarred from rope: "We burn the counting house. Whatever's left of it. And when Calloway comes back with his inspectors, we put these pages in their faces and we take back every coin the company stole, with interest, out of its stores."

Murmurs of assent, hot and quick. Jude felt the heat of it move through the room like a change of wind, and understood it, and feared it. Anger was honest. Anger was also exactly what the company would know how to answer. You could crush a riot. You could arrest a arsonist. The company had soldiers and lawyers and the colonial governor's ear. What the company did not have — what no one had ever figured out how to take from a town — was its memory.

"Tambo," Jude said, and waited until the young man turned. "If we burn anything, we become the story. *Workers burn counting house, order restored, ringleaders transported.* I've read that story before. It's in these ledgers too — entry after entry, towns that rose up, and what happened to them afterward. The company writes the ending every time." He laid his hand flat on the open register before him. "There's a reason they keep records instead of memories. Records can be seized. Memories have to be killed one person at a time, and even then they leak."

"Then what?" Tambo's jaw was tight. "We forgive them? We hand this to some inspector and ask nicely?"

"Not forgive. Not ask." Jude looked around the room, at the faces of the people the barge had left behind, the people the company had counted worthless, and he found the words rising from somewhere below thought, from the diver's depth where his father had lived. "We copy them. All of them. Every entry, every forged extension, every transfer, every recalled discharge. We make our own register — in our own hand, witnessed by everyone in this room — and then we make a second copy, and a third. One stays in this house, that has never flooded. One goes into the ground in a sealed jar, the way our people buried seed corn against the bad years. And one—" he paused, and chose the words with a diver's care, weighing each against the pressure, "—one waits for the inspector. Because Calloway will come back, and he will come back with men whose power depends on believing the company's books are true. And we will not burn his books and we will not beg. We will simply show him that the truth now lives in thirty houses instead of one cellar. You can raid a cellar."

Old Sister Amara lifted her head. "You cannot raid a whole town that remembers."

"That," said Elder Bassi, "is the whole of it."

* * *

They worked for nine days.

They worked in Bassi's front room by lamplight and daylight, in shifts, because there were not many of them and the copying was slow and the eyes tired quickly over corrupted pages. The ferrymen ferried. Mama Ekwueme cooked for the scribes and refused payment and dared anyone to insist. Sister Amara, whose grief had been given a shape at last, copied the death entries herself, every one, and insisted on reading each name aloud as she finished it, so that the room had to hear: Obi Nwosu. Etim Bassey. Kofi Mensah. Adaeze's late husband — Jude's father — Sunday Oyelaran, whose bond had been transferred to a seven-year-old boy by a clerk's neat hand, the transfer fee charged to the boy's account, compounding annually, for seven years, before the boy was old enough to hold a broom in the warehouse.

Jude copied his father's entry himself. His hand did not shake. He had expected it to shake. Instead there came a stillness, the stillness of deep water, and he understood that the shaking had happened months ago, in the counting house at night, when he had first seen the false berth list with his own name struck from it. Grief, it turned out, was not a wave. It was a tide, and it had already gone out, and what remained on the flats was workable ground.

Miriam came on the fourth day.

She came quietly, alone, in plain clothes with her hood up, and stood dripping in the doorway until Bassi waved her in. No one spoke against her. That surprised Jude more than her arrival — but he watched the room decide, in that silent instant, that the factor's daughter who had broken her engagement publicly, who had confessed to the board, who had chosen disgrace over the elegant knot of her duty, had already paid her toll at a different gate. She sat at the corner of the table and copied entries with a fine, quick clerk's hand far better than anyone else's, and when she reached the worst of them — the children's transfers, the eleven-year-old girls entered into debt — Jude saw her stop, and press her lips together, and continue. She did not weep and she did not leave. At dusk of that fourth day she said, without looking up: "My father initialed this one. Page 604. I want my copy to be exact."

And it was exact. Jude understood then what she was doing: not atonement, which is a coin that buys nothing, but testimony. She was building, stroke by stroke, the record of her own house's crimes, in her own hand, so that no one could ever say the daughter had shielded the father. It was the bravest copying in the room, and the room knew it, and honored it the way working people honor such things — by making space, by refilling her lamp oil, by saying nothing.

On the ninth night they finished. Three full copies. Bassi took the first and locked it in the iron chest beneath his floor, the chest his father had cast for exactly this purpose forty years before, though the old man had imagined gold and got truth instead. The second went into a glazed jar sealed with wax and tar, and the Ojo brothers carried it by canoe to the far bank at midnight and buried it above the flood line beneath a fig tree, and only five people knew the spot. The third copy lay wrapped in oilcloth on Bassi's table, waiting, patient as a pearl in its shell.

They gathered around it. Thirty-odd people, lamplit, exhausted, smelling of ink and rain.

"Tomorrow the inspector's boat arrives," Bassi said. "Calloway will be on it, or behind it, wearing his best smile and his best lies. He will come with lawyers. He may come with soldiers. He will expect either cowering mobs or burning buildings, because those are the only two responses he has ever planned for." He looked around the room, and Jude saw that the old man was not commanding them but asking — asking the way you ask the water before a dive. "What he will find instead is a town that has spent nine days writing down the truth in triplicate. When he stands before us, no hand will be raised against him. Nothing will be burned. Nothing will be broken. He will simply be *remembered*, in front of witnesses, in his own words, in ink he cannot drown."

Sister Amara spoke last, from her chair by the wall, her voice dry and level as reeds.

"My grandmother had a saying. *You do not fight the flood. You build above the waterline, and you write down what it destroyed.*" She touched the wrapped copy on the table, once, lightly, the way you touch a grave marker. "Let him come. We are above the waterline now."

Outside, far downstream, a lantern appeared on the black water — the lead boat of the inspector's convoy, hours early, its light wavering on the current like a question the river was asking of the town.

Everyone in the room saw it. No one moved. Jude stood with his hand resting on the oilcloth, feeling through the fabric the weight of every borrowed life in those pages, and watched the light come on, and thought: *we are ready to be believed.*

The lantern grew nearer.
