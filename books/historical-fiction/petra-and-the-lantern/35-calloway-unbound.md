## 35. Calloway Unbound

The inspector's barge came up the river on a morning the color of pewter, three days after Jude had laid the drowned ledgers out to dry across every flat surface in Elder Bassi's compound. They looked, drying, like a field of wounded things — pages pinned to lines with clothespegs, ink bleeding into pale ghosts of themselves, the registers of forty years exhaling their secrets into the air. The smell of them filled the whole yard: river mud and mildew and old paper, a smell like something exhumed.

Jude had not slept properly in three days. He had slept the way a man sleeps when he is guarding a fire — in snatches, one ear always open, rising at every sound to turn a page before it curled or stuck. Bassi had given him the compound's back room and its long drying lines and, more valuable than either, the old man's silence. Word had gone out through the quarter the way word always went, quietly, mouth to mouth: *the water gave something back. Come and see. Bring nothing. Tell no one at the company.*

They came anyway, of course. That was the nature of this town; it had never learned to keep a secret, only to keep it briefly. By the second evening there were a dozen people in Bassi's yard pretending to inspect his yams, and by the third there were thirty, and Adaeze stood among them with her arms folded and her chin high, having refused — over four days of argument — to board any barge until this was finished.

"You are free," she had said to him, not gently. "Your name is clean in that book. You could be on the water already."

"And Calloway would still be here," Jude had answered, "writing new names into new books."

She had looked at him for a long moment then, and he had seen her decide something — not to forgive him yet, but to stand beside him while she was still angry. It was, he thought, the most motherly thing she could have done.

Now, on the morning of the inspector's arrival, the yard was full and quiet in the way a courtroom is quiet. The drying lines hung heavy with the past. And out on the river, growing larger by the minute, came the board's barge — white hull, brass fittings, a flag at the stern that cost more than most men in the quarter earned in a year — carrying Mr. Aldous Wren, Inspector of Accounts for the Meridian Trading Company, a man Jude had never met and knew entirely by reputation: meticulous, humorless, incorruptible, which in the world of the Meridian Trading Company amounted to a kind of madness.

Jude stood at the gate with the ledgers stacked against his chest. He had chosen three of them. The bond register from the flood season nineteen years ago, the one whose margins held the small penciled annotations in Calloway's own hand — *held through season, extended, no berth allotted* — beside names he recognized now as the fathers and uncles and neighbors of people standing in this yard. The wage ledger from eleven years ago, where deductions appeared that no worker had ever been shown. And the current register, the one he himself had altered, its forged page dry and stiff and looking, he thought, almost convincing — though whether it would fool an inspector or damn him, he genuinely did not know.

Behind him, the whole quarter had turned out. Tamuno sat on Bassi's step with his hands on his knees, breathing the way he breathed before a dive, slow and deep and deliberate. Ngozi stood at her brother's shoulder. She had not forgiven him either — that wound was older than this week and would take longer than this morning — but she had come home from the counting house with copies of everything, made in her careful clerk's hand, because she understood, better than anyone, that a record was a weapon you could hold in both hands.

And at the edge of the crowd, apart from everyone, stood Miriam.

She had not spoken to Jude since the night in the counting house, since the watchman's lantern had swept the corridor and her voice had rung out — loud, laughing, drunk-seeming, magnificent — about a lost earring and a broken shutter, pulling every eye and every boot away from the register room while Jude pressed his father's mark into wet ink with a hand that shook so badly he had smeared it twice. Four days. In four days she had broken her engagement publicly, confessed to the company's local board in writing, and become, overnight, the most scandalous woman on the river. She stood now in a plain dress with no ornament in her hair, and she did not look at Jude, and Jude did not look at her, and between them lay the whole width of the yard like a river neither had yet found the crossing for.

The barge touched the landing. The gangway came down. And Mr. Aldous Wren stepped off onto the mud of the lower quarter with the expression of a man who has been asked to audit a latrine.

* * *

He was smaller than Jude had imagined. A compact, gray, precise man in a coat too fine for the mud, who picked his way along the landing boards with his lips pressed thin and his eyes cataloguing everything — the leaning stalls, the flood-stain lines on the warehouse walls, the crowd gathered at Bassi's gate with the particular stillness of people waiting for something they have decided is owed to them.

"Mr. Wren," said Calloway, coming down the landing behind him, and Jude watched the factor perform welcome like a man putting on a coat. "The journey was tolerable, I hope. The bridge being closed, we're rather cut off from the amenities of—"

"I have read your correspondence, Mr. Calloway." Wren's voice was dry as August. "All of it. Including the portions you may have preferred I read less carefully." He turned to the crowd, and his gaze went down the rows of faces and stopped, as if snagged, on the drying lines visible beyond the gate — on the pages hanging there like strange pale fish.

"What," he said, "are those."

Jude stepped forward.

"Those," he said, "are your records, sir. Forty years of them. The river kept them better than the company did."

Calloway moved then. It was small — half a step, a hand rising as if to adjust his cuff — but Jude had spent seven years learning to read the bodies of powerful men the way his father had read water, feeling the current shift beneath the calm surface, and he saw it all: the flicker, the recalculation, the moment the factor understood exactly what was hanging on those lines and how much of it bore his initials.

"Young man," Calloway said pleasantly, "these are company property. Removed without authorization, I might add, during a period of civil disturbance. I'll thank you to—"

"They were removed," said Elder Bassi, coming forward with the unhurried gait of a man who had buried two factors and outlived a third, "from a storeroom that flooded to the rafters. Salvage. Under maritime custom, salvage belongs to those who save it." He smiled, showing old teeth. "I was a boatman forty years, Mr. Factor. I know salvage law better than I know my prayers."

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Wren said, "I will see them," and the morning tipped over into whatever came next.

* * *

They laid the ledgers out on Bassi's longest table, in the yard, in front of everyone — because Bassi insisted, and because Jude wanted it so. Justice done behind doors becomes rumor; justice done in the open becomes memory, and memory, Jude had learned from the water itself, was the only thing in this town stronger than the company.

Wren worked the way rain falls — steadily, without passion, covering everything. Page after page, his finger moving down columns, his assistant — a young woman with ink-stained fingers whom Jude suspected was the real engine of the inspection — cross-checking against the copies Ngozi had prepared and against the duplicate books Wren had brought from the capital.

It took two hours. The yard did not stir. Children were lifted onto shoulders to see. Old Ekong, whose father's name Jude had found penciled in the margin of the oldest register — *held through season, lost to water, bond transferred to son* — stood with his hat in his hands the entire time, as if at a funeral.

At last Wren set down his glass, straightened, and looked at Calloway.

"The annotations are yours," he said. It was not a question. "I have compared the hand against eleven years of your correspondence. No one else in this office writes a Greek epsilon for 'e' in the middle of an English word, Mr. Calloway. It is a very distinctive habit."

Calloway's face did something complicated and then settled into dignity, the last coat he had left to put on. "Mr. Wren. If you knew the circumstances of this post. The pressures from above, the quotas from the capital, the—"

"The quotas," Wren said, "do not require you to extend a dead man's bond to his nine-year-old son." He tapped the page. It was the entry Jude had found first, weeks ago, and still could not read without his chest tightening: his own father's name, the dive debts, and beneath it, in pencil, *bond to J. Oyelaran, minor, approved C.C.* "Nor do they require berths to be withheld from workers' families so that their laborers cannot leave. Nor—" he turned a page with two fingers, delicately, as if it were soiled, "—nor does any quota require this."

He read aloud, then, in his flat dry voice, the entries of nineteen years. The names went into the air over the yard one after another, and with each one someone in the crowd made a sound — a breath, a cry, the low sustained note that a crowd makes when grief and vindication arrive in the same body. Ekong sat down slowly in the dirt. A woman near the gate began to weep without any noise at all.

Jude stood through all of it with his arms at his sides and felt — not triumph. He had expected triumph, or imagined he would feel it, the way you imagine a feast. What he felt instead was the weight of it, the sheer tonnage of small cruelties, each one rationalized, each one initialed, each one small enough that no single day's cruelty would have seemed worth drowning a man over — and all of it together, added up across forty years in a bookkeeper's neat hand, a fortune built out of other people's held breath. His father had died owing money to men who wrote like this. He himself had grown up inside these columns. There was no pleasure in seeing the cage from outside when you remembered the shape of it from within.

Wren closed the last ledger.

"Mr. Calloway," he said. "You are relieved of your office effective immediately. You will accompany me to the capital on my barge. There will be a hearing. Given the volume of documentation—" he glanced at the drying lines, at Ngozi's copies, at the crowd of witnesses standing shoulder to shoulder in a yard full of yam mounds, "—I suspect the hearing will be brief."

* * *

They let him pack a single trunk. Jude watched it happen from the warehouse steps, because some part of him needed to see it plainly, without the distortion of anger, the way you need to look directly at a thing to know its true size.

Calloway came out of the factor's house with the trunk carried behind him by his own porter, and he paused on the veranda and looked out over the town he had governed for nineteen years — the warehouses, the landing, the lantern poles going up for a festival that would now be a different kind of festival — and for a moment his composure cracked along a seam Jude hadn't known was there, and what showed through was not rage. It was something smaller and sadder: a man discovering that the ledger of his life had been kept by someone else all along, and the balance was not what he believed.

He saw Jude on the steps. He crossed the mud toward him, and the porters and the inspector's assistants let him go, and for a few strides the two of them were alone in the noise of the yard.

"You understand what happens to this place without me," Calloway said. Not pleading. Almost gentle. "The credit lines. The grain contracts. You think the board sent Wren because they love justice? They'll send someone worse. Someone who won't learn the names."

"Then I'll teach him the names," Jude said.

Calloway studied him. And here was the thing Jude had not expected, the thing he would think about for years afterward: the factor nodded. Once, slowly, as if confirming a figure.

"I liked you," Calloway said. "From the first month. Do you know that? You were the only person in this entire operation who checked the arithmetic twice." He glanced back at the house, at the life packed into one trunk. "I told myself that liking you made me decent. That's the trick of it, boy. Every man in that register thought he was decent. Find the one kindness you do each day and hold it up against the rest, and the rest goes dark." He adjusted his cuffs one final time. "You'll do better. Perhaps. If you remember that you nearly didn't."

He walked to the barge. Wren received him without ceremony. And the white hull pulled away from the landing with the factor standing at the rail, upright, unshackled, leaving exactly the way he had made so many others leave — rationed to a single trunk, on a vessel he had not chosen, bound for a place decided by other men's books.

Nobody cheered. Bassi had asked them not to, earlier, and explained why in one sentence: *Because we are not them.* The crowd stood along the bank and watched the barge diminish, and the silence it left behind was enormous, and clean, like air after lightning.

* * *

The yard emptied slowly after that, the way a fever breaks. People came to the table where the ledgers lay and stood over them, some touching the pages, some unable to. Ekong pressed his palm flat against his father's name and left it there a long time. Adaeze found Jude at the gate and did not embrace him, but she stood close enough that their shoulders touched, which in the grammar of his mother was the same sentence written more carefully.

"It isn't finished," she said. "That man's masters are still in the capital. The ones who set the quotas."

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't finished. But it's begun, and it's written down, and it has our names on the right side of the ink." He looked out at the river, at the white speck vanishing around the bend. "That's more than my father ever got."

Tamuno came up on his other side, moving with the deliberate slowness he permitted himself only when no one was watching closely. "The water gives back," the old man said, "what is truly sunk. The rest it keeps." He squeezed Jude's shoulder once, hard, and let go.

And then the yard was nearly empty, and Jude became aware that one person remained, standing by the drying lines, taking down the last of the pages with great care as though they were laundry she had personally washed. Miriam folded a sheet along its original creases and did not look at him, and the distance between them was suddenly only four feet of trampled mud, which was the farthest distance Jude had ever tried to cross.

"They'll want statements," she said, to the line, to the pegs. "From everyone. From me especially. My confession is already with the board, but Wren will want it again, under seal, and there will be questions about the night of the twenty-third, about the counting house, about—" She stopped. Her hands, he noticed, were not quite steady on the pegs. "About a staged distraction that a certain clerk's brother benefited from considerably."

"There were no witnesses," Jude said carefully.

"There is always a witness." Now she turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed and level and brave in the way he had first seen them across a lantern stall, and it undid him more than the fire had. "The question is only what the witness is willing to say, and what it costs her, and whether—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it the way her father never could. "Whether anyone will still be standing on the bank afterward. When everything is counted."

Jude looked at her — the disowned daughter, the scandal, the woman who had traded a name and an inheritance and an engagement ring for a forged page in a bond register — and he understood that the ledger metaphor had followed them both into this moment and would not release them, and that the only honest entry left was the plain one.

"I'm standing," he said. "I was standing before any of it. Before the fire, before the ledgers, before I knew your name was worth more than mine." He took the last page gently from her hands and set it on the table. "Whatever the hearing asks you, tell the truth. All of it. Even the part about me. Especially that part. I'd rather stand in the mud with you in the open than anywhere at all in the dark."

Miriam breathed out — a long, shaking breath, the breath of a diver surfacing — and for the first time in four days she smiled, small and crooked and real.

"Then come with me tomorrow," she said. "To Wren. Both of us. Let them write us down however they want." Behind her, the river ran bright with afternoon, and somewhere upstream the bridge timbers groaned as the flood began, very slowly, to fall.
