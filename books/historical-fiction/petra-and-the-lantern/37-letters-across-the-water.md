## 37. Letters Across the Water

The first letter came in the dry season's third month, when the river had shrunk to a brown ribbon and the mud banks lay cracked like old pottery in the sun.

Jude knew it was from them before he touched it. The paper was city paper — thin, white, machine-cut, nothing like the rough stock the town's stationer sold — and the handwriting on the front was Ngozi's, slanting and certain, each letter formed as though it had been rehearsed a hundred times before being committed to ink. She had written his name the way she used to write it on her slate: *Jude Oyelaran*, full and formal, as if the postman might not believe she knew anyone worth addressing.

He carried it home unopened. Some things you did not read standing in a doorway with salt on your hands.

The house on the divers' row had changed since they left. It was smaller now, or seemed so — two rooms instead of the four his father had built, because he had let the back rooms go to the Ekwueme family after their own bond papers burned in the flood, and because a house that had held five felt like a held breath when it held one. He kept his mother's kitchen swept. He kept Tamuno's diving weights racked by the door, oiled against the damp. He kept the lamp in the window lit at night, though no one was coming up the road who needed finding.

He sat at the table where the family council had once turned five lives into arithmetic, and he opened the letter with his thumb, careful of the seal.

*My son.*

His mother's hand. Slower than Ngozi's, rounder, pressed hard enough into the paper that he could feel the words on the reverse side, like braille, like something meant to be read twice — once with the eyes and once with the fingers.

*The city is louder than I imagined and kinder than I feared. These are the only two things I will say about it that are not practical, so listen well to the rest.*

*We have rooms above a seamstress named Mrs. Adeyinka, who feeds us more than we pay for and pretends not to. Ngozi has been accepted to the Grammar School on the hill — do not ask what it cost me to write those words without weeping, and do not tell her I wept anyway, because she reads over my shoulder and will be unbearable for a month. She walks there every morning past the governor's gardens, and she tells me the names of all the trees, because of course she has learned the trees' names already. Your sister collects knowledge the way your grandfather collects river stones: compulsively, and with no intention of putting any of it back.*

*I have work. Say it again to yourself slowly, because I am still saying it slowly myself: Adaeze Oyelaran has work. Mrs. Adeyinka's sister keeps a provisions store near the wharf, and they wanted someone who could count rice faster than the customers could complain. You know where I learned to count rice. You know whose kitchen taught me to make a cup of oil feed six people and taste like plenty. They pay me by the week, and on Fridays I hold the coins in my closed hand all the way home, and I do not spend one until Saturday, because I want to know what it feels like to have money overnight. It feels like sleeping next to a warm stone. It feels like something your father would have laughed at and understood.*

*The money enclosed is yours. Do not argue with me through the post, Jude, I will hear you arguing anyway, and I am telling you now: this is not charity and it is not repayment. It is inheritance flowing the other way. For seven years you carried us. Now the current runs the other direction, and you will not insult the river by refusing it.*

*Buy oil for the lamp. Buy salt. Buy whatever the boat needs. And if there is anything left, buy yourself one foolish thing — a good meal, a bolt of cloth, a hat you do not need — because I have never once known you to be foolish and I worry about what that much sense does to a young man alone.*

*Tell your grandfather I said he is stubborn, and that he is to eat the dried fish in the parcel before it spoils, and that if he gives it away to some widow I will know, because widows talk and letters travel.*

*Your mother,
Adaeze*

*P.S. Miriam Calloway came to the office last week. Not my office — the company office, where I went about the insurance on our passage. She asked after you by name. She stood very straight and very proper and asked if you were well, and I told her you were, and she said "good" in a voice that was neither straight nor proper at all. I am telling you this because I promised myself I would keep no secrets from my children, even the ones they do not want to hear.*

Jude read the letter three times. Then he folded it along its original creases, put it in the tin that had once held his father's tobacco, and sat looking out the window at the river for a long while.

The parcel, when he opened it, held dried fish wrapped in banana leaves, a bolt of indigo cloth, and a small clay pot of pepper paste made the way his mother made it, which was to say in a way no city cook could ever replicate, because the recipe lived in her wrist and not on any page.

Tamuno ate the fish that night and pronounced it adequate, which from him was an oration.

* * *

Ngozi's letter came eleven days later, and it was nothing like her mother's.

*Jude —*

*Four pages. That is how many pages I filled before deciding to start again, because four pages of grievance is too many even for you, who deserves every one of them. Here is the shortened version: I am furious with you. I have been furious with you since the day I found my name on the berth list and yours nowhere, and I expect to remain furious for approximately the rest of my natural life, so settle in.*

*But Mother says I must also tell you things, so here are the things:*

*The school is everything they said and more. There are books in the library that Mr. Bassi's stories were only shadows of — whole histories, whole sciences, men who measured the stars the way Grandfather measures the current. I have a tutor who says I have a mind for mathematics, which made me laugh until she asked why, and then I couldn't explain, because how do you explain to a city woman that the finest mathematician I ever knew counted rice?*

*That's you, in case the metaphor escaped you. It shouldn't have. You taught me figures on the warehouse floor between crates, remember? You'd chalk numbers on the boards and make me balance them before you'd tell me stories. I thought everyone learned arithmetic that way. I was twelve before I learned most children didn't.*

*Here is what I have decided, and you may as well know now because I will wear you down eventually: I am going to sit the civil examination. In three years. And I am going to pass it. And when I have a post — a real one, with a desk and a seal and the power to look inside ledgers — I am coming back down that river, and I am going to open every book the Meridian Company ever kept, including the ones you pulled out of the mud, and I am going to make sure that what happened to Father's debt happens to no one else's. You started it, Jude. I intend to finish it in ink.*

*The money in this envelope is mine. My own. From the scholarship stipend and from copying documents for the solicitor on Bridge Street, who pays by the page and whose handwriting is a crime against God. I want you to put it toward the boat. YOUR boat. The one you keep not buying because you're waiting until you can afford it outright, which at the rate you refuse help will be never.*

*You gave up your berth so I could sit in this room and write this letter. Do you understand what that means arithmetically? Because I've done the sums. Every page I copy, every sum I solve, every morning I walk past those gardens — it's all bought with your place on that barge. So take the money. Take it and build the boat and name it something sensible, and when I come home I expect to ride in it.*

*And Jude — one more thing, and then I'll stop, I promise.*

*Mother told me what you did with her savings. The coin jar. The years of it. You sent me to school with it. I cried for a night and then I stopped crying, because crying doesn't compound interest, and I made a decision: everything I earn above what I need goes home. Everything. Until the jar is full again and then some. You turned inheritance into a choice. Fine. Then watch me choose it back at you, year after year, until you drown in it.*

*Your loving sister,
Ngozi Oyelaran
(future examiner of ledgers, terror of factors)*

*P.S. Miriam writes to me. Did you know that? Short letters, terribly formal, asking about my studies. And always, buried somewhere in the middle as if she hopes I won't notice, a question about you. What you're building. Whether you still dive. Whether you've read anything lately. I answer all of it honestly except the parts about you, where I lie extravagantly, describing you as tall, mysterious, and given to brooding on cliffs. Forgive me. A sister needs her amusements.*

*P.P.S. I enclose a newspaper clipping about the bridge repairs. The article says reopening is expected within the season. I thought you'd want to see it. I also thought you'd want to think about why the article spends three paragraphs on the reopening and exactly one sentence on the flood damage to the lower quarter — and none at all on who lived there. Someone should write the other article, Jude. Maybe someone who was there when the lanterns went out.*

* * *

The letters kept coming, through the dry months, one and sometimes two a month, crossing the water in both directions now like a tide that had finally remembered its other shore.

Jude wrote back. Slowly, at first — his letters were short, practical, reports of weather and work and Tamuno's health — and then, as the habit took root, less so. He found that writing to them was like diving: you had to trust that what you carried down would still be there when you surfaced. He wrote about the diving trade he was building, the men he'd hired whom the company had discarded — old Etim with his ruined shoulder who could read water better than any surveyor; the Okon brothers, freed from their bonds by the board's disgrace, who worked the shallows with a hunger that shamed him. He wrote about Elder Bassi teaching the quarter's children under the mango tree, since the mission school had flooded and not reopened. He wrote about the records room at the old company office, where he spent evenings with Miriam copying the drowned registers into clean books before the ink of history faded entirely.

And once — only once, and late at night, and after staring at the page for an hour — he answered the part his mother hadn't asked and his sister hadn't dared.

*Mother,* he wrote, *tell Miriam I am well. Tell her the lamp in the window burns every night. Tell her I have read the book she left on the office shelf, the one about the cartographer who mapped coastlines he would never sail to, and tell her I understood why she chose it. Tell her — no. That is enough. Some messages should travel the way sound travels underwater: not spoken, but known.*

He sealed it before he could reconsider.

* * *

In the fifth month, a letter arrived that was different. Thicker. The envelope bore not Ngozi's hand but an official stamp — a government franking, blue and severe — and inside, beneath a single line from his sister (*Read this. I checked the figures myself.*), lay a printed notice:

*PUBLIC NOTICE — DEPARTMENT OF WORKS. The River Road Bridge, having passed final inspection, will reopen to all traffic effective the first week of the coming season. A ceremony will be held...*

Jude read it standing in the doorway, the way he'd received the first letter, and this time he did open it standing, because some news will not wait for a chair.

The bridge. The bridge that had creaked under Elder Bassi's dread, that had closed like a fist around the town and squeezed it down to its lantern-lit core, that had stranded him on the wrong side of his own life — reopening.

Below the official text, in Ngozi's slanting hand, added at the bottom of the clipping like a smuggled cargo:

*They'll want speeches, of course. The commissioner, the company men, everyone who ran when the water rose and everyone who profited while it fell. But you know what I keep thinking? Somewhere on that bridge, on the day it opens, there should be one person walking it end to end who stayed. Who carried nothing across it and lost nothing to it. Walk it for us, Jude. Walk it for Father. And then write and tell me what it feels like — because I have a theory it will feel like nothing at all, and that nothing is the whole point.*

Jude folded the notice and stood a long time in the doorway, the dry-season light lying gold and patient on the river, on the mudbanks softening toward the rains, on the far bank where the bridge's skeleton waited — mended, inspected, ready to carry the world back across.

Behind him, in the tin with the tobacco smell, four letters lay stacked like planks of a deck not yet built. On the table, the money from his mother and his sister sat in a clay bowl, and for the first time he allowed himself to count it — really count it, the way she had taught him, the way he had taught Ngozi — and found that it was enough.

Enough for the boat. Enough for the wood, the pitch, the sailcloth, the lantern at the bow.

Enough to begin.

He looked up the road, toward the town, where the evening lamps were being lit one by one — and paused. Because there, at the bend where the road met the water, a figure had stopped. Standing very straight. Very proper. Holding, he would swear later, a paper lantern.

Miriam had seen the notice too.
