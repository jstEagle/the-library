## 39. Master of His Own Weight

The first thing Jude Oyelaran did with his freedom was buy a rope.

Not a boat — the town had expected that, had wagered on it in the low voices of the market stalls, that the boy who'd outlasted Calloway would spend his coin on timber and sailcloth and make himself a merchant in the old mold, a small Meridian with his own name stitched above the door. But Jude walked past the boatwright's yard every morning for a week, running his palm along the hulls without stopping, until at last he turned down the river road to the chandler instead and laid his silver on the counter for forty fathoms of braided hemp and three stone weights, each the size of a fist, each smooth as an egg.

The chandler, a narrow man named Okoro who had sold cordage to the company for thirty years, wrapped the purchase in sacking and asked, not quite casually, what the rope was for.

"Diving," Jude said.

Okoro looked at him the way men look at a lamp carried into wind. "Your father's trade."

"My father's trade." Jude took the bundle onto his shoulder and felt its weight settle against his spine, familiar as a hand. "Which is to say — mine now."

He did not go back to the counting house. He never went near it again except once, in passing, to see Miriam through the window at her new desk beneath the repaired roof, her sleeves rolled, correcting the ledgers line by line in her own careful hand — writing the truth over the lies the water had already exposed, so that the record and the river would finally agree. She glanced up when his shadow crossed the glass, and neither of them waved, because they had learned by then that some recognitions are too large for gestures. He would see her at evening. That was enough to carry through the day.

* * *

The divers' quarter had emptied during the flood years like a bowl with a crack in it. Families gone upriver or down, to the capital, to the plantations, anywhere the water was something you drank rather than something you bargained with. But not everyone had gone. The ones who remained were the ones nobody else would take: Old Bassi's grandnephew, Emeka, whose left arm had been mangled in a winch accident the company had ruled *operator error* before docking his pay for the broken gear; a woman named Ifeoma with two sons and a debt she'd inherited from a brother-in-law's failed canoe venture, which Meridian had bought up cheap and held over her head for six years; a pair of brothers from the salt marshes, Tobiah and Sam, who had been dismissed — *released*, the papers said — the week the audit landed, as though distance from their names might save the company's face.

Jue found them the way his father had once found pearl beds: slowly, by patience, reading signs others missed. The smoke of a cooking fire where there should have been none. A net mended too many times. He went door to door in the quarter with his rope coiled on his shoulder like a question, and what he offered was simple enough to say standing up:

"I'm reopening my father's trade. Diving for shell, for salvage, for whatever honest work the river holds. I've no master and I'll take none. You'll be paid by the weight brought up, half to you, half to the venture, and the venture keeps the gear sound — that's my promise and my expense. No bonds. No advances against your labor. No ledger but one we all can read."

Ifeoma laughed when she heard it, a short hard sound with no joy in it. "Half. The company paid a third and called it generosity."

"The company also owned your debt while it paid you," Jude said. "I don't want your gratitude or your obedience. I want hands in the water and eyes on the accounts. If the numbers ever stop making sense to you, that's my failure, not yours."

She studied him for a long moment — this young man, barely more than a boy still, with his grandfather's jaw and his father's stillness — and then she named the one fear that mattered. "And if the river takes someone? Whose widow eats?"

"Mine does," Jude said. "Whatever I have. Whatever the venture has. It goes to the family first and the business second. My father died in that water and the company sent his family a bill. I will die in that water someday, if the river wills it, and if it happens my people will eat for a year off what I leave. That is the whole of my terms."

Tobiah, the elder of the marsh brothers, spat thoughtfully into the dust. "You talk like a man who's already done his arithmetic."

"I did nothing else for seven years," Jude said. "Arithmetic was the only thing they let me keep."

They gathered in Ifeoma's compound three evenings later, nine souls in all, sitting on benches and upturned crates around a single oil lamp, and Jude spread the account book flat where everyone could see it — a plain schoolroom ledger bought from the stationer, unruled by any company stamp — and wrote at the top of the first page, in letters slow and deliberate because his hand wanted to hurry and he made it wait:

**Oyelaran Diving Venture. Opened in the dry season after the Flood. All debts discharged. Nothing owed to anyone.**

Then he passed the pen around the circle and had each of them write their name beneath it, or make their mark if writing failed them, and when Emeka hesitated — ashamed, perhaps, of the crooked characters his ruined arm could manage — Jude said only, "It counts the same. Every mark weighs the same in this book," and the man wrote his name twice, once badly and once carefully, as though sealing it.

Old Tamuno came the night they finished, unannounced, the way he came everywhere now, moving through the quarter like a heron through shallows. He stood at the edge of the lamplight and read the names upside down, which his eyes could still do better than most men's could do right side up, and he said nothing for so long that the fire cracked twice before he spoke.

"Your father opened his account with the company the same way," he said. "First page, his name, his hope. Then they taught him that the page belonged to them."

"This page belongs to us," Jude said. "That's the difference. That's the entire difference, and it's the size of the world."

Tamuno lowered himself onto the bench beside his grandson, groaning the way old men groan when the groan is habit more than pain, and looked at the assembled faces — the maimed, the indebted, the discarded, all of them lit amber and leaning toward the book like plants toward sun. "You'll need a master diver's blessing," he said at last. "The river respects paperwork less than it respects lineage. Who trained you? Name him."

"No one," Jude said. "I apprentice under no one. I learned from a dead man and an old one, and both of them taught me free."

The old man's mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the ground a smile might grow from. "Good," he said. "Because a diver with a master is a diver with a chain, even if the chain is made of love. Your father chained himself to me gladly, and I to my own teacher, and look what the chaining built: a family that a company could hold hostage one link at a time." He tapped the ledger with one knuckle, gently, the way you tap a drum you mean to wake rather than break. "Unchained. Let it stay strange. Let the whole town be puzzled by it."

* * *

The work began in the shallows off the drowned quarter, where the flood had left a harvest of its own: mooring chains, lost cargo, the brass fittings of houses that no longer existed, and — in deeper water, where only the desperate or the skilled went — the company's records, which were no longer the company's anything, since the board inspector had signed them over to the town as evidence and the town, having made its decision, had given them back to the mud from which they came. Memory kept; the river as archive. Some things belong below.

But the living work was shell. The pearl beds his father had worked still lay off the sandbar point, thinner than in the old days — the river gives and takes in longer rhythms than men can track — but giving still. Jude dove first, always, on the opening morning, because he would not ask of anyone what he would not do himself, and because he needed, one more time, to learn whether his body remembered what his grief knew.

He stood on the gunwale of the borrowed skiff in the gray light before sunrise, the stone weight in his left hand, the rope paying out through his right, and he breathed the way his father had taught him without ever meaning to teach — the long drowning-in-air breaths, four of them, each one deeper than the last, the ribs spreading like the staves of a cask, the heartbeat slowing as though the heart itself were settling into sleep. Four breaths. His grandfather's count. On the fifth he stepped off the world.

The water took him the way it had always taken him: completely, without negotiation. Cold closed over his scalp, pressure leaned on his ears until he equalized with the little swallow-and-pinch his father's fingers had shown him when Jude was six, kneeling in the shallows, terrified, certain he would die of it. Below, the light went from silver to green to a darkness that was not empty but full — full of current, of silt turning like slow thoughts, of the shells lying in their beds with their lips open to feed, pale as a field of small moons. He filled his basket. He did not hurry. Hurrying spent air, and air was the only currency down here, and he had learned from two generations of dead and drowned men exactly how much a man could carry of it, and how little, and how precisely it must be spent.

When he broke the surface, gasping, alive, the sky had gone pink and Emeka was hauling the rope with his one good arm and grinning like a boy.

"How many?" Tobiah shouted.

Jude lifted the basket over the gunwale. Shells clattered against the boards, fat ones, deep-lipped, worth a week's rice apiece.

"That many," he said, and the skiff rang with laughter, and somewhere on the bank a heron lifted off, unhurried, indifferent, beautiful, and Jude thought: *this*. Not the pearls themselves. This — men working water they understood, paid what the work was worth, answering to no ledger but their own. His father had died for a fraction of this morning. The knowledge did not burn anymore. It had become ballast.

* * *

Fairness, Jude discovered, was harder than diving. The river had rules older than any contract, but men had only the ones they made, and men quarreled. When the season's first true haul came up — a bed of shell so rich it silenced even the marsh brothers — the division of shares nearly broke the venture in its second month. Ifeoma's sons had worked the sorting tables twelve hours a day; Emeka had rigged a new winch from salvage that doubled the daily lift; Tobiah had found the bed itself, reading the current the way his people had read it for centuries. Everyone had a claim. Everyone's claim was just. And on the third evening of argument, with voices rising and the old poison of scarcity seeping back into the air — *they'll cheat you, watch, they're all watching their own bowls* — Jude did the thing that would be told in the quarter for years afterward.

He put the season's entire proceeds on the table. Every coin, stacked in sight of everyone, and beside it the ledger open to the blank page.

"We divide it ourselves," he said. "All of us. I get a share for the gear and the risk, same as anyone gets a share for labor or finding. One vote each. And whatever we decide tonight is written here, and next season we begin from this page, not from grievance." He sat down. "My father's company taught me everything about how to steal from men who can't read. Let this be the lesson I teach instead."

They argued until midnight. They argued fiercely, with slammed palms and walked-away tempers and one thrown cap, and at the end of it they had a formula — crude, patched, theirs — that gave the finder of a new bed a bonus share, the sorter boys a fixed wage plus a dividend, the winch-man a maintenance stipend, and Jude, to his own surprise, less than he'd claimed, because when his own share came to the vote the room quietly decided the gear had already paid for itself and docked him without malice, and he accepted it, laughing, because it was the finest thing that had ever happened to his money.

Tamuno heard the story the next day and said nothing at all, which from the old man was a eulogy of praise.

* * *

By the height of the dry season the venture employed fourteen people and had bought its first proper boat — not from the boatwright, whose prices Jude still mistrusted on principle, but from a widow upriver whose husband had built her a fine thirty-foot craft and then died of fever before its second launch. Jude paid her asking price without haggling and never regretted it. They named the boat nothing at first. Naming, Tamuno insisted, was a ceremony, and ceremonies required the right season, and the right season was coming.

The work changed the quarter the way water changes stone — slowly, then suddenly. Children who had begged at the company store gate now sorted shell on Ifeoma's veranda for wages counted aloud in front of their mothers. Emeka, who had not entered the counting-house street since his accident, walked down it one afternoon with his chin level, on his way to deliver the venture's quarterly accounts to Miriam's office — because Jude, in the venture's founding spirit, had hired the new company office itself to audit his books, paying the daughter of the man who had once owned him to check the arithmetic of the man who refused to be owned. When Miriam had first heard the proposal she'd laughed until she had to sit down. "Your father would have fainted."

"My father never met my books," Jude said.

She audited them scrupulously, found two errors — both honest, both corrected publicly — and returned the ledger with a note in the margin that Jude kept folded in his breast pocket long after the ink had faded from relevance: *Cleaner than anything Meridian ever showed the board. Whatever else you inherited, you didn't inherit this.*

* * *

On the eve of the flood season's first high water, the whole venture gathered on the point to bless the boats — the unnamed thirty-footer and the two skiffs — with rum poured on the water and prayers muttered in three languages, because the quarter had never agreed on a god and had long ago decided the river didn't either. Tamuno presided, ancient and upright, and when the libations were done he turned to Jude before everyone, holding out the object he had carried wrapped in cloth all evening: a stone diving weight, worn smooth, strung on a leather thong gone black with age.

Jude knew it before it was unwrapped. He had pressed it into his mother's hands on the last barge, his inheritance surrendered so she would carry something of her husband across the water. And she — he understood now, with a fullness that made his throat close — had sent it back with Tamuno's letter months ago, because she had known, mothers always know, that a weight belongs with the man who dives, and that love is not diminished by returning what was only ever loaned.

"She says," Tamuno translated, though the words needed no translating, "'A man cannot master his own weight if he leaves it in someone else's house.'"

Jude took the stone in both hands. It was warm from the old man's pocket. Forty fathoms of rope, three fist-sized anchors, and this — his father's actual lead, the very mass that had pulled him down through the green silence toward the moons of the shell beds, the mass that had hung from his belt the day the river decided to keep him. Jude closed his fingers around it and felt the whole of his inheritance settle into his palm: not the debts, not the bond, not the ledgers of other men's claims, but this single smooth weight, which was only ever heavy if you carried it alone.

"Name the boat," Tamuno said. "The season turns tomorrow. The river is listening."

Jude looked at the water — brown-gold in the evening light, carrying the last of the sun down toward the sea, patient, terrible, generous — and the name rose in him the way his breath rose on the fourth of those long pre-dive inhalations, inevitable, earned.

"Petra," he said. "Stone that holds."

He spoke it to the boats, to the gathering, to the river, and the word went out over the water the way lantern-light goes out over water, and the people repeated it until it seemed the current itself had learned it.

That night he walked home along the bank, the weight on its thong against his chest, and saw far downstream a single paper lantern drifting on the dark water — someone's prayer, someone's grief, someone's release — burning its small stubborn gold against all that blackness, going wherever the river chose, and Jude stood a long time watching it go, thinking about what he would hang from Petra's bow when the floods came, and about who would stand beside him on the deck when he lit it.

Footsteps on the path behind him. A lantern raised, and a voice he would have known in the dark of any year of his life.

"You're late," Miriam said. "The counting house closes at sundown. Even yours."

"It's not mine," Jude said, smiling into her light. "That's the whole point."

"Come and tell me why that matters," she said. "I've been writing letters all evening — to your mother, to Ngozi — and there's news from the capital, and I think—" she paused, and the lantern trembled just slightly, the way a flame trembles when the hand beneath it does, "—I think you'll want to hear it before the season turns."

They walked home together along the darkening river, two lights where there had been one, while behind them the water carried the year away.
