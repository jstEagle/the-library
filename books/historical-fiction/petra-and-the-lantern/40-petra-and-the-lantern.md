## 40. Petra and the Lantern

The boat was finished on a Tuesday in the dry season, when the river ran low and green as bottle glass, and the whole of the lower quarter came down to see her slide into the water.

Jude had built her over three years — longer than anyone thought reasonable, longer than he had first intended himself. He had cut her timbers from the flooded groves upriver, wood that had drowned and been raised again, seasoned by water and sun until it was harder than anything that had never known drowning. He had caulked her seams with pitch and palm fiber in the old way, the way his father had described it once, half-drunk on festival palm wine, gesturing at the harbor with a lantern in his hand: *A boat is not made to keep the water out, boy. She is made to agree with it.* He had been eleven years old and had not understood. He understood now.

Elder Bassi named her, in the way of elders, by asking the question everyone else was too polite to ask.

"What will you call her?"

Jude had known for months. He had carved the name himself, letter by letter, into the transom board, sanding each stroke until the grain rose around it like flesh around a scar. But he let the old man wait, because Bassi had earned the waiting, and because some things must be said aloud to become true.

"Petra," Jude said.

Bassi turned the word over. "Stone."

"Stone that holds." Jude set his palm flat against the hull, still smelling of pitch and sawdust. "My mother used to say it when I was small. When the floods took things — the year they took my father's second boat, the year they took our roof — she would stand in the doorway and say, *We are Petra. Stone holds.* I didn't believe her then. I thought stone just sat there while everything washed past it." He looked out at the river, at the brown-green patience of it. "But that's what holding is. You don't fight the flood. You stay."

Bassi nodded slowly, the way he did when a thing pleased him and he wished it noted that he had expected nothing less. "Your grandfather would have approved. Tamuno always said the river doesn't take from those who know how to be heavy."

Tamuno had been dead four years now. He had gone the way he wanted, in the dry season, in his sleep, in the house by the water with the door open to the river sound. They had buried him above the flood line, on the rise where the ground went red and firm, and Adaeze had put his diving weight in the grave with him — no, Jude corrected himself, standing there at the rail of his finished boat, she had put *a* diving weight. Her own. The one she had pressed into his hands on the last barge, the one that had crossed the water and come back across it again, twice now, between her city and this town. His grandfather slept with stone. His mother had given hers away and never asked for its return, and when Jude tried, the year after the funeral, to bring it back to her, she had laughed at him — actually laughed, that low fierce laugh that had frightened company clerks and comforted her children through every lean season — and said, "It lives where I say it lives. It lives with you. That is what inheritance is, Jude. Not property. Direction."

So the weight hung now from a cord in the bow of the Petra, polished smooth by years of his father's grip and his own, swinging gently above the waterline like a dark heart.

* * *

The first flood season after the launching, Jude hung the lantern.

It was Miriam's idea, though she would deny the sentimentality of it if pressed, in the dry precise way she had learned from running the company office — her office now, the Meridian flag replaced by the town's own mark, the ledgers kept in a hand so clean that auditors from the capital wrote letters of commendation she pinned to the wall upside down out of spite. Every flood season, she said, the boats that worked the crossing should carry lights at their bows. Not for navigation — the channel markers handled that, since the council had finally funded them with the recovered company monies. For memory.

"For memory," Jude repeated, when she told him.

"Don't make it strange," she said. "It's practical. A light says someone is home. A light says the crossing is watched." She paused, and he saw her decide to say the rest anyway, which was one of the ten thousand small things he loved about her — that she always decided, always chose the harder true sentence over the easier silence. "And it says nobody is hidden anymore. That's all. Nobody working this river in the dark without a name."

So each year, when the rains gathered in the hills and the river began its slow brown rising, Jude climbed to the bow of the Petra and hung a paper lantern from the iron hook he'd forged for the purpose. The first one was red-gold, bought from the same market stall — rebuilt twice, burned once, owned now by the grandson of the woman who had sold Miriam that first joke of a lantern a lifetime ago. The boy who ran the stall always gave Jude the best one in the stock, unasked, and always pretended he hadn't.

The lanterns burned through the flood nights. Rain got into them sometimes, and they guttered and healed themselves, and once — the year of the great wind — one caught fire entirely and Jude stood in the downpour watching it go, laughing, because Miriam appeared beside him in the dark and said, "Well. We've done burning before," and handed him another one from under her oilcloth, because of course she had brought a spare. She always brought spares. She had been bringing spares for other people's disasters since she was a girl buying paper lanterns for boys she wasn't supposed to talk to.

* * *

The letters came with the dry-season boats, two or three at a time, tied in oilcloth against the spray.

Ngozi wrote long ones, dense ones, her handwriting slanting forward like a woman in a hurry to arrive somewhere. She had finished school in the capital — first in her cohort, though she mentioned it only in passing, the way she mentioned passing everything, as if excellence were merely the toll paid on the road to the actual business of her life. She was reading law now. In her latest letter she described arguing a case before a magistrate — a debt-bond case, an indentured dockworker defrauded by a shipping concern — and Jude read her account three times at his kitchen table, hearing her voice, hearing the fury banked and disciplined into argument, the coals she had learned to carry instead of swallow.

"The magistrate asked me," she wrote, "where I had learned that a ledger could lie. I told him: at my family's table, before I could write my own name. He laughed. I did not. We won."

She sent money home. That was the miracle none of them ever quite stopped marveling at — money flowing upriver instead of down, coin arriving in Adaeze's hands rather than leaving them. Adaeze had refused it for two full years, returning every remittance with a note in her careful clerk's hand: *Not needed. Save it for your books.* Ngozi had simply begun sending it addressed to Elder Bassi, who deposited it in the town fund and informed Adaeze, with magnificent elderly innocence, that he had no authority over what the town fund chose to do. Adaeze had called him a scheming old crocodile. Bassi had bowed. The money stayed.

Adaeze herself lived half the year in the capital now, with Ngozi, and half here, in the house above the flood line, where she kept a garden that produced vegetables in quantities that defied both soil and reason. She had taken up, in her sixties, the teaching of children — reading, arithmetic, and a third subject she invented herself and called *weighing*, in which she taught girls and boys how to judge the true worth of a thing, a bargain, a promise, a person. The company office sent her its hardest cases: contracts the illiterate had signed, bonds written in language designed to blur. Miriam would bring them over in the evenings, and the two women — the factor's daughter and the diver's widow, an alliance no one in the old days could have imagined — would sit at the table with the papers spread between them like patients awaiting surgery, and by lamplight they would take the lies apart piece by piece.

Jude came home late from the water one such evening and found them still at it, and stood in the doorway watching, and thought: *this*. This was what his father had died without knowing existed. This was what the river had been carrying toward them all along, under all that brown water, all those years.

* * *

The diving trade was his now, wholly, and it was nothing like his father's.

Oyelaran Diving employed nine people. Three were divers, trained in the breath Tamuno had taught Jude in the flooded shallows the season the bridge closed — the long exhale, the surrender, the body agreeing with the water the way the Petra agreed with it. Two were boatmen. One kept the gear. One was a girl of sixteen named Sefu whom the company had discarded at fourteen with a damaged ear and a bond it claimed she owed; Jude had hired her before he'd hired anyone else, and she had turned out to have the best eye for salvage on the whole river. The last three were record-keepers, because half the work, these days, was not pearl or lost cargo but *proof* — recovering documents from wrecks, from flooded warehouses, from the silt of the old counting house cellar where so much had gone down with the great flood and come back up changed.

That was the work the town remembered him for, though he never spoke of it easily: the season the flood peaked and the lower quarter drowned, and Jude had gone down into the black water with his grandfather's breath in his lungs and come up with armfuls of the company's own registers — decades of rigged bonds, held berths, extended debts, names entered and erased at the factor's pleasure. Calloway's empire of small corruptions, drowned in its own cellar, exhumed by the son of a man it had drowned in everything but fact.

Calloway himself had left on a barge, in the end. Not arrested — the board had preferred quiet removal to public trial, scandal being a thing even corrupt men understood the arithmetic of. Jude had watched the barge go from the bridge, and had felt — he admitted this only to Miriam, once, late, in the dark — not triumph but something stranger. Pity, almost. Because Calloway had spent thirty years measuring everything, and had never once measured himself, and now he was being carried downriver in a vessel exactly like the ones he had rationed, with exactly as much room as he had ever granted anyone else.

"He'll land somewhere," Miriam had said. "Men like him always land somewhere. And he'll build something small and mean and call it an empire again."

"And we'll be here," Jude said.

"Yes," she said. "That's the whole point of us. We're here."

* * *

They married in the fifth year, quietly, on the river itself, standing in the Petra with Elder Bassi officiating and the whole town lining the banks pretending they hadn't come to watch. Adaeze wept and denied it. Ngozi, home from the capital, gave a speech at the feast that began as a toast and became, by degrees, a legal argument proving that love constituted a binding contract enforceable in perpetuity, and had the entire quarter laughing until they couldn't breathe. Sefu hung six lanterns off the rigging without being asked.

Miriam wore white and carried no flowers. She carried, instead, a book — the one they had talked about over the counting-house lamp all those years ago, the one about the far country neither of them had ever seen, the one that had been contraband between them, passed like a secret. She had bought a new copy with her own wages, the first wages she had ever earned honestly, and she pressed it into Jude's hands at the moment of vows and said, "There. Now you owe me nothing and I owe you nothing. Let's see what we do with a clean ledger."

What they did with it, year upon year, was ordinary. That was the astonishment of it. Boats repaired, accounts kept, storms weathered, arguments had and finished — finished, both of them having sworn separately and privately never to leave a thing unsaid overnight, having both grown up in houses where silence did the accounting. Children came, in time: a daughter first, loud and fearless, who was swimming before she could properly walk and whom Tamuno would have called a natural and spoiled rotten; then a son, quieter, who liked to sit in the bow of the Petra and watch the lantern burn and ask questions his father answered carefully, because questions from that child were never casual.

It was the son, in the most recent flood season, who asked the question that undid Jude a little, standing at the rail with the red-gold light swaying above the black water.

"Papa. Why do we hang it? Really."

Jude considered giving the practical answer. Someone is home. The crossing is watched. But the boy was looking at him with Adaeze's eyes, and Adaeze's eyes had never once accepted the practical answer when the true one was available.

"Because once," Jude said, "a man could work this river all his life and never have a light with his name on it. The company kept the books, and the books kept us, and the water kept whatever the books threw away. My father was one of those the water kept." He touched the diving weight hanging at the bow, cool and smooth as bone. "This lantern isn't for seeing by. It's so the river knows: whoever passes under this light is counted. Is named. Is owed nothing and owed everything."

The boy thought about that with his whole face, the way he did. Then he said, "Did Grandfather Tamuno see it? The lantern?"

"No," Jude said. "He saw the flood. He taught me how to breathe in it." He lifted his son onto his shoulders, the boy's heels drumming the deck, the lantern light gilding them both. "You're seeing what comes after. That's your inheritance. Not the weight. The light."

* * *

Years later — more than he liked to count, fewer than the river had given him reason to fear — Jude stood on the reopened bridge at the end of a dry season, carrying nothing.

He walked it end to end, as he did every year, out of a habit that had ripened into rite. The planks rang under his feet, mended and remended, some of them cut from the same flooded groves as the Petra's ribs. Below, the town spread out along the banks: the market rebuilt and doubled, the company office with its honest flag, the school where Adaeze taught weighing to a classroom of children who would never know a rigged berth list except as history, as story, as the thing old Oyelaran had ended. On the water, his boats moved — three of them now, the Petra and her younger sisters — and at the bow of each, as the first rains fell soft on the far hills, a paper lantern woke and swung.

From the bridge he could see them all at once: three small flames going about their evening work on the broad brown water, and beyond them the lamps of the quarter kindling window by window, and beyond those the road east where the letters traveled, where Ngozi argued law and Adaeze grew impossible tomatoes and sent back seeds and instructions and, once a year, a single line in her clerk's hand that never varied:

*We are Petra. Stone holds.*

Jude stood at the center of the bridge, midway between the two banks, midway between the life that had been taken from him and the life he had built in its place, and he felt the weight of nothing at all. Not the weight of the bond, long discharged. Not the weight of the forged page, confessed to Miriam in their first year and forgiven before he finished the sentence. Not even the sweet grief of his dead — his father in the water, his grandfather on the hill, all the names the old ledgers had tried to erase and the new ones had written back in ink that would not run.

Only lightness. Only the river going by beneath him, patient and enormous, carrying on its dark surface — instead of debts, instead of barges loaded with the rationed lives of people it had been taught to consider cargo — a handful of small flames, moving steadily downstream, lighting the way for whoever came next.

He watched them go until the bend took them. Then he turned, and walked home along the bank in the warm rain, unhurried, weighed by nothing, carrying everything that mattered — which was to say, nothing that could be carried at all.
