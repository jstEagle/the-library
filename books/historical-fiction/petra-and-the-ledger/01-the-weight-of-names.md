## 1. The Weight of Names

The water came in at dusk.

That was how the nurses at Vellmere measured their days — not by the bell the orderlies struck in the yard, not by the changing of the guard on the bridge road, but by the slow black creep of the river through the floorboards of what had once been a wool merchant's warehouse and was now, by military decree, Ward Six. The water rose an inch, maybe two, every evening when the wind turned north off the ice. By morning it had withdrawn again, leaving a slick of silt across the stones and a smell like drowned rope. The men whose cots stood nearest the walls had learned to lift their boots onto their bedframes before they slept. The ones too far gone to notice didn't complain about wet feet anymore, which was its own kind of arithmetic, and Jude Dunmore had stopped doing that kind of arithmetic out loud.

She sat now at the end of the third row, where a trestle table had been dragged from somewhere and propped on bricks to keep it above the tide line. On the table: a candle guttering in a jam jar, a bottle of ink stoppered with a rag, and a ledger bound in grey cloth that she kept wrapped in oilskin when it wasn't open, because paper and floodwater are old enemies and only one of them can be replaced.

The ward around her was quieting. The evening dose of morphine had gone round; the moaning had thinned to breathing; somewhere near the stove a man with a bandaged jaw was humming something that might have been a hymn or might have been a marching song, and nobody asked him which, because at Vellmere the two had worn down to nearly the same thing.

Sister Aldous passed behind her without stopping, but her hand brushed Jude's shoulder as she went — the lightest touch, a permission and a warning in one gesture. *Do your work. Don't be seen doing it.* That was the whole catechism of the place, though no one had ever said it aloud in so many words.

Jude dipped the pen.

*Private Alfred Coen,* she wrote. *2nd Rifles. Left leg taken at the millrace crossing. Admitted 14 December. Alive as of this hour.*

None of that would appear anywhere else. She knew because she'd checked. She had gone to the adjutant's office three days after Coen was carried in — mud to the waist, screaming about his mother, then not screaming, then quiet in the terrible way the quiet ones were quiet — and she had read the casualty list pinned to the board with brass tacks, running her finger down the columns the way you'd run a finger along a shelf looking for one particular book. Coen wasn't there. The column for the millrace action listed eleven wounded. She had counted twelve cots herself, twice, on two different evenings, because she did not trust her grief to count honestly and wanted the number confirmed by someone colder than she was.

Twelve cots. Eleven names. One man subtracted between the ward and the report, the way you'd subtract a figure that made the sum come out wrong.

She had learned since then not to wonder too hard about *why*. There were reasons, and the reasons had a shape she could trace with her thumb if she let herself: a company that couldn't afford to look as bled-white as it was; an advance planned for spring that required the ledgers back home to show full ranks; officers who had learned, somewhere up the chain, that bad news traveled upward faster than good news and landed harder. She had heard Major Halloway himself say once, in the doorway of the adjutant's office, in that mild voice he used for orders that were actually verdicts: "A report is not a photograph, Miss Dunmore. It is an instrument. Instruments get calibrated."

Calibrated. As if the missing men were a few degrees off true.

She wrote until the candle needed trimming. Coen. Then a boy called Petrie whose fever chart told her he would not see January, and whose name appeared nowhere because he'd been picked up at a crossroads by a passing ambulance and entered into no muster book at all — a soldier so far fallen through the paperwork that officially he had never existed, and would therefore never be mourned by anyone waiting at a window for him. Then two more from the millrace list whom Sister Aldous had murmured to her over the dispensary basin, names passed mouth to ear like smuggled goods.

Four names. It took perhaps ten minutes. Some nights it took an hour; some nights, when a convoy came down from the front with forty stretchers and the officers' pens moved fast and struck faster, it took until dawn, and her wrist ached for two days afterward, and she thought: *this is what my life is now. I am a clerk of the erased.*

She had not always been this person. Eleven months ago she had been a woman who kept accounts for a grain merchant in Vellmere and was considered slightly odd for reading novels and walking alone along the levee. Then the war had come down the river like weather, and the warehouse had become a ward, and the clerks' office had become a dispensary, and she had discovered that a woman who is good with figures and neat with a pen is worth ten soldiers in a place where the truth has begun to leak out of the official record like the river leaking through the floor.

And then there was Thomas.

She closed the ledger on that thought the way you close a door on a cold room. Not tonight. Tonight there was ink still on her fingers and a walk to make through the dark, and grief kept for the right hour keeps better, like anything else stored properly.

* * *

The workshop stood four streets back from the river, in a lane where the houses leaned together like conspirators. You reached it through a print-shop front that hadn't printed anything for sale since the war began — the shutters painted over, the window glass fogged with the breath of the press inside — and then down a stair that smelled of lamp oil and hot metal, into a room where a single iron hand-press dominated the space the way an altar dominates a church.

Elias Voss was working when she knocked the twice-slow, once-fast signal on the door. He was always working. He worked the way other men breathed — continuously, without deciding to, pausing only when the body insisted.

He looked up when she came in, and the lamplight caught the ink on his hands, permanent as stain, ground so deep into the creases of his knuckles that she suspected it would still be there at his funeral. He was not a large man. He had a printer's stoop already at thirty-one, shoulders drawn forward around some invisible case of type he carried everywhere, and eyes that were quick and tired and, when they settled on her, briefly, unmistakably glad — before he got them under control again.

"You're late," he said. "I assumed the river had you."

"The river will have to queue." She unwound her scarf, shaking sleet off it. "It's rising again. Third night running."

"I know. It came up through my floor an hour ago." He tipped his chin toward the corner, where the type cases stood stacked on pallets, raised off the stones. "If it takes the fourteen-point, I'm emigrating."

She almost smiled. With Elias the jokes were thin and dry and arrived sideways, and you had to catch them quickly or they were gone. She set her satchel on the workbench — the good bench, cleared of everything but a lamp and a stone for truing type — and drew out the ledger, and watched his face do the thing it always did when the grey cover came into view: a small tightening, a settling of seriousness, as though the object weighed more than any book should.

"Four tonight," she said. "Coen, Petrie, and two from the millrace. Harrow and Lunt."

"Harrow." He said the name as if tasting it. "That's the one the sergeant-major argued about. I heard him through the wall of the pay office — arguing that the man died of his wounds, which is different from killed in action, which means it goes in a different column, which means—" He stopped himself. His hands, which never entirely stopped moving, went still. "Different column. Different widow. Same grave."

"Same grave," Jude agreed quietly.

They worked. This was the ritual now, three nights a week when the wards allowed it and more often when they didn't: she read aloud from the ledger while he set the names in metal, letter by letter, each name becoming a physical object, a line of lead that could be locked into a chase and inked and pressed onto paper until the paper held it the way nothing official ever would. A name in an officer's report could be struck through with one stroke of a pen. A name in type was a different creature. You could strike it out all you liked; somewhere in the world there remained a line of lead with the man's name on it, heavy, unerasable, waiting.

That was the whole theology of it, though neither of them called it theology. Elias had once said, wiping ink from a forme: "The army believes things stop existing when you stop writing them down. I make my living knowing the opposite."

She read; he set. *Alfred Coen.* The letters clicked into his composing stick with a sound like small doors closing. Outside, the sleet had thickened to snow, and the river could be heard even here, four streets back — a long groan and shift, the ice talking to itself in the dark.

"How many now?" he asked, not looking up.

"In the ledger? Two hundred and six."

"And in the copies?"

"Ninety-one pages pulled. Enough for—" She calculated, the merchant's habit asserting itself. "Three sets, if we keep them slim. Four if the last section runs short."

He nodded slowly, setting *Harrow*, setting *Lunt*. "Two hundred and six," he repeated. "There'll be a town somewhere that lost two hundred and six men and thinks it lost a hundred and eighty. Do you ever think about that? Somewhere a mother has already stopped setting a plate at the table, and she did it for nothing, because the man is alive in a ward sixty miles away eating boiled mutton and complaining about the nurse."

"That's the part that keeps me going," Jude said. "Not the dead ones. The alive ones nobody counted."

He glanced up at her then, and for a moment neither of them worked, and the press ticked faintly as the iron cooled, and the snow came down past the high dark window like something trying to bury the whole street gently, kindly, before anyone could object.

"You could stop," he said. It was not the first time he'd said it, and they both knew it wasn't really a suggestion. It was more like checking a knot. "You could put the ledger in a crate tomorrow and be a grain merchant's clerk again by spring. Nobody would blame—"

"Don't." She said it mildly. She was careful always to say it mildly, because she had discovered that the alternative — the truth underneath, which was that she would rather be burned in the requisitioned workshop than live in a world where Alfred Coen existed nowhere except a cot in a flooded ward — frightened people, and frightened people talked.

He didn't push. He never pushed. He bent back over the stick and set the next line, and after a while he said, in a different tone, quieter:

"There's talk at the depot. Paper requisitions coming through with new seals on them. Quarter reorganization, effective at thaw."

Jude's pen stopped above the page.

"What kind of reorganization?"

"The kind where buildings change hands and inventories get burned." He still wasn't looking at her. His thumb moved along the edge of the composing stick, back and forth, a printer's tic, worrying the metal like a worry-stone. "Nothing certain. Just — the seals are new. New seals mean new orders. New orders mean somebody upstream has decided this quarter needs tidying."

The word *burned* hung in the warm air of the workshop among the smells of ink and oil, and Jude found that her body had gone very still, the way it went still in the ward when a fever spiked — the stillness of a person recalculating everything.

"They wouldn't burn records," she said. "Records have value. Even unofficial ones. Even—"

"Unofficial ones especially." Now he looked at her, and his tired quick eyes held hers without apology. "You of all people know what an unofficial record is worth, Jude. You know it because you've spent eleven months building one. So does whoever signed those requisitions. Worth keeping, or worth destroying. Nothing in between."

The river groaned again outside, longer this time, a sound like a great door opening somewhere beneath the world.

Jude looked down at the ledger, at today's four names drying in their neat columns — *Coen, Petrie, Harrow, Lunt* — men who existed now in ink and in lead and in her own stubborn memory, and nowhere else. Two hundred and six. And somewhere upstream, a new seal on a new form, and a season ending, and ice that would break in March whether anyone was ready or not.

"We'll need more paper," she said.

Elias Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned back to the case, and began pulling letters.

"Yes," he said. "We will."
