## 2. Orders Come Down

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, nailed to the door of the provisioner's shop with four other notices, and it was the smallest of the five.

Jude saw it on her way back from the hospital, boots crusted with the gray slush that Vellmere called snow once it had been walked on enough. She had gone in for lint and camphor and come out with neither, because the provisioner had neither, and she was reading the notices out of habit — habit being the only form of vigilance left in a town that had already given the war everything it could think to take.

The first notice was about coal rationing. The second was about a curfew she already ignored. The third concerned the registration of all foreign nationals, of whom Vellmere now had four, three of them dead. The fourth was a reward for information regarding deserters, printed in a typeface she knew.

She knew it because she had spent a hundred nights watching that typeface being set, letter by letter, by a man whose hands moved like a pianist's and whose name did not appear on anything he printed.

The fifth notice was small. It was addressed *To the Occupier or Keeper of the Premises Known as the Old Rope-Walk, Riverfront*. It announced that the said premises, together with all contents, fixtures, and records, were requisitioned for military use effective at spring thaw, such date to be determined by the District Provost. All unofficial records maintained therein were to be surrendered for destruction. *Unofficial* was underlined, though whether by the clerk who composed it or by some other reader with a pencil and an opinion, Jude could not tell.

She stood in the street and read it three times.

The river behind her made its low winter sound, ice shifting against ice, the groan of a thing too large to be afraid and too heavy to be careless. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded and got no answer.

She thought, with a strange clarity, of the ledger. It was at that moment lying in the workshop, in the third drawer of the type cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, four hundred and six names long. Four hundred and six men who had been wounded at Vellmere or passed through Vellmere wounded, and whose names had been struck from the official rolls — struck by clerks, by majors, by the quiet arithmetic of armies that could not afford to count everything they broke.

Four hundred and six names, and the army intended to burn the building they lived in.

Jude folded the notice along its existing creases and put it in her coat pocket, where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and she walked the rest of the way to the rope-walk without once looking at the river, because she had learned that winter that you could not look at a thing that large and think clearly at the same time.

* * *

The workshop smelled the way it always smelled: ink and lamp oil and the cold-iron smell of type metal, with the hospital's carbolic clinging to her own sleeves underneath it. Elias was at the press when she came in, though *at the press* meant something different every day. Today it meant he had the near side panel off and was elbow-deep in the machine's chest, a wrench in one fist and a candle stub burning on the frame above him, dripping wax onto the iron with the serenity of a man who had never once had to scrub wax out of anything.

"You're back early," he said, without turning. "No lint?"

"No lint."

"No camphor?"

"No camphor. There's no anything. There's a notice."

He turned then. He was a narrow man, Elias, with ink in the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing would ever fully lift, and when he was worried he went still in a way most people mistook for calm. She had learned to read it. She set the notice on the press bed, weighting one corner with a quooin, and let him read it by candlelight.

She watched his eyes go down the paragraph. She watched him reach the underlined word.

"Unofficial records," he said.

"Yes."

"Destruction." He said the word the way he said most words, evenly, but she saw his thumb move against the wrench handle, once, a small unconscious motion, the way a man touches a thing he is measuring.

"At thaw," Jude said. "Whenever the provost decides thaw is. Which could be six weeks. Could be four."

Elias set the wrench down on the press bed with a click, very precisely, the way he did everything, and looked at the notice for a long moment as though it might contain a second paragraph if he stared hard enough. It did not. It was, Jude thought, a very complete little document. Armies were good at completeness. It was nearly the only thing they were good at.

"Four hundred and six," Elias said.

"Four hundred and nine. I added three last night. The two from the barge, and the boy from the artillery column who died on Tuesday with no one writing anything down at all."

"Four hundred and nine names," Elias said, "on paper that is legally, by this notice, contraband." He tapped the requisition order once. "And the paper lives in a building that is now, as of this paper, army property. We are squatting in the future site of our own evidence."

"We're not squatting. You have a lease. You had a lease."

"I had a lease with a printer who fled to his wife's people in the south and left me his press and his debts and his name on the paperwork." Elias's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "As far as the army is concerned, the rope-walk is kept by a man who does not exist, working a press that is registered to a man who cannot be found. This notice is the most official document I have been connected to in two years, and it is ordering me destroyed."

Jude sat down on the type cabinet, the one with the third drawer, and felt the drawer's weight against the back of her thighs, and was surprised to find that her fear had a shape now, a location. For weeks the fear had been ambient, like damp — everywhere, in everything, belonging to no particular wall. Now it had an address. Spring thaw. A date to be determined by a man with a stamp.

She found that she preferred it this way. Fear with an address could be planned around.

"We need a second copy," she said.

Elias looked at her. In the candlelight his eyes were the color of the river in March, which is to say unreadable, which is to say the color of a thing thinking.

"You know what you're asking."

"I'm asking what I came here to ask. I've been asking it since November in my head and I'm asking it out loud now because the army has put a date on it." She heard her own voice go flat and quick, the voice she used at triage, the voice that had learned that panic was a luxury billed later at interest. "One copy is a candle. One breath and it's gone. Two copies is a fire you have to work at. The press can do it. I've seen what the press can do."

"The press can do it," Elias agreed. "The press can do anything except be moved, fed, or hidden. Do you know what type metal costs? Do you know what paper costs? Do you know what it costs when the paper has to be paper that no supply clerk ever missed, because every sheet that can be traced is a sheet that can be read?"

"I know what the names cost," Jude said.

That stopped him. It was not a clever thing to say; it was not clever at all. It was simply the true thing, and she had learned from him, of all people, that the true thing set in plain type outlasted every clever thing in the world.

Elias turned back to the press. He put his hand flat on the iron frame, the way a man touches a horse's neck, and for a while the only sound was the river and the candle burning and, faintly, from the town above the bank, a bugle sounding some order or other that would ruin someone's evening.

"There's a crate of type in the back," he said at last. "The old foundry sort. Double-walled. I've been meaning to melt it down for a year and never had the heart — it's good type, it's just the wrong face, nobody's ordered a broadside in that face since the old king died." He was talking with his hands now, the worry going out through them. "Double-walled, and the lid seats, and there's a false bottom if I make one, and I can make one. Paper in oilcloth, wrapped flat, under a layer of type. Nobody requisitioning a crate of dead type is going to weigh it. Nobody is going to open a crate of type at all, if I'm honest. They'll burn the whole building and the crate with it, which is the problem, which is the entire problem, Jude — hiding it in the building only works if the building survives, and the notice says—"

"The notice says they burn the records," Jude said. "The building gets seized. The records get destroyed. Which means the ledger has to be somewhere else by thaw, or it has to be *copyable*, so that it can be in two places, three places, so many places that destruction stops meaning anything."

Elias was quiet.

"You've been planning this since before the notice," he said.

"I've been planning it since Thomas," Jude said, and did not say anything else, and he did not ask, because he was one of perhaps two people in the world who understood what *since Thomas* meant without requiring the sentence finished.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon by candlelight because the army had taken the glass from the river-facing windows in the autumn and the light that came through the boarded frames was thin and gray and went early. Elias pulled the crate from the back room — it took both of them, it was that kind of crate, built in the era when crates were built to outlive their contents — and set it on the floor by the stove, and then he sat back on his heels and looked at it, and Jude watched him do a thing she had seen him do at the press a hundred times: measure the job before touching it.

"False bottom," he said. "I'll need the saw, the glue, and about an hour of quiet, and I'll need you to not watch me do it, because I do bad work when I'm watched."

"Where should I be?"

"At the cabinet. Setting the first page." He looked up at her. "If we're doing this, we do it in order. You transcribe clean. I'll cast what we're missing — we're short on some letters, we've been short on E and T since November, God help us, the two commonest letters in the language and in the casualty lists both. I'll melt down something from the old face to fill the gaps. And you'll set the first page tonight, and we pull one proof, and we see what the ledger looks like when it isn't handwriting."

"Handwriting is evidence," Jude said. "It's *my* handwriting."

"Handwriting is a signature," Elias said. "Type is a fact. Anyone can write a name in a book. When it comes off a press, it's been *set* — someone chose each letter, someone locked it in a form, someone pulled it. There's a word for that." He almost smiled. "There's a word for it and I've spent my whole life inside the word, and the word is *composed*."

Jude went to the cabinet and opened the third drawer and took out the ledger, and the weight of it in her hands was the weight it always was, which was more than paper, which was four hundred and nine men plus one, plus Thomas at the front of it where he had always been, the first name, the reason, the debt the whole book existed to service.

She set it on the composing stone and opened it to the first page and looked at her brother's name in her own hand, and behind her she heard Elias begin to measure the crate with his saw, the first long stroke of it, a sound like a held breath being let out slowly.

* * *

The notice came down off the provisioner's door by evening. She knew because a boy told her — one of the provisioner's boys, sent running through the slush with a verbal message because paper was dear: the provost's clerk had taken the notice back in, all of them, there'd been an error, the dates were to be reissued.

"An error," Elias repeated, when Jude told him. He was wiping glue from his hands with a rag that had been a shirt in a former life. "The army made an error."

"The clerk made an error. The dates are to be reissued."

"Reissued how? Earlier or later?"

"He didn't say. He's twelve."

Elias looked at the crate, with its new false bottom seated and drying, and at the ledger open on the stone, and at the press standing in the dark with its panel still off and its chest open like a patient on a table, and Jude watched him do the arithmetic that she had already done, that she suspected every person in Vellmere did every day now without knowing they were doing it: *how long, how much time is there, how long do we have.*

"Then we assume sooner," he said. "We assume the error was in our favor by accident and will be corrected on purpose. We set type every night. We pull proofs every morning. And when the new notice goes up—" he tapped the crate with his boot, gently, the way you'd tap a coffin you weren't ready to agree with, "—this leaves this building before we do."

Jude turned back to the composing stone. She picked up the first letter of the first name — a T, one of the short ones, one of the ones they were running out of — and set it in the stick, and the small iron weight of it in her fingers was steadying, the way the weight of a scalpel was steadying, the way anything was steadying when you knew exactly what it was for.

Outside, the river groaned, long and low, ice talking to ice in the dark, and she set the second letter, and the third, and began.
