## 3. The Major's Offer

Major Halloway had a habit of counting things, and he did it now without meaning to. The windowpanes in the requisition office: four, one cracked. The chairs: two, both too small for him, which was the army's way of telling him nothing here had been built for comfort or for staying. The names on today's casualty list, the official one, the one that would go south in the morning pouch: forty-one.

He knew there should have been fifty-three.

He knew because he had written the number in his own notebook — a slim black thing, oilcloth-covered, that lived in his breast pocket where a man might carry letters from home — and because he had spent eleven years learning to count what the reports left out. Twelve men, then, struck from the rolls of the 2nd Field Hospital at Vellmere. Twelve men who had died on stretchers and pallets and, in two cases, on the wet stones of the corridor itself, and whose deaths would now be a rumor their families would carry like a stone in the shoe for the rest of their lives. *They said he died. They said they never found him. They said the report didn't have his name.*

Halloway knew exactly what that stone weighed. He carried twelve of them himself.

The stove ticked. Outside, past the frosted glass, a sledge went by with its bells muffled — orders, everything muffled this winter, as if the war were something you could keep quiet if you wrapped it well enough.

He had come to Vellmere to inspect the hospital's paperwork, which was a polite phrase for what he actually did, which was to sit in drafty rooms and put his signature under other men's arithmetic. He was good at it. That was the trouble with him; he'd been told so often enough. Good at making the numbers stand up straight. Good at the small, surgical violence of a line drawn through a name. It was remarkable, really, how little ink it took. A stroke no wider than a hair could unmake a man — could take a sergeant with a wife in Ashford and make him into an error, a clerical matter, a rounding-down.

The first time he'd done it, four years ago, his hand had shaken. He remembered that with a clarity that felt less like memory than like weather — always arriving, never leaving. Colonel Brandt had stood over him and said, *The division can't afford the transport figures, Major. Twelve men on one advance is a rounding error. You're not killing them. They're already dead. You're just deciding what gets written down.*

And Halloway, twenty-nine years old and wanting so badly to be the kind of officer who could bear things, had drawn the lines. Twelve strokes. He'd slept that night — that was the part that shamed him most, all these years later. He had actually slept.

* * *

It was the questions that made him notice her.

Miss Dunmore had been at Vellmere since autumn — a volunteer nurse-adjutant, competent, quiet, the sort of woman the medical officers praised in the flat way men praise furniture that never breaks. He had passed her a dozen times in the wards and corridors without a second thought. But three days ago, going over the intake books with Captain Reeve, he had looked up and found her standing in the doorway with a stack of bandages she was in no hurry to deliver, listening. Not eavesdropping, exactly. Listening the way a surveyor listens for water under ground.

And when Reeve read out the day's admitted-and-deceased totals, she had said, very evenly, "And the others?"

Reeve hadn't understood. "Others?"

"The men who were brought in and aren't in the book." She'd said it the way you'd ask about a missing spoon. "There were two on Tuesday. Off the transport from Kellin Ford. I carried one of them myself."

Reeve had looked at Halloway the way subordinates look at superiors when a civilian has stepped somewhere she shouldn't, and Halloway had said, "Clerical oversight, Miss Dunmore. The intake books lag," and watched her accept it — accept it visibly, completely, in the way she nodded and thanked him and went out — and had known, with the certainty of long practice, that she had accepted nothing at all.

That was yesterday. Today he had asked Reeve about her, casually, and learned what Reeve knew, which was little: that she kept to herself, that she was good with the dying, that she went down to the river district some evenings, nobody knew why. The river district. Where the print workshops were, and the paper stores, and the other things that armies in their tidiness preferred not to look at too closely.

Halloway stood at the cracked window for a long time, watching smoke rise off the hospital's boiler house, and thought about what it meant when a woman began asking after the unrecorded dead. He thought it meant grief. Grief almost always came first, wearing the clothes of diligence. And he thought it meant danger — hers, mostly, but eventually everyone's — because a person who started pulling at omitted names did not stop until someone stopped her, and the stopping was rarely gentle.

He decided to see for himself.

* * *

He found her in the third ward at dusk, at the bed of a boy from the 9th Rifles who was not going to see another dusk, and instead of interrupting he stood by the door with his cap in his hands and waited, because whatever else he intended, he wanted to watch her work.

She worked the way he imagined surgeons worked: no wasted motion, no performance of tenderness, just attention — absolute, unhurried attention — given to a dying private as though he were the last man on earth worth attending to. She held his hand. She leaned close when he spoke, and wrote something — he saw it clearly, a small notebook, quick pencil — and she promised him something, voice low, and the boy's face changed. It loosened. Whatever she had promised, it was the thing he needed more than morphine.

He died twenty minutes later, quietly, between one breath and the next breath that didn't come, and Jude Dunmore closed his eyes and sat still for a moment with her hand still holding his, and then she straightened the blanket, and turned, and saw the major standing in the doorway.

Most civilians flinched at the uniform. She didn't. She took him in — the rank tabs, the tiredness underneath them — with the frank assessment of someone triaging, and said, "He'll need registering. Private Aldous Wren, 9th Rifles. Though I expect the intake book will lag again."

It was not impertinence, quite. It was a blade shown flat, so you could see it wasn't hidden.

"Captain Reeve handles the registers," Halloway said.

"Captain Reeve handles the ones that get registered." She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried. "You came to watch me, Major. Was it worth the walk?"

He should have rebuked her. Some part of him, the part trained by Brandt and eleven years of service, reached for the rebuke like a familiar tool on a wall. Instead he heard himself say: "What did you promise him?"

Something moved behind her eyes — surprise, quickly governed. "That his mother would know where he fell. That someone would write to her who wasn't reading from a form." She folded the boy's glasses — he'd been twenty-two and wore wire spectacles — and set them on the bedside shelf. "Whether I can keep that promise is another question. There seem to be difficulties, administratively, with certain names."

"There are," Halloway said. "There always are."

She studied him then, longer, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being counted — added to some ledger of her own, assessed for weight.

"You've struck names yourself," she said. It was not a question either.

The ward was emptying around them; orderlies had come for the body. He let them pass before he answered, and when he answered it was quietly. "Twelve, once. In one night. Because the division couldn't afford the transport figures." He watched her take that in. "I've regretted it in proportion ever since. Which is to say, entirely, and uselessly."

"And knowing that," she said, "you've come down here to find me asking after names. What is it you want, Major? To warn me? To stop me?" A pause, and then, with a directness that would have gotten a soldier flogged: "Or do you already know what I'm doing?"

He had prepared, on the walk over, several versions of this conversation. All of them involved maintaining a certain opacity, a certain officerly fog through which civilians should not be able to see. Every one of those versions died in the cold air of the ward, in front of a woman who had just held a dead boy's hand and had the spine to interrogate a major beside the corpse.

"I think," he said, "that we should talk somewhere warmer than this."

* * *

The workshop smelled of ink and lamp oil and cold iron, and Halloway stood just inside the door of it feeling like a man who has walked into a church of a religion he doesn't practice. Type blocks in cases along the walls. A press under canvas in the corner, big as a coffin standing up. Paper everywhere — reams of it, the raw material of everything the army wanted written and everything it wanted unwritten.

Jude lit the lamp herself, having locked the outer door behind them with a key she wore on a cord at her neck, and did not offer him a chair, because there was only one, and it sat behind the composing stone.

"The printer," Halloway said. "Elias. He's a deserter."

"He's a printer."

"He deserted from the 4th Division press unit fourteen months ago. There's a file." He paused. "I haven't filed a report on him either. You may begin to perceive a pattern in my omissions."

She almost smiled. It transformed her face and was gone. "I perceive it."

He turned a type case with one finger, reading the backwards letters the way other men read scripture. "I'm not here about him. I'm here about you, and about Thursday."

"Thursday?"

"The bridge closes Thursday. Engineers confirmed it this morning — ice pressure at the piers, the whole season's traffic ends. After Thursday there is no crossing at Vellmere until thaw. Nothing moves across that river for four months. Not convoys, not ambulances, not mail." He looked at her. "There's a convoy leaving Wednesday night. Last one. I can put you on it. There's a place — I can arrange it formally, adjutant's attachment to the garrison hospital at Marrow Cross. Dry quarters, regular pay, work that counts, officially, toward something. Protection," he said, and heard how the word sounded, and finished anyway, "from what's coming here."

"And what's coming here?"

"You know what's coming. Spring thaw, the quarter gets requisitioned, the records get burned — all the unofficial ones, all the awkward ones, every scrap of paper that contradicts a report. It happens every year in every occupied town; it's policy, not malice. Anyone holding unofficial records when the provosts arrive will be treated as holding contraband." He said it flatly, because kindness dressed up as anything else insulted them both. "You've been writing down names, Miss Dunmore. I don't know where, I don't know how many, and I am not going to ask. But paper burns faster than people run."

For a while she said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the building settled, snow sliding off the roof.

"Why?" she said finally. "Why spend your protection on me?"

"Because I've done the arithmetic you're doing," Halloway said. "And because I know how it ends. It ends with a girl in a burning building, or a page in a provost's fire, and the names die twice — once in the flesh, once in the record — and the mothers never learn where their sons fell." He took his cap off, turned it in his hands. "I struck twelve names because a colonel told me the truth was a rounding error. I have spent every year since trying to decide whether I'm the kind of man who does that, or the kind who doesn't, and I have never once arrived at an answer. You—" he gestured, at her, at the composing stone, at the whole dim room, "—you appear to have decided. I find I want to protect that. Call it penance if you like. Penance is a respectable motive; the church has vouched for it for centuries."

"And silence?" she said. "You said protection in exchange for silence. Silence about what?"

"About me. About this conversation. About Elias and that press and whatever pages come off it. If the wrong ears learn that a major knows about an unregistered ledger and did nothing —" he shook his head — "then my protection is worth nothing, to you or anyone. Discretion is the coin. That's the price. It isn't much to ask. It costs you nothing you're using anyway."

He watched her weigh it. He was honest enough with himself to admit that he hoped — God help him, he hoped — that she would take it. That she would go east on Wednesday's convoy to dry rooms and safety and a future in which she was alive, and that some part of the debt he'd been carrying since that shaking-handed night four years ago would be paid down by her survival. It was a selfish hope wearing generous clothes, and he knew that too.

But Jude Dunmore looked at him across the composing stone with the lamp between them, and said, "Major Halloway. Do you know what the difference is between us?"

"Tell me."

"You drew lines through twelve names and have spent four years deciding whether you're sorry." Her voice was level, not cruel — that was the worst of it; there was no cruelty in her at all. "I started writing names because of one line somebody drew through my brother. So no. I won't be silent, and I won't leave, and I won't trade your protection for the one thing I have that the dead don't." She picked up a type block from the stick on the stone — a T, he noticed, one of the short ones — and held it in her palm like a coin she refused to spend. "You can arrest me, or report Elias, or report me, if that's what protecting the truth requires. Or you can leave here and forget you found me. Those are your options, Major. Those are the only two ledgers open tonight."

Halloway stood in the smell of ink and cold iron, in the church of a religion he did not practice, and understood that he had offered her temptation dressed as kindness, and that she had seen through the clothes to the naked thing underneath, and had declined it — declined *him* — with more courtesy than he deserved.

And understood, also, in the same instant, that he was going to do neither of the things she named.

"Keep the T," he said, turning for the door. "Wednesday's convoy leaves at midnight. The offer holds until then."

Outside, the river groaned under its ice, long and low, like something counting.
