## 4. Elias at the Press

The workshop smelled of ink and cold iron and, faintly, of the river itself — that mineral damp that got into everything from November onward, seeping through the stone floor, curling into the paper stock until even a blank sheet seemed to remember water. Elias Quill had lived with that smell for three winters now, and he had stopped noticing it the way a man stops noticing his own heartbeat. Tonight he noticed it. Tonight everything felt newly noticed, because Jude Dunmore had come down the outside stair at half past nine, in the dark, without knocking, and stood in the doorway with her coat still buttoned wrong — one button off, so the wool pulled crooked across her chest — and said, "I need to show you something."

He had known her for five weeks by then. Long enough to know she set type like a woman defusing something. Long enough to know she never came to the workshop after dark unless the thing she carried was too heavy for daylight.

"Show me," he said.

She crossed the room the way she always did, giving the press a wide berth, as if it might wake up. She'd done that since her first week — skirted the machine like a horse she didn't trust — and Elias had never asked why. There was a whole taxonomy of people who came near a press and flinched from it, and he had learned not to sort them aloud.

She set her satchel on the composing stone. From it she drew a book.

Not a book, exactly. It had begun life as a ledger — he could see the ghost of a stationer's stamp on the spine, *Vellmere & Sons, Accounts* — but it had been worked hard since. The cover was oilcloth lashed with waxed thread. The pages inside, when she opened it under the single lamp, were dense. Columns of names. Dates. Regiments written in a shorthand she'd invented herself, because the real designations would burn a page faster than a match. Margins crowded with notes in a hand that started controlled in the early entries and grew looser as the pages turned, as if the writing of it were wearing her down grain by grain.

"How many?" he asked.

"Two hundred and eleven," she said. "As of last night."

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Two hundred and eleven names that existed nowhere else. He knew what an official casualty roll looked like. He knew what got left off them, because he had once been paid, in a manner of speaking, to leave things off.

"You've kept this since October," he said.

"Since before October." She closed it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. "It started with one name. It got out of hand."

"That's usually how the truth works."

She looked at him then — really looked, the lamp between them throwing her shadow up the wall behind him, huge and slightly bent, as if even her shadow leaned toward the work — and said, "You never ask what I print here. Every week I bring you copy and every week you set it and pull it and never once ask why a nurse needs four hundred pamphlets on how to dress a wound when the army issues its own field manual."

"The army's manual doesn't mention that you can pack a chest wound with boiled moss," Elias said. "I assumed you had your reasons."

"I do." Her hand stayed flat on the ledger's cover. "But reasons aren't the same as permission, and I don't have permission, and if anyone ever traced those pamphlets back to their source, they'd trace them to this room. To you. So before I go any further, you should know what you're already part of."

"I know what I'm part of," he said. "I've known since the first night you paid me in army scrip and apologized for it."

"That's not—"

"Jude." He heard his own voice go quiet, which it only did when the truth was close. "Come here. Look at the press."

* * *

The press was a Columbian, iron-bodied, older than either of them, salvaged from a print shop in Marrow's Bend that had closed when the owner went to war and didn't come back. Elias had bought it for almost nothing and hauled it forty miles on a borrowed cart, and it had cost him two fingers' worth of skin learning its temper all over again, because every press has a temper and this one had been neglected before he got it. He oiled it on Thursdays. He talked to it sometimes, low, the way other men talked to horses. If Jude had ever caught him at it she'd never said so, and he suspected she'd have understood better than most.

"Made in 1841," he said, resting his hand on the counterweight eagle. "There are maybe six machines like it left in the province. Two are in army printing offices. One is in the garrison at Corven. One is in a schoolhouse being used as a doorstop." He ran his palm along the platen. "And one burned last spring with everything else in Harrowgate. Which leaves mine."

"I don't understand what you're telling me."

"I'm telling you that if you wanted copies of that ledger — true copies, clean copies, fifty of them, a hundred, printed fast enough to outrun whatever's coming — there is exactly one machine within a hundred miles that could do it." He looked at her. "And it's standing in this room. And the man who runs it is a deserter. So let's stop pretending we're strangers to each other's crimes."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the wind found a loose shingle and worried it.

"Deserter," she repeated. Not accusing. Testing the word's weight, the way she tested everything.

"Two years ago I was a compositor with the Third Army Printing Section. You wouldn't have known my face, but you'd have seen my work. Every posted casualty list east of the river for eight months — typeset by E. Quill, though they don't sign them, of course." He pulled the stool from under the composing stone and sat, because his legs had opinions about standing during this part. "In March of '15 I was given a corrected roll to set. That happened often enough — corrections, amendments, clerks make mistakes. But this one came with instructions. Not to correct the old list. To reprint it with certain names removed and certain others marked *returned to duty* who were nothing of the kind. Men who'd died of wounds in the clearing stations, marked fit. Men missing, marked deserted themselves — which spared the officers explaining what had actually happened to them."

"And you printed it."

"For six weeks I printed it." He made himself hold her eyes. He owed her that much honesty at minimum, and probably more. "Six weeks of setting type that unmade men. I told myself it was orders, it was the machine's work not mine, I was one compositor among — I was very good at telling myself things; it's a trade skill. Then in April a woman came to the section office looking for her husband's name. She'd had a letter from another man in his company saying he'd died at the Quarrel Ford. The official roll showed him returned to duty. She thought her informant was mistaken, or cruel. She stood at the counter asking me — me, the man who'd set the line that erased him — whether the roll could be wrong."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her rolls are copied by hand and hands err." His mouth tasted of the old lie, still, two years later, like a coin held too long. "I gave her the sentence I'd been trained to give. She thanked me. She thanked me, Jude, and walked out into the snow, and I stood there and understood that I had just sent her home to wait for a man who was already dead and would stay officially alive forever, and that somewhere in a drawer or a church register there was a family keeping a place set at a table for a ghost I manufactured."

He'd walked out that same evening. Left his kit, his pay book, his identity disc. Taken a cart south through the thaw mud and then gone north again when the search moved south, because deserters run toward home first and everyone knows it, and he had no home worth running to. He'd fetched up in Vellmere because it had a river, and rivers meant paper traffic, and paper was the one cargo nobody inspected closely.

"They searched for me for four months," he said. "Then the war got hungry and stopped searching. I changed my name twice. Quill is the third one. The first name I won't tell you, because it belongs to a man I'm not anymore, and I'd like to keep at least one grave dug."

Jude hadn't moved through any of it. She stood with her hand still flat on the ledger, and when he finished she said, "The woman at the counter. What was her name?"

"I never learned it."

"She'll be in here someday, maybe." Jude lifted the ledger and opened it to the middle, turning pages until she found what she wanted, and tilted it toward the lamp. "Half these names came to me the same way. A sister. A wife. Someone with a letter that contradicts the record, and no one official willing to look twice, because looking twice costs an officer a clean report." Her finger rested beside a name — *Aldous Wrenne, Pvt., d. 14 April 1915, Quarrel Ford* — and Elias felt the floor tilt gently beneath him, because the date was right, the place was right, and the man whose letter had contradicted the roll had been the dead man's friend, and the widow at the counter had been holding it.

"No," he said.

"Aldous Wrenne," Jude read quietly. "Struck from the March–April roll. Marked returned to duty. Died at the clearing station at Ferren Mill on the fourteenth, per witness, Cpl. R. Osei." She looked up. "Elias. This is him. This is the husband."

The workshop went very small around him. The press, the stone, the lamp, the woman — all of it contracted to the size of a single line of type.

"He's been in the ledger since the second week," she said. "Before I ever brought you a pamphlet to print. Before either of us knew anything about the other." She closed the book again and pressed both hands flat on its cover, and he saw now that the gesture wasn't protective but steadying — she leaned on the ledger the way sailors lean on rails. "You spent six weeks erasing men. I've spent fourteen months writing them back. We've been doing the same work from opposite ends, and we met in the middle over wound-dressing pamphlets, and neither of us knew."

"Neither of us asked."

"I'm asking now." She straightened. "Here's what I couldn't say upstairs, in the ward, where the walls have ears and one of them belongs to Major Halloway. The requisition order went up on the quarter notice board yesterday. Come spring thaw, this whole stretch of the riverfront goes to the army — stores, billeting, and the burning detail comes through after. Everything unofficial burns. Papers, records, ledgers, letters. They did it in Corven in January. Ash went into the river for two days."

He had seen the notice himself. He'd read it three times and told himself it applied to warehouses, not to workshops tucked behind a ropewalk. Cowardice does tidy little accounting like that.

"So when thaw comes," Jude said, "this—" she touched the cover "—dies with the building. Unless copies exist elsewhere. Many copies. Copies they can't burn all at once." Her eyes went from him to the press and back, and he watched her arrive at the thing he'd already arrived at, watched it land in her the way heavy news lands — not all at once but in stages, knees first. "Your machine."

"My machine," he agreed. "If we start now, tonight, setting the names a page at a time, I can pull maybe sixty clean impressions a night before the ink gets sluggish in the cold. Say two hundred and eleven names, thirty to a page, seven pages per full copy, plus the notes you'll insist on including—" she would insist; he already knew her "—call it ten pages. Sixty nights for one full copy. Fewer if we run double shifts and I re-ink hot." He rubbed his thumb along his jaw. "Thaw is four months out, if the river's kind. We could make perhaps twenty copies. Twenty copies in twenty different hands, scattered south and west — and then burning this building burns nothing that matters."

"Twenty copies." She breathed it rather than said it.

"It's not enough," he said. "I know it's not enough. Nothing will be enough. But it's more than one, and one is what they've planned for."

Jude looked down at the ledger for a long moment. When she raised her head there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — not gratitude, not relief, something rawer and less settled, the look of a person who has been carrying a load alone for so long that the offer of help is itself a kind of grief, because it retroactively measures how long she'd been alone.

"Why?" she asked. "You could refuse. You could tell me to take my subversive little book and my subversive little pamphlets and find another press. No one would blame—"

"Everyone would blame me. That's rather the definition of desertion." He stood and laid his hand flat on the Columbian's iron frame, cold as riverstone even in summer, and felt the familiar dumb patient weight of it, the best thing he owned and the only thing that had never lied for him. "I unmade men with a press once, Jude. I've been waiting two years to make one with it instead. I'd have waited longer if you hadn't climbed those stairs tonight, and I want you to understand that I mean that as a reproach — to myself. You shouldn't have had to come down here in the dark and risk a deserter to save your own work. But you did, and I'm glad, and now—"

"Now we start tonight," she said.

"Now we start tonight."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and the ink thickened and their fingers ached with cold. He taught her to lock the chase properly, to plane the form, to mind her knuckles at the tympan. She learned fast — faster than any amateur he'd shown, and he understood why: she'd spent months imagining this exact moment, rehearsing it, and her hands had already decided what they'd do before her mind caught up. By midnight she was setting her own shorthand regiments into columns with the tightness of a journeyman, and he stood pulling sheets and reading names pass under the lamp, name after name after name, and thought: *this is what the roll should have looked like. This is what I should have been setting all along.*

Near two in the morning she paused over the stone, type stick in hand, and said without looking up, "The woman at the counter in the army office. The one asking about Aldous Wrenne."

"Yes."

"When the copies are done — when they're safe in twenty hands — I'm going to find her somehow. Through his company, through the chaplain, through whatever road exists. And I'm going to put this ledger in front of her and show her the entry, and she's going to learn that the roll was wrong and that someone, eventually, wrote it right." She set the next piece of type with a small precise click. "You gave her a false answer once. This is how we give her the true one. Together."

Elias pulled the lever. The platen came down; the impression bit; the sheet emerged bearing thirty names in clean black ink, proof against fire now, multiplied beyond the reach of any single match.

"There's something you should know about timing," he said, hanging the sheet to dry. "The bridge closes when the ice runs. Could be a fortnight, could be days. After that, nothing crosses this river until thaw except by God's bad grace. Whatever we print, whoever carries it south — they cross before the ice does, or they wait with us."

Jude looked at the drying sheet, at the names going stiff and permanent in the cold air, and he watched her understand that the clock had just become audible.

"Then we'd best not waste the ink," she said.
