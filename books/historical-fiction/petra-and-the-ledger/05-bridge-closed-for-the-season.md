## 5. Bridge Closed for the Season

The river told us before the officers did.

I was at the workshop window, wiping ink from my fingers, when I heard it — a sound like a door being dragged open somewhere under the world. Low, grinding, patient. Elias looked up from his press with the expression of a man who has heard a gun go off in another room and is deciding whether it concerns him.

"Floes," he said.

"You're sure?"

"I grew up on this water." He set down his composing stick. "That's not wind. That's ice coming down from the north like it's owed something."

We went out together into the gray morning, down the frozen ruts of the lane to where the bank dropped toward the Vellmere crossing. The bridge stood against a sky the color of dishwater, its iron spine furred with frost, and below it the river had changed overnight from black glass to something moving — great pale plates of ice sliding downstream, shouldering each other, tilting up on edge and diving under, the whole surface in slow violent motion as though the water beneath had decided to shrug off winter all at once.

"They'll close it," Elias said. Not a guess. A fact, delivered the way he delivered everything, flat and final as a pulled lever.

"Until when?"

"Until thaw. Maybe later. The army won't risk the span on floes that size. One good jam against those piers—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. We had both seen what the spring flood did to the millrace two years ago, when the water came up brown and took the wheelhouse whole.

I stood there watching the ice go by and felt something settle in my chest, cold and definite, like a stone dropped down a well. Because I knew what a closed bridge meant. It meant the convoy Halloway had promised me — the last one, the one he'd spoken of with that careful officer's gentleness, *there will be a place for you, Miss Dunmore, if you want it* — would leave without me or not at all. And it meant the workshop on this side of the water, with its press and its crate of type blocks and the ledger hidden inside like a heart inside a ribcage, would be sealed off from the world until the river said otherwise.

The river did not care about my ledger. That was becoming clear to me as a fact about the universe, the way weather is a fact.

"We should get back," Elias said.

"In a minute."

He waited beside me. He was good at waiting; it was one of the things I'd noticed first about him, back when he was only the deserter-printer who made my pages possible and I was only the woman who kept asking officers questions they didn't want asked. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his breath steaming and watched the ice with me as if we were watching a funeral procession, which in a way we were — the procession of everything that was going to be taken away from us between now and thaw.

Downstream, near the pilings, a floe the size of a barn door struck the ice shelf along the bank and cracked it clean through, the sound carrying across the water like a rifle shot. Gulls lifted off the shallows, screaming.

"There's your answer," Elias said.

* * *

The order went up at noon, nailed to the customs post by a corporal who couldn't have been more than nineteen and whose hands shook hammering it in, whether from cold or from the crowd gathering around him I never learned. The crowd was small — Vellmere had been bled thin by three years of war — but it read the notice aloud to itself in fragments, the way crowds do, passing the words along like a bucket brigade:

**THE BRIDGE IS CLOSED TO ALL TRAFFIC BY ORDER OF THE NORTHERN COMMAND. NO CIVILIAN CROSSING. NO MILITARY CROSSING EXCEPT AUTHORIZED CONVOY. THE BRIDGE WILL REOPEN AT THE DISCRETION OF THE COMMANDING OFFICER WHEN ICE CONDITIONS PERMIT.**

"When ice conditions permit," an old man near me repeated bitterly. "And when will that be? Easter? Midsummer? When the war's over and there's no bridge left to cross?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody answers that kind of question anymore; we've all learned the arithmetic of silence.

I found Halloway at the convoy marshaling yard behind the customs house, because I knew he would be there — officers are drawn to departure points the way gulls are drawn to fish offal, standing straighter than they feel, counting trucks and men and giving orders about canvas straps as if straps were the thing standing between civilization and ruin. He saw me coming and something crossed his face too quickly for me to name it. Guilt, maybe. Or relief. Or the particular misery of a man watching a promise expire on schedule.

"Miss Dunmore."

"Major." I nodded at the convoy — six lorries, canvas-backed, engines ticking as they warmed. "That's the last one, then."

"That's the last scheduled one. There may be others—"

"Don't." I said it quietly, and he stopped, because we both knew how it sounded, the way men say *there may be others* to widows. "You told me the last convoy across. You told me there'd be a place on it. I'm asking you plainly: is there a place on it now?"

He looked at the lorries. He looked at the bridge, where the sentries were already rigging the barrier chains, red-painted links bright against all that gray. When he turned back to me his voice had dropped into the register officers use when they're telling the truth and hate themselves for it.

"The convoy is authorized personnel only now. The order came down an hour ago. No civilians. I argued it — I want you to know that. I took it to Colonel Merrick myself and he read me the same notice that's on the post out front." A pause. His jaw worked once. "There may be a ferry before full closure—"

"There's no ferry. Osk hauled his boat out onto the mud weeks ago and the ferry landing iced in. You know that as well as I do."

"I know it." He said it heavily. "Yes. I know it."

So there it was, laid out between us on the frozen mud of the marshaling yard: the offer had an expiry, and the expiry had come, and neither of us had quite believed it would arrive so soon even though we'd both counted the days. I want to be fair to Halloway here, because fairness matters to me — it's practically the religion I keep instead of church. He had offered me safety in good faith. Safety, like everything else in this war, simply turned out to be a perishable good.

"I could put you on the second lorry anyway," he said suddenly, and I saw the temptation move through him like weather. "Covered canvas. The sentries don't count heads past the barrier, they count papers, and I can write a paper. Medical auxiliary, attached to the field laundry. It would hold. It might hold."

"And if it doesn't? If someone counts heads after all?"

His silence answered me.

"Then they stop the convoy at the far end," I said, "and they search it, and they find me, and they ask me why a laundry worker from Vellmere has no unit record and no transfer stamp, and while they're asking they'll be pulling every unofficial document off every truck, because that's what happens when something irregular turns up — everything gets audited. Everything gets burned early." I heard my own voice go flat and cold and precise, and I thought: *that's Elias's voice, that's the press talking through me.* "You'd be spending your protection to save me and costing every name in my ledger to do it. Is that the trade you're offering?"

"It's the trade I'm afraid of," Halloway said. "It's the trade I've been lying awake making since the requisition order came through." He rubbed his face with one glove, briefly, unofficer-like. "Jude. Come to the yard tomorrow at first light anyway. If there's any way — any way at all —"

"Major."

"—any way, I'll find it."

Behind him, a sergeant began shouting for drivers to mount up. The lorries shuddered, coughed, caught. The sound of them filled the yard, that diesel stutter that has become the heartbeat of this entire war, and I understood with sudden clarity that I was standing at a border being drawn in real time, a line moving through the middle of my life with me on the wrong side of it, and that no amount of arguing with majors was going to move it back.

"Go and do your duty," I said, and immediately hated the word *duty*, hated how much iron it carried, hated that I meant it kindly.

Halloway held out his hand. We shook, formally, like strangers, which is what the army had made us — two people who could only meet at borders and departure points and never anywhere in the ordinary country where people actually live. Then he climbed into the lead lorry, and I stepped back, and the convoy pulled out of the marshaling yard in a grind of gears and a wall of exhaust smoke that hung in the freezing air like something wounded.

I walked to the foot of the bridge and watched them go.

* * *

They crossed in single file, six lorries, headlights burning uselessly against the afternoon murk. The ice ran beneath the span in long white rafts, grinding at the piers, and I remember thinking the bridge sounded different with traffic on it and floes underneath — a deep strained chord, steel and water arguing. The sentries at the far barrier saluted each vehicle through. The chains came down behind the last one. Red links swinging, settling, still.

Then Halloway did a thing I have thought about every day since.

The convoy halted on the far side, engines idling, and he climbed down from the cab and walked back out onto the span — alone, into the middle of the bridge, right up to the barrier chains — and stood there looking back across the water at me. At the town. At the small dark figure at the foot of the bridge, which was me, though from his distance I must have been barely a smudge. He stood there a long time. Long enough that a sentry moved toward him and then, recognizing the major's silhouette, thought better of it.

What was he doing? Saying goodbye. Or asking forgiveness. Or memorizing — I've settled on memorizing, finally, because I've done it myself: standing at a threshold fixing in place the thing you're about to lose, building the image plank by plank so it will hold weight later, when you need somewhere to live.

Then he raised one hand — just the hand, nothing theatrical, no salute — and turned, and walked back to the convoy, and the lorries pulled away north along the far road until the exhaust haze swallowed them, and the bridge stood empty over the running ice with its chains up and its sentries stamping cold, closed for the season like a grave with a lid on it.

I stayed at the rail until my feet went numb.

Here is what I understood, standing there, and it arrived not as a thought but as a physical event, something that happened in my body the way a bone sets: **no one was coming back for me.** Not the convoy. Not the army. Not the major with his decent conscience and his expiring promises. The bridge was shut and the season was shut and the world on the far side of that water had already begun forgetting this side existed. Whatever happened in Vellmere now — the requisition, the burning, the spring patrols with their cans of oil — would happen to us in private, unwitnessed, unwritten, unless I wrote it.

Unless I wrote it.

I laughed. Actually laughed, once, out loud, into the wind off the river, and a sentry twenty yards down the rail gave me a look, and I didn't care. Because there it was, the whole shape of it, absurd and complete: the officers had their reports, and their reports were lies of omission, tidy little lists of the accounted-for dead, and the river had just closed the book on this side of the water — and I was holding a ledger full of the names they'd struck, and the only copy machine in the district was a deserter's press in a condemned workshop, and the deadline was thaw, and thaw was months away, and the ice was already coming down like God emptying His pockets.

Everyone else had been evacuated from their own story. I hadn't. I'd been left in mine, deliberately, by arithmetic and weather, and there was a terrible freedom in it. Abandonment, I discovered, tastes a lot like permission.

I turned my back on the bridge.

* * *

Elias was waiting at the workshop door when I came up the lane, and he read my face the way he read type, letter by letter.

"Well?" he said.

"It's closed. The convoy went without me. Halloway stood on the span and looked back and then he was gone, and there are chains on the barrier, and the corporal's notice says *when ice conditions permit*, which means never, which means we're on our own until thaw." I stamped the snow off my boots on his step, hard, twice, like punctuation. "Which means we work."

For a moment he just looked at me. There's a quality Elias has that I've never managed to say properly — a stillness, a way of receiving bad news without flinching, as if he'd already priced it in. Deserters learn that. Men who've run learn exactly what fear costs per hour and refuse to pay retail.

"They'll burn this place at thaw," he said. "You understand that's still true. Nothing that happened today changes it. If anything—"

"If anything it makes it certain. Yes. I know." I pushed past him into the warmth, into the smell of ink and oil and coal smoke that had become, without my ever deciding it, the closest thing I had to a home. The press sat in the lamplight like a black animal at rest. The crate of type blocks stood where we'd hidden it, ordinary, innocent, full of names. "So here's what I've been working out while I watched the bridge close. We assumed we had until spring. We assumed the thaw was our deadline and we could pace ourselves, transcribe a few names a night, keep it quiet, keep it safe." I pulled off my gloves finger by finger. "But the bridge closing changes the math. They can't send anything heavy across that water either. Which means the burning detail, when it comes, comes light — a handful of men, a cart, whatever they can bring over the ice once it freezes solid enough to walk on. And that means the ice isn't just our prison, Elias. The ice is their road. The day this river freezes hard enough to carry a patrol is the day the patrol can reach us. Our clock isn't thaw anymore."

Elias went very still. I watched him arrive at it, the same arithmetic, the same cold stone dropping down the same well.

"The freeze," he said. "Not the thaw."

"The freeze. Weeks sooner. Maybe days, the way this cold is building." I looked at the ledger-crate, then at him. "How fast can you print?"

He smiled then, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a man loading a weapon. "Faster than they can march," he said. "Get your coat back off. We start tonight."

Outside, the river ground on in the dark, hauling its ice south, and I stood a moment in the doorway listening to it — that long dragging-door sound under the world — and I made it into a promise, the way I make everything into a promise now, because promises are the only currency that hasn't collapsed.

Every floe going by, I thought, is a page turning.

Write faster.
