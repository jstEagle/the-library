## 6. Inventory of What Can Be Carried

The satchel was a dead soldier's, of course. Everything in Vellmere was somebody's once — the coats on our backs, the candles we burned, the canvas over the windows. This one had belonged to a corporal named Aldous Finch, who had died at the flooded hospital in November with his boots off, and whose mother I had written to myself because no one else would. The leather was good. English leather. It had crossed the sea to hold his kit and now it would cross the ice to hold mine, if it came to that, and I thought he wouldn't have minded. I thought he might have insisted.

I laid everything out on the workbench by lamplight, the way you lay out a hand of cards when you're deciding which to throw away.

The ledger first. Two hundred and six pages, sewn spine cracked from handling, corners soft as cloth from my thumbs. I wrapped it in oilcloth and set it down at the center of the bench like a stone on an altar. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. It weighed more than the bridge.

Beside it: the ink. One full bottle and a third of another, stoppered with wax so they wouldn't breathe in the cold. A nib tin with eleven nibs, three of them bent past use but kept anyway, because you never knew. A pencil stub for emergencies, since ink freezes and graphite doesn't — Elias taught me that, first winter, tapping the stub against my knuckle like a schoolmaster. *Ink is a fair-weather friend, Jude. Pencil will write on a coffin lid.*

A photograph. Thomas, aged fourteen, squinting into sun on the pier at Vellmere, one hand raised against the glare as if even then he were trying to shield someone behind him from something coming. Mother's copy, smuggled out of her house before the requisition men got to it. I had carried it through the autumn of the advance and the winter after, and the paper had gone soft and foxed at the edges, and I could not have told you which creases came from handling and which from grief.

Ration tins. Four of them, hoarded honestly or nearly so — two of bully beef, one of condensed milk, one of those little fish in oil that Bram called cat food and ate anyway. That was four meals, maybe five, maybe seven if the cold slowed my hunger the way it slowed everything else. Enough to walk on. Not enough to live on long.

And then the rest of the bench, all the things that couldn't come.

The second blanket. The lamp with its good brass base. The axe. The kettle. The crate of type blocks, heavy as sin, each letter a small leaden soul. The drying rack with its last sheets pinned up like laundry, like flags, like surrender.

And the press.

* * *

Elias stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders, and I watched him see the inventory laid out, and I watched his eyes go where I knew they'd go. Not to the ledger. To the machine.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard anything yet."

"I've heard this argument already, Elias. In my head, every night, wearing your voice."

He came in and stamped the wet off his boots and took off his gloves finger by finger, which he did when he wanted time to think, and I let him take it, because I loved him, and because I was going to say no anyway.

"The press weighs forty pounds broken down," he said. "Less than the type. The frame comes apart at four bolts — I put it together, I know how it comes apart. Forty pounds, Jude. You carry the ledger against your chest and I carry the iron on my back, and somewhere south there's a room with a window and we print the whole book properly. Every name. Every family. Not rumor. Print."

"And the copies we have?"

"Go ahead of us. Wren's page goes with Wren. But the original—" He touched the oilcloth lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "The original is the proof. And the proof needs a press behind it someday, or it stays a story. Stories get corrected by whoever holds the next pen. Print doesn't. Print just *is.* You pull a sheet and there it is, ten years later, twenty years, saying what it said."

"I know what print is," I said. "You built my whole life out of it."

"Then you know."

"Elias." I made myself turn and look at him straight, because this was going to hurt either way and I preferred it clean. "If the ice takes us, the ledger drowns with us unless it can float, and it floats only if my hands are free to keep it above water. Forty pounds of iron on your back changes how you move. Changes how fast you are. Changes whether Osk will even take us across — you've seen him look at weight. He counts it in seconds of safety."

"Then we don't go until we can go properly."

"There is no *properly.* There's break-up, and it's early this year, Halloway says the patrol schedule moved up, and every night the river sounds a little more like it's clearing its throat." I pressed my palms flat on the bench. "We have a satchel, Elias. One satchel of world. I'm choosing what goes in it."

"And your choice is that my life's work stays here to be burned with the records?"

"My choice is that *you* stay here to be burned with it, if you're carrying forty pounds of iron across black ice!" It came out louder than I meant and hung between us in the cold air, breath and truth together. I hadn't known I was afraid of that until I said it. His hands. His frostbitten fingers, still clumsy some mornings, that fumbled a bolt last week three times before it caught. Him on the ice with a press frame shifting on his shoulders, leaning, correcting, leaning—

"That's not your decision," he said quietly.

"It became my decision the night you fell through and I pulled you out. I bought a share in your survival that day. I'm exercising it."

He laughed, short and bitter, and turned away to the window, and for a moment neither of us spoke, and the river spoke instead — that deep groan out under the dark, the sound I'd stopped being able to sleep without.

"You know what they'll do with it," he said finally. "The soldiers. They won't melt it down respectful. They'll smash it in the yard and burn the wood and leave the type scattered in the mud like teeth." He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the press, at the great black shape of it under its canvas sheet, patient as a tomb. "My father built half of it. I finished it when I was nineteen. Every job it ever ran, I remember — the wedding announcements, the broadsheets, the falsified rolls." His jaw worked. "Especially the falsified rolls. That machine printed lies with my hands on it. Saving it is the only way I know to make it mean the other thing."

I went to him. I didn't touch him yet. I stood beside him looking at the covered shape, and I understood him completely, and understanding him completely changed nothing, which is the cruelest thing love does.

"Make it mean the other thing by staying alive," I said. "The names are the machine now. We're the machine. You and me and Wren and Bram and that chaplain's saddlebag. Paper and hands. That's all a press ever was, Elias. Iron just makes it faster."

"Faster matters."

"Not more than you."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not surrender, not agreement. Arithmetic. He was doing the same sum I'd done at the bench, weighing his heart against the satchel, and I realized with a cold drop in my stomach that I couldn't control which answer he reached.

"We put it to a vote," he said.

"It's not a committee."

"Two people who love each other is the smallest committee there is." He almost smiled. "Osk decides what crosses. We decide what's worth crossing with. So we ask him. Tonight, if he'll see us. Let a man who's lost sons tell us whether iron or names weigh more on the ice."

It was a good argument. That was the worst part. He'd found the one door I couldn't bolt, because it led through a stranger's grief, and strangers' grief outranked ours both.

"All right," I said. "But hear me now, so you can't pretend later. If Osk says no to the iron, it stays. I won't watch you drown for a machine, and I won't be reasoned out of that by any vote ever held on this earth."

"And if he says yes?"

I looked back at the bench. At Aldous Finch's satchel, empty, waiting. At the photograph of my brother shielding his eyes from a sun that never reached this winter.

"Then God help us both," I said, "because you'll be carrying your father across the river, and I'll be carrying mine."

Outside, the ice groaned again — longer this time, lower, a note that seemed to go on and on under the town like a drawn bowstring. Elias and I looked at each other, and neither of us pretended anymore that spring was far away.

We put our coats on. There was a ferryman on Coal Row we needed to see, and the walking was bad, and the nights were getting shorter.
