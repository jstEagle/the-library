## 7. The First Name

The rain had not stopped for three days when Thomas's battalion went up the ridge.

Jude remembered it that way always — the rain first, then everything else arranged beneath it like bones under skin. October 1915. Vellmere was still a town then and not a wound; the hospital had beds with frames instead of pallets on floorboards, and she had been there eleven months and still believed the war was a thing that happened elsewhere, at a distance measured in trains.

She was sorting bandages in the linen room when the stretchers started coming down from the ridge road. She knew the arithmetic of an advance before anyone told her: the sound of wheels, the smell of mud churned to grease, the particular silence of men who had seen too much to spend words on it. The ward filled. It kept filling after it was full. Surgeons worked by lamplight with their sleeves rolled past the elbow, and the floors darkened, and the orderlies stopped carrying out what they carried out because there was no point describing it anymore.

She did not think of Thomas. That was the strange mercy of it — or the strange cruelty, she could never decide which. He had gone up that ridge two hours before the first stretcher came down, and all through the long gray afternoon she moved among strangers' blood and never once felt the cold hand close around her heart, because she did not know yet that it should have been closed there all along.

The lists came the next morning.

They came pinned to the board outside the adjutant's office, typed on onionskin, three sheets of names — killed, wounded, missing — and a crowd stood before them in the rain, women and old men and convalescents leaning on crutches, everyone reading upside down and sideways, everyone hunting one name out of hundreds the way a gull hunts one fish in a gray sea.

Jude read the list twice. Then a third time, slower, her finger moving under each line as if slowness could change the type.

Thomas Dunmore was not on it.

Not under killed. Not under wounded. Not under missing, where he most certainly belonged, because Private Ellery Cott, who shared his dugout, lay in Ward B with his hip shot through and had already told her — told everyone who would listen — that Thomas had been hit going up the second slope, hit bad, hit in a way you didn't walk away from, and that he'd held his hand and then the smoke came and then the counter-barrage and then there was no holding anything anymore.

*Missing*, Jude thought. At minimum, *missing*. Even the army's cold machinery understood that a man last seen bleeding on a slope under shellfire must be entered somewhere. But the third sheet ended at Wetherell, H., and Thomas Dunmore existed nowhere on any of them.

She went to the adjutant's office and waited her turn behind a woman asking about a husband and a boy asking about a father, and when her turn came the clerk looked up with eyes like coins.

"Name?"

"Dunmore. Thomas Dunmore. Second Battalion."

The clerk ran his finger down his own copy, which was longer than the posted list, and stopped, and frowned, and stopped again.

"Not here," he said.

"He was in the advance. Yesterday morning. There are witnesses."

"Then he'll be on tomorrow's list. Things come through late." He was already looking past her at the woman behind her, whose face said her question would be worse.

It was not on tomorrow's list either. Or the next. A week passed, and the ridge fell silent — the attack had failed, everyone knew it had failed, the whole thing had been a fist thrown against stone until the fist broke — and Thomas Dunmore remained unlisted, unreported, unwritten, as though the ground itself had swallowed him without bothering to inform the paperwork.

* * *

She learned later how it happened. Not the dying — she never learned that, not truly, though she built forty versions of it in the dark — but the omission.

A captain named Rokeby had compiled the casualty return in the field. Rokeby's company had lost over half its strength in ninety minutes, and rather than write the true number into a report that would travel up the chain and mark him as the author of a disaster, he had simply — stopped writing. Trimmed the list. Rounded down. Men who had no recovered body and no surviving witness within easy reach were left off entirely, erased not by malice but by arithmetic, by the quiet cowardice of a man with a pencil and an hour and a career.

Thomas had been hit early. Early meant far forward. Far forward meant no body brought back, and the two men who might have sworn to his death were themselves dead or scattered, and so the pencil passed him by, and the paper went up the line, and somewhere a staff officer entered a number smaller than the truth into a ledger of numbers that were all smaller than the truth, and the war went on.

Jude found this out from Major Halloway, though she did not know then that she would ever have reason to remember the man's name. He was a staff officer even then, attached to the division, passing through Vellmere on inspection, and she cornered him in the corridor of the requisitioned schoolhouse that served as headquarters, a girl of twenty-two with ink on her fingers from copying ward notes, blocking his path with more courage than sense.

"You're from division," she said. "The casualty returns come through your office."

"They do." He had a long face and tired eyes and the manner of a man perpetually late for something.

"My brother isn't on any of them. Thomas Dunmore, Second Battalion. He went up the ridge on the fourteenth and he has not come down and he is not listed anywhere, and I am telling you that is not possible unless somebody made a mistake, and I want to know whose mistake it was."

Something crossed the major's face then — quickly, but she saw it. Not surprise. Recognition. As if she had asked him a question he had been asking himself in the dark.

"Miss—"

"Dunmore. Jude Dunmore."

"Miss Dunmore." He glanced down the corridor, empty except for the two of them and the rain against the tall windows. "Come into this room."

It was a storeroom, shelved with files, smelling of damp paper. He shut the door and did not offer her a chair, because there wasn't one, and he did not offer her comfort, because there wasn't any of that either.

"The returns from the fourteenth were compiled under field conditions," he said. "There are discrepancies. They will be corrected in due course."

"In due course." Her voice cracked on it. "He is my brother. He is seventeen months younger than I am and he lied about his age to enlist and I signed the paper saying he was eighteen because he begged me, and now he is nowhere, not on a list, not in a grave, not in anything, and you tell me about due course."

Halloway was silent a moment. When he spoke again, the officer had drained out of his voice and only the man was left, and the man sounded hollowed.

"There are discrepancies on every return," he said. "You understand me. Not just this one. Every engagement produces a number on paper and the paper's number is always smaller than the field's number, because the field does not lie and paper does, because paper is written by men who answer for what they write. Your brother is one of perhaps dozens from that ridge alone. Dozens. And across the whole front—" He stopped himself.

"Then correct it," Jude said. "Correct it now. You're from division. Correct it."

"I can't." And she believed him, which was the worst part; his helplessness was so plain it was almost decent. "The return is filed. Amending it requires testimony I don't have and authority I don't hold, and if I raised the question officially I would be raising it about every return for two years, and the answer to every one of those questions is the same, and nobody above me wants the same answer sixty times. Miss Dunmore, go home. Grieve your brother. The ground keeps better records than we do, in its way."

"I don't want the ground's records," she said. "I want his name written down. Somewhere. By someone. In ink."

He looked at her for a long moment then, and she would think about that look for years afterward without ever fully decoding it — pity in it, yes, and shame, but underneath both something that might have been fear, as though she had shown him a mirror and he did not care for the face in it.

"Then write it yourself," he said quietly, and opened the door, and was gone.

* * *

That night she sat in the linen room after hours with a candle and a blank page — ward stationery, the cheapest sort, blue-ruled — and she wrote:

*Dunmore, Thomas. Private, Second Battalion. Age nineteen (enlisted at seventeen; his sister certified the lie). Last seen advancing up the northern ridge, 14 October, approximately 0640, wounded, per the account of Pte. E. Cott, Ward B. Omitted from all official returns.*

She read it back. It was not much. It was a paragraph. The war had taken an entire human being — a boy who whistled badly, who could not pass a dog without stopping it, who had once walked six miles in the rain to bring her a book — and what she had salvaged of him fit in a paragraph, and the paragraph fit nowhere, belonged to no file, would be read by no one unless she made someone read it.

So beneath it she wrote a second entry, because Ellery Cott had told her, before fever took him, about another man from the ridge whom no one had claimed: *Sergeant Aldous Byrne, said to have turned back for a wounded man and not returned.* And a third, from a chaplain's muttered list: *Corporal N. Fenn, believed dead on the wire, body unrecovered.*

Three names. She copied them fair onto a fresh sheet, and then, because a single loose page is a thing the wind takes, she sewed the sheets into the back of an old account book she'd bought for twopence at a stall, a grocer's ledger with half its pages still ruled and waiting, columns for goods and prices that would never be entered now.

A ledger. She almost laughed at it, alone in the candlelight — a ledger for debts the army refused to acknowledge. Every name an unpaid account. Every omission an invoice outstanding, presented to whoever, someday, would be made to pay attention.

She wrote at the head of the first page, in letters larger than she intended, pressing hard enough to score the paper:

*These men existed.*

It was not elegant. It was not even grammatical ambition. But it was true, and truth was the one currency she had, and she had just discovered she was richer in it than the entire apparatus of the division.

* * *

In the weeks that followed, the ledger grew the way a fire grows — slowly, then all at once.

Word travels strangely in hospitals. A nurse mentioned to a stretcher-bearer that the Dunmore girl kept a book of the ones the reports missed, and the stretcher-bearer mentioned it to an orderly, and within a month men were finding her between shifts, shyly, sideways, the way people approach a priest with sins they've already confessed elsewhere and had waved away.

"Beg pardon, miss. My mate went down at — well, wherever it was, they never put him in nothing. His mum writes every week wanting to know which hospital he's at, and I can't keep lying to her. Could you — do you have room?"

Room. She had room. She had two hundred blank pages and a pen and a fury that had cooled into something harder and more durable than fury, the way lava cools into rock.

She took testimony the way a magistrate takes it — date, unit, circumstance, witness — and she wrote it all down, and she began keeping a second column, *status of inquiry*, noting whether the name had since surfaced on any official list, whether a letter home had been answered, whether the family knew. Most entries stayed open. Some closed with a grave. A few, rarely, closed with a miracle — a man turning up in a prison column or a base hospital under a misrecorded name — and those she struck through gently, with relief, the strike-through itself a kind of prayer.

By spring the grocer's ledger was full and she had bought another. By the following winter there were four. She hid them in plain sight, shelved among genuine ward records, spines outward, indistinguishable — the bureaucracy's own camouflage turned against it.

And she learned other things too. She learned that omissions had patterns: heavy losses drew heavier pencils; certain officers trimmed and certain officers did not, and you could nearly map a man's character by the gap between his reports and his dead. She learned that families suffered twice — once for the loss, once for the not-knowing, the second worse than the first, because grief at least has a shape and uncertainty has none. She learned that the army's memory was paper and paper burned, but a woman with a pen and a grievance remembers.

And once — only once, in a supply depot at Vellmere, watching a young printer's assistant stack crates of movable type for shipment — she thought: *ink. If a press could be had. If these pages could be set and pulled and duplicated, ten copies, fifty, sent south to every parish register and every mother on the list — then the erasure would stop being so easy. You cannot burn what a hundred hands already hold.*

She pushed the thought away. It was too large for her, then. It required a machine, and money, and a conspirator, and courage of a kind she hadn't grown yet.

The thought waited. Thoughts like that do.

* * *

Two years later, almost to the day, she stood in the doorway of a riverfront workshop while a deserter named Elias wiped ink from a forme and looked at her ledger with an expression she recognized — the same expression Major Halloway had worn in the storeroom, recognition, the face of a man seeing his own secret held in someone else's hands.

"You want duplicates," Elias said. "Of a book the army doesn't admit exists."

"I want the names to outlive the paper they're written on," Jude said. "Everything burns. Reports burn, workshops burn, ledgers burn. Ink set in type and pulled a hundred times — that's harder to kill. I've watched them erase a man with a pencil, Mr. Elias. I'd like to see them try it against a printing press."

He was quiet a moment. Rain worked at the windows; somewhere downriver the ice was beginning to speak in its sleep, the first small mutterings of a season turning.

"My press printed falsified rolls once," he said at last. "Before I deserted. Names entered that shouldn't have been, names left off that should have been there. Same crime, opposite direction." He laid his hand flat on the ledger's cover, carefully, the way a man touches a wound that is not his own. "I've been waiting two years to print something true."

Jude looked at the press — the iron frame, the lever, the bed where a page could become a hundred pages — and felt the two-year-old thought rise in her at last and take its full weight, and she understood that the ledger had never really been the work. The ledger was only the gathering. The work was what came next: making the names impossible to lose.

"Then we'd best begin," she said. "There are four hundred and twelve of them. And the river won't stay frozen forever."

Elias reached for the first case of type.

* * *

Later — much later, on nights when transcription ran past midnight and the lamp burned low and her wrist ached from setting columns of the fallen — Jude would open the original grocer's ledger to its first page and read the scored letters she had pressed there at twenty-two, in a linen room that smelled of carbolic and rain:

*These men existed.*

Four words. Everything else — the workshop, the copies, Elias, the crossing she could not yet imagine — had grown out of them like a tree out of a seed split in the dark.

She dipped her pen. Somewhere below, the press breathed and stamped, breathing names into the world faster than the war could take them out, and she wrote the next line, and the next, and the next.
