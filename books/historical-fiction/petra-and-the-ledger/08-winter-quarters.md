## 8. Winter Quarters

The cold found the workshop before the men did.

Elias noticed it first in the ink — the way it thickened in the pot overnight until the morning's first pull came out grey and starving. He'd learned to keep the ink near the stove, learned the small economies of a room that had become, without anyone deciding it, a place where people stayed.

It began with Corrigan. A corporal with a shattered ankle and no regiment left to return to — his unit had been folded into another after the autumn fighting, his papers lost somewhere between Vellmere and the coast. He'd limped into the workshop in November asking if Elias could print him a duplicate paybook, and instead of printing one, Elias had put him in the corner by the chimney and told him not to ask again. Corrigan hadn't asked. He mended harness straps now for the quartermaster down the lane, paid in bread, and slept like a man who trusted nothing but the wall at his back.

Then came Aldous. Older, quieter, a sergeant who'd taken shrapnel through the shoulder and been discharged from the field hospital as *unfit for service* — which meant unfit for rations, too, once the paperwork caught up with you. Jude had brought him herself, walking him the last hundred yards with her arm under his good shoulder, and when Elias opened the door she'd said only: "He was in Thomas's company." That was all. That was enough. Elias had moved the drying racks to make a cot space, and Aldous had thanked him by silently repairing the drafty window frame within a week, working one-handed with a soldier's stubborn competence.

And then there was Bram.

Bram had arrived in December, half-frozen, having run from the garrison hospital with nothing but an orderly's apron still tied around his waist. He'd stood in the doorway shaking, saying he couldn't go back, that they were sending orderlies up to the forward dressing stations now, that he'd rather be shot as a deserter than carry another stretcher through what was waiting up there. Jude had fed him before she'd asked his name. Elias had watched her do it — the way she ladled out the thin barley soup with the same even hand she used to rule lines in the ledger — and understood that this was how her mercy worked: practical, immediate, uninterested in whether it was deserved.

Three extra mouths. The woodpile was shrinking faster than it should. The flour bin had gone light enough that Elias caught himself lifting it some mornings just to weigh their future in his hands.

And the press kept running.

* * *

They worked nights now, mostly. Daylight belonged to the patrols and the coal carts and the ordinary traffic of a town pretending the war was elsewhere. But after dark, when the shutters were barred and the stove was banked low so its glow wouldn't show through the gaps, the workshop became a different country.

Jude sat at the sorting stone with the ledger open before her, copying names onto slips of paper in her small, severe hand. Elias set them in type at the case, letter by letter, and pulled proofs on the hand press — two copies of each page, one for the ledger itself, one for the growing stack of duplicates wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards. It was slow work. It was sacred work. Neither of them called it either.

Corrigan had figured out what they were doing by his second week and never said a word about it, except once, watching the press come down on a fresh sheet: "My brother's name in there?" And Jude had looked up and said, "If he was struck from the reports, yes," and Corrigan had nodded and gone back to his harness leather, and that was the whole of the conversation.

Aldous pretended not to know. This was its own kind of courtesy.

But Bram — Bram wanted to know everything.

* * *

It started with the type case.

The boy — because that's what he was, really, nineteen maybe twenty, all wrists and worry — would stand at the edge of the lamplight while they worked, watching Jude's fingers move through the case with a fluency that seemed to hypnotize him. She read type the way other people read scripture: upside down, backwards, without hesitation. The letters faced the wrong way and she never stumbled.

"You're staring," Elias said one night, not unkindly.

"I'm sorry." Bram flinched back toward the shadows. "I'll—"

"Come here, then." Jude didn't look up. "If you're going to watch, you may as well hold something."

She taught him the way she did everything — precisely, without praise, without softness. She put a composing stick in his hands and showed him how the letters sat in it, how the quoins locked them tight, how a line of type was held together by nothing but tension and care.

"Lowercase e," she said, tapping the case. "Most common letter in the language. You'll be reaching for it every third second. Learn where it lives."

"Where does it live?"

"Under your thumb by Friday, if you're any good."

He wasn't any good, at first. His hands shook. He set *Vellmere* as *Veilrnere* twice, and once he dropped a full line of ten-point and scattered letters across the floor like teeth, and went white as paper thinking he'd destroyed something irreplaceable. Elias knelt and gathered them up beside him, sorting by feel in the dark, and said: "Type is patient. It waits for you to learn. That's more than the army ever does."

Jude corrected the boy's grip. She moved his thumb with two fingers, businesslike, and Bram blushed to the ears and stared very hard at the case, and Elias saw all of it and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't make it worse or better, and neither was safe.

By mid-January the boy could set a name — a whole name, rank and regiment and date — without error, at speed, and Jude would take the finished stick from his hands and check it against the ledger slip and say "good," one word, and Elias could see what that single word cost her and gave him both at once.

"He's quick," Elias said later, while Bram slept.

"He's frightened."

"So are we."

"We're frightened of the right things." She banked the stove. "He's frightened of himself. I've seen it before. In men who've done something they can't say out loud."

"You think he's done something?"

"I think everyone in this room has done something." She looked at him across the dying fire, her face half-shadowed, half-lit, and there was no accusation in it — only arithmetic. "That's why they're here."

* * *

The food was the thing that kept Elias awake.

Rations for one man, stretched across four, plus whatever Corrigan's harness work traded in from the lane. Jude walked to market twice a week with a basket and a widow's slouch and bought turnips like a woman who loved turnips, who had always loved turnips, whose entire personality was turnips. She was good at it. She was terrifyingly good at it. Elias watched her lie to shopkeepers with a serenity he found almost frightening, and once, coming home through the snow with a sack of barley she'd traded three hours of nursing for, she caught him watching and said:

"What?"

"Nothing. You're very convincing."

"I've been lying since October." She stamped snow off her boots. "You get fluent."

But fluency didn't fill bins, and by late January the arithmetic turned cruel. Four mouths, one stove, a woodpile measured in days. One night Corrigan came back from the quartermaster's with a face like a closed door and laid two loaves on the table instead of three.

"Tightened up," he said. "Garrison's counting heads now. Anyone drawing bread who isn't on a roll gets reported."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the river made a sound like a door being dragged over stone — the ice settling, shifting, finding its winter voice.

"They're counting heads," Aldous repeated quietly, from his cot. He said it the way soldiers say things — flat, factual, already calculating. "Then we're four names that don't exist, sitting in one building, eating bread that isn't ours."

"We exist," Jude said.

"To whom?" Aldous asked — not cruelly. Genuinely. He wanted an answer the way a man wants a map.

Elias looked around the workshop. At the press under its canvas. At the floorboards hiding two months of duplicated pages. At the boy asleep in the corner with ink still faintly blackening the creases of his knuckles, hands curled like a child's. At Jude, standing with the lamplight behind her, thinner than October, steadier than October, holding the whole fragile arrangement together by will alone.

"To us," he said. "For now, that has to be enough."

* * *

It nearly wasn't, twice.

The first time was a knock at the door on a Tuesday evening — three knocks, official spacing, the knock of a man with authority and cold hands. Everyone in the room froze the way animals freeze. Jude blew out the lamp in one motion, and in the sudden dark Elias heard her move the boys toward the back wall, heard Corrigan's crutch stop clicking, heard her own breathing go shallow and controlled.

The knock came again.

Elias opened the door on the chain. A billeting officer, red-nosed, bored, clipboard under his arm. "Requisitioning survey," the man said. "Spring quarter. This building's on the list for army storage. You'll have notice by thaw."

"Notice of what?"

"Of eviction." The officer peered past him into the dark, sniffed at the smell of ink and woodsmoke and sick men. "You living here alone?"

"Alone," Elias said.

The officer wrote something on his clipboard. Behind Elias, in the black of the workshop, someone coughed — a small, strangled sound, smothered fast — and the pen paused above the page for one endless second.

"Sick?" the officer said.

"My lungs," Elias said. "The ink fumes. You'd want to open a window."

The officer considered this, decided it wasn't worth caring about, and moved on down the lane. Elias closed the door and leaned against it and discovered his heart was going hard enough to hear.

In the dark, Jude's voice, barely a breath: "Thaw."

"I know."

"Sooner than we thought."

"I know."

The second time was smaller and worse. Bram set a page wrong — not the type; the page itself. He pulled a proof of a name and, following habit drilled into every army clerk, dated it. Current date. Garrison format. The kind of date that could tell any investigating officer exactly when and where this press had been running, and for whom.

Jude found it in the drying rack at dawn. She stood over it for a long time, and Elias watched her decide — watched the anger come up in her and get weighed, measured, filed against the boy's shaking hands and hungry eyes and everything else they knew and didn't know about him.

She burned the proof in the stove and said nothing to Bram at all.

But that night she moved the ledger to a new hiding place and didn't tell the boy where, and Elias lay in the dark listening to the river grind its teeth and understood that the workshop had stopped being a refuge and started being a wager. Every mouth was a risk. Every kindness was a bet placed against discovery. And the odds were shortening the way the days were lengthening — slowly, and then all at once.

* * *

Still. There were nights — Elias held onto these — when the wager felt like wealth.

Nights when Bram set type with steady hands and Jude ruled lines by lamplight and Corrigan stitched leather in the corner humming something tuneless and old, and Aldous read aloud from a tattered novel he'd carried since Ypres, one chapter a night, his voice gravelled and unhurried, four men and a woman listening in the dark like children at a fireside. Nights when the press came down clean and the proofs dried by the stove and the names went into the floor two by two, saved, saved, saved.

One such night, near the end of January, Bram finished setting a long column — twelve names, flawless — and looked up at Jude with an expression Elias had learned to read by then: the look of a man about to ask a question he'd been carrying too long.

"Why names?" the boy said. "Why not food, or — papers, or getting people out? Why spend everything on *names*?"

The room went quiet. Even Corrigan's needle paused.

Jude took the finished stick from his hands and checked it, letter by letter, the way she always did. Then she set it on the stone and looked at the boy for a moment before answering.

"Because the army writes men out of existence with a pen," she said. "And a pen can be answered with a pen. Everything else — the bodies, the graves, the war itself — we can't touch any of it. But a name written down and copied and carried is a fact. Facts survive. My brother is dead, and the report says he was never there." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "When this book is read aloud someday, in front of God and everybody, he will have been there. They all will. That's why names."

Bram nodded slowly, and looked down at his ink-stained fingers, and said nothing more.

But Elias, watching him, saw something flicker behind the boy's eyes — not gratitude, not quite. Something closer to recognition. The look of a man hearing a debt described that he already owed somewhere else, to someone else, in a town whose name he hadn't yet spoken.

Outside, the ice boomed once, deep, like a struck bell, and settled.

Elias counted the wood, counted the bread, counted the days until thaw, and did not sleep.
