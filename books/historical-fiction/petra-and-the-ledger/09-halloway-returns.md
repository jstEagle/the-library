## 9. Halloway Returns

The bridge closed on a Tuesday, and Major Edmund Halloway learned of it the way he learned most things — late, and from a man who took pleasure in telling him.

"You missed it by an hour, sir." Sergeant Pell, chewing something that might once have been tobacco. "Ice came down from the north bend like God shoving furniture. Captain ordered the span chained. Won't open till thaw, if then."

Halloway stood at the tollhouse window and watched the river confirm it. The Vellmere had been grey all week; now it was moving, genuinely moving, plates of ice grinding south with a sound like slow applause. On the far bank — the east bank, the workshop bank, the side he'd just crossed *from* — smoke rose thin and domestic from a chimney he knew too well.

He had been on the bridge when the order came. He had turned around. He could have argued, pulled rank, made some staff officer's morning miserable. Instead he had walked back across the planks as they were being chained, and let himself be stranded, and told no one — least of all himself — why.

Now there was no one to tell but Pell.

"Quarter's billeting situation?" Halloway asked.

"Full up at the garrison, sir. Civilians doubled in the wool warehouse. There's rooms above the cooper's on Tanner's Lane, if you don't mind the smell of oakum."

"I mind worse smells professionally."

He took the room above the cooper's. It had one window facing the river, which was the only recommendation it needed.

* * *

The first week he did what stranded officers do: wrote reports nobody would read until spring, inventoried stores that didn't need inventorying, drank tea that tasted of the tin. He walked the quarter each morning because walking was the only discipline left to a man with no command worth the name. And every route he chose, without admitting it to himself, bent eastward. Toward the tannery district. Toward a certain shuttered workshop with a stovepipe that breathed when someone inside was awake.

On the ninth day she found him instead.

He was studying a poster pasted to the grain merchant's wall — requisition notices, the army's handwriting everywhere now, red stamps like wounds — when her voice came from behind him, level and cool as well water.

"They're posting them earlier this year."

He turned. Jude Dunmore stood with a market basket on her arm, scarf wound to her chin, eyes doing the arithmetic of his uniform before his face. He had seen that look before, in men deciding whether an officer was a shield or a threat.

"Miss Dunmore."

"Major." A pause long enough to be a sentence. "I thought you crossed."

"The bridge closed under me." He gestured at the river. "I'm told I live here now. Above the cooper's, if the army wants its officers accounted for."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile. "Oakum," she said. "I'm sorry."

"It builds character."

"Character is the one thing this war hasn't managed to take off anyone yet."

She said it lightly, but he heard the ledger underneath it. She always had that — a second voice running beneath the first, the way a river runs under ice. He had spent two years pretending not to hear it.

"May I carry your basket?" he asked, and immediately regretted the formality of it, the way it sounded like a line from a peacetime he no longer believed in.

"I can carry my own basket, Major."

"I know it."

That surprised her. He saw it land — small, unwelcome, true. She adjusted the basket on her arm and looked back at the posters.

"Requisition orders went up yesterday," she said, not looking at him. "Tannery district. Spring thaw, they say. Everything unofficial burns."

His stomach performed a slow, cold turn. *Everything unofficial.* The words were aimed somewhere specific; she had thrown them where only two people in the town could catch them.

"That's standard," he said carefully. "Paper carries fever. Records carry confusion."

"Records carry people, sometimes. When the official ones won't."

"Jude—"

"Good day, Major." She was already walking, basket swinging, boots printing neat dark holes in the crusted snow. Then she stopped. Did not turn. Spoke to the street ahead of her: "There's a woman on Mill Row whose son died in October. The army says he never existed. She has his letters. She reads them to his chair."

Then she went, and Halloway stood in front of the requisition notices with the red stamps bleeding into his vision, understanding that she knew exactly what he had done last autumn — that he had struck names, signed clean pages, called it order — and that she had decided to say it to him sideways, through a woman and a chair and a basket, because saying it straight would have been mercy.

He deserved less than mercy. He went back to the cooper's and sat with his cold tea until the light died.

* * *

He began appearing where she appeared, and told himself it was patrol routine.

It was not patrol routine. There were eleven streets in the tannery quarter and he learned all of them in a week, along with the hour she drew water, the hour she bought bread, the hour the workshop stovepipe breathed. He learned that Elias — the printer, the deserter, the man whose existence Halloway was officially required to arrest and privately required to protect, since the man's press was the only thing standing between a thousand erased names and oblivion — came out at dusk to barter for lamp oil, always alone, always scanning rooftops first like a man who had taught himself that habit the hard way.

Halloway stood in doorways watching this and conducted, nightly, the argument he'd been conducting since October.

*Report them.* The duty voice. Clean, simple. One dispatch, one provost detail, one fire. And one ledger burned with everything else, and one woman on Mill Row reading letters to a chair forever.

*Don't.* The other voice, newer, hoarser. The one that had started as guilt and was becoming something with more weight and less excuse.

And beneath both voices now, quieter and more dangerous than either: the third one. The one that noticed the exact pitch of her voice saying *good day, Major*, and replayed it after dark like a man rationing something precious.

He had buried a wife. He had buried the version of himself that believed paperwork could be kind. He was forty-six years old and stranded on the wrong side of a frozen river, and he was falling — slowly, unwillingly, with full knowledge of the fall — for a woman who had every reason to despise him.

He brought her firewood. That was how it started. Not flowers, not words. Firewood, stacked outside the workshop door before dawn, because a soldier learns to give supplies and call it nothing.

She caught him at it on the fourth delivery.

"You're leaving wood," she said from the doorway, arms folded against the cold, breath steaming.

"Quarterment issue. Surplus."

"There's no surplus. You signed the forms yourself; I've seen your hand."

He almost laughed. "You've seen my hand?"

"On the October rolls. You strike names very neatly. Small, tight strokes. Like you're trying not to hurt them."

The words went in under his ribs and sat there. Snow ticked off the eaves between them.

"Jude." He set down the last stick. "I want to say something and I want you to let me finish it, because I may only get one attempt."

She waited. She was good at waiting; he'd seen her hold dying men's attention for hours with nothing but her own stillness.

"When I offered you the convoy," he said, "it wasn't kindness. Or it was, but it was kindness wearing armor. I wanted you silent and safe and both for selfish reasons. I struck names, Miss Dunmore. Eleven that I can account for. Probably more that I've stopped counting because counting was the part that hurt. I told myself the reports had to be clean for morale, for the corps, for God knows what. And then a woman with a ledger started asking questions in corridors, and I discovered that being asked is the worst punishment the army ever invented, because the army can't court-martial you for your own conscience."

Her expression didn't change. Her breath did — quicker, white, ragged at the edge.

"So here it is," he went on. "The bridge is closed. The convoy's gone. My offer expired with it, and I find I don't regret that, because the offer was a transaction and what I feel now isn't. I am going to be on this side of the river until thaw whether you want me here or not. And while I'm here, no patrol touches that workshop, no requisition finds those pages, and no report crosses my desk with another struck name. Not because you asked. Because it's mine to do. That's all. Good evening."

He picked up his empty sled rope and turned.

"Halloway."

First time she'd used his name bare. He stopped.

"Why eleven?"

"Because eleven is what fits in a confession. The real number is whatever your ledger says it is. I daren't look."

Behind him, silence, then the sound of the door closing — softly. He stood in the falling snow a moment longer, feeling lighter and more condemned than any inquiry board could make him feel, and walked home through the blue dusk with the rope coiled over his shoulder like a man returning from a war he'd finally admitted he was fighting.

* * *

Winter deepened. The river thickened. And the triangle tightened around its own center like a knot drawn slow.

He couldn't help seeing it — seeing *them*. Coming back from the chandler's at dusk he'd see lamplight move behind the workshop's frosted glass: two shadows, one bent over a table, one working at the great iron press with the economy of a man whose body belonged to his trade. Once, passing the alley, he heard her laugh — actually laugh, short and startled out of her — and something in Halloway's chest closed like a fist around a coal.

He was not a jealous man by nature. He was a fair-minded man, which was worse, because fairness forced him to audit the accounts honestly:

Elias had given her the means of her work. Elias had deserted an army rather than print lies, which put his conscience where Halloway's should have been two years earlier. Elias was her age, or near enough, and shared her air, her risk, her table, her cause. Against that, Halloway could offer protection, honesty arrived late, and firewood.

It was not a close contest, fairly judged. He judged it anyway, nightly, like a man picking a scab.

But then — the strangeness of it — the three of them began, without agreement, to function. He intercepted a provost lieutenant sniffing down Tanner's Lane and redirected him with invented orders. Elias, learning of it from Jude, sent word via the runaway orderly: a printed slip, beautifully set, reading simply *Acknowledged. — E.* No signature needed; the type itself was the signature. And Jude — Jude kept asking him questions, hard ones, about supply routes and inspection schedules, and he answered them all, knowing each answer made him more of a traitor and less able to stop.

One night in late January she came to the cooper's. Knocked at his door past nine, scarf down, snow in her hair, and stood in his narrow room looking at the maps he'd pinned to the wall like a man planning campaigns he had no authority to wage.

"You've marked the patrol routes," she said.

"I've marked the routes they *don't* walk. There's a difference, and it matters to you."

"Why are you doing this? Truly. Not the confession speech — the other reason. The one under it."

He considered lying, found he'd lost the knack somewhere on the bridge in December, and gave her the truth raw.

"Because I erased men and you refused to, and I have decided that whatever remains of me when this war ends will be decided by which of those two things I did more recently."

She looked at him a long moment. Snow melting in her hair, dripping onto his floorboards, marking time.

"He loves you, you know," she said quietly. "Elias. He'd die before saying it. But he watches you come down the lane every morning and he knows what it costs you, and he doesn't hate you for it. That's rarer than love, in my experience. Understanding between rivals."

"And you?" His voice came out lower than intended. "What do you—"

"Don't ask me that tonight." Not cruel. Almost gentle. "Ask me when the ice goes out. If we're all alive when the ice goes out, ask me anything."

She left. He stood at the window and watched her cross the lane, and on the far bank the river groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, ice shifting against ice, the sound of something vast being asked to hold and beginning to wonder if it could.

In the morning there was a new notice on the grain merchant's wall, fresh-pasted, ink barely dry. Not a requisition this time. An order, countersigned at division level, stamped twice in red:

*ALL UNREGISTERED PRESSES AND PRINTED MATTER IN THE OCCUPIED QUARTER TO BE SEIZED AND DESTROYED AT FIRST BREAK-UP OF THE RIVER. PROVOST PATROLS TO COMMENCE SWEEP WITHIN THE FORTNIGHT.*

Below it, smaller, the detail that emptied his lungs: *Informants rewarded.*

Halloway read it twice, tore it from the wall with hands that were not entirely steady, and stood holding the paper while the town walked past him and the river groaned again under its ice, closer now, like a clock winding down.

Thaw patrols. Within the fortnight. And he had promised a woman with snow in her hair that no patrol would touch that workshop — a promise the paper in his fist had just made a lie.

He crushed the notice in his pocket and turned toward Tanner's Lane at a pace no officer on routine patrol should ever manage.
