## 10. Copying by Lamplight

The lamp was the only honest thing in the workshop after dark.

Jude thought this most nights now, sitting at the long bench with the ledger open before her and the brass lamp turned low enough to hide the glow from the window but high enough to read by. Everything else lied a little — the crates of type blocks that were really a grave of hidden names, the convalescent cots along the north wall where two wounded men slept under the fiction of being nobody's soldiers, the orderly Bram who swept the floor and said almost nothing and watched everything. But the lamp told the truth about what they did. It burned whale oil they could not afford and it lit the names anyway, one line at a time, because that was what light was for.

"Read it back," Elias said from the press.

"Dunmore, J." She paused. "No — that's me. Corrigan, Ambrose, private, second battalion, left leg taken at the millrace, died the fourth."

"Corrigan goes on plate four," Elias said. "I set him last week. You're transcribing him twice."

She checked. He was right; she'd copied Corrigan onto page thirty-one already, in her own hand, and here he was again in her hand on page thirty-four, entered from Halloway's list rather than her original ledger. Two lists feeding into one another like tributaries. "He deserves to be written twice," she said. "Once for each report that lost him."

Elias didn't laugh, exactly, but something moved through his shoulders that was the shape of laughing, held down so it wouldn't wake the sleepers. He went back to the type case, sliding letters into the composing stick with those quick blind fingers of his, and the little iron clicks joined the tick of the lamp and the far-off groan of the river ice, and that was the whole music of their nights.

Three weeks since the bridge closed. Three weeks since she'd stood at the railing and watched Major Halloway's convoy shrink to a gray smudge on the far bank, taking with it every easy way out of Vellmere. She had stopped regretting staying somewhere around the second week. Not because the work had gotten easier — it hadn't, it had only gotten longer — but because she had understood, somewhere in the middle of a night much like this one, that the transcription wasn't grief anymore. It had become craft. And craft was the thing she had instead of hope.

"How many left?" Elias asked without looking up.

"Forty-one names to copy from Halloway's list. Then the merge is done." She wet her pen. The ink had gone thick in the cold and she had to hold the bottle against the lamp housing to warm it, which meant the light dimmed, which meant the room leaned in close around her, all shadows and iron and the smell of oil and paper. "Then we start the duplicate run properly. Six copies if the paper holds."

"The paper won't hold."

"I know."

They had known for days. The salvage ledgers they'd pulled from the drowned depot at Millbrook bend — blank regimental account books, water-stained at the edges but sound at the spine — had seemed like a miracle when Elias hauled the crate home across the ice, breathing steam like a locomotive, grinning like a man who'd robbed God. Eleven blank ledgers. Enough for six full copies of the merged roll with pages to spare.

But paper lied too. Damp got into a book's heart even when the cover looked dry, and the first three copies had come off the press beautiful and then curled overnight as the sheets dried unevenly, the ink feathering at the edges of the letters until some of the names looked like they were drowning all over again. Elias had cursed with a fluency she'd never heard from him, then gone quiet, then spent a whole afternoon stacking the ruined copies under weights with blotting sand, trying to press the warp back out. Half of it stayed warped.

"We'll need more," she said. "Before the thaw. Before they come."

"There's no more at Millbrook. I took everything that would come out of the water."

"There's the quartermaster store behind the chapel."

Elias set the composing stick down. In the lamplight his face did something complicated — not fear exactly, though fear lived in that face the way the river lived under the ice, always present, mostly still. "The chapel quarter is patrolled. And the store is Provost inventory. That's not salvaging, Jude. That's theft with extra steps."

"It's theft with the same steps," she said. "Everything we've done is theft. We stole these men back from the army's arithmetic. One more box of paper doesn't change what we are."

"And what are we?"

She looked at him across the workshop, across the sleeping forms of Sergeant Iles and young Prewitt, across Bram's neatly folded blanket on his cot where he lay either asleep or performing sleep with the diligence he brought to everything. What are we. Deserter and forger, keeper and thief, a woman who had made a religion out of other people's names.

"Careful," she said finally. "We're careful. That has to be enough."

* * *

They went to the chapel store on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays the patrol crossed to the eastern quarter at dusk and didn't come back until the nine o'clock gun, and because Elias said Tuesday was the day the chaplain's assistant drank himself past noticing anything.

The river road was blue with evening and hard as iron underfoot. They walked apart, ten paces between them, the way people walked who had nothing to do with one another, and Jude kept her hands in her pockets around the canvas sack and thought about how strange it was that the most criminal hour of her life felt like an errand. Behind the chapel the supply shed squatted under snow, its door held by a padlock that had frozen sometime in December and never recovered.

Elias worked it with a flatiron bar while she stood at the corner and watched the road. The lock gave with a crack like a knuckle. Inside, the dark smelled of rope and creosote and cold stone, and there, stacked against the wall under tarpaulin, were the ledgers — two crates of them, regimental issue, blank as snowfields.

"Quartermaster's been ordering double since November," Elias murmured, hauling the first crate out. "Somebody's been selling the surplus downriver. We're not stealing from the army. We're stealing from a thief. Practically charity work."

"You're a very moral criminal."

"I'm a printer. Morality is a font you cast yourself." He grunted, shifted the crate onto the sledge he'd brought. "Help me with the second one. Mind the ice on the step."

Between the chapel and the road, crossing the open ground where the snow lay unbroken, Jude slipped. Not badly — one boot skidding, one arm windmilling, catching herself on the sledge rope before the crate could go over. But the noise she made was enormous in the silence, a short sharp cry, and she froze afterward in the attitude of a startled animal, listening.

Nothing. Wind in the chapel eaves. The river muttering under its lid of ice.

Then, faintly, from the direction of the chapel's south wall: footsteps stopping. Starting again. Stopping.

Elias had gone absolutely still beside her, one hand on the crate. His breath came out in slow controlled ribbons. Ten seconds passed. Twenty. Then the footsteps resumed, unhurried, moving away toward the officers' billets, and whatever sentry or insomniac or drunk chaplain's assistant had heard a woman slip on the ice decided it was nothing worth investigating.

"Tuesday," Elias whispered. "Tuesdays are getting shorter."

They hauled the crates home by the longest route, through the tannery lane where the smell covered everything, and by the time they reached the workshop Jude's heart had settled back into its working rhythm and her hands had stopped shaking, and she found — standing in the doorway watching Elias stack the new paper beside the press — that she was almost happy. It was an obscene feeling and she examined it honestly, the way she tried to examine everything: here was a woman committing small crimes in a war, risking a hanging or a firing squad over blank account books, and what she felt was the particular warmth of shared labor with a man whose hands she trusted. The war had pared life down to such a fine point that a crate of stolen paper glowed like treasure.

"You're smiling at stationery," Elias said.

"I'm smiling at arithmetic. Forty-one names, six copies, eleven new ledgers. For the first time the sums come out on our side."

He came and stood beside her, not touching, but near enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him the way heat comes off a press after a long run. "You know what my master used to say? Back when I was apprenticed, before the war, before any of it." He picked up a sheet of the salvaged paper and held it to the lamp, checking the grain the way other men checked horses' teeth. "He said a printer never owns his work. He just keeps it alive long enough to be useful, and then he lets it go out into the world and it isn't his anymore."

"Is that how you think of the ledger?"

"No." He set the sheet down. "The ledger thinks of itself. We're just its hands."

* * *

The sessions settled into a ritual with the precision of a liturgy, though neither of them named it that.

At eight, after the sleepers had their ration of broth and their lamps were out, Jude took the long bench. Elias set type from her dictation — name, rank, unit, wound, date — pulling letters from the cases with the economy of a man dealing cards. At nine she read the finished lines back against the original while he locked the chase into the press bed. At half past he pulled the proofs, twelve sheets a night on the good nights, and hung them from the drying lines strung above the type racks like strange laundry, rows of names fluttering faintly in the draught from the bad window.

It was during the reading-backs that they talked. Not about important things — about flour, about whether Prewitt's cough was worsening, about the price of candle wax in a town that no longer priced anything honestly. But underneath the small talk ran the larger current, the one that needed no words: two people bent over the same evidence of the same dead, night after night, building the same stubborn monument with their four hands.

One night in the third week of February she caught herself watching him instead of the page. His hands were wrapped in rag strips against the cold — the frostbite from the depot had healed badly in two fingers, and the joints swelled in the evenings — and still they moved through the type case without fumbling, finding the letters by touch, by memory, by love. She had known men who talked about devotion. She had never seen a man practice it at this scale, letter by letter, hour by hour, for strangers.

"You're staring," he said, not looking up.

"You're slow tonight."

"I'm careful tonight. There's a difference, and the difference matters, because if I set a name wrong it stays wrong forever. There's no second printing for a dead man's spelling." He slotted an *m* into place. "My master said that too."

"Your master said a great deal."

"He did. Mostly he drank, but between drinks he was right." A pause, the click of type. "Jude."

"Yes."

"Why did you start? Truly. I've never asked and you've never said, and I've decided that not-asking is its own kind of cowardice."

The lamp ticked. On the drying lines overhead, the night's first sheets stirred. She thought about Thomas, about the advance at Harrow Field, about the telegram that had said *wounded* and the report that had said nothing at all, and she found the words were easier now than they had been in the autumn, worn smooth by handling.

"Because silence is a decision," she said. "Every omitted name is a decision somebody made, at a desk, with a pen, probably tired, probably meaning to fix it later. And later never comes, because the next report is due. So the man just — stops existing officially. His mother gets a rumor instead of a truth. His grave gets a number instead of a name." She turned back to her page. "I couldn't make the reports stop lying. But I could keep a second set of books. Somebody has to keep the second set of books, Elias. In every country, in every war, forever. Somebody has to be the one writing down what everyone agreed to forget."

The press stood silent. When she glanced over, Elias had stopped setting type and was looking at her with an expression she'd seen once before, on the night he'd told her about the falsified rolls — the look of a man recognizing his own face in someone else's story.

"That's why your handwriting changes," he said quietly. "On the early pages. The first twenty entries, the script gets heavier toward the bottom of each page. Like you were pressing harder."

"I was angry."

"And now?"

"Now I'm just a clerk," she said. "Anger doesn't survive forty-one names a night. Something steadier does."

He came around the bench then, and stood behind her chair, and after a moment his hand came to rest on her shoulder — lightly, the way you'd rest a hand on a machine you respected, mindful of its workings. Neither of them said anything. The lamp burned. Somewhere below the floorboards the river went on talking to itself in its frozen language, patient, immense, keeping its own count of the season. She let her head tilt back until it touched his chest, and they stayed like that through the length of one entire minute, which in the winter of 1917 in Vellmere was a form of extravagance no one could have taxed them for.

Then the sheets needed turning, and the moment folded itself away into the ordinary fabric of the night, which was, Jude understood, where the true moments always went. Not into declarations. Into work shared.

* * *

The trouble announced itself on a Thursday, small at first, the way all their troubles did.

Bram had taken to sitting up during the transcription sessions — ostensibly mending boots, actually doing nothing, his needle parked in the leather while he watched the press with an attention he never turned on anything else. Jude had noticed it the way you notice weather: gradually, then all at once. He watched Elias's hands. He watched the type cases. Most particularly, he watched the shelf where the finished copies were stacked under oilcloth, and his eyes followed every sheet from press to line to stack with the fixed tracking gaze of a cat at a mousehole.

"He counts them," Elias said, low, on Thursday night, pulling the last proof of the evening. "Our orderly. I've watched him. Every time I move a copy, his lips move. He's counting."

"He's bored."

"Bored men look at the fire. He looks at the paper."

Jude laid down her pen. Across the room, Bram blew out his candle with great deliberation and lay down, arranging his blanket in the exact folds he arranged it in every night, a man performing innocence with military thoroughness. She thought of the chapel steps, the footsteps that had stopped and started. She thought of nothing else for a long moment.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we move the finished copies. All of them. Out of the shelf, into the floor cache under the third flagstone, and we put the spoiled sheets in the oilcloth where the good ones were. If anyone's counting, let them count wreckage."

Elias nodded slowly. But as they worked by lamplight — prying the flagstone, lowering the bundled copies into the cold earth-smelling dark beneath — Jude caught herself listening to the room behind them, to the soft deliberate sounds of a man pretending to sleep, and she understood that the winter had just grown a new tooth. The river was not the only thing under the ice, waiting for the thaw.

Outside, far off, the ice boomed once — a deep report rolling up the valley like distant artillery — and neither of them spoke, because they both knew what it meant. The river had begun counting down.
