## 11. The Orderly's Confession

Bram counted the ways out of the workshop every night before he slept. There were two. The front door, which groaned on its hinge and could be heard from the loft where Elias slept above the press. And the coal hatch at the back, which opened onto the frozen yard and the alley beyond, and which Bram had oiled himself with a scrap of tallow so it would make no sound at all.

He had not used either door. Not yet. But he counted them anyway, the way a man counts his sins — not because counting changes anything, but because the counting is the only penance left when you know forgiveness is not coming.

The cold had come down hard that week. It came down like a judgment, the way cold does in Vellmere in deep winter, settling into the mortar of the walls and staying there, so that the stove worked all day and accomplished nothing and the water bucket grew a skin of ice overnight no matter how close they kept it to the fire. Frost climbed the inside of the workshop windows in ferns and feathers. When you breathed near the glass your breath turned to rime on the *inside*, as if the room itself were exhaling winter back at you.

Bram lay in his cot by the type cabinet with his blanket up to his chin and listened to the building talk. Old buildings talk in the cold. Timbers contract with small reports like distant rifle fire. The press — Elias's press, the great iron Heidelberg that squatted in the middle of the floor like a sleeping animal — ticked and settled and ticked again. Somewhere above, Elias turned over in his loft bed and the boards complained under him.

And across the room, in the corner they had curtained off with sailcloth for the convalescents, Corporal Fenwick coughed the wet, tearing cough that meant the fluid was rising again, higher than last week, and no one in the workshop said so out loud because saying so would make it true.

Three convalescents now. A runaway orderly. A deserter-printer. A woman who wrote down dead men's names.

*What did you think would happen,* Bram asked himself, staring at the ceiling beams, *when you walked into the warmest lie in Vellmere?*

He knew what he'd thought. He'd thought: here is a place where nobody asks questions. Here is a place with food and a stove and people too busy hiding their own secrets to dig into mine. Here is a place to wait out the war the way a rat waits out a flood — high up, dry, alive.

He hadn't counted on Jude Dunmore looking at him across the lamplight every evening with those level gray eyes and asking nothing at all, which was worse than asking everything. He hadn't counted on her teaching him to set type, letter by letter, her fingers correcting his on the composing stick, patient as a schoolmistress, until he could build a line of eight-point body without a single letter backward. He hadn't counted on the way it felt — setting type. The way the little blocks of lead clicked into place and made a word, and the word was real and held still, and holding still felt like something Bram Quill hadn't done in two years of war.

Most of all, he hadn't counted on the guilt arriving with the cold.

But it had. It came the night the temperature dropped so far the river ice boomed like a struck bell, and Bram woke from a dream of another town, another workshop, another printer whose name he had never learned until after.

* * *

His name had been Aldous Renn. That much Bram knew, though it had taken him three weeks and a bribe of tobacco to learn even that much, afterward, when knowing was useless.

Two winters ago, or nearly — November of '15 — Bram had been posted to the garrison hospital at Coldharbour, sixty miles upriver, a town so small the maps spelled it wrong. He had been nineteen years old and four months into his service and already he understood that the army ran on paper the way a mill runs on water, and that whoever fed the paper could grind whatever they liked.

Aldous Renn had run a print shop on Tanner Street. A jobbing printer — handbills, wedding notices, price lists for the fishmongers. And then, when the casualty lists began coming through Coldharbour, Renn had done a thing that seemed small and decent at the time: he printed the lists *complete*. Not the abridged versions the garrison released for public consumption, with their tidy omissions and their merciful silences. The full lists, including the names of men officially recorded as *missing — returned to duty* who were in fact missing in the permanent sense, lying in the mud east of the ridge where the advance had stalled. Families came to Tanner Street on printing days the way they once came to church. Renn set the names by hand, Bram later learned, exactly the way Jude set hers, and locked the shop door behind him while he worked, and printed fifty copies, and gave them away.

Bram never met him. That was the thing that sat in Bram's chest like a swallowed stone: he had never once spoken to Aldous Renn. He had only seen him through a window on a Tuesday afternoon, a round-shouldered man in a leather apron bent over a case of type, and thought, idly, *that must be quiet work, that must be a soft life.*

That same week, a captain from intelligence had come to the garrison hospital asking questions. Casualty morale, he called it. Seditious publication, he called it when pressed. He wanted to know who was distributing unauthorized lists. He offered cigarettes to orderlies who talked. Bram had taken the cigarettes — taken them without even thinking, the way you take anything free in an army — and then, because taking them made him look like a man with information, he had said:

"There's a printer on Tanner Street. I've seen him copying the official rolls."

He had said it lightly. That was the detail that woke him at night now, in this other workshop, beside this other press: how *lightly* he had said it. A remark tossed off between tasks. He hadn't known what it meant. He had been four months in uniform and he still believed that words were just words, that the army would do what was reasonable, that a printer printing names of dead men was at worst a nuisance and at best a public service.

Eleven days later the military police came to Tanner Street in the dark. They took the press apart with crowbars — Bram saw the wagonload of wreckage pass the hospital gates the next morning, cases of type spilled across the cobbles like teeth knocked from a jaw — and they took Aldous Renn too. Where, Bram never learned. There were places men were taken, in that phase of the war, from which the paperwork came back stamped in ways that ended careers if you asked about them twice.

Renn's wife stood outside the wrecked shop for three days. Bram passed her twice. She didn't weep. She just stood there, a small woman in a black coat, looking at the empty window where the printer used to bend over his type, and Bram had crossed the street to avoid her eyes, and had told himself for a year that she had never known who talked, and for another year that she had known but forgiven him, and finally, this winter, in this workshop, under this cold, he had stopped telling himself anything at all.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that the frost ferns on the window and the booming of the river ice had carved into him these past weeks: Aldous Renn's shop had looked like this one. The same iron smell. The same tick and settle of a press in the night. The same lamplight through frosted glass, the same hush of a man alone with letters, making something true.

Jude's ledger was Renn's list. Elias's press was Renn's press. This workshop was Tanner Street, rebuilt, and Bram Quill had already burned one Tanner Street down, and he was living in the second one eating its bread and warming his hands at its stove and learning its craft with its owner's fingers guiding his.

* * *

It was the coughing that broke him, in the end. Not the guilt alone — guilt he could carry; he'd carried it two years, packed it down tight like a mule's load — but the coughing, and what the coughing meant.

Corporal Fenwick had come to them in December with a shrapnel wound in the thigh that should have killed him and instead decided to kill him slowly, by way of his lungs, through the fever and the bed-sweats and now this wet, rising cough. Elias and Jude had taken him in off the road themselves, against every instinct of self-preservation, because he had collapsed at the woodpile of the chandler's next door and there was nowhere else. The third convalescent, young Sergeant Iles with his bandaged hand, had been Elias's find, traded for two reams of salvaged paper from a quartermaster who preferred not to ask.

They fed them. They bled the stove dry keeping them warm. Jude sat with Fenwick through the worst nights, propping him upright so the fluid wouldn't drown him, and read him the shipping news from an old newspaper because Fenwick had been a merchant sailor and ships calmed him. And Fenwick was dying anyway, slowly, in the sailcloth corner, and the medicine that might slow it — the sulfurous tablets, the cod-liver oil, the proper dressings — was across the river, behind the closed bridge, in the garrison hospitals that took soldiers with numbers and returned families with euphemisms.

Every tablet Jude couldn't get, every dressing she improvised from boiled linen, every hour she sat up with him — Bram watched it all and did the arithmetic he could not stop doing: *this is what my sentence bought. This warmth. These borrowed hours. And I am spending them under a false name.*

For he had given them a false name. Of course he had. "Bram Weller," he'd said, when he came in off the road in October with a sprained ankle and a story about being detached from his unit. Weller. A name from a signboard he'd once slept beneath. And Jude had written it in the margin of some working note — he'd seen it, *B.W., strong back, can lift the stone* — and had believed it, because she believed people, that was her ruin and her glory, she believed people the way other women believed in God.

The confession assembled itself in him the way a line of type assembles: one piece of lead at a time, each one cold and hard and final, until the whole thing stood solid in his chest and could be lifted.

He chose the evening. He reasoned about the evening the way he reasoned about everything now, carefully, fearfully: evenings were when the whole household was together, when the stove was fed and the lamps lit and the day's work done. If he confessed in the evening, in front of everyone, then no one person could decide alone to turn him out — or worse, quietly, to inform on him first, the way he had informed. Confession in daylight hours could end with him walking out the coal hatch into the dark and the patrol finding him by morning. Confession in front of witnesses was safer. Probably. He had stopped trusting *probably*, but it was the only currency he had.

He waited until Jude finished reading the shipping news to Fenwick. Until Elias wiped the press down and covered it with its canvas — a ritual Bram had watched forty nights running, always performed with the tenderness of a man covering a horse for the night. Until Iles banked the stove and the light went orange and low.

Then Bram stood up from his cot, and his legs shook, and he was ashamed of how they shook, and he said:

"I need to tell you all something. And then you'll want me gone, and you'd be right to, and I won't argue."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the stove to pop twice.

Elias straightened up slowly from the covered press, and Bram watched the man's face do something complicated — not surprise; recognition, almost, the face of a man watching weather he had predicted arrive. Jude set down the newspaper. Her hands were absolutely still in her lap, which frightened Bram more than shouting would have. He had seen that stillness before, on officers receiving bad news. Stillness was what discipline looked like on civilians.

"Fenwick's asleep," Iles said quietly, glancing toward the curtain. "Iles means — keep your voice down." His own voice cracked on it.

"I'll keep it down," Bram said. His mouth had gone dry as type metal. "You should hear it down, anyway. Because if anyone hears it, it shouldn't be me deciding that."

"Say it," Elias said. No heat in it. Just flatness, and under the flatness, something Bram recognized because he heard it in his own head every night: a man bracing.

So Bram told it. He told it badly and completely, the way confessions go — circling back, doubling over details, unable to leave things out and unable to say the important ones plainly. He told them about Coldharbour, about the garrison, about the captain from intelligence and the open cigarette tin on the desk. He told them about seeing the printer through the window and thinking *quiet work, soft life.* He told them what he had said — he made himself repeat it verbatim, standing in the orange lamplight like a schoolboy at recitation — *"There's a printer on Tanner Street. I've seen him copying the official rolls"* — and he watched Jude's face as he said it and saw her go whiter than Fenwick, whiter than the frost on the windows.

"He printed the full lists," Bram said. "Like yours. Like yours exactly, ma'am. Full names, no omissions. Families came to him. And I —" His voice failed and he pushed through it the way you push through a river ford, because stopping meant drowning. "I told the captain where to find him. Eleven days later they broke his press with crowbars and took him away and I never learned where. I bribed a man with tobacco just to learn his name. Aldous Renn. Two years I've known his name and nothing else, and I have eaten your bread for three months and learned your trade at your elbow and let you call me Weller, and I am not Weller. My name is Bram Quill, orderly, fourth field ambulance, and I informed on a printer, and every warm night I've spent under this roof has been theft."

He stopped. There was no more. There was never any more; that was the whole of him, poured out, and the dregs were shame.

Jude rose. Bram flinched — full-body, involuntary, the flinch of a man expecting a blow — and hated himself for the flinch, and she saw it, and something moved through her face that he couldn't read, grief or fury or both braided together.

"You knew his name," she said. Her voice was low and even. "Aldous Renn. You've carried it two years."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't *ma'am* me." But there was no cruelty in it. She came closer, close enough that he could see her eyes were bright and wet and furious. "Do you know what I would give? For one name. One confirmed name, of a man my ledger says died at the ridge — for someone, anyone, to stand in front of me and say, *he existed, I saw him, here is what he looked like through a window on a Tuesday.* You're standing there confessing a crime and you don't even see what you're holding. You're the only living witness that Aldous Renn ever printed those lists. That matters. That matters more than your guilt does."

"That's not —" Bram's voice cracked. "Ma'am. Miss. I didn't come here to trade on it. I came here because —"

"Because you couldn't carry it anymore," Elias said.

Everyone turned. Elias had not moved from the press. He stood with one hand flat on the canvas cover, and the lamplight caught the ink stains on his knuckles, the stains that never fully washed, that he wore like a tradesman wears calluses.

"I deserted," Elias said, "because they ordered me to print falsified rolls. Do you understand what that means, Quill? They put the corrected copy on my stone and told me to set it, and the corrections were lies, and I set them. I stood at that case and built the lies letter by letter, and pulled the lever, and sent them out. Four hundred impressions of a lie, and then I walked out of my own shop and left my name on the door." He lifted his hand off the canvas. "So don't stand there waiting for us to cast you out. There isn't a soul in this room clean enough to throw the first stone, and the two of us —" he nodded at Jude — "have already agreed on that."

"The difference," Iles said from the stove, and his young face was hard, harder than his years, "is that you got out before it cost anyone. He talked *after.* Talking after gets printers taken away in the night." He jabbed a finger at the wall, the direction of the river, the world of patrols and provosts beyond it. "There are men in this quarter right now who'd sell this workshop for a warm billet. We've been lucky. Luck runs out. And now we learn the luck has a hole in it that walks and eats and sleeps ten feet from the ledger."

The word landed in the room like a dropped tool.

*The ledger.* Bram watched Jude's hand move, small and fast, toward the drawer where the oilcloth bundle lived during the days — an unconscious motion, a mother's hand toward a cradle — and he understood in one cold flash that the crack Iles had named was real, that trust in this room had just taken a structural hit that might not show until the roof came down.

"I never saw it," Bram said quickly. "I never touched it. I swear on —"

"Don't swear," Jude said. "Swearing is what got Renn taken."

The silence after that was terrible. Outside, the river boomed again, long and low, the ice speaking in its sleep, and everyone in the room glanced at the windows at once, the way soldiers glance at the horizon at artillery sound.

Jude looked at Bram for a long time. He bore it. It was the least he could bear.

"Bram Quill," she said at last, testing the name, weighing it. "Fourth field ambulance. You'll write it down for me. Your unit, your dates, the captain's name if you ever learned it, the street, the week. Everything you told me tonight, in writing, signed. If it's ever needed — if Renn's family ever comes looking, if there's ever an accounting — there will be a record with your name on it, in your hand, saying what you did and what became of him. You don't get to confess and dissolve. You'll stand in the ledger yourself."

"Yes," Bram said. It was more mercy than he deserved and less than he feared, and he took it with both hands. "Yes. Tonight. I'll set it myself, if you'll let me. In type. So it's —" He gestured helplessly at the press, the cases, the whole craft he'd been learning at their elbows. "So it's real."

Something flickered across Jude's face at that — pain, he thought, and maybe the ghost of forgiveness, though he wouldn't presume to name it.

"It stays in the case," Elias said. "Locked. Nobody prints it. Not yet." He looked around the room, at each of them, ending on Iles. "And here's how it goes tonight, since we're all confessing the weather: nobody leaves this workshop before thaw. Nobody. Not to the chandler's, not to the well, not for cigarettes, not for love. The cold has everyone in this quarter shut up behind their doors and paranoid as cats, and paranoid men report things, and reporting things is how printers die. We keep the stove fed. We keep Fenwick breathing. We keep our heads down until the ice breaks. Agreed?"

Iles held his stare a moment, then shrugged, which in sergeant's language was assent. Fenwick coughed behind the curtain, twice, wetly, and everyone's eyes went there and softened, and the anger in the room lost a degree it could not recover.

Only Jude stayed hard. She looked at Bram a moment longer — measuring him, he thought, the way she measured type, checking each letter for cracks — and then she went to the drawer, took out paper and pen, and set them on the table in front of him.

"Write," she said. "Everything. Leave nothing out, especially the parts that shame you. Those are the parts that prove it happened."

Bram sat down. The pen scratched. Outside, the river boomed again — longer this time, deeper, a sound with a crack buried in the middle of it, and Elias lifted his head from the press and listened with his whole body, and Bram, writing his sins in a dead printer's trade, thought: *the ice is talking. And something out there has started to answer.*

He did not yet know that within the week, one page of everything he was writing would be gone.
