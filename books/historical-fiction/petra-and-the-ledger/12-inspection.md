## 12. Inspection

The knock came at half past four in the afternoon, and it was wrong.

Not the knock itself — three raps, knuckle on doorframe, ordinary enough — but the rhythm of it. Soldiers knocked like they were paying a debt, quick and apologetic. Provosts knocked like they owned the door and everything behind it. This was the second kind.

Jude had the ledger open on the worktable when she heard it, pen still wet with Corporal Amsel's name — *Amsel, R., left foot gone below the knee, fever day nine* — and for one long heartbeat she did not move. The ink shone. Outside, someone was crossing the yard with the particular heavy stride of a man wearing an officer's greatcoat who wanted you to hear him coming.

"Bram," she said, low, not looking up. "Bram — the back."

Bram was already moving. That was the thing about Bram she could never quite reconcile: a man who had informed on a printer in Marrowfield, who had sat across from Elias for two months eating their bread, who had confessed all of it one gray night by lamplight and then wept into his sleeves like a boy — that man moved fast when it mattered. He came around the table, took the ledger from under her hands without asking, and folded it into the oilcloth they kept ready now, always, since the confession, since trust had become something they maintained rather than assumed.

"Where?" he whispered.

She thought fast. Under the floorboards — no, the provosts knew about floorboards; they'd pulled up half of Vellmere's floorboards in November. In the crate of type with Elias's false bottom — too obvious, too clever, the first place a printer-searching officer would look. The chimney flue — too narrow for anything but ash.

Then she heard the cough from the far corner, wet and rattling, and she knew.

"Private Dole," she said.

Bram looked at her like she'd lost her mind. Private Dole was dying. He had been dying for six days, patiently, the way some men die — not dramatically, not with hemorrhage or collapse, just steadily going out like a lamp turned down notch by notch. He lay on the cot nearest the wall, under a blanket gone gray with use, breathing in a rhythm everyone in the workshop had learned to read the way farmers read weather.

"He can't have anything under his cot," Bram whispered. "They'll strip the room."

"They'll strip the room anyway." She was already moving, taking the oilcloth bundle from him. "But they'll be careful around a dying man. They won't want to touch him. They won't want to look at him too long." She crouched at the cot's edge. "Dole," she said gently. "Dole, I need your help."

His eyes opened. They were the eyes of a man who had already made most of his arrangements with the world — clear, unhurried, faintly amused. Nineteen years old. He'd been nineteen for eleven days; they'd marked it with watered tea because there was nothing else.

"You're going to hide something under my bed," he said. His voice was a thread.

"Yes."

"They'll think I'm smuggling letters." Almost a smile. "Or brandy. They'll be disappointed when it's only paper."

"Dole—"

"It's all right." He shifted — a terrible effort, she could see what it cost him — and lifted the edge of his blanket. Beneath the cot was darkness and dust and the smell of straw. She slid the bundle flat against the underside of the frame, pressed it into the webbing where the canvas sagged away from the slats, and worked it deep until her fingers found the gap between the cross-brace and the leg. It fit. Barely. A rectangle of shadow among shadows.

"If they find it—" she began.

"Then I'll say it's mine." He closed his eyes again, as if the conversation had tired him past continuing, which it probably had. "A dying man's entitled to keep a Bible or a love letter. Tell them it's a love letter. They'll believe that. Nobody writes love letters anymore, so they'll want to believe it."

From the front room: the door opening. Cold air. Boot heels on the boards, unhurried, plural.

Jude stood, wiped her hands down her apron, and became a nurse.

* * *

There were five of them.

A provost captain named Verlach, whom she knew by reputation if not face — a narrow man with a narrow mustache and the habit of standing slightly closer to people than politeness allowed. Two corporals with bayonets fixed, which was theater, since the workshop contained two convalescents, a runaway orderly, a woman, and a press. A sergeant carrying a clipboard and a candle lantern, though it was barely dusk. And a fifth man who stayed near the door and wrote nothing and said nothing and watched everything, and whose presence frightened Jude more than the captain's, because men who watch without writing are not collecting information. They are confirming suspicions they already hold.

"Garrison quarter seven," Verlach read from a card, as if she couldn't see the painted number above the door. "Registered as a convalescent station, auxiliary. You are—"

"Nurse Dunmore. Volunteer attachment."

"There is no volunteer attachment registered to this quarter."

"No," Jude agreed. "There isn't. There's me."

Something flickered in his face — irritation, or its cousin, the desire to punish honesty and finding no clean instrument for it. Behind him, in the yard, she could see the other teams working down the row: doors open, contents in the snow, a mattress slashed open with a bayonet and the straw scattered like entrails. Somewhere down the street a woman was crying in a way that suggested she would not stop soon.

"You'll open everything," Verlach said. "Cupboards, crates, bedding. If you have a cellar, the cellar. If you have a loft, the loft."

"We have neither."

"Then this will be brief." He stepped inside, and his boots brought the cold in with them.

Jude positioned herself the way she'd learned to position herself in the field hospital at the start of all this — between the officer and whatever he was about to damage. Not physically. Just present, just useful, just a woman with a basin and a cloth who kept needing to pass through whatever space he occupied. It was a dance she'd invented out of desperation in October and refined since, and it worked like this: you never obstructed, never argued, never gave them the satisfaction of resistance. You simply made yourself necessary, over and over, until the search bent around you like water around a stone.

"The far cot is private typhoid-suspicion," she said, nodding at Dole without looking at Dole. "I'd ask your men to wash before and after. We've no carbolic to spare."

Verlach glanced at the cot. At the boy in it. Something crossed his face that might once, in another life, have been conscience. "Sergeant. The wall side. Don't disturb the patient."

The sergeant went to the wall side.

Which meant, Jude realized with a coldness that started in her scalp and ran downward, that he would be standing directly over the ledger. Kneeling, possibly. Reaching under the frame to test the slats, exactly as any competent searcher would, exactly as she would do herself if the positions were reversed — and she was not searching for a hidden ledger right now only because she already knew where it was.

She needed to be in that corner.

"Triage," she said aloud, to no one, and moved.

Dole's fever had climbed through the afternoon — she'd known it, had been watching it climb the way you watch a river rise — and now she used it. She knelt at the head of the cot, took his wrist with professional authority, counted a pulse that was indeed racing, and eased him half onto his side with the practiced motions of a nurse turning a patient. Which put her body between the sergeant and the cot's underside. Which put her hands on the frame, steadying it, feeling through her palms whether the oilcloth bundle had shifted. It hadn't. The canvas webbing held it snug against the brace, invisible unless a man lay on the frozen ground and put his eye to the gap.

"Sergeant," she said, without turning. "If you're going to lift that frame, lift the foot end. The head end's where I've got his drainage line rigged, and if you pull it loose I'll need to re-rig it, and I haven't the tubing to spare, and he hasn't the night to spare either."

It was a lie built of true things — there was no drainage line, but there was no tubing anywhere in the building, and every man in that room knew what a night without intervention meant for a fever like Dole's. The sergeant hesitated. Men hesitated around dying the way horses hesitated around fire: not from cowardice, from something older than training.

He searched the foot end instead. Ran his hands along the legs, tipped the frame two inches off the floor and let it drop, swept the straw beneath with a bayonet point. The blade passed eight inches from the oilcloth. Jude felt the distance the way you feel a bullet go past — not hearing it, feeling the air of it.

"Clear," the sergeant said.

* * *

They took apart the rest of the workshop with methodical boredom.

The crate of type blocks came up first, of course. Verlach himself pried at the false bottom Elias had built into it, found it, stared at the empty cavity beneath with the expression of a man cheated of a discovery, and then had two corporals carry the entire crate into the yard and dump it. Four hundred pounds of lead type rang and clattered across the frozen mud like church bells falling downstairs. Jude heard Elias's nameless press-boys' hours in that sound — every letter sorted, every case composed — and kept her face still by thinking about pulse rates.

Elias wasn't there. He'd taken the delivery cart out at noon to fetch lamp oil from the chandler on Mill Lane, a trip that normally took forty minutes and today, by luck or by instinct she'd never be able to prove, had stretched to the whole grey afternoon. She caught herself praying — not formally, she didn't pray formally, but in the wordless bargaining way — *let him stay out, let the roads be bad, let him take the long way home.*

Verlach opened her medicine chest and read labels. He flipped through the blank pages of the salvage ledgers from the drowned depot — water-stained, mildewed, worthless-looking, and therefore perfect; she blessed the mildew silently. He examined the photograph of Thomas that lived inside her coat pocket, because she'd been fool enough to wear it during inspections after deciding hiding it drew more attention than showing it. He held it a moment longer than he needed to.

"Brother?"

"Yes."

"Killed?"

"Missing," Jude said. "Reported otherwise."

Verlach looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the narrowness of him cracked and something almost human peered through — recognition, maybe, of a kind he didn't enjoy. Then he put the photograph down with exaggerated care, as if handling it gently were a form of apology, and moved on.

The fifth man, the silent one, drifted through it all without touching anything. He looked at the press. He looked at the type scattered in the yard. He looked at Bram — longer than at anyone, and Bram bore it well, standing at the stove stirring a pot of barley that needed no stirring, a man performing the role of orderly so thoroughly that Jude wanted to applaud. The silent one wrote nothing. When Verlach finally called the team together, the silent one leaned in and murmured something Jude couldn't catch, and Verlach's mouth thinned.

"Quarter passes inspection," Verlach announced to the room, in the tone of a man announcing a postponement rather than a verdict. "For now. Nurse Dunmore — registration papers within the week, or this quarter gets reclassified and reclassified quarters get emptied. Is that understood?"

"Understood."

"And the type in the yard stays in the yard. Unregistered printing equipment is contraband under the December edicts. Consider it confiscated pending disposition."

*Pending disposition.* She knew what disposition meant. Disposition meant the same wagon that would come for the records in spring, the same fire, the same efficient erasure — just arriving in installments.

The door closed behind them. The boot heels faded down the row. From the next quarter came a woman's voice, rising in protest, and then the crash of furniture, and then nothing.

* * *

Nobody moved for a full minute.

Then Bram crossed the room in three strides and knelt at the foot of Dole's cot, and Jude was already there beside him, and together — without speaking, without needing to — they eased the oilcloth bundle out from the webbing and into her arms. It had absorbed the cold of the floor and felt like a thing pulled from a river.

"You all right, Dole?" Bram asked.

"I got lied about beautifully," Dole whispered. "Drainage lines. Very technical. Did you see the sergeant's face? He'd sooner have fought the whole Prussian army single-handed than argue with her about tubing." His breath caught, restarted. "Nurse. Was that a Bible?"

"A ledger," Jude said. There seemed no point lying to a dying man about it; dying men had earned the truth the way soldiers earned leave.

"What's in it?" 

"Names."

He considered that. The fever burned in his cheeks, but his eyes stayed clear — that terrible clarity the dying sometimes got, a transparency to the world that the living spend their health defending against.

"My name's in books somewhere," he said. "Pay rolls. Hospital lists. My mum gets a telegram if I die and a form letter if I don't. Neither one says who I was." He paused, and the pause cost him breath. "Put mine in yours. While I'm here to spell it for you. Dole, Alfred J. In case they shorten it. They shorten everything."

Jude knelt by the cot and opened the ledger to the current page and wrote it while he watched: *Dole, Alfred J., 19th Fusiliers, fever, quarter seven.* He corrected her spelling of his middle name — *Jerome, not Jerone* — with the fussy precision of a schoolmaster, and then lay back, spent, satisfied, as if a debt had been settled while both parties were still alive to witness it.

Outside, Elias's cart rattled into the yard.

* * *

She told him on the step, quickly, quietly, while Bram watched the road and the light died blue in the west. The type in the mud. The false bottom found and emptied. The registration ultimatum. The silent watcher who'd written nothing and looked at everything. The ledger under a dying boy's cot for twenty minutes while she counted a pulse she was inventing and meant every second of it.

Elias listened with his hands in his pockets, shoulders up against the cold, and when she finished he didn't curse or rage, which frightened her more than cursing. He just looked past her, through the doorway, at the press standing dark in the corner — his press, the machine that had made all of this possible, the machine he loved with the unembarrassed devotion of a craftsman — and she saw him do arithmetic with his eyes.

"They dumped the type," he said at last.

"In the yard. All of it."

"Then we pick it up tonight, every piece, before the frost sets it into the mud." He said it simply, the way he said everything that mattered, and started pulling on his gloves. "And tomorrow we melt down what we can't carry, and cast new sorts, smaller ones. And the week after that—"

"Elias. They'll come back."

"Yes."

"Sooner than spring."

He stopped with one glove half on. Through the window behind her, the last light showed him the yard, the scattered type glinting in the frozen ruts like spilt coins, the road beyond empty and pale, and — far off, where the road bent toward the bridge quarter — a single figure walking toward town through the dusk, officer's greatcoat, unhurried, unmistakable.

Halloway. Coming back across the ice-closed river, somehow, from the side of the river he'd promised never to need again.

Jude watched him approach and felt the fear in her chest change shape — sharpen, refine, become something closer to dread — because she understood, in the way you understand weather before it breaks, that whatever Major Halloway had walked all this way through the cold to tell her, it was not good news.

"Get the type in before dark," she said. "All of it. And light every lamp in the workshop. Tonight we count pages."
