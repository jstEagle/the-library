## 13. What Duty Costs

The discovery was an accident, which was how all the important things in that winter happened.

Major Edmund Halloway had come to the river quarter on legitimate business — a requisition audit of the convalescent stores, the kind of paperwork he performed the way other men said prayers, mechanically and without expectation of answer. The workshop stood at the end of Cordwainer Lane, its windows fogged with the breath of too many people living in too little space, and he had been inside it twice before: once to inspect Elias Brandt's press license, back when such licenses still meant something, and once to deliver a crate of lamp oil with no entry in any log, an act whose illegality he had chosen not to examine too closely.

He came a third time on a grey afternoon in late February, because Corporal Voss reported a discrepancy in the paper inventory and Halloway had learned that discrepancies in wartime were never small. Paper was ammunition now. Paper was orders, casualty rolls, discharge certificates, death notices — the whole machinery by which the army converted human beings into columns of figures. A man who controlled paper controlled what had happened, or at least what would be remembered as having happened.

Halloway knew this better than most. He had spent two years controlling it himself.

* * *

He found the discrepancy within twenty minutes.

The storeroom behind the press was cold enough that his breath hung visible, and the shelves held crates of type blocks sorted by size, reams of salvaged stock, and — beneath a tarpaulin that someone had weighted with bricks against the damp — a stack of printed pages that did not match anything in the quartermaster's catalogue.

They were lists. He saw that immediately. Names in three columns, regimental numbers where they could be recovered, wounds described in a shorthand hand, and dates. Dates of admission to the flooded hospital at Vellmere, dates of transfer, dates of death. And beside certain entries, in a second ink, a notation he recognized with a sensation like a blade sliding between his ribs:

*Struck from roll.*

He counted eleven pages before his hands stopped being steady enough to count. Eleven pages, perhaps forty names each, some annotated twice — struck from one report and then struck again from the corrected report, erased so thoroughly that somewhere in a staff office in Vellmere these men had been unwounded, untransported, unburied. Unnamed.

He knew the handwriting. He would have known it anywhere, though he told himself otherwise for the length of time it took him to fold the top page closed. It was Jude Dunmore's hand, the same disciplined slant she used on her hospital requisitions, but here pressed harder into the page, as if the pen itself were being made to carry weight the writer could not lift.

He should have taken them. That was the thought that arrived first, clean and official, wearing his own voice: *You are a major of the Provisional Authority. These are falsified records. You will confiscate them, file a report, and let the machinery do its work.*

And beneath that thought, older and quieter, another voice said nothing at all. It simply showed him a face. Lieutenant Ansel Rook, twenty-three years old, gut-shot at the Harrow crossing, alive when Halloway signed the morning report and dead by evening, absent from the amended roll because the amendment had already gone south and there was no mechanism — none — for unprinting a lie. Halloway had written the correction on a scrap of paper, kept the scrap in his breast pocket for a week, and burned it over a brazier because keeping it was mutiny and sending it was useless and doing neither felt like murder.

He had stopped keeping scraps after that. He had started something worse instead. But that was not a thing he permitted himself to think about standing in a cold storeroom with another man's ledger in his hands.

* * *

He heard them coming and had just enough time to decide.

It was not a long decision. It took the width of a doorway. He could stand in the storeroom with the pages open and let whatever followed happen according to law, or he could close the crate, step out into the workshop, and become — for one more hour, one more day, however long he could bear it — a man who had seen nothing.

He heard Jude's voice first, low, giving instructions to someone about water rationing, and behind it the clank and shuffle of Bram the orderly hauling pails. Then the door opened and there she was, thinner than autumn, sleeves rolled over forearms that looked built out of coat-hanger wire, and her eyes went from his face to the storeroom door behind him and he watched her understand everything in a single heartbeat.

"Major," she said. Her voice was level. Her hands were not; they found each other and gripped.

"Miss Dunmore." He stepped aside from the storeroom door, deliberately, so she could see past him into the dark. "I've completed my audit. The inventory is satisfactory."

"Satisfactory." The word came out flat.

"There's a discrepancy in your paper stock," he said, "which I've noted and resolved." He met her eyes and held them, and what passed between them in that moment was the closest thing to honesty either of them could afford. She knew he had seen. He knew she knew. Neither of them would say it aloud, because saying it aloud would require both of them to decide, right there among the drying sheets and the smell of ink, whether they were going to be officers and subjects or two people trying to keep forty men per page from vanishing off the earth.

"I see," she said finally. "Thank you for resolving it."

Bram appeared in the inner doorway with a pail, took one look at the room's temperature, and evaporated back through it. Somewhere beyond the wall, faintly, the press was running — Elias working the evening shift, printing names while the light lasted.

Halloway straightened his gloves. "A word outside, Miss Dunmore. On another matter."

* * *

Outside, the lane was empty and the river was talking to itself. It had begun doing that in the last week — a low grinding under the ice, like millstones turning far underground — and everyone in the quarter pretended not to hear it, the way soldiers pretend not to hear the guns until the shells come close enough to make pretending impossible.

They walked as far as the corner where Cordwainer Lane met the embankment road, and there he stopped, because there was a bench, and because if he did not sit down soon his knees were going to inform him of their opinion of the day.

"You have questions you haven't asked me," he said.

Jude sat beside him, upright, gloved hands folded. "I have questions I've been told not to ask."

"Yes. Well. I'm countermanding myself, then, which is a thing majors can do." He looked at the river. The ice shelf ran bank to bank, grey-white, scarred with pressure ridges, and somewhere beneath it moved the whole cold weight of the season looking for a way out. "I'm going to tell you something that is not in any order, and is not authorized, and if you repeat it I will deny having said it with a conviction that may even convince me."

She turned to look at him. In the low light her face was all planes and patience, and he understood suddenly why Elias Brandt had deserted an army rather than leave this woman alone in a burning quarter — not because she needed protecting, but because being near her made a man want to deserve his own life.

"The thaw patrols," Halloway said. "You know the requisition order. Workshop seized at spring thaw, unofficial records destroyed. You believe that gives you until the ice goes out. Weeks yet. Perhaps a month."

"That's what the order says."

"The order is true," he said, "and also irrelevant. Orders are documents, Miss Dunmore. Documents can be accelerated." He heard his own voice go dry and careful, the staff-officer register, the voice that delivered bad news without letting it touch him. "Colonel Merrick has requested early clearance of the eastern quarter. His stated reason is disease control — typhus in the flooded wards, vermin in the storehouses. His actual reason is that a supply convoy is rerouted through Vellmere in March and he wants the roads clear and the quarter presentable. Burning takes three days and inspection takes one. He intends to begin the first week the river shows open leads. Not the thaw. The *first break-up*. The first morning the ice cracks along the shore."

Jude did not move. But he saw her hands tighten on each other, knuckles whitening inside worn leather, and he saw her do the arithmetic — he could nearly hear it, the click of days subtracting themselves.

"How long does that give us?"

"Last year? First break-up came the second week of March." He paused. "This year the ice formed late and thin over deep water. My guess — and I am a professional estimator of disasters — is earlier. Much earlier."

"Days."

"Weeks. Days. The difference between those words is not mine to guarantee." He made himself look at her. "I'm telling you because you strike me as a woman who plans around official timelines, and official timelines are going to get you killed. Or worse — they're going to get the work unfinished."

For a long moment she said nothing. Down the embankment, two convalescents leaned on each other toward the latrine trench, moving with the slow choreography of men learning their new limits. The river ground its stones.

"Why?" she said at last. Not *why are you telling me*. She skipped that question entirely, which told him she had already answered it, and the answer frightened him more than any accusation could have. She asked, instead: "Why does Colonel Merrick want it burned? Truly. Disease doesn't burn ledgers. Typhus doesn't care what's written on paper."

And there it was — the question underneath every question, the one he had been carrying since autumn like a stone in his boot, and he understood that if he answered it honestly he could never again be the man who offered her safety across the bridge in exchange for silence. Honesty would cost him the last clean version of himself. He weighed it, standing there, the way he had once weighed casualty estimates: what can be saved, what must be written off, what the operation costs.

"You know why," he said quietly.

"Say it anyway."

He exhaled. His breath hung between them and dissolved. "Because those pages are evidence," he said. "Every name struck from a roll was struck by an officer protecting a figure, a promotion, a reputation. Some of them are dead men's reputations. Some of them are mine." He watched her flinch — small, controlled, but real. "Yes. Mine. I told you once I'd done it. I'll say it plainer now: I struck names. Fourteen of them, over eight months, and I can account for every one with reasons that sounded like duty at the time. Supply figures. Morale reports. A general's conviction that bad news travels faster than good news and lands heavier." His jaw ached; he realized he was clenching it. "Merrick isn't destroying records because of typhus. He's destroying them because somewhere in this quarter there exists proof of what we did, and proof is the one thing the war cannot absorb and continue functioning."

"And you didn't confiscate it today."

"No."

"Why?"

He laughed — a short, airless sound with no humor in it, surprising them both. "Because I'm a coward in the only direction that matters. Because taking those pages would have been easy. Signing a form, filing a report, watching a corporal carry them to a furnace — that requires nothing but the career I've already sold. Doing nothing requires—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "Doing nothing means standing in front of a fire someday knowing what's in it, and choosing to have put it there. I've burned enough, Miss Dunmore. I find I can't carry the weight of yours too."

She studied him for a long time, and he let her, because he had decided — God help him — that he would rather she see him plainly and despise him than respect the polished lie.

"My brother's name was struck," she said at last. "Thomas. Third Battalion, the advance at Kessel Ford. I didn't learn it from a telegram. I learned it from a chaplain's private letter, six weeks later, because the official report listed the action as a successful withdrawal with light losses." Her voice did not break. It simply went very quiet, the way deep water is quiet. "So when you ask why I keep a ledger, Major — that's why. Not defiance. Arithmetic. Someone has to correct the sum."

"I know," Halloway said.

And then, because the day had already cost him more than he'd planned to spend, he reached into his greatcoat and drew out an envelope — plain, unstamped, unmarked — and held it out to her.

"What's this?"

"A schedule. Not an official one. It's the patrol rotation for the embankment district as I understand it will be constituted once Merrick's directive activates. Times, routes, composition. It will change — treat it as a sketch, not scripture. But a sketch is better than nothing when you're deciding what can be carried out of a building and what has to be left to burn." He pressed it into her hands before she could refuse. "Burn this after you've read it. I mean that literally. If it's found on you, it hangs us both."

She looked down at the envelope and then up at him, and something moved across her face that he couldn't name — gratitude, pity, grief, all three braided tight. "Why are you really doing this?"

He considered lying. He had a lifetime's practice at it; the lies assembled themselves eagerly, ready-marched. But she had earned the truth, and besides, he suspected she could taste one through any wrapping.

"Because in October I offered you silence and safety in the same breath," he said, "and I told myself that was kindness. It wasn't. It was a bribe. I was buying absolution with a berth on a convoy." The river groaned, long and low, and they both turned toward it, and neither commented. "I can't buy it anymore. So I'm paying a different way. Consider this the first installment."

"And the rest?"

"The rest comes later." He stood, and his knees objected, and he ignored them. "Work faster than you planned, Miss Dunmore. Assume you have half the time. When the first lead opens along the north shore, count that morning as your last."

He walked back down Cordwainer Lane without looking behind him, and behind him the press kept its patient rhythm — the steady mechanical heartbeat of names being rescued from oblivion one pull of a lever at a time — and above it all the river spoke in its sleep, turning over, dreaming of the day it would wake and take everything back.

That night, in his quarters on the far side of the quarter, Halloway unlocked the iron box under his cot and took out his own list — fourteen names in his own hand, carried eighteen months like shrapnel he'd refused to have cut out — and set it on the desk beside a blank sheet of paper.

He dipped his pen.

He did not yet know what he was going to write. But he had stopped believing, somewhere on a bench beside a groaning river, that duty and conscience were the same thing. The question that remained, staring at him from the white page, was which one he would follow — and how much of himself it would cost to find out.

Far off, through the frost-furred window, the ice cracked once, sharply, like a rifle shot. Then silence. Then, fainter, the sound beginning again: the slow, patient grinding of a river preparing to end the winter whether anyone was ready or not.
