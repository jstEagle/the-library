## 14. The Drowned Depot

The paper ran out on a Tuesday, though Elias only knew it was Tuesday because Bram had counted the days aloud at supper, ticking them off against the wall with a stub of pencil like a man keeping score in a game nobody else was playing.

"Fourteen weeks since bridge closed," Bram said. "Give or take."

Elias didn't answer. He was looking at the stack of salvaged stock by the press — three fingers of paper, no more. Enough for perhaps two hundred names if he set them tight and printed close to the margins, and there were four hundred still untranscribed, plus Halloway's list, which Jude had merged into hers page by page with the concentration of a woman defusing something.

Two hundred names' worth of paper. The arithmetic sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"There's the depot," Bram said.

Elias looked up.

"The supply depot. Off the mill road — the one that flooded when they blew the weir in October." Bram turned his pencil over in his fingers. "Half of Vellmere knows about it. Half of Vellmere also knows nobody's been fool enough to go out on that ice since December."

"The ice held for the soldiers who stripped the rifles."

"The ice held for *them*," Bram said. "It doesn't know you."

That was true enough. Ice didn't know anybody. It was indifferent as a ledger line — it bore what it bore until it didn't, and then it closed over you the same way an officer's pen closed over a name, without ceremony, without record.

"I'll go," Elias said.

Jude, who had been setting type across the room — she'd gotten fast at it, faster than him now, her fingers finding letters in the dark of the case like a pianist — stopped mid-motion. She didn't look up. But she stopped, and that was worse than any argument.

"It'll hold," he said.

"You said that last time you went near water."

"That was the dock. That was planks."

"Elias." Now she looked at him, and her face did the thing it always did, the thing he'd learned to read across a winter of shared lamplight: the jaw setting first, then the eyes going flat and steady, the grief packed down somewhere beneath where it couldn't be seen but could always be felt, like heat from a stove through a wall. "If the depot's flooded, the paper's flooded. Wet paper is pulp."

"Wet paper dries. I've dried out a cellar full of type after a burst pipe. You press it between boards, you change the boards, you keep the room cold so it doesn't mold. It comes back. Not all of it, but most."

"And you come back how? Across a frozen inlet carrying a hundredweight of salvage?"

"One armful at a time."

She stood. She came across the workshop and stood close enough that he could smell the ink on her hands, the lamp oil, the faint iron smell of the stove that never quite left anything in that room. When she spoke again her voice was low, meant only for him, though Bram was right there and pretending very hard to study his pencil.

"Don't go alone. Wait until morning. Take Bram."

"Bram's hands shake."

"My hands shake too, sometimes," Bram said mildly, not studying his pencil anymore. "They shook the day I carried a man's whole leg off a table in Vellmere hospital. Shaking hands can still carry paper, friend."

So it was settled the way things got settled in the workshop: not by anyone winning, but by everyone conceding just enough that the work could continue. In the morning, Elias and Bram would take the sledge and the rope and the long pole, and they would walk the mill road to the drowned depot, and they would come back with enough paper to finish the book.

Jude packed them bread and rendered fat and a flask of the spirits the convalescents used for wounds. At the door she took his wrist — not his hand, his wrist, where she could feel his pulse, as if checking that it was running before she let the machine out of the shop.

"Come back," she said. "That's the whole instruction. Come back."

He wanted to say something equal to it. What came out was, "I'll bring you paper."

And he saw — briefly, before she turned away — that she understood both sentences were the same sentence, and that neither of them was ready to say the third thing that lived underneath.

* * *

The river valley in February was a country of white silence. The mill road had vanished under snow; they followed the telegraph wire instead, Bram ahead with the pole testing drifts, Elias hauling the sledge. The cold was the kind that made sound brittle — their breath cracked in the air, the runners squealed, and beneath it all, once they came within earshot of the water, the river muttered to itself under the ice like a debtor counting coins.

The depot showed as a dark shoulder above the white: a low brick warehouse, its roofline broken, one wall slumped inward where the water had found it when the weir went. The flood had spread a shallow lake over the flats around it, frozen now into a plain of grey-green ice, wind-scoured, terrifyingly clear in places — you could look straight down through a hand's thickness of glassy sheet at the drowned floor below, at shelved crates standing in green twilight like furniture in a sunken house.

"Christ," Bram breathed. "There's paper in there. Look. Third row. Those bales under the tarp — that's stock, that's printing stock, someone requisitioned it and never moved it."

"Someone requisitioned a lot of things and never moved them," Elias said. "That's the war entire."

They worked along the shore-fast shelf first, where the ice grew from the land itself and was honest about its strength. Then the shelf gave way to the flood-plain ice, thicker in appearance and less trustworthy in fact, because moving water underneath is a liar's foundation. Bram went out first with the pole, weight low, testing every step. Twenty yards out, thirty. The ice rang under the pole like a struck bell — deep note, solid note.

Then, forty yards out, the note changed.

Bram heard it before Elias did. "Back. Back, off the line—" And then the world simply removed itself from under Bram's boot, a panel of ice hinging down like a trapdoor, and Bram went in to the chest, the pole skidding away across the surface, and the cold came up out of the hole like a living thing with arms.

Elias didn't remember deciding to move. He was already sliding on his belly, distributing himself across the sheet the way you distribute weight across a press bed, sledge rope payed out behind him, shouting words he couldn't hear himself say. The hole was black and steaming. Bram's hands were on the rim and failing, fingers gone past feeling into stupidity, slipping off the wet edge each time he heaved.

"Rope. Rope, give me—"

The rope reached him. He got a loop around Bram's chest, under the arms, and braced, and the ice spoke beneath him — not a crack now but a groan, a long structural complaint, the sound of a thing deciding — and then the sheet let go under his own knee and the river took him too.

Cold like nothing in his life. Cold as a verdict. It wasn't pain at first; it was absence, total, a white erasure from the feet upward, and some calm clerk in the back of his mind noted: *you have perhaps four minutes of use in you, printer, spend them well.* His head broke surface inside the hollow of broken sheets, and there was Bram's face six feet away, grey as ash, mouth working, and beyond him the impossible distance of the shore.

"Kick. Kick for the shelf — the shelf will hold, kick—"

They kicked. The ice fought them, slick rims collapsing under their grip, cutting their forearms. Twice Elias went under and came up swallowing black water that tasted of iron and old timber. Somewhere behind his eyes a thought surfaced with perfect clarity: *the pages drying between boards, Jude's hands smoothing them flat, the book finished, the families reading.* He held onto that thought the way you hold a lever. He kicked.

His elbow found the shelf ice at last — real ice, grounded ice — and he screamed at Bram to climb, screamed until Bram got chest and then knees and then all of himself out onto the solid shelf, and then Elias followed, dragging his body out of the river like something being born wrong-side-first.

He didn't remember the walk back. That was the mercy of it. There were fragments: Bram's arm under his shoulder; the sledge abandoned; the sky enormous and indifferent; his own teeth making a noise like the press in motion. At the edge of town Bram hammered on doors, and hands took hold of him, and someone poured spirit into his mouth that burned all the way down and did nothing at all.

The last thing he registered clearly was the workshop door opening, and Jude's face, and the sound she made — small, involuntary, torn out of her — before the cold finished its paperwork and closed the ledger on him entirely.

* * *

Fever, when it came, was the river again.

He was in the water and out of the water and in it again; he was at the press printing pages of black water, each sheet coming up printed with names he couldn't read because they kept rearranging themselves into Thomas Dunmore's name, into the names of men whose rolls he'd falsified in another town in another life, into his own name spelled wrong. He was arguing with an officer who had no face. He was trying to tell someone — Jude, he thought, or God, or the compositor who'd taught him the trade — that he hadn't wanted to print the false rolls, that he'd done it for eleven days and then walked out of the print works and kept walking and had never once, not once, forgiven himself, and that the whole press, the whole ledger, the whole winter, was one long attempt to set that right, letter by letter, name by name—

Someone was holding his hand.

He surfaced partway. Lamplight. The workshop ceiling, smoke-darkened beams he knew better than his own heartbeat. His hands were wrapped — frostbitten, someone had said, or he'd dreamed someone saying it, the fingertips blistered white. The pain arrived whenever the fever ebbed, arriving like a bill collector, thorough and itemized.

Jude sat beside him on a stool dragged from the type cases. Her sleeve was rolled. A basin of water and vinegar stood on the crate beside her, and a cloth, and she was bathing his forearm with a steadiness that seemed almost cruel in its calm, except her eyes were wrecked — red-rimmed, sleepless, fixed on him with an attention so total it frightened him more than the fever had.

"You're crying," he managed. His voice came out scraped.

"No, I'm not."

"All right."

"You're delirious. You don't know what I'm doing."

"I know you've been here a while. The lamp's burned down twice."

Her hand stilled on his arm. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the stove ticked, and outside the wind worried at the shutter the way it had all winter, patient as a creditor.

"They pulled Bram out first," she said at last, as if answering a question he hadn't asked. "He wouldn't leave the bank until they promised to go back for you. He stood there in wet clothes screaming your name into the ice. They had to hold him up."

"He's a good man."

"He thinks he's damned. You should know — he told me everything, after. About the other town. About the printer he informed on." She paused. "I told him what you told me once. That a man is not the worst thing he's done; he's the next thing he does. You said that?"

"I think I stole it from a compositor."

"Well, it's yours now. He needed it more than you."

Elias tried to laugh and it turned into coughing, and she helped him sit forward, her arm behind his shoulders, careful of his bandaged hands, and for a few seconds he was simply held, and the fever receded to a muttering at the edges, and he thought: *I could die now and it would be an acceptable entry.* Which was a stupid thought, and a feverish one, and also completely sincere.

"Lie back," she said.

"Jude."

"Don't talk. You need sleep."

"I need to say a thing while I'm too sick to be sensible about it."

She went still. Outside, far off, the river groaned — a long shifting complaint of ice settling, the sound that had become the season's heartbeat, audible even here through walls and wind.

"When I was under," he said, "the second time — when the water had me — I didn't think about the ledger. I want you to know that. Everyone would assume a man thinks of his great work, his purpose, his redemption. I thought about this room. I thought about you setting type with your sleeves rolled, and the sound you make when you find a misprint — that little exhale through the nose, like disapproval of the universe — and I thought, if that's the last thing, it isn't fair, because I never said it."

"Elias—"

"I love you." The words came out rough, unpracticed, like type set in haste with a dirty case. "I love you. I have loved you since the night you brought me the first seven names and asked me to print them and didn't ask me for anything else, not my story, not my guilt, nothing — you just assumed I was the kind of man who would say yes. And I decided to be that man. Do you understand? You made me that man by assuming. I love you, Jude Dunmore, and I am telling you now because my hands are ruined and the thaw is coming and the patrols are coming and I refuse — I absolutely refuse — to die with it unsaid like some coward's casualty report."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the loft overhead, one of the convalescents shifted in his blankets.

Then Jude leaned forward, and took his bandaged hand — carefully, so carefully, the way you handle a forme of loose type — and pressed her lips to his scorched knuckles, and said:

"I knew."

"You knew?"

"I've known since the dock, Elias. Since before the dock. I've known since you stayed up three nights making the press run quiet so the officers on the street wouldn't hear it." Her voice was steady but her thumb was moving over his knuckles, back and forth, back and forth, as if she couldn't stop it. "I didn't say it because saying it makes it a debt. And I couldn't owe you a debt I might not live to repay. But you went under the ice today for paper, for *names*, and you came up, and you're here, and I find that my arithmetic has changed."

"What's the new figure?"

"The new figure is that I love you too. Have loved you. Will love you past the thaw, past the burning, past whatever the river decides to do with us." Her eyes were bright and dry and fierce. "But hear me, Elias — you are not allowed to die. That is the condition attached. You are not permitted to leave me with a ledger full of other people's names and none of our own future. You will heal those hands. You will pull that lever again. We are going to finish this book, and we are going to get it across that river if we have to teach the ice to read."

A laugh broke out of him — real, painful, joyful — and hurt his ribs and he didn't care.

"That's the most romantic threat I've ever received."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. That's why I'll obey."

She bent and kissed him then, properly, and the fever and the cold and the fear of the patrol and the groaning river all withdrew to the far edges of the world, and for a while the workshop held only the two of them and the lamp and the slow, stubborn warmth of a stove refusing to go out.

When he woke next, it was full night, and the fever had broken, and she was asleep sitting upright beside him with her hand still resting on his wrist, keeping time.

In the doorway, silent as a man who has learned to arrive quietly, stood Major Halloway — snow on his coat, a paper packet in his hand, his face unreadable in the dark.

"We need to talk," he said softly. "The burning detail's orders have changed. It's sooner than thaw."
