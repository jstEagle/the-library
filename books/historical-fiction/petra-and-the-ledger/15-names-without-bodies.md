## 15. Names Without Bodies

They came on the last sledges before the river closed for good — thirty-one of them, or thirty-one and a half, because one man died between the counting and the unloading, and no one could agree afterward whether he had crossed the threshold as a living soldier or arrived as cargo.

Jude heard the wagons before she saw them. The sound came up the frozen road like something being dragged: runners shrieking on ice-glazed ruts, wheels that had not been greased since autumn. She was in the workshop with Elias, setting a page of names in reverse so the press would read them forward, when Corporal Fen put his head through the door without knocking, which he never did.

"They're bringing them to us," he said. "The ones nobody wants."

"Nobody wants wounded men?"

Fen's face did something complicated. "These aren't wounded. Mostly."

* * *

She went down to the old granary by the water, where the army had stacked grain sacks two winters ago and never come back for them. The doors were already open, and the cold inside was the honest kind, the kind that didn't pretend to be anything but what it was. Candles had been set out — someone had thought of candles — and they guttered in the draft from the doors, throwing shadows that made the rows of stretchers look like a field of stubble after harvest.

Thirty-one men. Some breathing. Some not. All of them stripped of everything that would have told her who they were.

"No tags," said Sergeant Aldous, who had ridden with them. He stamped his boots on the boards, trying to feel his feet again. "No paybooks. No letters. Whatever they had on them went into a sack at the collection point, and the sack went into a wagon, and the wagon—" he made a vague gesture eastward, toward the war, "—went where wagons go. They're coming off the line at Marrow Ford. Whole companies, mixed up, pushed back faster than anyone expected. These are the ones that got separated. Fell behind. Got picked up late."

"Picked up late," Jude repeated.

Aldous wouldn't meet her eyes. "Some of them have been lying in the open since Tuesday."

She walked the rows. She had learned to walk the rows slowly, because speed looked like triage and this was not triage; there was nothing left here to sort into categories of salvageable and lost. This was a different kind of sorting entirely. A boy of perhaps nineteen, frostbitten black to both wrists, unconscious but alive, breath whistling through cracked lips. A sergeant with a head wound wrapped in what had once been somebody's shirt. An older man — forty, maybe more, too old for any of this — whose chest rose and fell with the terrible patience of a man determined to finish something.

None of them wore a name.

"Where are their officers?" she asked.

"Dead or scattered." Aldous shrugged, and the shrug was the whole answer. In an army moving backward, accountability moved fastest of all.

"And the reports?"

"What reports?" He almost smiled, then saw her face and stopped. "Look, miss. There'll be paperwork eventually. There always is. Somebody in some bureau will make these men into numbers, and the numbers will go into a column, and the column will balance, and everyone will sleep fine." He pulled off a glove with his teeth. "But I drove them here. Four hours. I had time to think. And it seemed to me that if nobody writes down who they *were* while anybody still knows, then nobody ever will."

Jude felt the ledger against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"That's why you brought them to me," she said.

"I brought them to the nearest shelter." He met her eyes now, and held them. "The nearest shelter happens to be yours."

* * *

Elias stood in the doorway of the granary with his hands jammed in his pockets, looking down the rows the way he looked at a locked type case — measuring, cataloguing, refusing to be daunted.

"We have six blankets," he said. "We have maybe four days of food for ourselves, less now. We have—"

"Elias."

"—we have a provost inspection that could come through this quarter at any hour, and you want to fill a granary with thirty-one unregistered men who appear from nowhere carrying no papers whatsoever."

"Yes," Jude said.

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind came off the river and set the loose door swinging on its hinge, and the candle flames all leaned together, all one direction, like grass.

"All right," he said. "But we do it properly. If we're going to commit a crime, we commit it with good typography."

It was meant as a joke and wasn't one, and they both knew it, and that was the shape of their life now.

* * *

The first man who could speak was the oldest one, the patient breather. Jude knelt beside him with the ledger open on her knee and a candle stuck to the floorboards with its own wax.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," she said. "Not medical ones. I can't help you medically, and I won't lie about that. But I know your name is going to be written somewhere tonight, and it should be the true one."

His eyes found her. They were pale eyes, winter-colored, and very tired.

"Corporal Ambrose Steen," he said. Each word cost him something; she could see it cost him. "Fourth Vellmere Rifles. That's — that's gone now. The Fourth's gone. You should write that too."

"I will."

"My wife's name is Hetta. She thinks I'm at the front. She doesn't know the front came apart." A breath, long and careful, like a man handling glass. "If she gets a letter someday saying I died, it'll say killed in action, which sounds clean. It wasn't clean. I lay in a ditch for three days next to a boy called Pim who kept asking for his mother until Thursday morning, and then he stopped asking, and I couldn't even bury him because my legs don't work anymore." His hand moved an inch across the blanket — toward her, or toward the ledger; she chose to believe the ledger. "Put it in your book however you want. But put in that Hetta Steen is owed the truth. Not the clean version. The truth."

Jude wrote it. All of it. Her pen moved fast and her handwriting stayed steady, and somewhere beneath the steadiness she understood that this — this exact thing, this kneeling and this writing — was what the ledger had been for all along. Not defiance. Not even memory. *Debt.* Every name was money owed, and she was keeping the accounts.

* * *

By midnight the granary smelled of candle smoke and the particular sweetness of wounds going bad, and Jude had eleven names.

Eleven, out of thirty-one. Nine men were past questioning entirely — alive in the way that a banked fire is alive, giving off heat and light but past conversation. Seven were children of twenty or under, concussed or fevered, surfacing into delirium where they spoke freely to mothers, brothers, sergeants, God — none of whom were her. She learned to sit close and listen anyway, because delirium tells the truth sideways. A boy named himself twice in an hour: once as Willem, once as Willem Arden, once, heartbreakingly, as "Arden, W., Fourth Vellmere, tell them I held the wall." She wrote all three versions and drew a line connecting them, because a record that admits its own uncertainty is worth more than one that pretends.

The thirteenth name came from the frostbitten boy near the door. He surfaced around two in the morning, screaming — not loudly, that was the awful part, screaming the way a kettle screams, thin and constant — and when Jude held his blackened hands and talked to him, he stopped and stared at her with pupils blown wide.

"Am I dead?" he asked.

"No."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"Because I dreamed I was dead, and nobody knew me, and they buried me in a pit with a plank instead of a stone." He swallowed. "My mum has a stone for my dad. She'd get me one. She'd sell things, but she'd get one."

"Then give me your name," Jude said, "and I'll see she gets more than a stone. I'll see she gets the whole story."

"Pieter Lass," he whispered. "Pieter Jan Lass. I'm eighteen." Then, urgently, gripping nothing with fingers that could no longer grip: "Tell her I didn't cry."

Jude wrote: *Pieter Jan Lass, 18. Did not cry.*

Later, alone with the page, she added: *and lied bravely to his mother in advance.*

* * *

Elias worked the press in the workshop through the small hours, printing duplicate sheets of every name the moment she brought them over, so that no single fire, no single confiscation, no single careless officer could undo the night's work. Around three she carried him the eleventh page and found ink on his cheekbone and exhaustion sitting on him like a coat.

"How many?" he asked.

"Thirteen. Eleven confirmed, two probable."

"And how many can't be saved?"

She didn't answer immediately, and he nodded, because he hadn't really been asking for the number.

"The dying ones," he said carefully, "are the best witnesses. You know that. A man with an hour left will tell you everything a healthy man will take to his grave."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"It's true, though. It's the only reason I ever got the real rolls out of the officers' clerks — I printed their casualty forms, remember? I watched what they wrote. Healthy men edit. Dying men testify." He took the page from her and squared it against the others with the automatic tenderness of his trade. "So talk to them first. The ones with the least time. Names can wait for the sleeping; they can't wait for the leaving."

She stood in the doorway a moment, watching him lock chase and tighten quoins in the dark, his fingers moving with a certainty his body no longer fully supported.

"When did you become the practical one?" she asked.

"When I fell in love with an idealist," he said, not looking up. "Someone has to keep the ink mixed."

* * *

Dawn came gray and late, the way January dawns do, and with it came the second problem: the living needed feeding, the dying needed tending, and neither the army nor the town would acknowledge that either thing was happening.

Bram solved it, in his way, by stealing.

He appeared at the granary door mid-morning with a sack of barley, a tin of rendered fat, and a jar of honey whose label had been scraped off with suspicious thoroughness, and he would not say where any of it came from, and Jude decided — deliberately, with her eyes open — not to ask. There would be a day for accounting with Bram. Today the accounting that mattered was alphabetical.

"You're making soup for dead men," he said, setting the pot on the brazier.

"For men whose bodies haven't caught up to their paperwork yet," Jude said. "There's a difference, and it matters."

Bram looked at her strangely then, and something moved behind his face — guilt, she thought later, though she didn't have the word for it yet, didn't have the shape of it. He stirred the pot and said, quietly, "My mother waited nine years to learn where my father fell. Nine years. A woman came to the door once and said he was listed as missing, and missing was worse than dead, and my mother aged ten years in that sentence." He ladled soup into a tin cup. "Whatever else I've done wrong in my life, I know exactly why this matters."

For a moment Jude thought he was going to say something more. His mouth opened. Closed. He carried the cup to the frostbitten boy and fed him spoonful by spoonful with a gentleness that didn't match anything else about him, and Jude filed the unfinished sentence away in the place where she kept things she wasn't ready to examine.

* * *

The afternoon brought the hard cases.

Sergeant Aldous had been right about the sack — whatever identification the men had carried had gone into it at Marrow Ford, and the sack had gone east, and there was no getting it back. So for the men who could not speak, Jude turned detective. She worked the rows with the candle and the ledger and a patience she didn't know she owned.

Boots, she learned, told stories. The stitching pattern marked the regimental cobbler; the Fourth Vellmere patched at the outer heel, the Ninth at the toe. A button of the wrong pattern on one man's coat meant a uniform scavenged from a dead friend, which meant a friendship, which meant somewhere a family might connect two sons through one button. Inside one man's collar, written in laundry ink faded nearly to nothing: a fraction — *7/12* — which she recorded on the chance it was a date, a debt, an anniversary, a birthday. Inside another's cap: a pressed sprig of some herb, dried flat, smelling faintly of kitchens. She held it under the candle a long time and wrote, finally: *Carried something from home that smells of cooking. Someone fed this man well once, and he remembered it enough to carry the proof.*

It was not a name. But it was not nothing, and not-nothing was the currency of the whole enterprise.

The hardest was the man who woke lucid at dusk and looked at her with perfect clarity and said, "I know what you're doing, and I thank you for it, and I can't help you, because I don't know myself."

"Concussion," he explained, when her face must have fallen. "The doctors at the ford said it might come back. It hasn't. I remember my mother's kitchen and I remember the wall we held and I remember nothing between. I am a hole shaped like a soldier." He tried to smile. "Write that down too, if you've room. Somewhere there's a woman waiting for a man who used to be a person, and she deserves to know he ended as a hole with good manners."

Jude wrote it. She gave him a temporary designation — *Man of the Wall, unknown, ~35* — and hated herself for the clerkishness of it, and understood at the same moment that this was precisely what the officers' bureaus did to everyone, all the time, and that the difference between her and them was only that it hurt her.

That was the whole difference. It had to be enough.

* * *

On the third night, the patient breather died.

Ambrose Steen went between one candle-trimming and the next, so gently that Jude was talking to him before she realized he'd stopped hearing her. She finished the sentence anyway. It was about Hetta — whether Hetta liked the truth delivered plain or wrapped in kindness — and she finished it aloud to a dead man because the question deserved an answer and the answer deserved a listener.

Then she opened the ledger to his entry and added the final line in her smallest, most deliberate hand:

*Died 14 January, granary shelter, Vellmere, among friends. Last words were his wife's name.*

Around her, the other men breathed. The candles burned down. Through the walls came the sound of the river, working at its ice, patient as a creditor.

* * *

She was carrying the seventeenth page to the workshop near midnight when she realized the door stood open and the lamplight inside was wrong — too many shadows, too much movement — and her heart went to ice before her mind caught up and identified what she was seeing: Elias, Bram, and the two convalescents from Winter Quarters, all gathered around the press, all silent, all looking at her.

"We heard the sledges," Elias said. "Three nights ago. Coming from the north road, heavy-laden." He hesitated. "And tonight Bram counted lanterns on the far ridge. Provost lanterns. They're marking quarters, Jude. Ours is marked."

"Then we print faster," she said.

"It's not the printing I'm worried about." Elias glanced at Bram — a glance with weight in it, a glance that carried something the two of them had discussed without her — and Bram looked away, out the dark window, toward the ridge and its little constellation of official lights.

"What?" Jude said. "What aren't you telling me?"

Neither man answered. On the press bed, the seventeenth page waited under the tympan, seventeen names deep in ink and truth, and outside, far off on the ridge, one of the lanterns went out.
