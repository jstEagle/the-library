## 16. Bram Disappears

The cot was empty before the kettle sang.

I noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth — the tongue goes to the gap again and again, refusing the evidence. Bram's blanket had been folded. Folded, not thrown. That was the detail that sat wrong in me all through breakfast and would sit wrong for days after. A man who runs in panic does not fold his blanket. A man who means to come back does not leave it folded either. It was the gesture of someone tidying up a room he would never see again, or of someone leaving it presentable for whoever came next.

Jude touched the mattress with two fingers. "He went out for water," she said.

"The pail's full."

She looked. The pail stood by the door, ice skinned over its top, untouched since the night before. She looked at it for a long moment, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd already done, and watched her refuse the answer.

"He'll be back by the inspection rounds," she said.

* * *

I should say what we knew of Bram, because what we knew was almost nothing, and almost nothing is its own kind of warning when you are keeping illegal names in a requisitioned workshop on a frozen river.

He had come to us in the second week of winter quarters, an orderly from the field hospital with frostbitten ears and a story that held together only if you didn't press it. He'd been dismissed from Vellmere for drunkenness, he said. He needed work, warmth, a place out of the patrol routes. Jude took him in the way she took everything in — as if mercy were simply the correct procedure and sentiment had nothing to do with it. He hauled water. He learned to set type, clumsily at first, then not badly. He kept his mouth shut around strangers. For six weeks he had been one more body in the workshop's fragile arithmetic, and bodies were what we had instead of trust.

And now his cot was empty, his blanket folded, and one page of the ledger was gone.

We did not know that last part yet. We knew it within the hour.

* * *

It was Marta who counted. One of our convalescents, a corporal with a shoulder wound that had gone septic and then mercifully quiet, she spent her recovery doing what soldiers do when they can't fight: inventorying. She had appointed herself keeper of the drying rack where each night's printed sheets hung like laundry, and every morning she checked them against Jude's master list with the seriousness of a paymaster.

"Sixteen," she said, and stopped.

"Sixteen what?" I asked.

"There should be seventeen."

The workshop went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when everyone understands at once. The stove ticked. Outside, the river made its low grinding sound, the sound it had been making for a week, the sound that had compressed all our lives into a countdown.

Jude went to the rack herself. Her hands moved down the hanging pages — each one a night's labor, each one carrying thirty, forty names that existed nowhere else in the empire's paperwork — and I saw her find the gap. Not a torn page. Not a miscount. A clean removal, taken sometime in the dark by someone who knew exactly which sheet carried which names, because he had helped hang them there himself.

"Bram set this one," Marta said quietly. "Tuesday night. I remember because he asked why we bothered copying the ones without bodies."

* * *

Here is what fear does in a small room in winter. It doesn't shout. It gets into the joints of things. By noon nobody would sit with their back to the door. The convalescent boy, Pell, started checking the type cases as though letters might desert us too. And I caught myself looking at Jude across the workshop and thinking a thought I hated instantly and could not kill:

*She brought him in. This is hers.*

I want to be honest about that thought, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone. Six weeks of shared bread and lamplight, and the first thing suspicion taught me was to file blame under the nearest name. That is how betrayal works even when no betrayal has happened yet — it makes you smaller in advance, so that when it comes you'll fit inside it.

Jude, meanwhile, was doing none of that. She was standing at the window with her arms crossed, watching the road, and when I came to stand beside her she said, without turning:

"You think he's gone to the provosts."

"I think the page is gone and so is he, and those two facts are holding hands."

"And I think a man who informed on us wouldn't bother taking one page. He'd tell them where the ledger sleeps and let them take everything." She turned to look at me. Her face in the grey window light was the face I loved most in the world and the face I trusted least when it wore this particular stubbornness. "One page, Elias. Why would a traitor steal one page?"

"To prove his value. To walk into their office holding something real."

"Or because he believed the page was in danger here, and he took it somewhere safer."

"Safer than— " I gestured at the workshop, the racks, the press, the whole ridiculous ark of us. "Where? Where is safer than this?"

"I don't know," she said. "That's what faith is. It's believing there's an answer you haven't seen yet."

"That's not faith. That's gambling with other people's necks."

The words came out harder than I meant them, and I saw them land. She flinched — barely, a flicker at the corner of her eye, gone before it finished happening — and then she straightened, and I understood that I had just shown her something true about myself, and that she would carry it.

"Maybe," she said. "But I've gambled my own neck on less certain men than Bram. On you, for instance."

* * *

That evening I went through his things, because I am not as good as she is and never will pretend otherwise.

His kit was still under the cot. That argued against flight — a deserter running for the border takes his kit. His boots were by the stove, drying, both pairs. No man crosses country in winter with one pair of wet socks unless he's being marched. His razor was there, his tin cup, a bundle of letters from a woman in Harrow's Ford tied with string. I read none of them. I held them and put them back and felt like a thief anyway.

Under the letters, folded flat, was a scrap of printer's waste — a proof sheet, spoiled, the kind of thing that litters any press. But on its back, in Bram's careful orderly's hand, were lines of practice: *the quick brown fox*, alphabets, and then, repeated three times, growing steadier each time:

*Pvt. Aldous Renn, 2nd Btn., died of wounds near Colver Mill, Nov. 3rd.*

A name. One of ours. One of the names from the very page that was gone.

I sat on the cold floor with that scrap in my hand for a long time. It proved nothing. It proved everything. A man practicing setting a name in type might be learning the trade. Or he might be memorizing what he intended to sell. The evidence lay perfectly balanced between innocence and guilt, the way evidence always does when you need it to tip.

When Jude found me there, I held it up without a word.

She read it. Then she did the last thing I expected. She smiled — a small, tired, terrible smile.

"He was practicing Thomas's format," she said. "My brother's entry. The way I write them. He asked me once why I put the place of death in every line, and I told him because somewhere a mother needs to know *where*. He remembered."

"You're reading scripture into scraps."

"I'm reading a man into scraps, yes. Because the alternative is reading a traitor into them, and I've decided I won't do that until he makes me." She took the proof sheet from my hand gently, the way she handled the old ledgers, and put it back under his letters. "Faith isn't stupid, Elias. It's just expensive. And I can afford it."

"We can't all afford it. Some of us have frostbite and a price on our heads."

"Then let me pay for both of us this once."

* * *

Three days. He was gone three days, and the river got louder every night.

On the second day a patrol walked the quarter road, and we killed the stove and sat in the dark listening to boots crunch past, and I understood viscerally, in my ribs, what one stolen page in the wrong office could buy us. They didn't stop. Maybe they hadn't been sent. Maybe they were only walking. Suspicion is a religion too; it requires exactly this kind of faith.

Marta wanted to move the remaining pages. Pell wanted to move himself. Even the dying man in the corner — Sergeant Iles, who had perhaps a month in him and spent it listening — weighed in with a wheeze: "In my experience, the ones who vanish quietly come back loudly. Usually with company."

Only Jude held still. She kept working. She set type, pulled sheets, transcribed new names from the trickle of wounded that still reached us — slower now, the war congealing along with the river — and every night she hung the fresh pages on the rack and every morning Marta counted them and every count was right. Life went on in the shape of a held breath.

On the third night, I found her awake by the stove at two in the morning, and I sat down beside her because I could not bear the distance anymore, whatever it cost.

"If he's sold us," I said, "we'll need a plan. Osk. The ice shelf. Everything moved up."

"I know."

"And if he hasn't — if he's lying dead in a ditch somewhere with our page thawing in his coat —"

"I know that too."

"What don't you know, Jude?"

She was quiet long enough that the stove ticked twice. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and it was the voice she used at the cots of dying men, the voice that told the truth because the truth was all that was left to give.

"I don't know whether loving people is the same as being right about them. I've built everything on the idea that it is. That keeping faith with someone is a thing you do ahead of the evidence, or it isn't a thing at all." She looked at the fire. "Thomas's officers had plenty of evidence about that advance. Every report said it was hopeless. They followed the evidence and my brother has no grave." Her jaw tightened. "So forgive me if I've lost my appetite for it."

I reached over and took her hand, and she let me, and we sat like that while the river ground its teeth outside, and I thought: *this woman will believe a man innocent all the way to the firing post.* And I thought also: *and that is precisely why the names get saved, and why I love her, and why I am afraid.*

* * *

He came back on the fourth day, at dusk, half-dead.

We heard him before we saw him — a dragging step on the frozen mud, then a fall, then nothing. I was out the door first, God help me, faster than I'd moved for anything all winter. He lay in the blue snow-light ten yards from the threshold, and he was crusted white to the waist, and his left hand was wrong, blackened at the fingertips, and pressed against his chest with his good arm, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, was a bundle of papers.

Not one page. Copies. A dozen copies of the missing sheet, and beneath them, folded, a paper bearing a chaplain's stamp and a route marked south in pencil.

"Chaplain Wren," Bram whispered, when we'd gotten him inside and gotten brandy into him. His lips were grey. His eyes found Jude first, the way a drowning man's eyes find a light. "Told me... you'd believe. Said you were the only one who would." He tried to smile and couldn't make his face do it. "Took four days. Roads. Patrols. Had to go around Colver." His good hand tightened on the oilcloth bundle. "Didn't want to risk one copy. So I made — " a racking cough — "so I made sure."

Jude knelt and took the bundle from him and held it against her own chest, and she looked at me over his ruined body, and she did not say *I told you so.* She didn't have to. Her eyes said it, gently, the way she did everything: *this is what the expensive thing buys.*

Then she bent to the work of saving him, and I went to stoke the fire, and my hands were shaking, and outside the river cracked like a rifle shot — long, rolling, unmistakable — the first true crack of break-up, far upstream but coming.

Coming for all of us, sooner than anyone had planned.
