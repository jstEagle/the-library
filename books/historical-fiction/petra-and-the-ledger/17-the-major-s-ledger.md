## 17. The Major's Ledger

Halloway had not slept in a bed in eleven days, but it was not the cold that kept him awake. It was the ledger.

He knew about it now. He had known since the inspection — since he'd stood in that workshop doorway with the provosts at his back and watched Jude Dunmore perform triage over a dying man's cot with hands so steady they seemed carved from something older than fear. She had looked at him across that room and understood, in one long second, that he knew. And she had not flinched. He had thought about that look every night since. Most officers would have flinched. Most officers would have lied. She had simply gone on binding the man's arm as if Halloway were furniture, as if the entire machinery of the army could be reduced to a thing to be ignored while more important work was done.

That was when he understood what she was. Not an idealist. Not a spy. A keeper. And keepers did not flinch.

He sat now in his quarters above the quartermaster's stores — a borrowed room, since the bridge had closed and stranded him on the wrong side of his own command — and took from beneath the false bottom of his campaign trunk a document he had shown to no living soul.

It was a ledger too.

Smaller than hers. Bound in grey canvas, army-issue, the kind used for ammunition inventories. The spine was cracked white from handling. Inside were names — forty-one of them — written in his own hand in three different inks, because he had begun it before he understood what he was doing and had continued after, and the inks told the story of that progression like tree rings tell a dry year.

The first name was Private Alfred Soames, Second Vellmere Rifles. Halloway remembered writing it. Remembered the order coming down from Division: casualty figures for the October advance to be consolidated, discrepancies reconciled, the rolls cleaned up before the parliamentary liaison arrived. *Cleaned up.* As if the dead were a stain. As if the arithmetic of grief were something you could tidy.

Soames had been struck from the consolidated report because he'd died of wounds after the reporting window closed — a technicality, a clerk's convenience, a lie by omission. Halloway had made the mark himself. He could still feel the nib drag. And afterward he had walked out behind the dressing station and been sick into the frozen nettles, and then washed his face with snow and gone back inside and done it again, and again, until there were nine names on the first page and something broken loose in his chest that never settled back into place.

He had started the list that same night. Not to undo anything — you could not unstrike a name, could not call back a telegram that was never sent, could not give a mother back a son the paperwork had decided never existed. He had started it because the alternative was to become the kind of man who could do that work and sleep soundly after, and some stubborn sediment at the bottom of him refused.

Forty-one names. Three years. Every one of them a man whose death he had erased or watched erased, recorded in secret the way other men recorded debts. Because that is what they were. Debts. And Halloway had come to understand, somewhere in the second winter, that the ledger was not his penance. Penance implied an ending. The ledger was interest accruing.

He wrapped it in oilcloth and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it sat like a second heart, heavier than the first.

* * *

The workshop smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river — that mineral breath that got into everything this close to the water in winter. Elias was not there. Halloway had chosen his hour deliberately. What he had to do required no witnesses beyond the one who mattered, and whatever passed between a major of the Second Vellmere and a deserter was better left unwitnessed anyway.

Jude was at the worktable, transcribing. Of course she was. She transcribed the way other people breathed — constantly, unconsciously, as though stopping would kill her. Her pen moved in small precise strokes. The lamplight caught the edge of her jaw. She did not look up when the door opened, which meant either that she had heard his boots and recognized them, or that she trusted the door's hinge to announce trouble loudly enough.

"Major." She still didn't look up.

"Miss Dunmore."

"You're supposed to be across the river."

"The river disagrees." He came to stand at the end of the table, and only then did she lift her head. There were shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold shadow's shadow. She had been working through the nights. They all had. The thaw was coming early this year; everyone felt it the way animals feel weather.

"I'm not here on official business," he said.

"You're always here on official business. You just don't always admit it."

"This time I'm not." He took off his glove, finger by finger, an unnecessary slowness that gave his hands something to do while his mouth found its courage. "I need to show you something. And I need you to understand what it costs me to show it, because I don't fully understand it myself yet."

She set down her pen. That small act — the pen lifted, the line abandoned mid-stroke — was the closest thing to surprise she allowed herself.

He drew the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat and laid it on the table between them. For a moment neither of them touched it. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the ice spoke — a long groaning note, low as a struck bell, rolling up from the river through the floorboards. Both of them glanced toward the window. Neither commented. The river had been talking all week. Everyone pretended it wasn't saying anything.

"Open it," he said.

She unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon and stopped when she saw the cover. Grey canvas. Army-issue. She looked up at him sharply, and he saw her arrive at the wrong conclusion first — that it was a requisition, an inventory, another instrument of the machine — and then watch her correct herself as she opened it and saw the names.

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door opens onto winter.

"Forty-one," he said. "That I know of. There may be more I've forgotten, which is worse, if you want the truth of it. Men whose names went through my hands and out the other side, and I didn't even have the decency to write them down. These are only the ones I couldn't live without recording."

She turned pages. Her fingers hovered over the entries without quite touching them, the way people touch relics. He watched her read his cramped, guilty hand and waited for the question.

"Why?" she said finally. Not accusing. Worse — genuinely asking.

"Because I struck the first name myself." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Alfred Soames. Died of wounds four days past the reporting window. One line through his name, one entry in the consolidated roll, and Alfred Soames became a rounding error. His mother received nothing. No telegram. No grave notice. As far as the War Office is concerned, her son walked off the face of the earth in October of 1915 and was never seen again." He looked at the first page over her shoulder, though he could have recited it. "I made the mark. And that night I discovered that a man can know exactly what he is and be unable to stop being it. So I wrote him down. And then I wrote down the next one. And the next. Forty-one entries in three years, Miss Dunmore, each one a small confession I never handed to anyone."

"And now you're handing it to me."

"Now I'm handing it to you."

"Why?"

It deserved a real answer, and he made himself give one. "Because yours will survive and mine won't. Because your ledger has a press behind it and a plan behind the press and a printer who'd die before letting it burn — and I say that knowing exactly who and what he is. Mine has a trunk with a false bottom and a major who may be transferred, court-martialed, or killed before spring. A record that lives in one place, in one man's keeping, isn't a record. It's a hostage." He paused. "And because I've spent three years keeping those names away from everyone, and I find I can't do it anymore. Secrecy was supposed to protect them. All it did was make me their only witness, and a witness who dies alone buries his testimony twice."

Jude closed the ledger gently, both hands flat on its cover, as if taking its pulse.

"There's a man in here," she said, turning back two pages, "— Corporal Edric Vane. My brother served beside him. Thomas wrote about him in letters. If Vane died, my mother's parish will want to know. Families are still waiting on these men, Major. Some of them have been waiting years for a name they'll never hear unless someone carries it to them."

"That's why it goes to you and not to a chaplain or a colonel or God. You carry them to people. I only carried them around."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then: "You understand what this makes you. If the provosts ever learn you kept this — if it's found with my papers, traced to your hand —"

"It will be traced to a dead major's sentimentality and filed accordingly. I've considered the arithmetic." He almost smiled. "Besides, I struck the names. Let the record show I also kept them. Let posterity split the difference."

She studied him then, fully, and he endured it — endured being seen by a woman whose opinion had quietly become the only audit he feared. Whatever she found in his face seemed to settle something, because she nodded once, briskly, and pulled her own ledger toward her across the table.

"Then we merge them," she said. "Tonight. Your forty-one go into mine in your presence, so you can confirm spellings, units, dates of death. Errors in a ledger like this are their own kind of cruelty — a name misspelled is a family sent searching in the wrong direction." She uncapped her ink bottle. "Sit down, Major. You're going to dictate."

Something loosened in his chest that he hadn't known was clenched. He sat.

* * *

They worked for three hours.

It was the strangest duty he had ever performed. Stranger than the falsified rolls, stranger than the inspections, stranger than standing on the near bank watching ice floes take out the bridge pilings one by one like teeth. He read aloud, entry by entry — name, rank, regiment, date, manner of death, the reason given for the omission — and she transcribed, and the scratch of her pen was the only sound in the room except the river muttering under the floor.

At the eleventh name, she stopped him.

"Lieutenant Harrow. You've written 'omitted — wounded list falsified to preserve unit strength figure.' Who ordered that?"

"Brigadier Cassel. Personally."

"And nothing was ever done."

"Cassel was decorated the following spring." Halloway held her gaze. "Write it down. Write who ordered it, and that he was decorated. If we're making a true record, Miss Dunmore, let's make it true all the way down. Names of the dead and names of the men who buried them on paper while they were still breathing."

She wrote it. He watched her write it, and felt something he could only describe as relief with teeth.

By the twentieth name the candle had burned down two inches. By the thirtieth his voice had gone rough, because reading forty-one deaths aloud in a warm room, to a listener who wrote each one down as if it mattered — as if each one *had* mattered all along — was a different act entirely from striking them silently in a cold office. Saying them aloud returned them to the world. That was the thing he had never understood until tonight: a name spoken is a body counted; a name written is a body mourned; and a name both spoken and written, witnessed by another living soul, is a body *grieved*. He had denied forty-one men their grieving. He could not restore it. But he could sit in a lamplit workshop and read them into a book that would outlast him, and perhaps that was worth the hours he'd spent being sick in frozen nettles. Perhaps not. Perhaps nothing was. But it was the only payment on offer, and he intended to pay it in full.

At the last entry — Private Alfred Soames, first in his book, last in hers by her insistence that the founder of a debt should close it — Jude set down her pen and blotted the page.

"There," she said softly. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one," he agreed.

"They'll be read someday. All of them. In a church, probably — Wren has ideas along those lines, and Wren's ideas have a way of becoming events. Names read aloud, families summoned." She looked up at him. "Your men will be grieved properly, Major. I give you my word on it."

Halloway found he could not immediately speak. He nodded instead, and stood, and buttoned his coat, and was nearly at the door when she called after him.

"Major. Why tonight? Why now, after three years of the trunk with the false bottom?"

He stood with his hand on the latch and considered lying, and found he had run out of lies he respected.

"Because the patrols are moving up the schedule," he said. "Because the burning detail will reach this quarter within days of the first break-up on the river — days, not weeks, whatever the official orders say. And because a man who has spent three years keeping a secret ought to spend at least one evening telling the truth before everything burns." He opened the door onto the blue dark and the smell of the river. "First crack will come early, Miss Dunmore. Sooner than any of us wants. Finish your copying. All of it."

The ice groaned again as he stepped out — longer this time, lower, a note with an ending in it — and Halloway stood a moment on the frozen mud listening, and understood that the deadline he had just spoken aloud was already ticking.
