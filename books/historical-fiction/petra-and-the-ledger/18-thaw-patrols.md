## 18. Thaw Patrols

The word came with the pigeons.

Not literally — the pigeon was a fat gray thing that had roosted in the workshop rafters since November and refused all entreaties to leave — but with the man who brought word. He came at dusk, stamping snow off his boots on the threshold like a man who had been walking for hours and wanted the world to know it. A courier from the southern garrison, one of Wren's people, a boy of maybe nineteen with a chilblained face and a way of looking at the floor as though it might be mined.

Jude took the message from him because Elias's hands were still bandaged and Bram was out hauling water, and because she had learned that in this workshop whoever was standing nearest became the officer of the moment. The paper was folded small, sealed with a dab of candle wax instead of proper sealing wax, which told her everything about how official it wasn't.

She read it twice by the light over the door before her hands stopped shaking enough for a third reading.

*Patrols moved up. Burning ordered at first break-up on the river — not full thaw, not spring orders. First break-up. Scouts report the ice going rotten at the bends already. You may have days.*

Days.

She folded the paper along its original creases and stood very still, listening to the sounds of the workshop: Elias's press under its tarp in the corner, breathing the particular silence of a machine at rest; the two convalescents asleep in the back room, one of them coughing in his sleep with a wet, deep sound; the wind working at the loose shutter; the river itself, three hundred yards away, doing something under the ice that she could feel through the soles of her boots if she let herself feel it — a low shifting groan, like a giant turning over in bed.

"Bad?" Elias asked from his chair by the stove. He'd been trying to hold a pen between two bandaged fingers, practicing, and had gotten ink on the wrappings. His hands were still swollen from the depot, the frostbite having taken some feeling in the left index finger that might or might not come back. The doctor who came through had used the word *might* a great deal.

"The burning comes early," Jude said. "First break-up."

Elias looked at his hands. Then he looked at the press. Then he did something she hadn't seen him do in all the months she'd known him: nothing. He simply sat there, empty, the way a man does when the last comfortable lie has been taken from him.

"When the ice goes," he said finally, "it goes fast. My father worked the ferry landings. He said you can stand on shore ice solid as a church floor in the morning and by noon hear it coming apart like crockery." He flexed his bandaged fingers experimentally and failed to make a fist. "We've been counting in weeks. We should have been counting in days."

"We're counting now," Jude said.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, so she did what she always did when she couldn't sleep: she worked. She sat at the table with the lamp turned low and copied names into the duplicate ledger — Halloway's names first, then hers, cross-checking against the master list, making sure no man appeared twice and none appeared once when he should appear twice. It was work that could not be rushed without risking errors, and errors were the one thing this whole enterprise could not survive. A name misspelled was a name lost. A date wrong was a widow sent looking in the wrong regimental archive forever.

But tonight her hand kept hurrying. Tonight the clock was in the room with her, audible in the tick of the cooling stove, in the drip — drip, God, already dripping — of meltwater finding a seam in the roof.

Bram found her there past midnight. He set down a cup of chicory substitute beside her elbow without being asked and stood looking at the pages.

"How many left?" he asked.

"Two hundred and six to copy clean. Forty-odd we haven't verified — men whose units are scattered and nobody living knows their Christian names for certain." She said it evenly, the way she recited it every night now, an inventory like ration counts. "If the press holds, Elias can pull copies of what's done in two nights' work. If the press doesn't hold—"

"It'll hold," Bram said. "He won't let it not hold."

"He nearly drowned getting the paper for it."

"Yes," Bram said, "and he'd do it again tomorrow. That's not a plan, Miss Dunmore. That's just him."

She almost smiled. Bram had a way of saying the truest things in the flattest voice, as though truth were weather and he merely reported it. Since his confession — since the night he told them about the printer in Marlowe Fen, the one whose address he had passed along and who had died for it — he had become the workshop's quietest and most reliable laborer. He hauled water. He fed the stove. He sat with the dying soldier in the back room through the worst nights so the rest of them could rest. He had not asked for forgiveness and they had not formally given it, and somehow the work had gone on anyway, which was perhaps the only kind of absolution any of them would get.

"Bram," she said. "When the patrols come. If they come while we're mid-copy. What do we save?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. "The ledger," he said. "Always the ledger. Presses can be built again. Names can't."

"That's what I think too."

"But he won't see it that way," Bram said. "And when it comes to it, you won't either. Nobody ever saves the sensible thing. They save the thing they love."

He took his cup and went back to the back room, and Jude sat with the lamp and listened to the river, and thought about the difference between a deadline you fear and a deadline you can hear.

* * *

In the morning the river made its opinion known.

They all went down to look — Jude and Elias and Bram and even the convalescents, propped on crutches or carried on a stretcher, because a thing like this you want witnesses for. The ice that had been white and seamless since November had gone the color of old bone. Along the southern bend, where the current ran strongest under the surface, the floes had begun to separate and grind, leaving seams of black water a foot wide, then two. And the sound — the sound was the thing. Not a crack, exactly. A conversation. Long groaning utterances that rose up through the ground and into the teeth, punctuated by sharp reports that everyone in the party flinched at, because after four years of war a sharp report meant something else entirely.

"First break-up," Elias said quietly. "That's it. That's the order being read aloud."

Halloway arrived within the hour. He came alone, no escort, which was itself a message — a major does not walk unguarded through a quarter slated for burning unless he has stopped caring about appearances. He had aged in the weeks since the bridge closed, or perhaps Jude had simply never looked properly before. There were new lines around his mouth and his uniform had the particular sag of a garment worn too many days without brushing.

"You got the word," he said. It wasn't a question. Wren's network was better than his own, and he knew it, and it clearly stung him.

"We got the word."

"I came to tell you the shape of it, because you deserve the shape of it." He spoke in the clipped cadence of a briefing, but his eyes kept moving — to the press under its tarp, to the crate where the ledger lived, to the smoke-hole, to the door, mapping exits the way soldiers do. "It won't be a formal detail. No provosts, no paperwork. When the order comes down it'll be a squad of twelve, maybe fifteen, with cans of oil and a cart for anything worth salvaging. They'll burn the tannery row first, then the granary annex, then this street. You'll get perhaps half an hour's warning from the smoke. Less if the wind is from the east."

"And after?"

"After, the patrol reports the quarter cleared of irregular records and unauthorized personnel." He said it flatly, and then, quieter: "*Unauthorized personnel*, Dunmore. That means anyone found here. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It isn't only the paper they'll burn."

The silence stretched. Down at the river, the ice gave a long groan like a ship settling.

"So we're gone before they come," Jude said. "All of us. The ledgers, the wounded, Bram, the press—"

"The press stays," Halloway said.

"No."

"Dunmore—"

"No." She heard her own voice go hard and didn't soften it. "You don't get to stand in this workshop and allocate what we lose. The press is the only reason any of this exists. If the copies fail, the press is the proof that copies *could* be made. If we're ever asked — when there's an inquiry, when someone finally asks why four hundred men vanished from the rolls — the press is evidence."

"A press is iron and screws. Evidence is paper. Get the paper out and let them melt the rest."

"That's your arithmetic," she said. "It isn't ours."

For a moment she thought he would argue. Instead he took off his cap — a gesture she had never seen him make indoors — and held it, turning it in his hands, a man holding the last piece of a uniform he was no longer sure fit.

"My arithmetic," he said, "kept a ledger of its own for two years. Two hundred and eleven names. I wrote them at night in my quarters and burned each page after copying because I was afraid of exactly what you're afraid of, and it bought me nothing except the knowledge that I knew better and did worse." He put the cap back on. "So take my arithmetic for whatever it's worth, which is less than nothing, and do the other thing. But do it *fast*. Because I am telling you as plainly as I can: the ice will give you days. The patrol will give you minutes."

* * *

They spent the afternoon measuring.

This was Elias's idea, and it was the right one: stop talking about the disaster in the abstract and start talking about it in pounds and inches. They laid everything out on the workshop floor like a shopkeeper taking stock. The master ledger. The duplicate, three-quarters finished. The loose verified sheets. The type case — Thomas's block among them, the little rectangle of lead with the letters T-H-O-M-A-S D-U-N-M-O-R-E cut into its face, which Elias had pressed into her palm weeks ago and which she now kept sewn inside her coat lining like a relic. The ink tin. The oilcloth. The photograph of her and Thomas on the Vellmere pier, taken the summer before everything.

Then Elias unwrapped his hands, wincing, and began dismantling the press's lighter components — the composing stick, the galley trays, the smaller brass fittings — laying them beside the pile.

"Elias," she said.

"I know what I'm doing."

"You can barely hold a pen."

"I can hold a screwdriver." He did hold one, awkwardly, between bandaged thumb and forefinger, demonstrating. "These parts weigh nothing together. Fifteen pounds. A satchel. And if Osk will take the ledgers, he can take fifteen pounds of brass."

"Osk takes three passengers and the paper. That was the bargain. Every pound is a question mark on that ice road, and you know it."

"Then I'll carry the brass myself and walk behind the sled like a mule," Elias said, and there it was — the flash in him, the stubbornness that had deserted an army rather than print one false roll, the same spine that had built a secret press in a requisitioned workshop and kept it running through inspection and shortage and flood. "Jude. Listen to me. Everything we've done, everything those names are worth, exists because a machine existed that could multiply them. If we save the paper and lose the means, then next winter — and there will be a next winter, there's always a next winter — somebody else starts with nothing. With a pen and a prayer. Give them the pen and the machine."

"And if the machine costs us the crossing? If the extra weight puts us on the ice an hour later, or makes Osk refuse the run altogether?"

"Then it costs us," Elias said. "I'd rather lose the crossing carrying everything that matters than win it carrying half."

"That's easy to say when you've decided you might not be the one walking."

The words came out sharper than she intended, and she saw them land. He went still. Outside, the river spoke again, long and low, and neither of them pretended not to hear it.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"No," she admitted. "It's not. I'm sorry. I'm—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I count steps in my sleep now. Did you know that? I dream the ice road and I count, and every time I wake up before the far bank. Every single time. And now I'm supposed to add fifteen pounds of brass to the dream."

Elias crossed the room — slowly, because his balance was still bad — and put his bandaged hand over hers where it lay on the table. The pressure was slight, a bird's weight.

"Then we finish faster," he said. "That's all. That's the whole answer. We finish faster than the river. Two hundred and six names. At forty a night, that's five nights. The ice won't beat five nights."

"The ice," Jude said, "is not negotiating with us."

"No," Elias agreed. "Which is why we don't negotiate with ourselves."

* * *

They worked by lamplight until the lamp oil ran low, and then by candle stub, and then by the strange green-gray light of the moon coming up over the breaking river. Bram set type for the final forms while Elias directed with his voice, calling measurements from his chair, and the press — their press, the deserter's press, the machine that had committed more treason in its quiet way than most regiments — stood ready under its tarp like a horse saddled for a night ride.

Near dawn, Jude stepped outside to breathe air that wasn't ink and lamp-black, and stopped on the threshold.

The eastern sky was doing something it hadn't done in months. It was pink. Actual pink, thin as watercolor, above the black line of the hills. And below it, the river had opened another seam in the night — she could hear the water moving now, actually moving, a whispering underneath the groans, the sound of the current reclaiming its own.

From somewhere downstream, faint but unmistakable, came a sound she had been dreading without knowing she dreaded it: voices. Men's voices, carrying over the ice. A patrol, or scouts, or just soldiers posted to watch the river the way everyone watched the river now, waiting for it to decide the timetable for them.

Bram came up beside her, a water bucket forgotten in his hand.

"Hear that?" he said.

"I hear it."

"Not the men. Underneath." He tilted his head. "That ticking. Like a clock."

And she heard it — she would hear it for the rest of her life, she suspected, whenever water froze or thawed anywhere near her: the ice ticking to itself in the cold morning, millions of small fractures propagating through the floes, the river counting down in a language older than any ledger.

"Five nights," she said aloud, to Bram, to the river, to herself. "We need five nights."

The ice ticked on, indifferent, keeping its own count — and from the east, thin and getting thinner, she could swear she smelled smoke already, though the burning was days away and the wind was wrong and she knew, she *knew*, it was only someone's stove. Knowing changed nothing. The smell stayed in her nose all day.
