## 19. The Press Must Break

The press had a voice.

Not the clatter of the lever or the hiss of ink on dampened paper — anyone's machine had that. I meant the voice underneath it, the one you only heard if you'd spent eleven years with your palms against the iron the way other men put their hands on a wife's shoulders. A low settling note when the temperature changed, like an animal shifting in its sleep. The tick of the frame cooling after a long run. On bad nights in the workshop, with the wind coming off the river and the stove down to its last coal, that voice was the difference between solitude and company.

I lay awake listening to it now, because tomorrow — or the day after, depending on how fast the thaw patrols moved — I was going to take it apart.

"You're doing it again," Jude said from her cot across the room. She didn't open her eyes. "Talking to it."

"I wasn't talking."

"You were breathing at it in a way I've learned to mistrust."

I almost laughed. That was the trick of Jude Dunmore: she could find the joke inside a grief without making the grief smaller. I'd watched her do it over the ledger for months — a dry word here, a wry line there — and every time it landed, I understood a little better why she kept the names. Not because she was made of iron. Because she refused to let sorrow have the last word.

But tonight there was no joke big enough to sit on top of what I had to tell her, so I told her plainly.

"It won't fit through Osk's hatch," I said. "The ferryman came back at dusk. I asked him straight out. His boat has a cargo well two feet wide at the throat. The platen alone is wider than that. Even broken into halves it's too heavy for the ice run he's planning — he'd sit low enough to swamp before he cleared the first floe."

Jude sat up. In the dark I could see only the outline of her, the way she held very still when she was deciding something. She'd been holding still a lot lately. The whole quarter held still these days, listening for boots.

"So we don't move the press," she said. "We move the copies. We've known that since the bridge closed."

"We've *hoped* that since the bridge closed." I swung my legs off the cot and went to the press. I couldn't help it; my feet knew the way the way they knew the way to water. "There's a difference. Hoping says maybe the machine goes south someday, after the thaw, when the roads clear, when someone with a cart and a reason comes through. Knowing says—" I laid my hand flat on the bed of the press. Cold iron. Always cold iron, even in August. "Knowing says the machine stays here, and everything the army can't carry away, it burns."

"Then it burns," Jude said quietly. "It bought us the copies. It did its work."

*It did its work.* As if it were a mule, a coat, a pair of boots worn through at the heel. I understood why she said it. To her the ledger was the living thing and the press was just the lung that fed it. But she hadn't built the lung.

* * *

I bought the press in the spring of 1906 with money I'd saved setting type for a newspaper whose editor watered his politics like a houseplant — a little sun on whichever side the window faced. It was a Columbian, secondhand, older than I was by twenty years, cast in a foundry that no longer existed by men who were certainly dead. The eagle on the lever had lost half its beak to some earlier owner's carelessness, and the counterweights were painted a green that had faded to the color of old pond water. Every part of it was wrong and worn and mine.

I taught myself its temper the way you learn a river. Too much packing under the tympan and it threw its impression soft on the left. Ink too warm in summer, too stiff in winter; it wanted the shop kept at a temperature no rational man would keep a shop. It pulled to the right on long forms, so you learned to shave your lockup a hair past center and let it drift home. For years I thought of these as flaws. Later I understood they were a language, and that the press and I had become fluent in each other, and that this — not the newspaper, not the pamphlets, not even the ledger — was the closest thing to a marriage I would ever make.

Then came the war, and the order that broke me.

They brought me the rolls in the autumn of 1915. A captain with clean gloves and a clerk's smile, and a stack of muster sheets with names struck through in red — struck before printing, struck deliberately — and instructions that the printed rolls show those men as present for duty. Men who were dead, or dying, or missing in the mud somewhere between Vellmere and the coast. The army needed them alive on paper. Paper was cheaper than truth.

I set the type. That's the sentence I have never managed to say aloud without my stomach turning. I set the type. I locked the form. I inked the rollers. And then, at the last moment, with the lever in my hand and the first sheet waiting under the platen, I stopped. Not out of courage — I want to be honest, even alone in my own head, that it wasn't courage. It was the press. It was eleven years of knowing exactly how a lie looks when it comes off a forme, the slight thickening of a struck name's ghost beneath the paper, and I could not make my hands feed the first lie of my life through a machine I loved.

I walked out the back and kept walking, and by the time I stopped walking I was a deserter, and the press — the army seized the printshop, but the press itself, thank God, thank God, had been crated for transport two days prior and sat forgotten in a freight shed while the front collapsed around everyone's schedules — followed me here by ways I will never fully be able to explain to a court, involving a bribed carter, a forged manifest, and three weeks of sleeping in hay.

So: the press was my desertion and my penance both. It was the thing I fled with instead of honor, and the thing that let me earn a little honor back, one sheet of names at a time. When I pulled the first proof of Jude's ledger that first night — her handwriting converted to metal, Thomas Dunmore standing up out of the paper in ink — I felt something I can only call forgiveness arriving through my fingertips.

Tomorrow I was going to take it apart with a wrench.

* * *

Jude found me at first light already working, which surprised her, and I'll admit it surprised me. I'd expected to stand in front of the thing all morning like a man at a graveside. Instead my hands had started without asking me.

"You're sure?" she said. She stood in the doorway with two tin cups of chicory steam, watching me loosen the first of the side bolts.

"No," I said. "But certainty isn't on offer this winter. Come hold this lamp."

Dismantling a press is a science of sequence. You don't simply unbolt it; you walk it backward through its own assembly, and every step assumes the ones before. The counterweights come off first — each one near ninety pounds of painted iron, and my fingers, still stiff from the ice in February, complained about every nut. Then the frisking fingers and the tympan, the platen eased forward off its gibs with wedges and prayer, the carriage slid free of the tracks, the tracks themselves unshipped from the bed. The frame last. The frame never. Some part of me had planned, without telling the rest of me, to leave the frame standing as long as possible, the way a man keeps his coat on because taking it off means admitting the cold.

We worked most of the morning. Bram helped where his hands allowed — the boy had come back to us hollow-eyed and quieter than before his disappearance, carrying whatever happened between him and the chaplain like a stone he hadn't decided whether to swallow — and he was gentler with the parts than I expected. He handled the type cases like a man handling eggs he'd once dropped.

"The copies are packed?" I asked him.

"Oilcloth. Weighted. Three bundles." He hesitated. "The chaplain's page is in the middle bundle, like you said. So if any one bundle is lost—"

"Any two can survive losing the third," I finished. "Good."

He nodded and went back to wrapping the smaller furniture in sacking. And I turned back to the frame, and the morning caught up with me all at once.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said to Jude, the arithmetic running underneath the wrench-work all night: the press was the only machine in forty miles that could set and strike this ledger. When the war ended — if it ended — there would be mothers in Vellmere and towns upriver and villages south of the marshes who would want those names in their own hands, and there would be no way to give the names to them except by hand-copying, which meant rumor, which meant error, which meant a grandmother in some kitchen being told her son died at a place nobody could spell. The press was not a machine. It was the difference between memory and record. And I was going to break it apart and leave it for the fire, and the copies we carried out would be all there ever was, until some other fool with savings and stubbornness built another one in another town, years from now, for reasons he wouldn't fully understand himself.

"Elias." Jude's voice, close. I hadn't heard her cross the room. "Your hands stopped."

"They stopped."

"Talk to me."

I looked at the frame. Green paint, pond-water color, the eagle with its ruined beak staring down at me from the lever housing with the expression iron always has, which is none, which is worse than reproach.

"I deserted the army to keep this machine from printing lies," I said. "Eleven years later I'm taking it apart with my own hands so the truth can outrun the fire. There's a symmetry in that that I'd admire if it didn't feel like being flayed in alphabetical order."

Jude set down her cup. She came and stood beside me, shoulder nearly touching mine, and looked at the press the way she looked at the pages — the full attention, the reading attention.

"My brother used to say," she said slowly, "that a name written down is a body you can't bury wrong." She touched the frame, once, lightly, the way you touch a coffin lid. "This machine gave four hundred bodies their proper graves. That's not destroyed by breaking it. That's *completed* by it. A tool that's done its work isn't a corpse, Elias. It's a harvest."

"And the field?"

"The field gets burned either way." She met my eyes. "You know that. Halloway warned us. The patrols don't requisition anymore — they burn. Whatever we leave, we leave to smoke. The only choice we have is what we carry out."

I knew it. Of course I knew it. But knowing a thing and consenting to it are different countries, and I stood at the border for a long moment, my hand on the cold iron, saying goodbye to the only witness of my whole adult life — the machine that had known my worst hour and my best, that had printed propaganda and poetry and falsified rolls and four hundred true names, that had never once judged me because iron doesn't judge, it only remembers pressure.

Then I picked up the wedge and drove the first pin out of the main shaft.

The sound it made was nothing. A small knock of metal on metal, duller than I'd imagined, almost apologetic. Eleven years, and the end of it sounded like tapping a pipe. I think that's what undid me — not the weight of the parts coming off, but the lightness, the ease, the machine letting go of itself as if it had been waiting for permission. Within an hour the platen was off and lying on sacking. By mid-afternoon the carriage was free and the bed stood bare, scarred steel shining where a decade of formes had rested, and I sat down on the floor beside it because my legs had opinions of their own.

Bram and Jude took the pieces to the cellar hatch, wrapping each one in sacking, burying the bolts in a flour sack marked FLOUR in Bram's careful capitals. If the patrols dug, they'd dig. But soldiers burn fast and search slow; fire is quicker than curiosity. It was the best plan available, and the best plan available is the national product of wartime.

* * *

Night came early, the way it does in March when the sky can't decide. The stove was down to embers. The workshop stood strangely empty around us — the space where the press had been was a rectangle of cleaner floorboards, pale wood in the shape of everything I'd been since I was twenty-three.

Jude sat beside me on the floor. Neither of us spoke for a while. Outside, far off, the river made its noise — that deep irregular groaning it had started a week ago, the ice talking in its sleep, louder every night. Everyone in the quarter heard it now. Nobody mentioned it. It was the kind of sound you mention and then can't stop hearing.

"There's something else," I said finally. "Something I should have said when we packed the type."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the thousand things about her.

I reached into my shirt pocket and took out the piece of type I'd been carrying for two days, wrapped in a scrap of proof paper. I unwrapped it. Even in the ember-light, even sideways, the letter was legible the way only type is legible — reversed and perfect and waiting to be true.

A D. One character. From the case I'd set Thomas Dunmore's name from, eleven weeks ago, the night the ledger began to exist twice — once in ink, once in metal.

"When the press comes apart," I said, "the type scatters. That's how it goes. Cases get dumped, mixed, melted. Somewhere south of here there's a printer who'll set a wedding announcement with a letter that stood in your brother's name, and he'll never know." I closed my fist around it. "I pulled this one out. I don't know why. I told myself it was sentiment. But I think — I think I wanted proof that something of the machine survives that isn't iron. Something that fits in a pocket. Something that can cross the ice."

Jude took it from me. She turned it in her fingers, reading it the way she read everything, thoroughly, and then she pressed it into my palm again and folded my fingers shut over it.

"You keep it," she said. "You carried it out of the case. That makes it yours."

"It belongs to Thomas."

"It belonged to Thomas. Now it's the letter D, Elias. Letters outlive their words. That's the entire point of them." She almost smiled. "Keep it. And when you build the next press — the one in the free town, the one you haven't admitted to me yet that you're already designing in your head —"

I laughed despite everything, because she was right, I had been, since roughly noon.

"— you start it with that letter in the first forme. First page. His initial. Let him set the order of the whole book."

The groan came up through the floor then — the river, longer and lower than any night before, a sound less like ice now and more like a door opening somewhere under the world. We both went still. Down in the cellar, three oilcloth bundles waited with four hundred names between them, and across the quarter a burning detail was assembling somewhere we couldn't see, and above the town the last hard dark of winter sat on the rooftops like a lid.

Jude rose and lit the lamp. "One more pull tonight," she said. "There are nine pages left to strike. Nine pages, Elias. While we still have ink and lamplight and hands."

And that was the thing, in the end. The press was broken, but it wasn't dead — not tonight, not yet. There was still one forme to lock, one last run to make, and I got up off the pale rectangle of floor where my life had stood for eleven years and went to the type case, and my hands, which had shaken all afternoon over wrenches, were steady as they'd ever been.

The first letter I set was the D.

Before I'd locked half the forme, boots sounded on the lane outside — running boots, more than one pair, and then Bram's fist hammering the door, and his voice through the wood, cracked and urgent:

"They're moving up the east road. Tonight. They're coming *tonight*."
