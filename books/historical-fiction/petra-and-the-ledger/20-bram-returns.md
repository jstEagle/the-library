## 20. Bram Returns

The road south was a rumor and the river was a wall, and I walked between them for six days on feet that stopped belonging to me somewhere past the second one.

I had left with a single page of Jude Dunmore's ledger folded into oilcloth against my chest, tucked under my shirt where it sat like a coal that wouldn't cool. One page. Forty-one names. I had taken it in the night like a thief, which is what I was, and what I had been before, and what I would be until somebody decided otherwise.

Here is what nobody tells you about informing: it doesn't feel like anything at the time. That's the horror of it. In Corren, two winters back, a provost sergeant bought me a drink and asked me casual questions about a printer named Aldous Vey who ran a press behind the tannery, and I answered them, and answering felt exactly like breathing. Vey printed casualty rolls that didn't match the army's. The sergeant said it was sedition. I said yes, sir, I'd noticed that too. Three days later they took Vey's press apart with crowbars and took Vey himself to a yard behind the garrison, and I stood in the crowd and watched, and my hands were cold, and that was all I felt. Cold hands. A man's whole life ending twenty feet from me and my body's only comment was cold hands.

The feeling came later. It always comes later. It came to me in the workshop by the river when Elias looked at me across the type cases — Elias, who prints truth the way other men breathe — and I understood that I had done to someone else's Elias what the army did to mine, except worse, because Vey hadn't even been guilty of anything but arithmetic.

So when I took the page, I wasn't stealing. Or I was, but theft can go two directions.

* * *

I need to say how bad the walking was, because if I soften it I'm lying again, and lying is the trade I'm trying to quit.

Six days. The first two were hard in the ordinary way — cold, hunger, the road rutted with supply traffic going the wrong direction for me. On the third day the fever found me. I don't know from what; there was a cut on my shin from the ice at the depot that had gone angry and red, and the red was climbing. By the fourth day I was talking to people who weren't there. I remember arguing with my mother about the price of boots. I remember apologizing to Aldous Vey over and over, out loud, to empty fields, and meaning it more than I have ever meant anything, and knowing that meaning it changed nothing for him, because he was dead, and dead men don't take apologies.

What kept me moving was the page. Not the paper itself — though I'll admit I'd grown superstitious about it, checking it at every halt, terrified of losing it to a stream or a search detail — but what it meant. Forty-one names of men the army had erased. If I dropped it in a ditch, those men went back to being nobody. And I knew something about being made into nobody. I'd made Vey into nobody. I'd volunteered for the work.

On the fifth day I heard bells. Church bells, wrong-sounding, thin, and I followed them off the road because fever logic told me bells meant sanctuary and sanctuary meant the chaplain.

Wren. The name came to me the way names come in fever — complete, certain, unearned. Wren, the chaplain who'd been through Vellmere in the autumn, who'd argued with a major about casualty lists loud enough that orderlies talked about it for a week. Wren, who was stationed now at a garrison church south of the quarantine line, a man with a reputation for asking questions in a voice too gentle to punish.

I found the church at dusk on the sixth day. I don't remember arriving. I remember stone, and candle-smell, and then nothing.

* * *

When I woke, I was under blankets that smelled of woodsmoke and wax, and a man was sitting beside me reading, and the light through the window said morning, and my shin was bandaged and my head was clear enough to be afraid.

"You're awake," the man said. He was younger than I expected, gray at the temples already but young in the hands and the eyes. "You collapsed against the door. You've been talking for two days."

"I'm sorry," I said, because it was the only sentence I owned.

"Most of it was apologies." He set the book down. "To a man named Vey."

My whole body went still. The blanket might as well have been a shroud.

"He's dead," I said.

"Yes. I know. I wrote to his widow last spring." Wren leaned forward. "You informed on him."

It wasn't a question. I stared at him. "How could you possibly—"

"Because you said his name forty times in your sleep, and you said *I told them* after every one, and no innocent man says *I told them* like a prayer." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. Cruelty I could fight. This was just a man stating weather. "And because when I searched your shirt — you were burning, I needed to know what you carried — I found this."

He held up the oilcloth packet. My hand moved before my mind did, snatching at air.

"Forty-one names," Wren said. "Cross-referenced against dates and units. Written in a woman's hand, corrected in a second hand, annotated in a third. Do you know what this is?"

"It's not mine to explain."

"No," he agreed. "But it's mine to read. And I've read it. Twice." He turned the packet over in his fingers, careful, the way you handle something that has weight beyond its weight. "There's a man here, Private Ansel Rooke, eleventh battalion, listed as *returned to duty* in the official roll of October ninth. I buried Ansel Rooke on October eighth. I signed his card myself. I have spent four months thinking I was mad — that I'd misremembered, that grief had bent my ledger — and here is a stranger's handwriting telling me I buried a man the army says is alive."

His voice cracked on the last word. Just once, just slightly, and then it was steady again, and I understood that this man had been carrying his own version of my guilt, except his was innocence, which is its own kind of wound.

"So," Wren said. "Tell me where this came from. All of it. And understand that I am a chaplain, not a provost, and that whatever you tell me stays inside these walls unless the names themselves require otherwise."

So I told him. The workshop. Jude and her ledger, the names the officers struck. Elias and the press. The requisition order, the thaw, the burning that was coming. Halloway and his own list — I didn't know about that yet, actually; that came later — so I told him what I knew: the bridge closed, the copies being made, the plan fraying at the edges because everything frays in winter. And I told him about Vey. All of it. The drink, the questions, the answers, the yard. I told it plainly because I had decided somewhere on the road, between the fever-arguments and the ditches, that if I ever got the chance to do one true thing in my life, I would not decorate it.

Wren listened without interrupting. When I finished, the silence went on long enough that I counted the cracks in the ceiling plaster.

"You walked six days with a poisoned leg," he said finally, "to deliver one page."

"The rest are coming. They're copying it. The press—" I stopped. "They're doing what they can. But paper runs short and the patrols will burn the quarter at break-up, and someone outside needs to hold names too, somewhere they can't be burned. I thought — I thought maybe a church. I thought maybe you."

"Why you?" he asked. "Why does it fall to you?"

"Because I owe a debt I can't pay to a dead man," I said. "And because everyone else in that workshop has given more than I have any right to ask. Jude gave her brother. Elias gave his name, his trade, his safety. The major gave — I don't know what the major's given, but it's written on him. Me, I've only ever taken. So I took a page and I ran the wrong way with it, toward instead of away, and that's the first thing I've ever carried for anybody." My voice went rough. "It's not much. I know it's not much. But it's what I've got."

Wren was quiet again. Then he stood, and crossed to the small table by the window, and opened a drawer, and took out paper — good paper, rag paper, the kind that survives — and a pen.

"Then we start now," he said. "While your fever's broken and before anyone asks why an orderly from the river quarter is sleeping in my vestry. Tell me the forty-one names again, slowly. I'm going to write them into the parish register, interleaved with baptisms, where no clerk will look for dead soldiers. Then you're going to rest, and eat, and heal, because when the rest of that ledger comes south — and it will come, if half of what you've told me is true — I'll need a courier with legs that work."

"And after that?" I asked.

"After that," Wren said, dipping the pen, "we see whether a man can be more than his worst act. I don't know the answer. I've never tested it this far." He glanced up at me. "Have you?"

"No," I said. "That's why I walked."

* * *

I stayed at the church nine days. Wren fed me from his own rations and dressed my leg twice daily and never once called me brave, which I appreciated, because I knew what I was. I was a coward who had finally found a direction worth running in. Courage would have been staying in Corren and standing in front of Vey's press. Everything since had been interest payments on that failure.

But I learned things in those nine days. I learned that Wren kept a list of his own — burial records, cross-checked against official rolls, discrepancies marked in red — and that his list was longer than my stolen page. I learned that the garrison mail clerk owed him a favor and could move packets south without inspection. I learned that a church register, properly handled, is the best hiding place in the world, because nobody suspects the book that records births of also recording the erased.

And on the ninth day, a boy arrived half-frozen with a message: the river quarter was holding, the copies were nearly done, and a chaplain's gamble was being planned — Wren himself would carry half the ledger south through the lines, and the rest would go another way, over the ice if it came to that.

Wren read the message twice and burned it in the stove.

"They'll want to know if you reached me," he said. "And whether I'll take the risk."

"What will you tell them?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "I'll tell them yes. I'll tell them a man crawled out of a fever to put forty-one names in my hands, and that a debt that size doesn't get refused." He paused. "I'll also tell them you're alive, and waiting, and ready to carry whatever comes next. Whether they forgive you is their choice. I can't make it for them."

"I know," I said. "I'm not asking them to. I'm asking for work."

"That," said Wren, "is the only honest request a man in your position can make." He began writing his reply. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow you start north again — the mail clerk can get you as far as the quarantine post, and after that it's your legs and God's ice."

North. Back toward the burning quarter, the closing trap, the people whose trust I had cracked open like a dropped plate. Jude would want to believe in me — she was built that way, faith first, evidence second — and her believing would be harder to face than her hatred. Hatred I deserved. Belief I'd have to earn daily, hourly, forever, and might still drop.

Elias wouldn't forgive me at all. Not soon. Maybe not ever. I'd watched him with the press; I knew what Vey's press had been to Vey. Some debts don't convert.

But the page was copied safe into a parish register, and Wren was going south with fire in his collar, and there was a ledger on the far side of a frozen river that needed every pair of working hands before the thaw came to erase it a second time.

I lay down under the woodsmoke blankets and let myself sleep, truly sleep, for the first time since I'd run.

Tomorrow, north. Tomorrow, the choice again — offered, not granted. Forgiveness, I was learning, isn't a thing you receive. It's a thing you keep showing up for until somebody decides you've earned the word.

The ice would decide how long I had.
