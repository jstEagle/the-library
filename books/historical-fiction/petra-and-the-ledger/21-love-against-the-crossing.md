## 21. Love Against the Crossing

Halloway came at dusk, and he came the way he always came — too straight, too visible, a man who had never learned to move like a guilty thing. I saw him from the workshop window before Bram's knock confirmed it: a long coat, a staff officer's posture, snow gathering on his shoulders because he would not stand under the eave like an ordinary man waiting for an ordinary door.

"He's alone," Bram said. "I checked."

"Open it anyway."

The cold came in with him, rolling off his coat like fog off the river. He stamped once, twice, and looked around the workshop with the expression of a man taking inventory of a place he already knew he shouldn't be in. The press stood under its canvas in the corner. The type cases were stacked where we'd hidden them behind the flour barrels. Two convalescents slept in the loft above, breathing slow and even, and the smell of ink and lamp oil and boiled barley hung in the air like a signature none of us could wash off.

"Major," I said.

"Miss Dunmore." He unwound his scarf. His hands were red at the knuckles. "I need ten minutes and your silence, and I'll take them in that order."

Elias came out of the back room wiping ink from his fingers with a rag that only made them blacker. He stopped when he saw Halloway. The two men regarded each other across the workshop the way two dogs regard each other across a frozen yard — no movement, all measurement.

"You're stranded," Elias said. "Since the bridge closed."

"I'm stranded since the bridge closed." Halloway didn't deny it, didn't dress it up. That was something about him I'd learned over the winter: he lied the way most men breathe, automatically, but when it mattered — when the truth had weight enough to matter — he set the lie down like a pack he was tired of carrying. "And being stranded has made me useful. There's a ferryman on the south bend. Osk. Old man, flat-bottomed boat, a grudge against the army you could heat a barracks with. He says he can run the ice channel if the wind holds from the west for one night. Three passengers. No more. No cargo worth mentioning — the boat rides too high as it is."

Three passengers.

I heard the number land in the room like a coin dropped on stone. Elias heard it too. I could tell by the way he went still, the rag forgotten in his blackened hands.

"When?" I asked.

"Tomorrow night. Maybe the night after. The ice is talking already — you've heard it. Once it starts breaking in earnest, even Osk won't gamble." Halloway turned to face me fully, and I saw what the winter had done to him. He'd arrived in Vellmere in autumn as a man carved out of regulation oak, every line of him squared and sanded. Now there were cracks running through the grain, and he wasn't hiding them anymore. "Jude. I'm asking you to come."

Not Miss Dunmore. Not the ledger-keeper, not the woman who asked questions officers hated. Jude.

"I have work here," I said.

"The work is copied. Wren takes half south within days. The rest—" He gestured at the press, the crates, the whole fragile architecture of paper and ink we'd built against the army's forgetting. "The rest burns with this quarter whether you're standing in it or not. You know that. I've told you what the thaw patrols are. They don't inspect. They don't inventory. They burn."

"And the names?"

"Are in your head and in Wren's saddlebag and in the copies drying upstairs. Carrying the original across the ice doesn't save the names. It saves the paper they were first written on." He paused, and his voice dropped into something quieter, less officer and more man. "It saves you."

In the loft above, one of the convalescents coughed — a wet, deep sound, the kind of cough that had been deciding things all winter. Nobody spoke until it settled.

"Elias," I said.

He flinched. Just slightly. A printer's flinch — the small correction of a letter set wrong, caught before the impression is made.

"She should ask you," Halloway said, "because I can't answer for you. But I'll say my piece first, since I came through the cold to say it." He squared himself, and for a moment I thought he was going to give me a speech, some polished thing about duty and safety and the future. Instead what came out was plain and unpolished and worse. "I struck names myself. Eleven of them. I wrote them down after, in a book I kept in my kit like a wound I couldn't stop touching. You know this. What you may not know is that every one of those eleven had a mother who got a telegram saying *missing* instead of *dead*, and every one of those mothers died a little differently because of what I did, and I cannot undo it. But you — you've spent a winter undoing it for other men. For Thomas. If you die on this river or in this fire, then the only person who ever fought the erasing gets erased herself, and I will not—"

His voice caught. Actually caught, in his throat, like a page tearing.

"I will not watch that happen," he finished. "Come across. Marry me or don't. Argue with me the rest of your life if you want. Just be alive on the far bank while you do it."

The lamp hissed. Snow ticked against the window glass. Somewhere out on the river, low and long, the ice groaned — not cracking yet, just shifting its weight, a giant turning over in sleep.

I looked at Elias.

* * *

He hadn't moved. That was the thing about Elias — he could go so still that you forgot he was there, a habit I supposed he'd learned in the years of printing things that could hang him. Deserters learn stillness. Men who set forbidden type learn it. And now he stood by the press he'd built with his own hands, the press that had given me my ledger, my purpose, my winter, and he let the silence stretch until Halloway shifted his weight and the floorboard creaked.

"Say it," I said to him. "Whatever you're going to say. Say it now."

"You know what I'm going to say."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it in your voice, not mine."

He came out from behind the press slowly. His hands — I looked at them without meaning to, the hands that had been frostbitten at the drowned depot, the hands that still curled stiff in the cold mornings, the hands that pulled the lever four hundred times a night — hung at his sides like tools put down.

"Halloway's right," Elias said.

"Don't."

"He's right. Listen to me. The copies are good but they're copies. Ink on salvage paper, printed fast, some of them smudged — you know how smudged, you've been pulling sheets by lamplight with your eyes bleeding. The original is the proof. The original is the thing with your hand in it, every entry, every correction, every name written while the man was still breathing or an hour after he stopped. When the inquiry comes — and it will come, Halloway's going to make sure it comes — they'll want the original. They'll want *you*." He stopped in front of me, close enough that I could see the ink ground into the creases of his knuckles, stains that would never come out, that had become part of him the way calluses become part of a rower's palms. "You go."

"With you."

"No."

"Elias—"

"Look at my hands, Jude." He held them up, turned them over, palms and backs, an honest exhibition. "Two weeks ago I couldn't hold a composing stick for more than a minute. Yesterday I dropped a quoine and couldn't pick it up. Osk's crossing isn't a ferry ride — it's a run across shore ice that's already talking, in the dark, carrying whatever you've got strapped to you. You'd end up dragging me off a crack in the river, and then neither of us crosses, and then everything burns and everyone drowns and the names go into the water with us." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I've done the arithmetic. I do it every morning. It comes out the same every time."

"There are three places," I said. "Three. You, me, and—"

"Bram." He said it gently. "Bram goes. He's earned it worse than either of us — he carried Wren's page forty miles through the snow with a fever, and he's got a brother in Corvane who'll take him in. And before you argue: yes, I know what he did in Harrowgate. I know what informing cost that other printer. I also know what his penance cost him, and I watched him pay it. He goes."

"And you stay here with a press you can't lift and a quarter that's going to burn."

"I stay here with a press I'm going to break." Something moved through his face then — grief, yes, but underneath it a strange lightness, almost relief, the look of a man who has finally been given permission to set down a load. "You think I haven't thought about this? Every night since the ice started? This machine is the only thing I've ever made that mattered, and I love it the way you love the ledger, and that is exactly why it can't fall into their hands intact. They'll use it. They'll print their clean rolls on it, their tidy lies, and my press will spend the rest of the war forging the very thing we built it to fight. No. If I can't carry it out, I'll break it. Every screw, every platen. I'll melt the type down if I have to." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and a true one. "A printer's funeral. Some men get flags. I get to smash my own machine. There are worse ends."

"You could rebuild. In the free towns, you said — you told me yourself, a small press, a back room—"

"On the far side of a river I can't cross." He took my hands then, both of mine in both of his, and his grip was weaker than it had been in autumn and I felt that weakness like a knife. "Jude. Look at me."

I looked at him.

"I deserted because they ordered me to print a lie, and I chose the truth and lost everything — my rank, my trade, my name, my country's tolerance. Everything except this." He pressed my hands together between his, folding my fingers closed as if he were closing a book. "And then you walked into my shop with a stolen notebook full of dead men's names, and I found out the truth had a sister, and her name was you. I am not telling you to go because I don't want you. I am telling you to go because wanting you is the first selfish thing I've felt in six years and I refuse to let it kill you."

Behind us, Halloway had turned away, studying the type cases with the fierce concentration of a man giving privacy he had no right to give. Bram had appeared at the foot of the loft ladder at some point — I hadn't seen him come down — and stood with his cap in his hands, watching us, his eyes wet and furious with the effort of staying silent.

"The boat leaves tomorrow night," Halloway said quietly, to the type cases rather than to us. "South bend, below the willows. Osk waits one hour past moonrise and not a minute more. Wind from the west or he doesn't run at all — you'll hear it in the wires on the telegraph pole. If they sing, he runs."

"If they sing," Elias repeated.

I stood there with my hands folded shut inside Elias's ruined ones, and I understood that the choice had been made for me, and that this was the cruelest kindness anyone had ever done me. He had not asked. He had decided. He had taken the one decision that was mine to make and lifted it out of my hands the way you lift a hot pan from someone about to drop it — and I loved him for it and I would never forgive him for it, both at once, both forever.

"Then I'll pack tonight," I said.

Elias nodded once, briskly, a foreman approving a job well set. But his eyes were shining, and when he turned away toward the press I saw his shoulders shake, once, before he mastered them.

* * *

I packed by lamplight while the others pretended to sleep.

The ledger went first — wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, sized against my chest to know how it would sit beneath my coat. Then the second copy, the one Wren wouldn't take, insurance against the road. Ink cake, two pencils, the photograph of Thomas in his enlistment uniform looking younger than I could bear. Ration tins: five. A tin cup. Bram's spare socks, because Bram would forget them and complain the entire crossing.

At the bottom of my satchel, wrapped in a scrap of newsprint, I found the thing Elias must have slipped in while I'd been saying goodbye to Halloway at the door: a single type block, lead-bright, cool against my palm. I tilted it to the lamp.

**T** — no. I turned it over. The face read:

**THOMAS DUNMORE**

Set in twelve-point, the way a casualty roll sets a name. He had composed it — when, I couldn't imagine, unless it was one of the nights I'd slept at the type case and he'd worked on — and locked it in its own little chase, a name with no body, a line of type that would print true forever no matter what happened to the man it named.

I sat down on the crate and held it and did not cry, because crying would wake the convalescents, and because I had learned this winter that some griefs are better carried dry.

Out on the river, the ice groaned again — longer this time, a note that bent downward at the end like a question. From the dark of the back room, Elias's voice came, quiet, awake after all.

"That's the west wind coming," he said. "Osk will run tomorrow night."

"Yes."

"Then sleep, Jude. Tomorrow you cross the river, and the day after, the names start doing their work." A pause. "And I start mine."

I lay down on the cot beside the dying stove with the type block under my pillow and the ledger against my ribs, and I listened to the ice talk all night, and near dawn it answered itself with the first sharp crack of the season — a sound like a rifle shot, like a door slamming, like a world dividing cleanly into the side I was leaving and the side I was bound for.

And on the far side of that division, faint but unmistakable, I heard the telegraph wires begin to sing.
