## 22. The Ferryman's Price

The river had a voice, and Osk had learned it the way other men learned scripture.

Forty years on the water — first with his father's barge, then the ferry, then nothing but the ferry when the army took the barges and burned the boathouse for kindling. Forty years, and he could tell you what the river meant by the sound of it under the ice. The low moan at dusk was the current arguing with the frost, wanting to keep moving, being told to hold still. The sharp ticks near midnight were air pockets collapsing in the black water below. And the long groaning that had started three nights ago, rising through the pilings of his dock like a hymn sung by something enormous and unwilling to die — that was the shelf ice beginning to think about letting go.

Osk sat on an upturned crate at the edge of his dock with a tin cup of chicory going cold in his hands and listened to all of it, and he thought about names.

He thought about names because of the letter he'd gotten in November, though the letter was only paper and the name in it was only ink. *Private Anders Osk, missing following engagement near the ridge, status under review.* Under review. His boy, his middle boy, the one who'd once steered the ferry alone at fourteen while Osk lay feverish in the house, gone into a column labeled *under review*, which every mother and father in the province knew by now was the army's way of saying *we have not bothered to find out*.

Anders had a body somewhere. Or he didn't. Either way there was a place on the river where he'd stopped existing as a man and started existing as a line in a clerk's book, and the clerk who wrote that line had never seen him laugh, had never watched him fight his brothers for the last of the plum jam, had never known that the boy hummed when he worked the rope, tuneless and constant, like a kettle deciding to sing.

That was the thing about the army's lists. They were written by men who had no reason to love the names, and so the names came off them easy, like scales off a fish.

Osk drank the cold chicory anyway. Downriver, past the bend where the drowned supply depot had gone under in the autumn floods, the ice lay flat and gray as sheet iron. Somewhere beyond it, in the burning quarter, people were packing up the dead men's names in oilcloth. He knew this because the woman had come to him two days ago — the small dark-eyed one from the workshop, Dunmore, the one the major vouched for with a look on his face Osk hadn't been able to read and hadn't tried to.

She'd stood on his dock in the wind with her hands bare and red and said, "I need you to carry something across the river, Mr. Osk."

"Carry what?"

"A ledger." She'd hesitated, and he'd seen her decide to trust him, the way you decide to step onto ice. "Several ledgers. Names of soldiers the reports left out. Hundreds of them. Maybe more by now."

Osk had looked at her for a long moment. Then he'd said the true thing, because she had earned the true thing: "My son is in one of those omissions. I know it the way you know weather. I can feel the shape of the hole where his name should be."

She hadn't flinched. She'd said, "Then help me fill it," and Osk, who had not wept since the armistice rumors began and turned out false twice over, had felt his eyes sting in the wind and had blamed the wind out loud, and she had let him.

Now he sat on his crate and made his arithmetic, because everything in this life came down to arithmetic in the end — cargo against current, weight against ice, what you could take against what you must leave.

* * *

They came at dusk, as agreed. All of them, which surprised him; he'd expected the woman and the printer and maybe the major. But they came down the frozen tow-path in a loose straggling line like a funeral that had decided to hurry — the woman first with a satchel across her chest, then the tall gray officer, then the printer walking with his hands bound in rags against the cold, then a young soldier with a bandaged arm carrying a crate, and last a chaplain with a canvas mail-bag slung crosswise like a bandolier.

Osk stood and knocked the ice from the cleats.

"You're all coming?" he said. "I said three passengers."

"We know what you said." It was the woman speaking, breath smoking. "We're asking what it costs."

"It costs nothing to ask. It costs lives to answer." He looked at each of them in turn, the way he looked at a loaded barge before taking it into current. "Show me what I'm carrying."

The woman unslung the satchel. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn shut at the seams, were the ledgers — hers, thick as a brick and swollen with paste-ins and corrections; the major's, slimmer, its cover stamped with an army inventory mark someone had tried to scrape away; a bundle of loose pages tied with string; and beneath it all, wrapped separately, the type block she'd shown him without explaining, a single square of printer's lead with a letter cut into its face.

"And the rest?" He nodded at the crate the young soldier carried, and at the chaplain's bag, and at the long shadow of the press parts — he'd heard about those, everyone in the quarter had heard about the printer and his machine — stacked under oilcloth back at the workshop, waiting on an answer.

"The rest is copies," the chaplain said. "Duplicates. Half go south with me tomorrow, if I live to walk out. These go with you tonight, so the work exists in two places at once, and burning one doesn't burn both."

Osk crouched and put his hand flat on the oilcloth. It was strange, he thought, how light it was. A brick of paper and thread and ink, less weight than one sack of flour, less than one crate of rifle cartridges, less than the army moved by the ton every day without thinking. And inside it: men. Not statistics. Men who had mothers waiting by windows in towns he could name from his ferry runs, who had sons like his sons, who had stood in queues and told jokes and been afraid of dying and died anyway and then been erased — erased by clerks, erased by majors protecting their commissions, erased by the simple arithmetic of a war that preferred its losses round numbers.

"My boys," Osk said, and his voice came out lower than he intended. "I had three. Joren went in the first autumn — that one they at least reported, killed in action, a clean word, a letter, a grave with a wooden marker some corporal painted with a stencil. You could stand at that grave and say *here*. You could put flowers. You could be a fool and talk to it." He paused. "Anders went last November. No report. No marker. No word. *Under review.* There are men in this province right now whose sons are under review, and they will grow old saying *under review* to themselves in the dark, and they will die not knowing whether to mourn or hope, and that — that is worse than the killing. That is the part of war that keeps killing after the shooting stops."

Nobody spoke. The river groaned beneath them, long and slow, like something turning over in its sleep.

"So yes," Osk said, standing. "I'll carry the names. I'll carry them across tonight on ice that may open under us like a mouth, and I'll carry them tomorrow, and next week, until every family on the south bank has had its letter or its knock on the door or its name read aloud in a church. That I'll do for free. That I'd do bleeding."

He held up one finger. "But not the press."

* * *

The printer stepped forward. Up close, in the last gray light, Osk saw that his hands were ruined — frostbitten at the fingertips sometime this winter, the nails still bruised blue-black, the fingers curling stiff as winter twigs. A man like that couldn't work a press anymore, Osk thought. A man like that had already given the press everything he had.

"You don't understand what it is," Elias said. His voice was rough, a man used to talking over machinery. "It's not iron. It's the only press in forty miles that can set a page true. When spring comes, when the roads open, that ledger has to become a book — hundreds of books — so that no family ever again depends on rumor or a chaplain's memory. That press prints the truth faster than the army can burn it. You're not leaving machine parts, ferryman. You're leaving the weapon."

"I'm leaving thirty kilos of iron," Osk said, "that will sit in my boat's hull like an anchor while we cross moving ice."

"The ledgers weigh less than—"

"I know what they weigh. I've lifted them." Osk turned and pointed downriver with his whole arm, the way his father used to point at weather. "You see that stretch below the point? Shore-fast shelf, maybe, maybe solid to the channel. Past that, the pack has already started working. Floes the size of chapel floors, grinding. My boat isn't a boat tonight — it's a sled on runners, and runners want light. Every kilo is a decision about where the ice decides to fail. The names come first. The names always come first. Everything else is iron, and iron sinks, and I have spent enough of my life pulling iron and what iron drowns out of this river."

"The press can't sink," Elias said, and now his voice cracked along a seam, "because I'm not asking you to float it. I'm asking—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. Looked at the woman.

Jude Dunmore touched the printer's sleeve. "Elias."

"No." He pulled his arm gently free, not angry — grieving, which looks the same from a distance. "You carry the names, Jude. That's your vow and it's a good one. But my vow was the machine. Twenty-two years I fed it. It printed wedding announcements and grain prices and, for two years before the war, a little weekly sheet for the mill workers that the owner called *trouble in eight columns*. It's the only honest thing I ever built with my hands and now my hands can't even hold it, and you want me to let the army have it to melt down for shell casings? Shell casings. It would end its life making more of the thing we're trying to stop."

"That's exactly why it can't matter more than you do," Jude said. "Iron melts. It can be recast. You can't."

"I can't grip a lever either," Elias said, and laughed, and it was the worst sound Osk had heard all winter, and he had heard men laughing in triage tents. "So we'll spend ourselves evenly, won't we? The names and the press, and the two of us, all going into the same arithmetic."

Osk let them argue. He'd ferried enough couples and enough partners and enough brothers to know when an argument wasn't really about the cargo. He walked to the end of his dock and checked the tie-offs and listened to the ice, and the ice said what it always said this time of year: *not yet, not yet, soon.*

* * *

It was the major who settled it, in the end, and Osk noted that too — noted that the officer had stood silent through all of it, hands clasped behind his back like a man reviewing troops made entirely of grief.

"Halloway," the woman said finally. "Tell him. Tell him what these copies are worth."

"What they're worth," Halloway said slowly, "is that I struck names myself. Eleven years an officer, and I signed casualty rolls with gaps in them where men I'd ordered forward had stopped existing on paper. I did it to protect the regiment's numbers. I did it because the forms had columns and grief does not fit in columns." He looked at Osk. "Ferryman, you asked what this costs. I'll tell you what it cost me: it cost me eleven years of sleeping well. Then a woman in a flooded hospital started writing the names down, and I haven't slept well since, and I am grateful to her for it."

"Poetry," Osk said. "The river doesn't run on poetry."

"No," Halloway said. "It runs on decisions. So here's mine." He turned to Elias. "The press stays. Not because iron matters more than men — because *you* matter more than iron, and you cannot make the crossing carrying it, and you will not let it go, so you will stay with it, and staying means the patrols find you, and the patrols burn what they find. Is that what your vow requires? That the last thing your honest machine ever does is get you killed beside it?"

Elias said nothing.

"Or," Halloway went on, relentless, quiet, "you break it tonight — smash it, scatter it, sink the frame in the depot hole where no clerk will ever requisition it — and you come across the ice with empty hands and your life, and in a year, in any free town with a roof and a floor, you build another one. Better. Smaller. Yours. The press isn't the vow, printer. The press was only ever how you kept the vow. Vows survive their tools. I've watched men learn that the hard way, and I've watched men refuse to learn it, and I've buried the ones who refused."

The wind came down the valley then, hard from the west, and under their feet the ice gave a crack like a whip, sharp and close, and every head turned toward the river.

Osk listened. Then he said: "That was the shore-fast shelf settling. We have hours. Not days. Hours."

Elias looked at the river a long time. Then at his ruined hands. Then at Jude Dunmore, who was watching him with an expression Osk recognized from his own mirror on the morning the letter came — the expression of a person measuring what can be carried and finding the arithmetic merciless.

"All right," Elias said at last, and his voice was steady and terrible. "All right. The names cross. I'll deal with the iron."

Osk nodded once, and turned to business, because business was mercy too — it gave grieving people something to do with their hands.

"Here's how it goes," he said. "One crossing. The ledgers, the chaplain's duplicates, the type block, and three bodies — the woman, the printer if his hands allow, and one more of your choosing. I don't care which. The rest of you find your own salvation; I'm a ferryman, not a miracle." He looked at Halloway. "Major, you've a uniform and a talent for drawing fire. I expect you've already decided what you're for."

Halloway almost smiled. "You expect correctly."

"Then we load at moonrise. Nothing metal that clinks. Nothing that shines. If anyone speaks on the ice, they whisper." He bent and gathered the oilcloth bundle himself, testing its weight, and found it absurdly light, lighter than a sack of feed, lighter than one shell casing's worth of consequence, and heavier than anything he had ever lifted in forty years on this water.

At the top of the dock, the woman stopped and turned back.

"Mr. Osk. Your son. Anders. Give me everything you know about him — regiment, company, last letters, the town he shipped from. Whatever exists. Before we cross."

"Why?"

"Because the ledger isn't finished," Jude Dunmore said. "It was never finished. It finishes when the last family knows. Your son's name goes in tonight, in my hand, before the ice takes its chance to refuse me."

Osk stood very still on his dock, holding the dead men's names against his chest, and above them the sky went the color of a bruise, and beneath them the river said *soon, soon,* and far off downstream, where the eastern quarter smoldered, the night smelled faintly of smoke — and of ink.
