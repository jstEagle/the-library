## 23. Packing the Dead Men's Names

We worked by daylight because we could not trust the lamps anymore. The patrol had been seen twice on the far road in the last week, and lamplight through the workshop's cracked shutters was a signal as good as any flare. So Elias dragged his table to the window, where the winter light came down flat and grey and honest off the river, and we spread out three ledgers like bodies on a mortuary slab.

Mine. Halloway's. The chaplain's page.

"Read them first," Wren said. He stood at the door with his collar turned up against the draft, hands folded, as if he were about to officiate. "Every name, once more, before it goes under the oilcloth. After that there's no correcting anything."

"It's been read four times," Elias said.

"Then it will be read five." Wren did not move. "I have buried men whose papers went wrong by a single letter. A mother waits twenty years for a son who died under someone else's spelling of her own surname. We do this properly or we do not do it at all."

So we read them.

Elias took the first ledger because he could read my hand better than anyone — he'd printed beneath it long enough to know its habits, the way my *h* slanted when I was tired, the way I pressed too hard on names I knew personally and left faint ghosts on the reverse of every page. He read aloud in a low, level voice, and I followed with my finger, checking each entry against my memory, and where memory disagreed with ink we stopped and argued until one of us won.

There were forty-one disagreements.

Forty-one names where I had written *died of wounds* and the record said *missing*, or where a sergeant's report gave one regiment and the man's own whispered confession, taken at two in the morning in the flooded ward, gave another. Forty-one small wars fought over paper while the river waited outside.

Halloway arrived halfway through the second ledger. He came in without knocking, which was new for him, and stood dripping snowmelt just inside the door, looking at the table with an expression I couldn't read — something between a man entering a church and a man entering a courtroom where he already knew the verdict.

"Major," Wren said, with a nod that held neither warmth nor cold.

"I brought this." Halloway set a fourth book on the table — thin, water-stained, bound in the grey cardboard of army requisition forms. His own list. The one he'd kept through all those years of striking names from official reports, writing down what he erased because some part of him could not bear the arithmetic of it going nowhere. "I've checked it against yours. There are overlaps. There are also ninety-three names in mine that aren't in yours."

The room went quiet enough that I heard the river.

It had been making noise for two days — not cracking yet, not breaking, but muttering underneath, a deep tectonic grumble like artillery heard across valleys. Everyone in Vellmere had stopped pretending not to hear it. You could see it in the way people walked: faster, heads down, the whole town leaning into its work like a ship's crew before weather.

"Ninety-three," I said.

"Some I struck myself. Some I inherited struck." He took off his cap, which I had never seen him do indoors, and turned it in his hands. "They deserve the same pages as the rest."

"They'll get them," I said.

He nodded once, sat down at the corner of the table, and began the slow work of merging — reading each of his names aloud while I checked mine, then Wren's page, then the margins where Bram's recovered sheet had been copied back in after its journey south and return. It was terrible, patient work. It was the best work I had ever done.

* * *

By late afternoon the merged master was finished: one ledger now, thick as a brick, every name entered once in fair copy with cross-references to duplicates and contradictions noted in the back matter. Nine hundred and six names. I had thought, when I started, that I might reach two hundred. I had been a smaller person then.

"Now we pack it," Osk said.

He'd come up from the ferry landing an hour before, uninvited, unspeaking, carrying a coil of tarred cord and a block of cork under one arm like a man delivering firewood. Now he stood over the table studying the ledger the way he studied ice — reading it, weighing it, finding its weaknesses.

"You seal it in oilcloth," he said. "Three layers. Not two — two leaks. You fold the seams toward the spine and you wax every fold. Then cord, tight, but not so tight the cloth strains. Then cork, lashed at both ends, so if she goes under the water she comes back up." He looked at me. "She being the book. Though maybe she being you, too, if the shelf lets go early."

"How much does it weigh wet?" Elias asked.

"With cork? Four pounds. Five." Osk turned the ledger over in his big hands, handling it with a gentleness that didn't match anything else about him. "Five pounds of dead men. Lighter than most of them carried into the field."

That landed hard in the room. Even Osk seemed to feel it, and he wasn't a man who felt things loudly.

We built the package right there on the printing table, all five of us, and it became a kind of ceremony none of us had planned. Wren read the sealing prayer from his pocket testament — not aloud, his lips moving — while Elias cut the oilcloth with the straight razor he used for trimming type. Halloway melted wax over a candle stub, dripping it along the folds in careful beads. I folded. My hands shook on the first seam and I made myself stop, breathe, start again, because this was the one task in the whole enterprise that would not forgive trembling.

Three layers. Folded toward the spine. Wax in every fold.

When it was done it didn't look like a ledger anymore. It looked like a parcel of provisions, something a soldier might carry — bread and tinned meat, perhaps, or socks and letters from home. That was the point. No patrol searching us would look twice at a ration bundle. But knowing that didn't stop my chest from aching. Nine hundred and six men, wrapped up like lunch.

"Cord," Osk said.

I lashed it. Cork at both ends, tarred cord pulled tight with my teeth and my fists, knots tied the way Osk showed me — the ones rivermen use because they hold when everything else lets go. When it was finished I held it in both hands and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything, and nobody spoke for a moment, and outside the river muttered its low continuous threat.

"We rehearse," Osk said. "Tonight. Before dark. You walk it."

* * *

The rehearsal nearly killed me, and it was only a rehearsal.

Osk took us down past the ferry landing to the shore-fast ice below the eastern bluff — the shelf he'd spoken of, the road he believed might hold until break-up. It was a strip of grey-white maybe forty feet wide, running along the bank where the current ran slower, frozen fast to the land on both sides. Beyond its edge the main river lay in plates and ridges, black water showing between them in places already, even now, even before the thaw.

"The shelf holds because it's anchored," Osk said, stamping one boot on it. "Land ice. It doesn't ride the current. It stays until the water under it warms and lets it go all at once." He crouched, put his palm flat on the surface. "But when it goes, it goes in minutes. Not hours. Minutes."

"So I run," I said.

"You don't run." He looked up at me with an expression of genuine pity, as though I'd suggested swimming in chain mail. "You run, you hit soft ice and go through before you know you've fallen. You walk. Fast walk, short steps, weight centered, never stopping, never hurrying." He rose. "Show me your satchel."

I'd packed it that morning: the sealed ledger riding high against my chest under my coat, strapped there with a bandage roll so it wouldn't swing; ink and pen gone — no, kept, I'd kept them, God help me, in case I found someone dying on the far bank who needed a name taken; the photograph of Thomas; two ration tins; the flint. Everything measured, everything weighed, nothing extra. Chapter by chapter I had been learning what a life weighs when you reduce it to what fits against your body.

Osk circled me, prodding the straps, adjusting the satchel's hang half an inch, tightening the bandage roll. "Good. Now walk."

I walked the length of the shelf and back, forty feet each way, boots ringing on the solid grey surface, the river groaning somewhere beneath me like a sleeping animal turning over.

"Faster."

I walked faster.

"Again. Eyes up. If you watch your feet you'll freeze there. Pick your line on the far bank and walk to it."

I picked the line — a dead alder on the far shore, pale and leaning, unmistakable even in bad light — and I walked to it, again and again, while the light failed and the wind came up the river valley and Osk called corrections in his flat ferryman's voice. Short steps. Weight centered. Don't stop. Don't hurry. Don't look down.

Elias watched from the bank. He tried to hide how he watched and couldn't. His hands hung at his sides with the frostbitten fingers slightly curled, and every time I reached the far end of the shelf and turned, I saw him tracking me the way a man tracks something he is about to lose.

On the fifth crossing I stopped in the middle — deliberately, because I wanted to know what it felt like to stand on the river — and the ice spoke.

Not the muttering. A sound like a giant knuckle cracking, sharp and close, somewhere ahead of me to the east, and the shelf shivered under my boots, a vibration I felt in my knees and my teeth. I stood very still. Five pounds of dead men pressed against my chest. The crack rolled away downstream and faded and the river resumed its low grumble, and I made myself finish the crossing at a walk, eyes up, short steps, exactly as taught.

"That sound gets louder every day," Osk said quietly, when I reached the bank. "Tomorrow we rehearse at night. With wind. Because whatever morning it happens, it won't be a calm one."

* * *

We ate together that evening — all of us, in the workshop, around the stove — and it had the feeling of a last supper that everyone understood and no one named. Wren blessed the food without making a show of it. Halloway sat closer to the fire than usual, as if he were cold in a way the stove couldn't touch. Elias carved bread with his ruined fingers, awkwardly, refusing help, and I let him refuse because I understood that the refusal was the point.

The sealed ledger lay on the table between us, cork and cord and wax, and we looked at it the way you look at a coffin closed for the last time — except this was the opposite of a coffin, I reminded myself. This was the thing that kept nine hundred and six men from ever having coffins without names.

"Bram should be here," I said.

"He's where he needs to be," Elias said. "With Wren's copies, on the road south. Every day he's not in this room is proof he chose right."

"And tomorrow?"

Wren set down his cup. "Tomorrow I leave as well. Half the copies go with me. If the crossing fails—" he touched the sealed ledger gently, "—if this fails, the work survives anyway. That's why we made two of everything. Grief doesn't get to win by accident."

"Grief doesn't get to win at all," I said, and heard how hard my own voice had become, and didn't apologize for it.

Halloway cleared his throat. "I've drawn a map of the far bank. Patrol rotations, sentry positions at the bridge ruins, the gully path north out of town." He slid a paper across the table to me. "Memorize it tonight and burn it."

"Why burn it?"

"Because if they take you with it in your pocket, they take every name on that shelf with you." He met my eyes. "You're not carrying a book across that river, Miss Dunmore. You're carrying the only honest record this war has produced on either bank. Start acting like it."

It was the harshest thing he'd ever said to me, and the kindest. I memorized the map by stove-light while the others talked logistics around me — Osk's timing, the wind, the signals, what Halloway would do at dawn to pull the patrols east — and then I fed the map to the stove and watched it curl and go.

Later, when the others had gone to their corners and their blankets, Elias sat beside me at the window where the press used to be — where its ghost still marked the floorboards, four darker rectangles where the feet had stood. He didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did I. Outside, the river worked at its muttering, patient as a siege.

"I keep thinking about the weight," he finally said. "Five pounds. I spent eleven years learning to make marks that weigh nothing — ink on paper, air and light. And it turns out the whole trade was always about this. Weight. What words cost, and who carries them."

"You carry them too," I said. "Half those names exist because your press pulled them."

"My press is rubble and a burned lever." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "You're the press now, Jude. Tomorrow you print them across the river yourself."

I put my head against his shoulder and we listened to the ice.

And then the ice answered — not the muttering, not the knuckle-crack, but a long rising groan from upstream, building like a train whistle, rolling down the valley past the workshop, past the shore, out into the dark, and under it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of running water where no water had run since November.

Osk appeared in the doorway with his coat already on.

"That's not rehearsal," he said. "Shelf starts moving tonight. We go at first light — earlier if she speaks like that again. Get your sleep, girl." He paused, looking at the sealed ledger on the table. "And say goodbye to the ones you can't take with you. There won't be time in the morning."

I looked around the workshop — at the type cases, the stove, the darker rectangles on the floor, the men I loved arranged around me in blanket and greatcoat — and I understood that Osk hadn't meant the objects.

He meant the people.
