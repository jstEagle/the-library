## 24. First Crack

The sound woke me before I understood it.

It came up through the floor of the workshop like a word spoken under the earth — a long, groaning exhalation that started somewhere downriver and traveled the whole frozen spine of the Vell until it passed beneath us and kept going. Not wind. Wind didn't come from below. Wind didn't have a throat.

I lay in my cot in the loft above the press room and listened to the river speak.

For a moment I thought it was artillery. That was the first lie my body told me, the old reflex from the field hospital at Vellmere, when the guns had been close enough that the stretchers shook and Thomas — no, not Thomas, Thomas was two years gone and a name in a ledger — the wounded men had learned to sleep through it anyway, because there was nothing else to do. My hand went to the satchel before my eyes were open. The ledger was there. It was always there now, strapped and wrapped and ready since Osk's ferryman terms had made everything real. My fingers found the oilcloth seam and held it, and only then did my mind catch up to my nerves: not guns. Ice.

The river was breaking its back.

* * *

By the time I got down the ladder, everyone was awake. That was the strange thing about the workshop these last weeks — how small a community we had become, how completely we had learned each other's sounds. Bram was already at the window with a shutter cracked to a finger's width, his breath fogging the gap. The two convalescents, Pell and young Adsett, sat up on their pallets by the stove with blankets around their shoulders like men attending a service. And Elias stood in the middle of the press room floor in his shirt, bare feet on the cold boards, head tilted, listening the way he listened to the press — as if the sound had parts he could name.

"Shore ice first," he said quietly, without turning. "Then the midstream sheets. It's talking shore to middle. You hear how it travels?"

"I hear it," I said.

"That's the shelf letting go along the banks. The fast water underneath is eating it." He turned then, and the lamplight caught the bandages still wrapped around his fingers — the frostbite from the depot had healed crookedly, and the doctor in Vellmere had told him what we both already knew: he would never set type the same way again, might never pull a lever cleanly, might lose a finger or two if the cold took another bite. "Jude. How many nights do you think we have?"

I didn't answer right away, because answering meant doing arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. Osk had said the shelf of shore-fast ice on the northern bend would hold a walker's weight "until full break-up," and full break-up had seemed like a season, a horizon, a thing far off. But the river had just said otherwise. The river had said *soon* in a voice you could feel through your boots.

"A week," I said. "Maybe less."

"Three nights," Elias said. "Maybe four. I've watched this river eleven winters. When she starts talking shore to middle, she's counting down."

Pell made a low noise — not quite a word. He'd been convalescing with us since October, a sergeant with a hip wound that wouldn't knit, and he had the soldier's habit of translating every sound into threat. "That's bombardment," he said. "That's exactly what it sounds like when they're walking fire up a ridge."

"It's the river, Sergeant," Bram said. But his voice wasn't steady, and I saw why when I followed his eyes through the shutter crack: he wasn't looking at the ice at all. He was looking across it.

"Bram?" I said. "What is it?"

He didn't answer. He opened the shutter another inch, and the cold poured in like water, and beyond him, through the black lattice of our own window frame, I saw what he saw.

Lights. On the far road. Not lanterns moving singly, the way couriers traveled, but a string of them, ordered and slow, spaced like beads on a wire — wagon lights, or torches, following the eastern bank road northward toward the quarter where the requisitioned buildings stood in their rows.

"The burning detail," Bram said.

* * *

Nobody moved for a moment. Even the stove seemed to hold its crackling. Then Adsett, who was nineteen and had been an infantry boy before his lungs filled with mud at the autumn advance, said, "They can't burn anything tonight. Nobody crosses the ice at night."

"They're not crossing," I said. My voice came out flat and calm, which surprised me; inside, something was running very fast. "They're staging. They'll wait for the road detail, the escort, whatever Halloway warned us about. They'll move at first light." I looked at the lights strung out along the far shore, orange and patient. "Halloway said days. He said we'd have warning. This is the warning."

Elias crossed to the window beside Bram. Up close I could see what the fever hadn't taken from him and what it had — the hollows under his cheekbones deeper than November, the hands he cradled against his chest like something breakable. He studied the lights for a long time.

"That's the eastern quarter first," he said. "The granary, the tannery row. They'll start where the buildings stand apart, so the fire doesn't jump the lanes. Then they work inward." His jaw tightened. "We're inward."

"We knew," Bram said. "We've known for weeks. Why does knowing feel different at three in the morning?"

Nobody answered him, because there was no answer that wasn't true and useless: it felt different because at three in the morning the knowledge had teeth.

Pell hauled himself upright using the stove rail, favoring the bad hip, and looked around the room with the expression of a man taking a defensive position. "How long to load your press parts onto that ferryman's boat? If it comes to it."

"There is no boat yet," Elias said. "Osk runs the ice, not the water. And the press isn't going anywhere — we settled that. The ledgers go. The copies go. Wren carries half south, the rest goes over the shelf with Jude." He said it evenly, but I heard the cost in it, the same cost as the night he'd agreed to dismantle his machine: every sentence about saving the names was also a sentence about burying the press, and the press was thirty years of his life, his apprenticeship, his flight from the army, his whole stubborn self, cast in iron.

"We should wake Wren," I said.

"He's at the garrison chapel," Bram said. "Across the lane. He'll have heard the river if nothing else."

"Then someone goes now, while it's still dark and no one's watching the streets. Bram—" I stopped, because the old suspicion rose in me like sediment stirred from the bottom, the memory of what Bram had confessed by lamplight weeks ago, the informer he'd been in the other town, the printer doomed by his report. I had chosen to believe him when he came back half-dead with the stolen page carried safe to Wren. Believing was a practice, not a feeling. You did it daily, like setting type. "Bram, will you go?"

He looked at me, and I watched him understand exactly what the question contained — not just *go fetch the chaplain*, but *I am asking you, specifically, again, after everything*. Something moved behind his eyes that might have been gratitude or grief or both.

"I'll go," he said. He dressed quickly, wound his scarf twice, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "For what it's worth — those lights don't move like men who know the quarter. They're following orders written by someone who's never walked these lanes at night." A ghost of his old orderly briskness. "We know the lanes."

Then he was out into the cold, and the door shut behind him, and the wind took his footsteps almost immediately.

* * *

We worked until dawn, because working was the only argument against dread that had ever done me any good.

The final pages were drying on the racks above the stove — Elias had pulled the last full signatures of the merged ledger two nights ago, mine and Halloway's lists interleafed, Bram's recovered page copied in, the whole terrible census of the erased set clean and legible in ink that needed forty-eight hours before it would survive handling. We checked the racks now by candle stub, page by page, holding each sheet to the flame-light at an angle to catch wet spots. Three pages near the bottom of the stack were still tacky. Elias cursed under his breath — softly, so as not to wake Adsett, who had gone back to a shallow, exhausted sleep — and rehung them higher where the stove heat reached.

"Oilcloth," I said, laying it out on the worktable. We measured and cut while the river spoke beneath us, that long groan traveling shore to middle, shore to middle, like a sentry pacing his beat. Twice it cracked sharp — a single report, whip-like — and both times Pell's head came up off his pillow, and both times I kept my hands moving, folding corners, testing knots, because if I stopped I would hear the artillery too.

The weighted float was Elias's design: the sealed packet lashed to a cork-and-timber core salvaged from a broken punt, so that if the ice took me — if the shelf failed halfway and I went into the black water — the names would rise and drift and perhaps be found downstream by someone who understood what they were. We had not discussed what would happen to me in that scenario. There had been no need. Everyone in the room could do the arithmetic.

"You'll strap it high," Elias said, tightening a lashing with his bandaged fingers, clumsy, patient, refusing help. "Chest and shoulders, not the waist. If you go through, the ice will want your arms free."

"If I go through—"

"You won't." He pulled the knot tight. "But we plan for the river we have, not the one we want."

Outside, the sky was turning the gray of dirty wool, and the string of lights on the far road had gone out — doused at first light, meaning the detail had stopped moving and settled in. Waiting. Somewhere among those wagons was the authority to burn the eastern quarter, and then ours. Somewhere, perhaps, was the patrol Halloway had promised would come regardless of orders, the one he'd warned me of with his conscience split open in his face. I thought of him standing wherever he stood this morning — on the far side, since the bridge had closed with him stranded on the wrong bank all these weeks — and wondered if he was watching those same wagons, and what a man does with his body when he sees the thing coming that he has sworn to stand in front of.

The door banged open and Bram came in with Wren behind him, both of them steaming with cold, snow in the chaplain's beard. Wren went straight to the window, looked at the far bank for a long moment, and then turned around and surveyed our little room — the press, the drying racks, the two soldiers, the girl with the oilcloth — with an expression I couldn't read at first. Then I could. It was the look of a man memorizing.

"They're early," Wren said. "Everyone said thaw, everyone said spring. They're here at first break-up because somebody senior decided a burned record burns just as well in February." He unbuttoned his coat. "Which means my schedule is not my own anymore. If they're staging tonight, they move tomorrow or the day after. I leave with the southern copies at dusk today — the roads are quietest then, and the chaplain's collar still opens doors."

"Dusk," I said. "So soon."

"Sooner every hour, Miss Dunmore." He came to the table and put his hand flat on the oilcloth, on the packed copies bound for the south, the ones that would travel in false-bottomed vestments past checkpoints where discovery meant a wall and a firing squad, and he had known this for weeks and signed himself up anyway, this soft-voiced man who buried other people's sons and wrote to their mothers. "Everything I carry leaves here at dusk. Everything you carry leaves when the shelf decides. And everything left in this building—" he glanced, gently, at the press, at Elias, at the walls themselves, "—stays."

The stove ticked. Above us, faint through the floorboards, the river groaned its long shore-to-middle note, and this time nobody flinched, because we had all learned the sound in a single night, the way you learn a language when your life depends on it.

Elias straightened up from the last knot and looked at me across the finished packet — the names, sealed and floating-ready, heavier than iron and weighing almost nothing.

"Tonight we say goodbye to Wren," he said. "And tomorrow night, God willing, the river gives us our answer."

Through the window, on the far road, the first thin smoke of the burning detail's cook fires began to rise against the gray sky — small, domestic, almost peaceful — and I understood that we had run out of winter.

* * *

We spent the daylight hours in a silence broken only by necessary words, and the river breaking everything else.

By noon the cracks were coming faster, closer together, some of them deep enough to shudder the bottles on the shelf. Adsett sat by the window most of the day with his chin on his fists, watching the midstream ice — and midstream it had changed visibly now, the great white sheet split by a lead of open black water down the channel, steam rising off it like breath, the floes on either edge tilting and grinding. Pell stood beside him for an hour without saying anything, two soldiers watching a battlefield assemble itself.

At dusk we loaded Wren.

There was no ceremony possible; ceremony attracts eyes. We packed the southern copies into his vestment case, into the false bottom Bram had built, into the lining of his coat, and Wren blessed the workshop in four quiet sentences that were mostly thanks and partly apology — I caught the words *these names* and *what we owe* — and then he shook each of our hands. When he came to me he held on a moment longer.

"When the book exists," he said, "when it's printed and bound and in the hands of the families — I'll write to you. Whatever town you're in. The letters may take months."

"I'll be somewhere they can find me," I said.

"No," Wren said, and smiled, tired and certain. "You'll be somewhere the names are. The rest is just an address."

And then he was gone into the blue evening, south along the lane, a chaplain's figure carrying half the dead men of the Vell valley toward whatever waited on the roads — checkpoints, questions, the wall — and we stood in the doorway until the dark took him, five of us, none of us saying the thing we were thinking, which was that we might be watching a man walk to his death and that he had chosen it with his eyes open.

The river cracked, very close, very loud, like a gun fired under our floor.

Elias put his bandaged hand over mine on the doorframe.

"First light," he said. "Be ready."
