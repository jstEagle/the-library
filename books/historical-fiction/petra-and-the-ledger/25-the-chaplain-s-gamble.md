## 25. The Chaplain's Gamble

The copies were ready at midnight, and Chaplain Wren counted them twice because he did not trust his own hands.

Forty-one packets. Each one wrapped in oilcloth, each one sewn shut with waxed thread, each one small enough to disappear inside a prayer book. He laid them out on the workbench in rows of seven, the way he'd once laid out communion cups in a garrison chapel that no longer existed, and he found himself whispering the old words over them — *take, eat, this is my body* — before catching himself. These were not bread. These were better than bread. These were names, four hundred and six of them, set in type by a deserter's ruined press and copied in ink that Jude Dunmore had thinned three times to make it last.

He picked up the first packet and held it as if it might go off.

It might. That was the truth of it, the truth he'd been circling for two days without saying aloud. A man caught carrying these papers would not be shot as a smuggler. Smugglers got trials, sometimes. A chaplain caught carrying subversive documents — forged casualty records, unofficial rolls, evidence of falsification at the highest level — would be shot as a traitor, quickly, in whatever yard was nearest, and the report of his death would be one more name entered nowhere.

Wren had thought about this a great deal, which was unusual for him. Most of his thinking, these past three years, had been of the other kind: the kind you did with your hands, hauling stretcher poles through mud, holding a boy's head steady while a doctor worked, writing letters home to mothers in handwriting steadier than your own. The hands-thoughts had kept him alive. But lately the head-thoughts had come back, and they kept returning to one question, asked by a girl with ink on her fingers in a workshop that smelled of oil and lamp-black:

*If nobody writes it down, how will anybody know?*

He had no answer for her then. He had an answer now. The answer was a satchel, twenty of these packets, and a road south.

* * *

He told them at dawn, standing in the doorway of the workshop with the frost ferning up the glass behind him.

"Half the copies," he said. "Twenty packets. I'll take them south with the medical convoy when it moves — tomorrow, or the day after. There's a garrison church outside Harrow's Ford where I'm still expected. Nobody questions a chaplain going back to his parish."

Jude looked up from the ledger, her pen still moving. She didn't stop writing. Wren had learned that about her — that she wrote the way other people breathed, continuously, as though stopping would be death. "Harrow's Ford is four days' march," she said. "Through three checkpoints."

"I know the checkpoints," Wren said. "I've buried men at all three."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's exactly what you asked." He smiled, and saw it land wrong, and let it fall away. Honesty. They deserved honesty; they'd given him nothing else since Bram came stumbling back half-dead with a page sewn into his coat lining. "No. It isn't safe. If I'm searched — properly searched, not the usual wave-through — they'll find them, and they'll find me wanting, and there's a field behind every checkpoint in this province where the digging is easy."

Elias set down the type stick he'd been cleaning. His hands were still bandaged from the night he'd smashed his own press — Wren had redressed the burns himself, two nights ago, peeling the cloth back from skin that looked like candle wax gone soft — but even bandaged, they were restless, always moving, always composing something invisible. "Then don't take twenty," Elias said. "Take ten. Split the risk."

"Ten packets travel worse than twenty," Wren said. "A man carrying one secret looks like a man. A man carrying ten secrets looks like a courier. Twenty looks like a chaplain who can't count, and chaplains who can't count are beneath notice." He tapped the satchel leaning against his leg. "Besides. The whole point is redundancy. Half here, half south. If the river takes everything on this bank, the names survive across it. If I die on the road—" he paused, and chose the next words carefully, because they were true and because they needed hearing— "then whoever strips my body will find papers they can't read the importance of, and some quartermaster will use the oilcloth for boot linings, and God forgive me, the names will be lost anyway. So I intend very much not to die."

Silence, except for the wind working at the eaves.

Jude put down her pen at last. She crossed the room and stood in front of him, and up close he could see what the winter had done to her — the hollows under her eyes, the chapped split on her lower lip that never healed — and also what the winter hadn't touched, which was everything else.

"You could refuse," she said quietly. "Nobody here would think less of you. You've already done more than any chaplain I've ever met."

"That's a low bar," Wren said. "I've met most of them."

"I mean it. This isn't your vow. Thomas wasn't your brother."

"No," Wren agreed. "But I read the burial services for forty-six men last autumn whose names weren't in any report. I read them because their officers handed me a list that was missing pages, and I stood in the mud and commended souls to God whose names I pronounced correctly only because I'd written them down myself, beforehand, in my own little book, because I'd learned not to trust anyone else's lists." He looked at his own hands — clean hands, unburned hands, hands that had dug graves and held heads and now, finally, were being asked to do something worth the having of them. "I have spent three years burying the evidence of what this army does to its own people. This is the first time anyone has asked me to *keep* some instead. I'll not refuse that. I'd sooner refuse communion."

Jude studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the way she nodded at a finished page, and went back to her ledger, and that was the whole of the negotiation. Wren understood that he had just been admitted to something — not a conspiracy exactly, conspiracies were smaller — a congregation, maybe. A congregation of record-keepers. He'd served worse ones.

* * *

The rest of the day was preparation, and preparation, Wren discovered, was its own strange liturgy.

Jude packed the packets herself, refusing help. She sealed each one, tested the stitching, and pressed into the oilcloth of each packet the flat of her palm — a gesture so unconscious she seemed unaware of it, a printer's blessing, or a sister's. When she reached packet nineteen she paused, opened it, checked the pages inside against the master ledger, found them perfect, and closed it again with fingers that shook only slightly.

"The southern ones need to open easy," Elias said from the press table, where he was assembling the last of the loose sheets into bundles for the second crossing — the crossing that would happen over Osk's ice road, the crossing everyone spoke about in the flat careful voices people used for things they were afraid of. "Whoever receives them has to be able to verify against the master. Mark them."

"They're marked."

"Marked so a stranger could understand."

Jude looked at him. "They're marked so *you* could understand," she said. "Isn't that enough?"

"No," Elias said simply, and went back to his bundling, and Wren watched the two of them not look at each other for a full minute, and understood that he was watching a farewell being rehearsed in the grammar of paperwork, because neither of them could bear to say anything plainer.

Bram appeared in the afternoon, moving slowly, his ribs still strapped where the patrol's rifle butt had found him on the road back from the chaplain's. He carried a tin of tea he'd traded two days' rations for, and he brewed it on the stove without asking, and poured four cups, and that was his apology and his welcome-back and his confession all at once, and nobody made him say any of it aloud.

"To the names," Bram said, raising his cup.

"To the names," they echoed, and drank tea that tasted like pine needles and iron, and Wren thought: *this is what a sacrament is. Not the words. The drinking of it together while the fire dies.*

* * *

Night came early, the way it did now, and with it came the sound.

The river had started talking three days ago — long groans that rolled up the valley like distant artillery, cracks that snapped and echoed off the far bluffs. Everyone in the quarter had stopped pretending to sleep through it. Tonight it was louder. Tonight, lying on his cot in the corner of the workshop with his satchel packed and buckled beside him, Wren listened to the ice speak and found himself composing the sermon he would give at Harrow's Ford if he lived to give any sermon at all.

It was a short sermon. It consisted mostly of names.

He must have slept eventually, because a hand on his shoulder woke him, and the lamp was lit, and Jude was crouched beside his cot with her coat already buttoned and the master ledger strapped to her chest under it, where she wore it now day and night, like armor, like a pregnancy.

"Come," she said. "Before the convoy marshals. I want to give you something."

The workshop was cold enough that their breath hung. At the press table — the wrecked, beloved hulk of it, its frame cracked where Elias had taken the hammer to it, its screw still holding the ghost impression of the final sheet — Elias sat alone in the dark, awake, watching them with eyes that reflected the lamplight like a cat's.

Jude took Wren's hand and turned it palm-up, and into it she placed one of the type blocks. Small, cold, lead-heavy. He tilted it toward the lamp.

**THOMAS DUNMORE**, it read, reversed, in mirror-writing, the way all such blocks were. A sorter's block. One letter of a name among hundreds, salvaged from the crate where the ledger had hidden all winter.

"He should go south too," Jude said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "He began this. He should be there when it ends — at the reading, in the church, when they say all the names out loud. He should hear it." She swallowed. "Even if I don't."

Wren closed his fist around the block. It bit into his palm, cold and hard and real, and he thought: *this woman has turned grief into typography.* "I'll carry him," he said. "On my word. He'll be at the reading if I have to walk into that church on my knees."

"You won't have to," Elias said from the dark. "You'll walk in on your feet, and you'll stand at the lectern, and you'll read until your voice gives out, and then someone else will take the page, because that's how this works. That's how it's always worked." He rose, wincing at his bandaged hands, and came to stand with them, the three of them around the dead press like mourners around a bier. "One thing more, Chaplain. When they ask you where the copies came from — and they will ask — tell them the truth. Tell them a dead man printed them."

Wren frowned. "You're not dead."

"Not yet." Elias almost smiled. "But the man who owned this press is. I broke him the night I broke the machine. The man going over the ice is someone new. Let the army chase a ghost. Ghosts are harder to shoot than printers."

Jude made a sound — half laugh, half something else — and pressed her forehead briefly against Elias's shoulder, and Wren looked away and studied the rafters and gave them the moment, because moments between living people were running short everywhere in this quarter, and hoarding them felt like the only honest economy left.

* * *

Dawn, and the medical convoy marshaling in the eastern road, wagons breathing steam, mules complaining, orderlies stamping.

Wren dressed as what he was: a chaplain, collar correct, satchel light-looking, face arranged into the mild vacancy that three years of army service had taught him read as *harmless* to every sergeant who ever waved a man through a line. Inside the satchel, under a false bottom of hymnals, twenty packets rode against his spine. Against his chest, in a pocket over his heart, a lead block bearing a dead man's name in mirror-writing.

At the edge of the quarter, where the road bent and the river showed itself grey and groaning below, Jude and Elias and Bram stood to see him off. Halloway was there too, further back, uniformless, a civilian coat hanging wrong on a soldier's shoulders, his face doing the arithmetic of the patrols he meant to draw off tomorrow. He raised two fingers to Wren — a salute, or the ruin of one — and Wren returned it.

Jude stepped forward last. She didn't embrace him. Instead she straightened his collar, the way you'd fuss over a brother on a wedding morning, and said, "Packet twelve has a misprint. Page nine. An E where an A should be, in Aldous Merrick. Don't let them correct it. Leave it. It's proof of the press. Proof it was us."

"I'll defend that misprint with my life," Wren said solemnly.

"You'd better not need to."

"No," he agreed. "With any luck I'll defend it with nothing but stubbornness and a clerical collar."

He shouldered the satchel. It weighed nothing and everything. Down the road the convoy was forming up, and beyond it, four days south, a garrison church waited with its bad stove and its good acoustics, and beyond that, letters, families, a reading, a reckoning — all of it hanging on whether a middle-aged chaplain could walk past three checkpoints looking boring enough to live.

"Chaplain." Jude's voice stopped him at the bend. He turned. She stood with the wind pulling at her hair and the river's groaning rising behind her like a choir tuning up, and she said the thing she had evidently walked down here to say, the thing she'd rehearsed: "When you read them. Read them slowly. They died fast. Let them be slow now."

Wren looked at her a long time.

"Yes," he said. "That's the whole liturgy, isn't it. Slowly."

He turned and walked, and did not look back again, because chaplains learn early that looking back is for the bereaved and he intended to arrive as a messenger instead. The checkpoint at the mill race came within the hour. A young sergeant with a chapped face waved his lantern over the satchel, glanced at the collar, glanced at the face, and found nothing there worth the finding.

"Hymns, Father?"

"Always," Wren said, and walked south, twenty names' worth of hope riding between his shoulder blades, while behind him the river groaned again — louder now, impatient, counting down the hours to break-up, to fire, to the crossing that would decide everything the church bells at Harrow's Ford were waiting to announce.
