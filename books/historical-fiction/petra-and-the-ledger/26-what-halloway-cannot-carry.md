## 26. What Halloway Cannot Carry

Halloway counted the ferryman's coins twice, because the first count had frightened him.

Three passengers. Osk had been clear about it — three, and the ledgers, and nothing else. The ice was already talking in its sleep out there on the river, long groans that came up through the ground like something turning over beneath the floorboards. Every hour Osk waited shaved a foot off his shelf of shore-fast ice. Three souls was what the river would forgive, maybe. Not four.

Halloway sat in the cold back room of the cooper's shed with the coins in his palm — not money, but ration chits and a pocket watch and a signet ring, everything he owned that a ferryman might take — and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only after weeks of lying to yourself, which three they would be.

Jude. Elias. Wren.

Not him.

He had known it, he realized, since the night at the drowned depot, or maybe before that — since the inspection, since the moment he'd stood in that reeking ward with the ledger hidden under a dying boy's cot and said nothing. A man who says nothing at the decisive moment has already made his decision. Everything after is just the slow business of catching up to it.

* * *

He found Jude at the workshop window, watching the far road through a gap in the frost-ferned glass. She didn't startle when he came in. She never did anymore. That was its own kind of wound — that she had stopped being afraid of him, somewhere along the winter, without ever quite trusting him.

"The patrol's moving," she said. "Bram's chaplain saw them billeted at Marnholt. They'll come up the river road when the break-up starts."

"When, not if."

"When." She turned from the window. Her face in the lamplight was thinner than he remembered from autumn, sharper, as if the winter had carved away everything ornamental and left only the load-bearing parts. "Osk says two days. Maybe three."

"Then we go the first night the shelf will hold a walker."

"We?"

The word hung there. He let it.

"I've been doing sums," Halloway said. He set the watch and the ring and the chits on the workbench between them, next to the type blocks, next to the great silent press that Elias loved the way other men loved wives or countries. "The ferry takes three. There are four of us going south — you, Elias, Wren, myself. And Bram, if he lives, makes five."

"You could stay for the second crossing—"

"There is no second crossing, Major's daughter's arithmetic notwithstanding. You know it and I know it. The shelf goes when the river says it goes, and the river is done negotiating." He kept his voice level. It was the voice he used for orders, and he hated himself a little for using it now, because this was not an order. This was the opposite of an order. This was a man asking to be released from his own rank. "Someone draws the patrol off so the burning detail looks east instead of at the water. Someone buys you the hours. That someone cannot be Elias — his hands can't hold a rifle, let alone a horse's reins, and both of us know it. It can't be Wren; he carries half the copies and his death would kill them too. And it cannot be you, because you are the ledger now. You are the only original left in the world, and I have spent eleven years learning exactly how much an original is worth."

Jude looked at him for a long time. Outside, the ice groaned again — a sound like a cathedral door being forced.

"You're volunteering," she said.

"I'm volunteering."

"To die."

"To be *useful*," he said, and heard how thin the distinction was, and pressed on anyway. "There's a difference. Dying is what happens to careless men. Useful is a thing you choose. I have been neither careful nor useful for most of my career, Miss Dunmore, and I find I've run out of patience for it."

"That isn't true." She said it fast, almost angry. "You struck names off reports for two years and then you kept your own list of every one of them like a man keeping score against his own soul — that's not carelessness, Major, that's—"

"Guilt. Yes. Guilt is cheap. I've carried tons of it and it weighs nothing at all, it's the lightest cargo in the world. What costs is this." He touched the edge of her ledger where it lay wrapped in oilcloth, waiting. "What costs is making the numbers come out right for once."

* * *

He went to see Elias that evening, and it was harder than telling Jude, though he couldn't have said why until he was standing in the doorway of the print room watching the man work.

Elias printed with ruined hands. Frostbite had taken the fine feeling from his fingers; he'd told Halloway once that he could feel the type but not the temperature, which meant he burned himself on the ink stove two and three times a day and simply didn't notice until he smelled it. He worked anyway. He worked the way penitents pray — mechanically, totally, with the whole body. The last sheets were drying on lines strung across the ceiling like laundry, forty names to a page, page after page after page, the whole lost regiment of them hanging in the warm air.

"You should be resting," Halloway said.

"I'll rest in spring." Elias didn't look up. "Or I won't. Either way there's a sheet coming off this press tonight."

"Elias."

That got him. The man looked up, and Halloway saw him read the situation the way printers read a page — quickly, completely, top to bottom. His eyes went to Halloway's face and then to the greatcoat Halloway hadn't taken off and then to the riding gloves tucked in his belt.

"You're going somewhere," Elias said. "Alone."

"The eastern road. Tomorrow night, when the burning starts. The patrol comes up from Marnholt; someone needs to make them chase a ghost down the wrong fork for as many hours as a ghost can run." Halloway held up a hand before the other man could speak. "Don't waste breath on me. I've argued it with myself all day and I won by a landslide."

"She'll refuse it."

"She doesn't get a vote. Neither do you." He stepped into the room, into the smell of ink and tallow and drying paper, and found that his throat had gone tight around what came next. "But you get a message, and I'd rather deliver it than leave it unsaid, because I've spent a lifetime leaving things unsaid and I'm told the habit is curable."

Elias set down the brayer. Waited.

"I love her," Halloway said. "You knew that. She knows it. It doesn't matter — not because she loves you more, though I believe she does, but because loving a woman isn't the same as being able to stand beside her, and I have finally learned to tell those two things apart. I could cross on that ferry tomorrow and spend the rest of my life in some southern town being the man who got out, and every morning over breakfast I would know precisely what I was: a man who saved himself and called it sacrifice because it hurt. I'm done calling things what they aren't. It's the family trade, lying on paper. I'm retiring."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "She'll hate it."

"Yes."

"She'll carry it across the ice with her, on top of everything else, and it will weigh more than the ledger."

"Yes."

"And you want me to tell her—" Elias stopped. Started again, slower. "What exactly am I telling her?"

"That it wasn't a bargain. That I didn't trade my life for her forgiveness, because forgiveness was never mine to ask for — I erased men and then wrote their names down in secret, which is like robbing a house and then drawing a map to the loot. Tell her I finally found one piece of duty worth doing clean." Halloway looked at the hanging sheets, all those names swaying gently in the draft. "Tell her the list ends well. Some of it. One line of it."

* * *

He spent the last evening the way he imagined condemned men spent theirs, except that no one had condemned him — he had volunteered, which was either better or worse, he hadn't decided. He cleaned his revolver. He ate the whole tin of rations he'd been hoarding, because wasted food offended him even now. He took out his own ledger — the small black book, the confession he'd carried for two years — and checked it against Jude's merged master copy one final time, name by name, and found four he'd recorded that she hadn't, men struck from rolls in garrisons she'd never reached.

He added them himself. Set the type was beyond him, but Elias had taught him to write the block letters large enough to cut, and Elias cut them, saying nothing, working beside him while the lamp burned low. Four names. Forty minutes. When it was done, Elias pulled a proof sheet and handed it over without ceremony, and Halloway held it to the light and read four ordinary names in fresh black ink and felt something in his chest unlock that had been rusted shut since the autumn of '15.

"That's it," he said. "That's all I have."

"It's not nothing."

"No," Halloway agreed. "It's not nothing. It's the whole of it, actually. Two years of my life reduced to four lines of proof, and it's the only honest work I ever produced." He folded the sheet carefully and put it inside his coat, over his heart, which was sentimental, and he permitted himself the sentiment because tomorrow sentiment would be a luxury item.

Near midnight, Jude came to him.

He was checking the revolver again — third time, a man's rituals shrinking as his world did — and she stood in the doorway with a blanket around her shoulders and the lamplight behind her, and for a moment she was just a tired young woman in a cold room, and his heart did the old treacherous lurch, and he let it, because he'd earned one lurch.

"Osk says the shelf might take four," she said. "If we go light. If we go early."

"Osk says the shelf might hold a piano if the piano apologizes first. He's guessing. They're all guesses now — the ice, the patrols, the burning. Guesses with weather in them." He set the revolver down. "Jude."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't do the thing where you make it sound reasonable. I've watched you do it to officers and it works on them because they're paid to be reasoned at. I'm not paid."

"Then hear it unreasonable." He stood. Up close she smelled of ink and woodsmoke and the camphor they used on the wounded, the whole winter distilled into one smell, and he knew he would carry that smell into whatever came next, whatever that was. "I spent eleven years as the kind of officer whose signature made widows. Efficiently. Correctly. I told myself it was the system, the war, the necessity — everyone tells themselves that, it's practically the uniform. And then a woman with a notebook started asking questions in my casualty tent, and I discovered that the system had a face, and the face was asking me why Thomas Dunmore wasn't in my report, and I had no answer that survived daylight."

She had gone very still.

"So here is the unreasonable version," he went on. "I cannot cross that river with you. Not because the shelf won't bear me. Because *I* won't bear me. Because the man I am trying to become — badly, slowly, with relapses — that man does not get on ferries while other men ride out to draw fire. That man stays. That man walks down the eastern road tonight with a lantern and a loud horse and makes himself so visible that every soldier in Marnholt watches him instead of the water." He smiled, and found it was easy, which surprised him. "I've been invisible my whole career, Jude. Invisible men survive wars. Let me be visible for six hours. Let me be legible."

"You could die."

"I could. Or I could ride hard, lose them at the millrace, circle back north and turn up in Vellmere in April smelling of horse and lies, insufferable about it for the rest of our lives." He watched her face try to decide whether to believe that. "Give it even odds. Better than the ice gives any of us."

"It isn't fair," she whispered. "You giving this to us. It isn't fair and I won't—"

"You'll accept it. That's the whole discipline of it. Receiving is harder than giving; I learned that from watching you take every name those officers threw away. You never wanted the burden either. You carried it." He took her hand — ungloved, ink-stained, cold — and pressed the folded proof sheet into it. Four names in his own blocky letters. "For the ledger. Merge them properly. And when you read the dedication of the book Elias will inevitably print someday, remember that chapter twenty-six of your life had a major in it who finally got one thing right."

She was crying. She did it silently, the way she did everything, tears running without a single sound, and she gripped his hand hard enough to hurt, and he thought: *this, this is the payment, this is more than I ever earned,* and he stood in it for as long as she allowed.

Then she said, "Come back," and it wasn't an order or a plea, it was simply a fact she was putting on record, entering it in the ledger of the world, and he nodded, because there was no honest answer but assent.

"At dawn," he said. "Before the smoke. I'll say goodbye to the others and be gone before the first match is struck." He hesitated at the door. "One more thing. The bridge — Petra. When they rebuild it, and they will, stone this time —"

"I know," she said softly. "Rock. I've known all winter."

Of course she had. She knew everything written down anywhere. He went out into the dark to saddle his horse, and the ice groaned on the river like a giant clearing its throat, and east along the far road, faint as a lie, the first fires were lighting.

By dawn the smoke would rise over the quarter, and Major Edmund Halloway would ride straight into it, visible at last — and whether the road gave him back or kept him, the names were going south over the ice, and that, he told the horse, was the entire point of a man.

Behind him, in the workshop, Elias began breaking down the press.
