## 27. The Burning Begins

The smoke came first as a smudge on the eastern sky, the way a bruise comes before the pain.

Jude saw it from the workshop window while she was hanging the morning's sheets — forty pages of names, ink still tacky, pinned to lines of twine that Elias had strung across the rafters like a laundry of the dead. She had a clothespin in her teeth and a page in each hand, and for a long moment she simply stood there, arms up, watching the gray bloom rise over the rooftops of the eastern quarter and flatten into the low clouds.

"Bram said they'd start at the granary," she said, not turning. "He said the granary first, then the chandlery, then the records office."

Elias did not answer. He was at the press, and the press was speaking — that iron heartbeat she had come to think of as the workshop's own pulse, the lever falling, the platen biting, the bed sliding home. He had been at it since before dawn, since Wren left with the first half of the copies sewn into the lining of his coat, and he had not stopped except to re-ink the rollers or to press his ruined fingertips, wrapped now in strips of her petticoat, against a block of ice from the window ledge.

"Elias." She let the clothespin fall into her palm. "They're burning the eastern quarter."

"I know." The lever fell. The bed slid. "I can smell it."

She could too, now that he'd named it. Under the coal-smoke smell of the press and the linseed smell of the ink there was something else, something older and fouler — wet wood burning green, and under that, faintly, the sweetish reek of burning stores. Grain. Rope. Tar. A town's winter being fed to the flames.

She hung the two pages and went to the door. The street outside was nearly empty, which was worse than if it had been full of panic. Panic at least meant people believed they had somewhere to go. The few figures she could see were moving with the terrible unhurried purpose of people who had already done their arithmetic — a woman rolling a barrel that was too heavy for her, stopping, rolling it again; two boys carrying a door off its hinges, for what use she couldn't imagine; old Marta Voss standing in her doorway with a carpet bag on each arm, looking east, looking west, looking at nothing.

The wind was from the east. That was the thing she kept coming back to, the fact her mind circled like a bird that wouldn't land. The wind was from the east, and the smoke was coming toward them, and the workshop was three streets from the river with a yard full of drying pages and a press that could not run in a draft.

She went back inside and shut the door.

* * *

"How many?" she asked.

Elias didn't pretend to misunderstand. He pulled the lever again — the sheet came off the form crooked, and he swore, a small precise oath, and fed another in. "Eleven forms left. Eleven pulls a form if I don't foul one. Say two hours if the paper holds and my hands hold."

"And if neither holds?"

"Then it's however long it takes." He looked at her then, and she saw what the night at the depot had left in his face — the fever had broken a week ago but it had taken something with it, some layer of insulation between him and the world. His eyes were too clear now. "Jude. Go and look at the river. Tell me what it's doing."

"The river's doing what it's been doing for three days."

"Tell me anyway."

So she went to the back window, the one that faced the water through a gap in the chandlery's wall, and she looked. The ice was no longer ice in the way it had been all winter. It was a country coming apart at its seams. Down the middle of the channel the current had sawed a lane of black water, and the lane was widening by the hour, and the floes that drifted in it were the size of tables, then of rooms, turning slowly like great pale animals. The shore-fast shelf still held — Osk's shelf, the shelf he had promised them, a tongue of ice hugging the near bank where the current ran gentler — but it groaned when she listened to it, a long low complaint like a door in an empty house.

"Shelf's still there," she said. "Lane's wider. Floes the size of the chandlery roof."

"Then we have until the shelf goes," Elias said, "and not one minute past it. Which is more time than the fire will give us, if the wind holds." He pulled the lever. "So we don't talk about it anymore. We talk about pages."

She wanted to argue — wanted it with a physical ache, the way you want to scratch — but she had learned this about him in the weeks since the depot, since the fever, since the night he'd said her name in delirium and she'd answered it aloud and neither of them had ever taken the words back. He did not argue about the things that frightened him. He worked. The fear went into the work like ink into paper, and came out the other side as something you could hold.

So she worked too.

The system they had built over the past days was simple and they ran it like a machine themselves. Elias pulled the sheets. She carried them, wet, to the lines, hung them by the corners, and then took down the ones that had dried overnight and stacked them in the oilcloth — the good oilcloth, the sailcloth-weight stuff Osk had traded them from a boat he'd sworn was beyond saving, cut now into wraps sized to the ledgers, each wrap with its bundle of pages and its cork float sewn into the hem. Halloway's list, merged with hers, in three copies. The chaplain's page, restored to the whole, in three copies. Her original ledger, the one that had started with a single name written in a flooded ward while a boy who was not going to live watched her do it — that one she kept apart, in its own wrap, against her own body when the time came. It was not for the river to carry. It was for her.

She was on her fourth trip between press and lines when the light changed.

It was not a dramatic thing. The gray morning through the east window simply went the color of tea, and then the color of rust, and then a shadow moved across the floor that had no object to cast it, and she understood that the fire had jumped whatever line the patrol had meant to hold it to. She went to the east window. The granary was gone — she could see that at a glance, the great humped silhouette of it simply absent, replaced by a standing column of flame that leaned east and then, horribly, leaned west, leaned toward them, as the wind gusted and shifted. The chandlery's roof was smoking along its whole length like a wick.

"Elias."

"I saw it." He did not stop. "How far?"

"Two streets. Maybe less. The wind's turning."

"Then we're down to paper and hands." He pulled the lever. A sheet came out clean. "Hang it."

She hung it. Her hands were steady and she noted that they were steady with a kind of distant surprise, the way you note the weather. There was soot in the air now, actual flakes of it, drifting through the window gap and settling on the drying pages like gray snow, and she found herself brushing each page clean with the side of her hand before it went into the oilcloth, because a name with a smut of ash across it was still a name but she could not bear for it to look less than legible. These pages were going to outlive all of them. They were going to be read aloud in a church she would never see by a man who might be shot on the road south. They could not arrive dirty.

The thought of Wren on the south road steadied her and unsteadied her at once. He had left at dawn with half the copies and a face like a man going into cold water on purpose. He had shaken her hand. He had said, *Tell them I read every name twice,* which was the kind of sentence you only say when you are not sure you'll have another chance. She had watched him go down the lane with his collar up and the ledger-weight of him sewn into his coat, and she had not let herself think *I may be watching the last of this work walk out of the world.* She was not letting herself think it now, either. There was no time for it. There was only the next page.

* * *

The heat arrived before the fire did.

It came down the street in front of the flames the way a tide comes in front of the wave, a pressure against the face, a drying of the lips, and with it came the sound — not the roar she had expected but a kind of vast busy crackling, like the whole world being unfolded and refolded by enormous hands. Somewhere a window burst. Somewhere a beam went down with a boom she felt in her back teeth.

"Jude." Elias's voice cut through it. "The lines. Take the lines down. Everything that's dry comes off, everything that's wet stays on your body, we're done hanging pages."

She looked at the twine. Thirty-odd sheets, the morning's work, the last forms — and among them, she could see by the weight of ink, the final pages of Halloway's list, the names he had carried alone for two years, the names he had written in his own hand in a tent by lamplight because he could not sleep for them. She took them down with a care that felt insane given the noise, sheet by sheet, and laid them flat in the crate, and then she took the crate's contents and wrapped them, all of them, in oilcloth, and sewed the last hem with the needle she kept on a string around her neck, her fingers doing the work while her eyes kept flicking to the window.

"The press," she said. "Elias. The press."

"Not yet."

"Osk said—"

"Osk said he'd take the ledgers. I know what Osk said." The lever fell. The platen bit. "Two forms left, Jude. Two. Give me the two."

She gave him the two, because there was no argument in the world that could beat two forms, and because she understood — had understood since the night he'd told her about the falsified rolls, since the first moment she'd truly seen him — that the press was not a machine to him. It was the place where he had decided what kind of man to be. He had been ordered to print lies once, and he had deserted rather than do it, and every clean sheet that came off this bed now was a sentence in a long argument he was having with his old self. She would not take the last two pages of it from him. She would only stand at the window with the oilcloth bundles stacked at her feet like ballast, and watch the fire come, and count.

The chandlery went while the second-to-last form was on the bed. It went all at once, the tarred roof catching with a sound like a gasp, and the heat through the window gap became a physical hand on her cheek, and the light in the room went orange. Sparks came through the gap in a windblown stream and landed on the floor, on the twine, on the stack of empty paper, and she beat them out with the wet blanket they'd kept ready for three days, and her hands were shaking now, finally, but only her hands, only the surface of her.

"Elias."

"One." He pulled the last-but-one sheet and it came out perfect and he did not even look at it, just fed the last form, inked the rollers with hands that were wrapped and ruined and did not hesitate. "Last one. Watch the door."

She watched the door. Through its gap she could see the street, and the street was unrecognizable — the light was wrong, the shadows were wrong, the whole world had been reprinted in red. The house at the corner of the lane, the one with the blue shutters where the convalescent sergeant's sister had lodged, was burning from the inside like a lantern. Embers were crossing the street overhead in a slow terrible migration, a river of them, going the way the wind went, going toward the workshop roof, toward the yard, toward the lines where the last wet sheets still hung.

She took the wet sheets down. There were four. She draped them over her forearm like towels, like washing, like the most ordinary things in the world, and stood there while they steamed faintly in the heat, and behind her the lever fell for the last time.

The sound it made was different. She knew the press's every voice by now — the bite, the slide, the sigh of the bed coming home — and this one had a finality in it, a period at the end. She turned. Elias stood with his hand still on the lever, and the last sheet was on the form, and he was looking down at it the way you look at a face.

"Take it off," she said gently. "Let me hang it."

"It doesn't need hanging." He lifted it, both hands, like something lifted from water. "It's dry enough. The fire will dry it." He almost smiled. "Everything dries near a fire."

He laid it on the top of the stack and she wrapped it, and her needle went through the oilcloth hem, and she sewed the last name of the last copy into its float-wrapped shroud while the window glass in the east wall began to sing.

* * *

Then he went to the press.

She understood what he was doing one beat before he did it, and she did not stop him, and she would carry to the end of her life the fact that she did not stop him — and the other fact, the one that sat beside it like a twin, that she would not have been able to.

He took the pry bar from under the bench and put it to the frame, to the joint where the iron met the iron, the joint he had shown her once in a quieter week, saying *this is where a press is a person, everything else is furniture.* And he leaned his whole weight on it. And the press — the press that had printed her first pages, that had printed the ledger through the inspection and the fever and the nights of lamplight, that had been the only heart this quarter had left — the press screamed, one long iron note, and came apart.

He did it again. And again. He broke the platen free and smashed it against the bed until the bed was a dish. He broke the frame at its knees. He fed the type — the beautiful type, the type she had learned to set under his hands, the type that had held every name — into the fire barrel and stood there while the lead went soft and the letters lost their faces. He was burning his fingers doing it; she could see the wrappings smoking; she pulled him back by the shoulders and he let her, finally, and they stood together in the orange light, the two of them, breathing, while the thing he had built his whole self around lay in pieces on the floor where no patrol would ever print a lie with it again.

"They can't have it," he said. His voice was very quiet under the roar. "They can't have any of it."

"I know," she said. "Elias — the roof. Look at the roof."

A spark had found the thatch over the drying lines. It glowed there like a coal in a pipe, then caught, a small bright tongue licking up into the old straw. The yard was lost; the workshop had minutes; the river was through the back gap and down the lane, and the shelf, Osk's shelf, was out there in the smoke-hazed light, groaning, holding, for now.

She took up the bundles — all of them, the whole merged weight of the dead men's names, Halloway's and hers and the chaplain's page, wrapped and floated and strapped — and she slung the satchel across her chest, and she put her hand flat against the original ledger where it rode beneath her coat, against her heart, where it had ridden since the first night.

"Out the back," she said. "Now. Elias — *now*."

He came. He came away from the wreck of his press with his burned hands open at his sides like a man who has set down something too heavy to describe, and they went out through the back gap into the lane, into the ember-river and the roaring light, and behind them the thatch went up with a sound like a held breath released, and ahead of them, through the smoke, she could see the water — the black lane of it, the drifting floes, and the pale tongue of the shelf that was the only bridge left in the world.

And on the shelf, small in the smoke, a figure stood waving them on — and beside it, another, and the second figure was not moving, and her heart went down like a stone before she saw it was only the wind bending him, only Osk holding his arm, only two men waiting at the edge of the breaking ice for her to run.
