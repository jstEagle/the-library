## 28. Last Impression

The smoke came first, before any order to run, before the bell or the shouting — it came down the river road like weather, gray and patient, and Elias Voss stood at his press with ink to his elbows and thought: *so this is what you smell like, the end of things.*

Not burning yet. Burning was coming. The eastern quarter had gone up an hour past dawn, and the wind carried the taste of it across the water — wet ash, charred timber, something sweeter underneath that he refused to name because he knew exactly what it was. He had printed casualty lists for three years. He knew what mass burning smelled like when it wasn't paper.

"Sheet's set," Bram said behind him. The orderly stood at the stone with the last forme locked in, his hands steady now in a way they had not been in November, when he'd come through the door half-starved carrying his guilt like a second kit bag. "Last one. Type's yours if you want to read it."

Elias read it. You always read the last one. It was a rule nobody had taught him and everybody who worked a press learned anyway: the final impression of anything deserves a reader.

It was a short page. Forty-one names, the last stragglers from the flooded ward at Vellmere, the ones Jude had pried out of dying men by candlelight over four nights while the river groaned and the patrols sharpened their torches somewhere out of sight. At the bottom, below the rule line, in type he had set himself without telling anyone, a single line:

*Set down so they were never not here.*

He looked at it for a long moment.

"It'll do," he said, which was the most praise he gave anything, and Bram almost smiled.

* * *

The press had come to him in pieces, in a farmer's cart, in the spring of 1914, before the war had a name anyone believed in. He had reassembled it alone in this workshop over nine days with blistered palms and a borrowed manual written in a language he barely read, tightening each screw as though the machine were a thing that could be wounded. His master in Lysse had called presses *the congregation* — every iron member with its office, its duty, its place in the body — and had made the apprentices kiss the platen on their first day, which was ridiculous, and which Elias had done, and which he had never told anyone about until he told Jude, one lamplit night in February, and she had laughed until she cried and then kissed his ink-stained mouth and said, *of course you did.*

The press had printed falsified rolls once. That was the sin he could not burn out of himself. In the spring of '15, in another town under another officer, he had set type declaring men alive who were dead, and dead who had deserted, and he had pulled the lever forty times, and every pull had gone through his arms like a confession he couldn't stop making. He had walked away from the garrison two weeks later with nothing but his coat and his composing stick, and the army called it desertion, and he called it the first honest act of his life.

Then he had met a woman in a flooded field hospital who was writing dead men's names in a ledger no officer would sanction, and she needed pages, and he had a press, and for two years the machine that had lied for a living had done nothing but tell the truth.

"You're staring at it," Bram said.

"I'm memorizing it."

"Elias." Bram's voice did something careful. "They're coming."

"I know they're coming. That's why I'm memorizing it."

* * *

Jude had left an hour ago with Osk, down toward the bridge site with the merged ledger sealed against her chest, and the image of her going — hood up, satchel strapped tight, turning back once at the door to look at him with an expression he had decided not to interpret, because interpreting it would have meant standing still forever — was folded away somewhere behind his sternum where he kept everything he intended to feel later, when there was time. There was no time. There was a forme locked in the chase and a fire crossing the eastern quarter street by street, and there were copies still hanging on the drying lines like strange pale laundry, and there was Wren already gone south with half the run hidden in hymnals, and there was Halloway out on the far road doing whatever a man does when he decides to spend himself buying minutes for other people.

And there was the press.

"Pull it," Elias said. "I'll feed."

Bram laid the dampened sheet. Elias watched the tympan close over it like a hand folding shut. And then he took the lever in both hands — his right one still clumsy from the frostbite, the fingers slow to answer, slow as they had been since the depot ice let go beneath him and the river tried to keep him — and pulled.

The machine spoke. It always spoke; even the army hadn't managed to make it quiet. A breath, a bite, the great iron heart compressing ink into fiber, and then the release, and Bram turned the sheet free, and the last page of the last copy of the Vellmere ledger lay flat on the stone, still wet, still true.

Forty-one names. Set down so they were never not here.

Bram hung it with the others. Eleven copies of the full run, plus Wren's half, plus the original in oilcloth walking east on Jude Dunmore's chest. Somewhere south, in a church he would never see, a chaplain would read them aloud. Somewhere in years he would never predict, someone's daughter would find her father's name in a printed book and know he had existed.

That was the arithmetic. Elias had done it a hundred times and it still didn't balance, because the other column held a workshop, a trade, a craft, a machine — and a man's whole self, the self built between the age of eleven and the age of thirty-one out of lead and iron and the particular smell of ink, and all of it was forfeit, all of it burned or broken or left, and the only question left in the world was how much of it he chose to destroy rather than surrender.

"Take the copies," he said. "All of them. Down to Osk's shed, under the nets. Go now, don't argue."

"They won't burn the shed—"

"They'll burn everything with a roof. Under the nets, under the fish boxes, under whatever stinks enough to make a soldier lazy. Go."

Bram went. Through the window Elias watched him hurry down the lane with the copies wrapped in sacking, a slight man bent under a light-looking burden that weighed more than iron, and Elias thought, absurdly, of the day Bram had confessed — standing in this same room, shaking, telling them about the printer he had informed on in Harrow Fen, the one taken away in a closed cart — and of Jude saying, *then you'll carry double,* and meaning it kindly, and Bram carrying double ever since, and carrying well.

The smoke thickened outside. Somewhere a whistle blew, twice, the patrol signal. Minutes, then. Not hours. Minutes.

* * *

He talked to the press while he worked. He had always talked to it; masters did, apprentices did, everyone who spent ten thousand hours alone with a machine developed the habit or went quietly mad, and Elias had never been sure which he'd done.

"Well," he said, wiping the rollers. "You remember Lysse. You remember what we printed there before all this. Wedding invitations. A run of hymnals for Father Anselm that he never paid for, the old thief. Playbills for the Gildenhall theater, and one of the compositors put a rude word in the middle of Act Two and we caught it one sheet too late and the whole town laughed for a month."

The press said nothing. Presses are good listeners.

"And then the rolls," he said, and stopped wiping. "You remember the rolls. I've never blamed you for that. A press prints what it's given. That's your whole virtue and your whole damnation, isn't it — you can't refuse. Whatever hand feeds you, you speak. So it was never your lie. It was mine. And then she came in here with her ledger smelling of river water and carbolic, and you got fed the truth instead, and look what you became."

He looked around the workshop. Two cots against the wall, empty since the convalescents had gone with the ferry list. The type cases, his alphabet in wood and lead, twenty years of his handwriting in metal. The composing stick with a setting still in it — he checked, out of habit, and found the ghost of a job abandoned mid-line, some ornament ordered by a customer who would never collect. The lamp. The stool worn hollow in the middle by his own weight, year after year, the shape of him pressed into pine like a fossil.

An army was coming to burn this room. And requisition orders being what they were, and quartermasters being what they were, they might not burn everything. They might take. Machines were scarce; iron was money; a working Albion press was worth more to a quartermaster than the truth ever would be. They would haul it out on a cart, stamp a number on it, set it up in some garrison depot, and feed it.

Feed it rolls. Feed it orders. Feed it whatever lie the next officer needed spoken, because that was what presses did, that was what they were *for*, they spoke whatever they were given —

No.

"No," Elias said aloud, to the machine this time, and his voice cracked in the middle of it like green wood.

* * *

He got the crowbar from under the floorboards where Osk kept boat tools, and he stood in front of the press for the length of three breaths, and in those three breaths he was eleven years old kissing cold iron on his first morning, and seventeen pulling his first solo edition, and twenty-four printing lies, and twenty-nine printing truth, and thirty-one standing in smoke holding a bar of iron with no idea that grief and liberation were the same weight and would arrive in the same instant.

Then he swung.

The first blow took the side brace and the whole frame shuddered like a struck animal, and he heard himself make a sound — not a word, just a sound, low and involuntary — and he swung again. The frisket tore loose. The platen tilted off its gibs with a scream of metal that he felt in his teeth, and he swung again and again, and the crowbar rang against the iron ribs of the thing that had been his congregation, his confessor, his only honest witness, and it broke, it broke in ways that could never be reassembled by any quartermaster with any manual, gib keys sheared, the screw spindle bent double, the beautiful machined face of the bed gouged to scrap.

Somewhere in the middle of it the crowbar slipped.

Or maybe it didn't slip. Maybe he simply stopped caring where his hand was, because the metal was hot — the friction, the blows, the iron itself seemed fevered — and when he wrenched the platen free of its last bolt the heated edge came across his bare palm and the bare backs of his fingers and the pain arrived a half-second late, white and total, like the flash of a press biting down on a careless hand.

Elias dropped the crowbar. He stood in the wreck of his own life's work with his hand open at his side and blood starting along four lines across his knuckles and the burn rising in blisters already, and he did not cry out, because the sound he made next was laughter, ragged and astonished and real.

"There," he said to the wreckage, to the army that wouldn't get it, to the war, to nobody. "There. Now you can't lie for anyone."

His fingers were shaking. He looked at them — the right hand, the frostbit one, the one the river had already claimed part of — and saw the skin across the knuckles going the deep wrong red of a real burn, and he wrapped it in the rag from the ink slab without much tenderness, because there was no time for tenderness, and because some small clear part of him noted that a printer with ruined hands was a printer finished, and that this, today, was somehow correct. The press was smashed. The hands that served it should retire with it. Let the scars match the wreckage. Let him carry the proof on his body the way Jude carried the names on her chest.

He took one type block from the case before he left. Only one. The T. Thomas Dunmore. He'd cut it himself in January, a single letter in a sea of thousands, and he'd pulled one impression of it alone on scrap paper and given it to her, and she kept it inside the ledger's back board, and he had told himself he didn't need a twin of it. But a man leaving a burning country takes one small thing that isn't practical, or he doesn't survive what comes after. He pocketed it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

Outside, the world had gone the color of the inside of a stove.

The eastern quarter was fully alight now, a wall of smoke leaning on the wind, and the fire had crossed the canal road and taken the tannery, and the tannery burned with a greasy black column that stained the clouds like spilled ink — and Elias, coughing, squinting through streaming eyes, thought: *even the sky is getting typeset today.* The whistle blew again, closer. Boots on the lane, more than one pair. The burning detail, methodical as a press run, working quarter by quarter, house by house.

Osk appeared out of the smoke at the end of the lane, moving fast for an old man, and grabbed Elias's sleeve with a grip like a mooring cleat.

"The shed's loaded. Nets over the copies. Now we move, printer, unless you've grown roots."

"The press—"

"Is scrap," Osk said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat mercy of a man who had buried sons. "Scrap they can't use. Best work you ever did. Come."

Elias came. At the corner he stopped — one turn of his head, half a second he couldn't help — and looked back down the lane at the workshop door standing open on darkness, at the smoke beginning to curl out of it like the building exhaling, and he did not see the press anymore. He saw the lamp, the stool, the type cases, two years of true pages, a woman laughing at his story about kissing the platen, Bram learning to set his first line with his tongue between his teeth, the whole fragile congregation of it.

Then the first flame showed in the doorway, orange and small and horribly domestic, like a hearth lit for someone expected home.

Elias turned and ran, Osk hauling him by the good arm, toward the shore, toward the nets, toward the shelf of ice where Jude had gone and where the river was already talking in its sleep — long groans rolling under the fog like distant guns, the sound of a season ending, the sound of the way out closing.

Behind them the workshop caught properly, and the light of it reached them even through the smoke, and ahead of them the bank dropped away into white mist, and somewhere out in that mist, on a road or a ruin or the far edge of the breaking ice, a woman was running with forty-one hundred names strapped to her chest — and Elias Voss ran after her with burned fingers and one letter of type in his pocket, empty-handed, unburdened, freer than he had ever been in his life, and terrified that free would not be enough to reach the other side.
