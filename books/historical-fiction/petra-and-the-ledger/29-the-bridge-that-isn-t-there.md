## 29. The Bridge That Isn't There

We ran the last half-mile with the smoke at our backs and the river's roar in our ears, and I remember thinking — absurdly, stupidly — that the bridge would be there. That it had to be. Everything we had done, everything we had burned and broken and bled for, had been arranged around one fixed point: the crossing at Vellmere, the iron span that had carried convoys and coffins and men like Halloway back and forth all winter. It was the one thing the war could not take from us because it was the one thing we needed more than the army did.

The road bent along the bluff, and I saw the gap before I understood what I was seeing.

Where the span should have been — where I had watched Major Halloway's convoy crawl across in December, a line of black dots against gray water — there was nothing. Open sky. The far abutment stood alone on the opposite bank, its stonework sheared clean, frost hanging off the raw edge like torn lace. The near end of the bridge still existed: two hundred feet of iron trusswork reaching out over the water and then simply ending, curled down into the current like a hand that had let go.

Elias stopped first. He had been ahead of me, satchel straps cutting his shoulders, and he pulled up so hard I nearly ran into him. Osk came behind us with the oilcloth bundles, breathing hard, and then he too went silent.

"Days," Elias said. "It must have gone days ago."

I couldn't answer. My mouth was full of smoke and the taste of copper where I'd bitten my cheek during the run. I walked to the edge of the roadway, past an abandoned supply cart with its shafts snapped, and looked down.

The river was not a river anymore. It was a moving country.

Ice floes the size of barn roofs ground downstream in slow, irresistible processions, tilting, colliding, riding up over one another with sounds like cannon shot. Between them the water showed black and quick, full of spring melt, running faster than any horse could swim. Where the piers had stood, there were only stumps of stone with white water breaking over them. One pier had a length of truss wrapped around it, twisted steel strangled in ice, groaning every time a floe struck it.

"They'll have heard it go," Osk said quietly. "Miles away. Like the sky cracking."

"When?" I asked. Nobody answered, so I asked again, louder, as if the question were aimed at someone responsible. "When did it fall?"

Osk shrugged. "With break-up. Maybe three days past. Maybe four. Ice takes what it wants." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, a man who had spent his life reading water the way priests read scripture.

Three days. Four days. All winter the bridge had been our arithmetic — the ledger weighed against the calendar, the burning weighed against the crossing — and the whole time the answer had already been decided somewhere upstream in the dark, in the pressure of a million tons of ice deciding to move.

I laughed. I heard myself do it and hated the sound, a short ugly bark that scared a crow off the dead cart. Because it was funny, wasn't it? We had outrun the patrols. We had carried the names out of a burning quarter on our backs. And the river had simply closed the book days before we reached it.

* * *

We came down off the road to the old ferry landing below the bridge site, because standing above that gap was unbearable and standing beside it was only terrible. The landing was a rotted platform on pilings, half its planks gone, a mooring post worn smooth by fifty years of rope. The ice here was shore-fast — held to the bank, unmoving — but forty yards out the channel ran open, black water shouldering between grinding floes.

Elias sat down heavily on the mooring post. His hands were ruined — I knew that better than anyone, having changed the dressings myself these past days, the frostbite from the depot having taken feeling from three of his fingers permanently — and he cradled them now in his lap like something borrowed and fragile.

"The ferry," I said. "There has to be a boat."

"There was a boat," Osk said. He nodded upstream, toward a shape snagged in the shallows: a flat-bottomed ferry hull, stove through, driven halfway onto the bank by a floe and left there tilted like a beached whale. "That's it. Or what's left. The ice took it the same night it took the bridge."

I stared at it. "You crossed this river your whole life."

"In seasons," Osk said. "Not this season. Not now." He crouched at the edge of the landing and studied the water with the flat attention of a man pricing something. "Look at her. Full melt. Every day more water coming down from the hills. Those floes are moving at a gallop under all that calm. A man goes in there, he's under the next floe inside a minute. They don't even leave you on top. They pull you down."

"So there is no way across." I said it plainly, wanting it contradicted. Wanting one of them — either of them — to tell me I was wrong.

Neither did.

I turned and looked back at the town. Smoke still rose from the eastern quarter, a dirty brown column leaning in the wind. From this distance it was almost peaceful, almost beautiful, the way destruction always is from far enough away. Somewhere in that smoke was the workshop. The press. Whatever Elias hadn't smashed, whatever the patrols hadn't burned yet, going up in ash while I stood here doing arithmetic with a dead river.

"We carried them out for nothing," I said. "All of it. Thomas's name. Your list, Halloway's list, the chaplain's page — we sealed them in oilcloth and we ran, and there is no south side of the river anymore. There is no anywhere."

"Jude." Elias's voice was quiet. "Sit down."

"I will not sit down."

"Then stand and listen." He got up slowly, favoring his hands, and came to stand beside me at the landing's broken edge. Below us the water ran. "The names aren't across the river yet. But they're also not ash. We're holding them. As long as we're standing here breathing, the ledger exists. You taught me that. First chapter of your own book."

"That's not enough," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Elias, I didn't carry these names out of a fire so they could die with me on the wrong bank. There are families. There is a church in the south where Wren is reading them right now, or trying to, and if our copies never come—" I pressed my fist against my mouth. "My brother waited three years for his name. Three years. I will not make another mother wait because of a river."

The wind came off the water, cold despite the season, carrying grit and spray. Downstream a floe the size of a chapel struck the remains of the third pier and the whole wreck shuddered; the twisted truss work gave a long metallic scream that I felt in my teeth. It sounded, God help me, like the workshop had sounded when the press broke apart under Elias's hands.

Osk was still crouched at the edge. Still watching the water. But he was watching it differently now — not pricing it, but measuring it, his eyes tracking the seam where shore-fast ice met the moving channel, following that seam downstream along the bank, then further, then out of sight behind the bluff.

"Elias," he said at last. "Your hands. How bad?"

Elias flexed them. I saw him hide the wince. "They grip. Badly."

"And you, missus? Can you walk on ice?"

"I've walked on ice my whole life. Every child here has."

"Not this ice," Osk said. He rose, joints cracking, and pointed downstream along the base of the bluff. "Shore-fast shelf runs down that way. Holds to the bank all the way past the mill ruins, maybe farther — it holds every year, because the current bends away from that shore. Out past the bend it thins. Past the mill, nobody knows. Some years it holds to the narrows. Some years it lets go in February."

"You're saying there's a path," Elias said.

"I'm saying there's sometimes a path. Today. This hour." Osk turned and looked at us both, and for the first time since I'd known him I saw something move behind the flat calm of his face — not hope exactly, but its dangerous cousin. "Full break-up hasn't come. What's running in the channel is the vanguard. The main ice is still lying on the river waiting. A walker could go out on the shelf — maybe. Before the main body moves, before the water rises another foot. Hours, not days. And it's a gamble measured in hours, which means it isn't a plan. It's a wager."

"With what stakes?" I asked.

Osk looked at me a long moment. "Everything," he said simply. "Same stakes as always."

* * *

Elias and I stood apart from Osk after that, at the landward end of the ruined landing, while the old man walked slowly down the bank, pacing out distances only he could see, muttering numbers to himself — depths, currents, years of springs he had survived.

"He can't mean it," Elias said low. "Crossing on foot. In this."

"He does mean it."

"It's suicide dressed up as geography." His voice roughened. "Jude, look at the water. Look at it!"

So I did. Black water between white grinding fields, the roar of it constant now, a sound I realized I had been hearing in my sleep for a week — the river talking in its artillery voice, telling everyone with ears what was coming. The main break-up. The thing that would swallow the shelf Osk described, swallow the landing, swallow the stumps of the bridge, and turn Vellmere's river into a sea.

"If we wait," I said, "the patrol comes down this bank. If we wait, the thaw finishes what the fire started. If we wait—" I stopped. I made myself say the rest of it. "If we wait, the names drown anyway, just slower."

Elias shut his eyes. Wind moved his hair, grayer than it had been in autumn. When he opened his eyes again he looked at me with an expression I had seen once before — the night he told me about the falsified rolls, about why he deserted, about the moment a printer decides what his ink is for. Grief and decision in the same face.

"Then we don't wait," he said.

And that was when I saw it — movement on the far bank, small against the shear-cut abutment. A figure in a greatcoat, standing where the bridge used to begin. Even at this distance I knew the set of those shoulders, the way the man stood as if reporting for inspection.

Halloway. Stranded on the wrong side all along, or arrived too late — it didn't matter which. He raised one arm, and though no words could cross that roaring water, the gesture needed none. He pointed downstream, along our own bank, toward the mill ruins and the shelf of ice beyond.

He had counted the same arithmetic we had. He was telling us: *it holds, or it doesn't — but go.*

Behind him, on the far road, a second column of smoke was beginning to rise.
