## 30. Osk's Ice Road

Osk had lived on this river his whole life, and the river had taken from him the way old men take from a bottle — slowly at first, then all at once, then every day after that.

He knew its moods better than he knew the faces of his sons, and that was the truth of it, and the shame of it too. He could read ice the way a priest reads scripture. He could tell you by the sound alone whether a floe was thick enough for a cart or thin enough to drown a horse. He had ferried soldiers across in autumn when the water ran high and brown as tea, and he had refused them in spring when the current turned greedy, and no officer's rank had ever moved him an inch, because the river did not care about rank and neither did Osk.

But he had never seen it come apart like this.

He stood on the near bank in the grey hour before dawn, breath smoking, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that smelled of tar and fish and forty winters, and he listened.

The river spoke.

Not the low groan of January, which was only the cold settling into its bones. This was different. This was a language. Somewhere out past the bridge pilings — the broken stubs of them, black teeth in white water — the ice sheet cracked like a whip and then sighed, long and wet, and Osk felt the sound through his boots before he heard it with his ears. The whole winter's armor was letting go. Three days, maybe four, if the warm wind held from the south. Less if the rain came.

Behind him, up the slope, the eastern quarter burned.

Smoke stood over the rooftops in a straight grey column, unnervingly still in the dead air, and beneath it he could smell the thing that had been coming all winter — paper and pitch and whatever else they fed fires like that. He'd watched the patrol wagons go down the far road yesterday with their barrels lashed tight. He knew what was in the barrels. Everyone in Vellmere knew, and everyone pretended not to, because pretending was cheaper than knowing.

The workshop would be next. The printer's place, down by the water where the ground went soft in thaw. He'd delivered paper there twice in December, before anyone understood what the man was printing, and the woman — the Dunmore woman — had paid him in coins counted out one at a time like each one hurt her.

Osk spat into the shallows.

Three sons. That was the arithmetic he carried everywhere, the ledger under his own ribs that nobody struck names from. Henrik, twenty-two, gone at Kessel Field and never reported, just — absent, a hole shaped like a boy where a casualty list should have had a line. Marten, nineteen, drowned in a transport the army called a weather event. Little Anders, seventeen, who had lied about his age and lasted eleven weeks, and whose name their mother had waited two years to see in print and died without seeing.

Unnamed graves. That was the army's gift. A cross without a word on it, somewhere east, and a mother going blind reading official reports that had been written by men who thought grief was a supply problem.

So when the Dunmore woman had come down to his hut three days ago — mud to her knees, a satchel strap cutting her shoulder, eyes like someone who hadn't slept since the war started — and asked him what he knew about the ice, he had not pretended not to understand. He had looked at her a long moment, and then he had said: *Come back tomorrow.*

And then he had spent the whole night walking his river.

* * *

The shelf was an old secret, older than the war.

Every riverman on the Vell had one — a stretch of shore-fast ice, anchored to land where the current curled away instead of biting, where the freeze went deep and honest while the midriver sheet rotted first. Osk's ran along the north bluff below the old quarry road: a half-mile tongue of blue-black ice hugging the bank, sheltered from the main channel by a spine of rocks that stuck up like knuckles. In hard winters you could walk it end to end and never hear your own weight complain.

This was not a hard winter anymore. This was the winter dying.

He walked it now in the dark with an iron bar, testing, and the iron told him the truth in taps. Solid here. Solid. Then — a hollow note, a drummer's note, and he stopped and crouched and scraped the snow away with his boot heel and saw the trouble: a seep, water welling up through a pressure crack and spreading thin as window glass over the good ice beneath. Deceiving. Beautiful, even, in the starlight — a mirror laid flat on the river, and step on it wrong and you'd go through into water moving at four knots under eight feet of roof.

He marked it with a broken oar stuck upright in the ice, and moved on.

Halfway along, the shelf widened where a creek mouth had frozen flat, and there the bar rang true all the way across, a deep bell-note, ice five feet to the bottom of the sounding. But at the far end — the far end was the problem, the far end was always the problem — the shelf met the open channel, and the channel had already begun to move. He stood at the edge and watched the pack drift past in plates and slabs, grinding, hissing, shouldering against the fast ice with a sound like furniture dragged across a floor by giants. Where the moving pack met the stationary shelf, the shelf was being eaten. He could see the fresh bite-marks, raw white scars where new ice had sheared away overnight.

It was holding today. It might hold tomorrow morning. By tomorrow night the pack would have chewed through the narrows and the whole tongue would calve off and join the procession downstream, carrying whoever stood on it toward the sea and the dark.

Measured in hours. Everything now was measured in hours.

He finished the walk, came back up the quarry road with his lungs burning and the smoke column rising ahead of him like a bad omen given a job, and made his decision the way he made all his decisions: alone, in the cold, without asking anyone's permission or forgiveness.

Then he went to find the Dunmore woman and tell her how her gamble would be run.

* * *

They gathered in the workshop because there was nowhere else, and because the press was still running — he could hear it through the door, the steady iron breathing of it, *thump and pull, thump and pull*, like a heart that refused to consult the news.

Inside it was lamplight and ink-smell and desperation arranged neatly on shelves. The printer — Elias, they called him — stood at the press with bandaged hands, and Osk looked at those hands and understood something immediately and unwelcome. He'd seen frostbite take men on the water. Those fingers were not going to grip a rope, let alone a rope in a panic.

The major stood by the door, hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at. The orderly — Bram, hollow-eyed, back from wherever he'd gone with his stolen page — sat on a crate of type blocks looking like a man who had been forgiven and couldn't bear the weight of it. And Jude Dunmore stood in the middle of the room with ink to her elbows, and when Osk laid his iron bar on the table between them all, she looked at it the way other people looked at relics.

"Here's the truth," Osk said, because he was too old for speeches and too tired for kindness dressed up as comfort. "There is no bridge. There is no ferry. Any man who tells you otherwise is selling something." He put his finger on the bar. "There is this. Shore ice along the north bluff, half a mile, maybe more if the Lord is generous and I'm not counting on Him. It will hold a walker. One walker, light-footed, carrying nothing that swings or drags."

"And after the half-mile?" the major said. His voice was careful. Osk had carried officers before; he knew the breed. This one had the look of a man who had spent the winter learning to be ashamed.

"After the half-mile there's the narrows, and the narrows are eating themselves as we speak. You cross at first light tomorrow, maybe the day after. After that—" He shrugged. "After that the river closes the book."

Silence. The press kept its rhythm behind them, patient as gravity.

"One walker," Jude said. Her voice was level but Osk heard the seam in it. "You'll take the ledgers yourself? By boat?"

"No boat." He didn't flinch from it. "No boat on that water. Not mine, not the devil's. The ledgers go strapped to the walker's chest, oilcloth, weighted to float if the ice gives. If the walker goes in —" He met her eyes and did not look away, because she deserved that much. "— the package floats. The walker may not. We fish for the package with lines from the far point. I've rigged it. It's not pretty, but it's the best arithmetic on offer."

"And me?" the major asked quietly.

"You're heavy, Major." Osk said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Officers eat better than printers. The shelf decides such things without consulting me."

Something crossed Halloway's face — relief, Osk thought, or its twin — and the major nodded once and looked at the floor.

Jude had gone very still. Osk watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done, and watched her arrive at the number she'd been avoiding since the bridge closed: one crossing. One walker. The rest of them stayed on this side with the fire coming.

"Elias," she began.

"Don't." The printer said it mildly, wiping his bandaged hand on his apron, and Osk saw the whole shape of the argument before anyone spoke it, because he'd watched lovers divide a death before and it always went the same way. "I'm not walking anywhere on that ice and we both know it. Look at my hands, Jude." He raised them, the bandages gray at the fingertips. "I can't grip a line. I can't even grip this." He touched the press lever gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "I stay. I finish the copies tonight, every name, and Wren takes the southern lot at dawn, and the rest goes on your chest. That's the whole plan. It's a good plan. Stop trying to ruin it with your face."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to say my name in that voice." He almost smiled. "Save it."

Osk cleared his throat. There was one more thing, and it sat in him like a stone, and he was not a man who enjoyed stones.

"The fire," he said. They all turned. "It's moving west along the quarter. I watched it take the cooperage last night. Your workshop is on the patrol list — I've heard the wagon drivers talk, they drink at the Anchor, they talk. When the burning detail comes, it comes down this street with the barrels, and they don't knock and they don't argue. Whoever stays—" He looked at Elias. At Bram. At the press, breathing its iron breath. "Whoever stays needs to be gone from this building before the smoke gets close, or be ready to explain themselves to men with torches and no questions in them."

"We'll manage," Elias said.

"People say that," Osk said. "Right up until the moment they don't."

* * *

He left them to their packing and walked the shelf again, because a route was a living thing and you checked its pulse.

Midmorning now, the sun a pale coin behind the smoke-haze, and the river louder than it had been at dawn. The pack in the channel had multiplied overnight; whole fields of it rode the current past the broken bridge, and where the floes met the fast ice the collisions had grown from knocks to crashes. He tested with the bar at every pace. Good ice. Good. The marked seep had widened — the oar he'd planted leaned at a drunk angle now, and beyond it the surface had gone dull, sweating, the shine of ice that has begun to think about becoming water.

He crouched at the narrows and listened with his palm flat on the surface, an old trick his father taught him, useless by any science and reliable as scripture. Through his hand he felt the shelf working — small shifts, small settlements, the sound of a structure renegotiating its terms. Holding. For now. The way a rope holds, right up until the strand that mattered parts.

Hours. He gave it until tomorrow's dusk, and that was the generous count.

On the far bank, tiny with distance, he could see the garrison chimney smoke and, nearer, the black scar of the cooperage still smoldering. Somewhere between here and there lay a half mile of ice that a woman with a satchel full of dead men's names was going to trust with her life, because a printer with ruined hands had told her to go, and because the alternative was letting the fire finish what the officers started.

Osk thought of Henrik, whose name existed nowhere but in his own head and his wife's, until his wife died and then it existed nowhere at all. He thought of the ledger sealed in oilcloth, weighted to float. All those names in one bundle, heavier than any cargo he'd ever carried and lighter than anything, because names weigh nothing until you're the one carrying them.

He got up off the ice, stiff-kneed, and drove a second stake at the narrows' edge — a marker, a promise, a warning, depending on the hour you read it.

Then he climbed the bluff to fetch rope and lines and the flask of brandy he'd been saving for a worse day than this one, and found, when he reached the top, that the wind had turned.

Out of the south. Warm. Smelling faintly of mud.

The river heard it before he did. Down below, the pack surged against the fast ice with a long grinding roar, and somewhere out in the middle a sheet the size of a church roof split clean through with a crack that rolled up the valley like artillery, and every crow on the far bluffs went up at once, screaming.

Osk stood in the warming wind and did the arithmetic again, honestly this time, the way he'd promised himself he would.

Tomorrow's dusk was a lie. The river had voted.

They crossed at first light — or the woman crossed alone, and everyone else became a story she'd have to carry too — or they didn't cross at all, and the names went into the fire with everything else, and some morning years from now an old man would stand on a rebuilt bridge and not be able to tell his grandchildren why.

Below him, the workshop door opened. Jude Dunmore came out into the smoke-light with the satchel already strapped across her chest, and stopped when she saw him standing there against the sky, and read his face the way he read ice.

"It's worse," she said. Not a question.

"It's worse," Osk said. "We go at first light. And after that, woman, there is no after that."
