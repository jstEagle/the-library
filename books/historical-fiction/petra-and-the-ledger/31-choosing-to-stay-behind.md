## 31. Choosing to Stay Behind

The hands told him first.

They had been telling him for days, in the language of frostbite — the burning, then the numbness, then the terrible silence where feeling used to live. Elias Voss had set type since he was nine years old. He knew his fingers the way a priest knows his prayers, every knuckle and callus mapped in muscle memory, and now he sat on an upturned crate in Osk's boathouse with his hands in his lap like two dead birds, watching them fail to do what he asked.

"Make a fist," Osk said.

Elias made what he could of one. The fingers curled halfway and stopped, trembling, as if they'd forgotten their purpose halfway through. He tried to close them the rest of the way and could not. It wasn't pain that stopped him. Pain would have been honest. It was nothing at all — a gap between intention and flesh, a wire cut somewhere under the skin.

"Now grip," Osk said, holding out the haft of an ice pick.

Elias took it. That is, he put his hand around it. The moment Osk let go, it slid through fingers that had no more hold on iron than smoke does. It clattered against the planks. Neither of them reached for it.

"That's the truth of it," Osk said quietly. "I won't dress it for you."

"I can walk," Elias said.

"You can walk." Osk crouched, joints cracking, and picked up the pick himself. His face was a map of old weather, seventy years of river carved into it, and he looked at Elias the way he'd look at a hull he was deciding whether to trust. "But the shelf out there isn't walking ground. It's a mile and a half of ice that's deciding every hour whether it's still ice. You'll go down once. Maybe twice. When you do, you'll need hands — hands that grab, hands that pull, hands that get you back up onto your belly before the water takes the rest of you. What you've got are decorations."

"They're my hands," Elias said, and heard how small it sounded.

"They're the price you paid for the paper," Osk said. "River doesn't refund."

* * *

He didn't tell Jude right away.

That was the coward's arithmetic, and he did it anyway: every hour he kept the knowledge in his own mouth was an hour she could prepare without grief slowing her. She had packing to finish. The ledger was sealed in oilcloth now, strapped and weighted, rehearsed twice on the shingle behind the workshop until her shoulders knew its drag. She moved through the last hours like a woman loading a boat she intended to survive, and Elias watched her and understood that whatever he said next would either be ballast or anchor.

So he waited. And in the waiting, he did the thing his hands could still manage, because the mind remembers what the body forgets.

There was a case of type in the corner of the boathouse — salvaged from the press before the dismantling, a single drawer of sorts that Osk had agreed to carry because it weighed almost nothing and because Osk, who had lost two sons to unnamed graves, had developed a superstition about type. Letters were names. Names were the whole religion of this winter. He'd take a drawer of letters across hell before he'd leave them.

Elias found the compartment by feel, which was nearly all he had left, and lifted out the block he wanted.

Thomas Dunmore. Capital T, lowercase om, capital D — no. He corrected himself even in thought. Thomas was set plain, the way Jude had insisted when they first cut it: *In memory of Thomas Dunmore, private, 2nd Battalion,* and beneath it the date of an advance that official paper had never acknowledged. They'd printed forty of them that first night, before the ledger had a name, before either of them understood what they were starting. Forty small cards. She'd burned thirty-nine of them in the stove rather than risk discovery, and kept one pressed flat inside the ledger's back board, and the block itself had lived ever since in the top-right corner of the case where a compositor keeps the sorts his fingers find blind.

His fingers found it blind now too. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it — the hands remembered even as they failed. He could not have gripped a hammer. He could not have held the lever of the press for one final pull; he had smashed the machine three nights ago with a mallet clamped between his forearms while Jude wept beside him, and the burns across his palms from that destruction had been the last honest pain his hands would feel. But he could find a single piece of type among ten thousand, because ten thousand hours had built a map no cold could erase.

He turned the block over in his ruined palm. A small thing. An inch of lead and antimony, the letter worn shiny at the edges where his thumb had worried it across years of setting and resetting. He had pulled it from the frame after the smashing, alone, while the others slept, because some instinct older than sense told him the press should not die entirely anonymous.

* * *

Jude found him at dusk.

She came into the boathouse carrying a lamp and two ration tins, moving with the quick economy of a person whose body has already begun the journey ahead of it. She'd tied her hair back. She had river-mud on her boots from the rehearsal runs. She looked, Elias thought, exactly like the woman who would make the crossing alive, and the sight of her hurt worse than his hands did.

"You haven't eaten," she said, setting the tins down.

"Neither have you."

"I ate." She sat beside him on the crate, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while neither spoke. Outside, the river spoke instead. It had been speaking all day — long groaning reports rolling up and down the valley like distant artillery, the shelf ice shifting in its sleep, testing its own weight. Every crack made her flinch slightly, and every flinch made her jaw set harder. She was not afraid, he realized. She was past fear into something colder and more useful.

"The ferryman says dawn," she said. "First light, before the sun warms anything."

"Osk says the same."

"And Halloway will be on the far bank by then. He sent word through the boy — he'll count my steps. As if counting helps."

"It might," Elias said. "A voice on the far shore tells your feet which side of the world they're aiming for."

She turned to look at him then, and he saw her eyes go to his hands where they lay in his lap, and he saw her see it — the way they rested wrong, the way the right one hadn't moved in minutes. He watched the understanding arrive in her face the way ice arrives on water, from the edges inward.

"No," she said.

"Jude—"

"No." She stood up. "You don't get to decide this by silence. I've been watching you all day hiding those hands in your pockets like a schoolboy. Say it out loud."

He said it out loud. "I can't grip. I went down on the shingle this morning — just practicing, just the bank — and I couldn't pull myself up. Osk tested me. A mile and a half of breaking ice, and I can't hold on to my own life, let alone yours."

"We go slow. We rope together. I can pull you—"

"With what? You'll be carrying the ledger. You'll be carrying yourself. Every ounce on that ice is a vote against both of us, and you know the arithmetic better than anyone alive." He stood too, slowly, because standing took longer now. "Jude. Listen to me. This isn't sacrifice. This is inventory. Chapter and verse. We spent a whole winter measuring what can be carried, remember? Well. I'm what can't."

Her face did something he had never seen it do — broke and refused to break in the same instant, a dam with a hand pressed against the crack.

"There has to be another way. Osk—"

"Osk offered me the ledgers' weight in his boat if I'd stay aboard and let the ice decide me. I thanked him. But someone has to stay on this bank and keep a fire lit and watch for Wren's signal from the south road, or the chaplain walks into a patrol with no warning. Someone stays. That's not dying, Jude, that's a job." He was lying with the second half and they both knew it, and the lie sat between them like the lamp between their shadows. "And when thaw comes, when the ferries run again, I cross like any man. I write to you like any man. You'll be sick of my letters by summer."

"You hate writing letters."

"I'll learn to love them."

She laughed — one broken note, half sob — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and Elias did the only thing his hands were still good for. He reached up with both of them, clumsy as a convalescent, and held her face. He couldn't really feel her skin. He felt the pressure of it, faintly, the way you feel a memory of a place. So he looked instead. He spent his failing hands' last wages on looking.

"When the bridge comes back," he said, "when they build it again out of stone the way they always swore they would — I'll be on the near bank. First one across. I'll bring you something printed. I've been planning it since the night I smashed the press. You'll laugh at how bad the kerning is."

"Elias—"

"Let me give you the thing I came here to give you."

He took the type block from his pocket. Her name for it — Thomas — hung in the lamplight between them, the little inverted letter shining, backwards and holy, the way all true things look before they're impressed upon the world.

"Your brother started this," Elias said. "Not the ledger. Not the vow. Him. One name that wouldn't stay buried, and you dug, and everything we built grew out of that grave. I want him to cross with you. Not the card — the metal. The actual letter. Because letters are what survive, Jude. Paper burns, bodies go down, bridges fall. But a letter is an idea wearing armor. Put it in your pocket. When the ice talks to you out there — and it will talk — you hold this and you remember that every step you take is typesetting. You're composing him. You're composing all of them. And when your foot goes through—" 

"Don't."

"—when your foot goes through, and it may, you hold on. You grip. Because your hands work, Jude, and mine don't, and that's the whole difference between us now, so use them. Grip the ice. Grip the name. Come out the other side and let him be printed forever."

She took the block. She closed her fist around it — a real fist, a living hand — and brought it against her sternum, and stood there shaking, and he stood there holding the shape of her face with fingers that were already leaving him.

"I love you," he said. "I should have said it in fever, properly, sober, in daylight. I love you the way ink loves paper — badly, permanently, all the way through."

"I love you," she said. "Come across. Promise me you'll come across. Swear it on the press."

"On the press," he said. "On its grave."

Outside, the river gave a long tearing groan, louder than any before it, and somewhere down the dark water a sheet let go with a sound like a door slamming in a house the size of the world. Both of them turned toward the sound. Dawn was hours off. The ice was already changing its mind.

Jude looked at the black water and then at him, and Elias saw the decision complete itself in her — grief folded away, resolve unfolded — and she said, in the voice of a woman reading the first line of a page that must be set correctly:

"Then at first light, we start."
