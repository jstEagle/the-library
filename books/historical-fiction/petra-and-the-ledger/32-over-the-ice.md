## 32. Over the Ice

The river did not look like a river anymore. It looked like a road that had been abandoned by God — a mile of grey-white plate stretching from bank to bank, heaved into ridges where the floes had rammed each other in December, seamed with dark lines like the cracks in old porcelain. Somewhere under all of it was water, black and fast and cold enough to stop a heart before it could finish drowning you.

Jude stood at the edge of the shore-fast shelf with the ledger strapped against her chest.

Osk had done the strapping himself, his rope-scarred hands working the oilcloth bundle flat against her sternum, crossing the straps over her shoulders, cinching them down until she grunted. "Too tight," she'd said.

"Good," he said. "Loose things catch. Tight things hold." He'd pressed his palm flat against the bundle one last time, the way a man touches a doorframe he may not see again, and then stepped back without another word.

Elias stood three feet away and could not come closer, because if he came closer he would try to follow, and they had already settled this, already argued it through in the grey hour before dawn — his hands wrapped in rags, the frostbite having taken the feeling from eight of his ten fingers, his grip nothing now but a suggestion of grip. He couldn't hold a rope. He couldn't hold her hand, not really, not with weight behind it. If he went out onto moving ice and fell in, someone would have to go after him, and then there would be two bodies instead of one ledger delivered.

So he stood three feet away, which was its own kind of torture for them both, and held out the type block.

She had already put it in her pocket. She checked anyway — her fingers finding the small hard rectangle through the wool, the letter *T* reversed and raised on its face, the letter that would print her brother's name right-way-round when it kissed the paper. It was the only piece of the press that had been allowed to leave. Osk had refused everything else. The type, the chase, the iron frame — too heavy, too much, and besides, Elias had smashed what remained rather than let the army have it. But Thomas's block Elias had kept back from the wreckage with his ruined hands, and now it rode in Jude's coat pocket over her heart, next to the ledger.

"Count your steps," Elias said. His voice came out cracked, the cold and the smoke and the sleeplessness all in it. "Don't count the distance. Count the steps. A man can do anything for a hundred steps."

"How many will it be?"

"I don't know." He almost smiled. "More than a hundred, probably. So start counting again when you get there."

Behind them, upriver, the eastern quarter was still burning. The smoke column leaned west in the morning wind, a great grey arm reaching across the sky, and every few minutes something inside the fire collapsed with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house. The patrol wouldn't reach the waterfront workshops until afternoon — Halloway had seen to that, had ridden out at first light to draw them east with a forged dispatch and a borrowed horse and whatever was left of his career — but afternoon was not far. The light was already climbing off the hills across the river. They had hours. Maybe fewer.

Halloway's voice carried from the far bank. He had crossed an hour earlier by a different route — the ferryman's knowledge, a shelf of shore-fast ice hugging the northern curve where the current ran slower — and now he stood on the opposite shore with his hands cupped around his mouth, small as a toy soldier at this distance, shouting instructions Osk had drilled into all of them and that Jude was about to discover meant nothing at all once her boots were actually on the moving world.

"The shelf holds to the second bend!" Halloway's voice flattened over the ice, arrived thin and strange, as if the river itself were speaking. "Stay on my line! Do not stop! If you hear cracking behind you, don't turn — keep walking!"

"Don't turn," Elias repeated softly, for her alone. "Whatever you hear."

"What will I hear?"

He didn't answer that. That was the answer.

* * *

Osk went first. Not out of gallantry — out of arithmetic. He weighed more than she did, and if the shelf would hold him it would hold her, and if it broke under him she would know exactly where the river's opinion lay and could choose differently. He walked with a boatman's roll, weight low, feet wide, testing each stride with a little sideways press before committing, the pole he carried — cut that morning from a birch, stripped bare — held crosswise like a tightrope walker's bar.

Jude watched him go. Twenty paces. Forty. The ice took him silently, without complaint, and she hated it for that. She wanted it to groan under him. She wanted warning.

Then it was her turn.

The first step onto the river was the easiest step of her life, because the shore ice was thick and blind and certain, frozen to the ground itself, and her boot landed on it with a sound like knocking on a door. The second step was worse, because the sound changed. Deeper. Hollow. The note of ice that had water underneath it and knew it.

By the tenth step she could feel the river breathing.

It was not a metaphor. The whole surface moved — a slow swell passing beneath her boots like the flank of some enormous animal inhaling, lifting her half an inch and setting her down again, and the cracks around her feet opened and closed by the width of a hair with each breath. Black water showed in some of them. Not the grey slush of the shallows but true black, the color of depth, the color of a place with no bottom worth mentioning.

She counted. Eleven. Twelve. Her breath came out in white rags that the wind tore sideways. The ledger pressed against her sternum with every heartbeat, oilcloth slick and strange against her skin where her collar gapped, and she found she was grateful for the pressure of it — proof of weight, proof of cargo, a reason to be doing this thing that no sane person did.

Twenty steps. The shore was still close enough behind her that she could see Elias's face, pale, motionless, both arms wrapped around himself as though holding his own body together. Thirty. Forty. Osk was a hundred paces ahead, patient as winter, waiting at each ridge of heaved ice to watch her climb it.

The ridges were the worst part. Where the floes had collided in December, the plates had buckled upward into walls two and three feet high, and to cross them she had to lean her weight forward onto a slope of ice angled over darkness, and pull herself up with hands that were already going numb inside their mittens, and swing one leg over the crest like a child climbing a fence. On the third ridge her boot skidded on the descent and she went down on one knee, hard, and heard — felt — a crack run out from under her like a rifle shot.

Every instinct screamed at her to freeze. To become small. To wait.

*Don't stop.* Halloway's voice, thin across the mile. *Do not stop.*

She got up. The crack did not widen. Or it widened somewhere she couldn't see, which amounted to the same thing, which amounted to nothing she could control, and she understood with sudden clarity that this was the whole lesson of the river: you could not stand still and be safe. Standing still was just dying slowly in a different posture. She walked.

Fifty. Sixty. The middle of the river.

Here the character of the ice changed entirely. Near the shores it had been one plate; here it was a mosaic, floe laid against floe, seams running in every direction, some of them old and frozen solid, some of them new since dawn — thin black veins that flexed as she crossed them, that wept a bead of water when she pressed them. The wind came down the river's channel unobstructed and hit her full in the face, cold enough to ache in her teeth, and she lowered her head and counted and did not look at the black seams.

Seventy. Seventy-five.

Something spoke underneath her.

Not a crack. A groan — long, rising, tectonic, the sound of a mass the size of a cathedral being asked to move, and it came from everywhere at once, from ahead and behind and below, and the entire surface shivered like a struck bell. Jude stopped. She could not help stopping. Every muscle in her legs locked at once, and she stood alone in the middle of a frozen mile while the river made a sound like artillery, like the war itself had gotten under the ice and was trying to come up.

*Don't turn.*

She didn't turn. She looked straight ahead, at Osk's distant back, at the far bank where Halloway stood with his hands raised — signaling what? Keep coming? Come back? At this distance she couldn't read him, and it didn't matter, because there was no coming back. Behind her the shore ice was already changing; she could feel it without looking, the way you feel a room's mood without seeing faces. The thaw had started in the night. Everyone had heard it. First break-up, Osk called it, and had measured the gamble in hours, and the hours were spending themselves whether she walked or not.

Eighty. Eighty-one.

The ice began to talk constantly after that. Little reports under her boots — *tik, tik-tik, tik* — like a telegraph key tapping out a message in a language she almost understood. Osk had told her what the sounds meant. Sharp cracks that shot outward fast meant load traveling, weight being distributed, generally survivable. Long moans meant movement, plates shifting against each other, also survivable. The sound to fear was silence followed by a wet pop, the sound of a seam giving up all at once.

At ninety-three steps she heard the wet pop.

It came from her left, thirty feet away, a sound absurdly small for what it did — a seam opening like a zipper, black water welling up into it, a line of open river racing away downstream faster than a man could run. The floe she stood on lurched. Not much. An inch. But an inch was the difference between standing on a floor and standing on a door, and Jude's stomach dropped through the soles of her boots, and for one white instant she saw herself in the water — saw the cold closing over her head, saw the ledger drinking the river through its oilcloth seams, saw every name she had copied by lamplight for eleven months dissolving into the current that had drowned the supply depot and the bridge and half the town's hope —

— and then she moved. Not because she decided to. Because Osk was shouting, his voice carrying clear and furious across the ice: *"Walk! Girl — walk NOW!"*

Ninety-four. Ninety-five. The lurching floe ground against its neighbor with a sound like millstones. Ninety-six, ninety-seven, her breath sobbing now, not from cold but from the animal effort of putting one foot in front of the other while the floor of the world rearranged itself. The seam to her left yawned wider. Wind came up out of it, river-smelling, alive.

One hundred.

She had promised herself nothing at one hundred, but her body had apparently made a bargain without consulting her, because at one hundred steps her legs remembered Elias's voice — *start counting again* — and started over. One. Two. Three. As if the count were a rope thrown across the gap between the person who had begun this crossing and the person who would finish it.

And then, faint and steady, threading under the wind and the groaning ice and her own ragged breathing:

Halloway was counting her steps.

His voice came across the water in pieces, torn by wind, but the rhythm held — he had matched her somehow, had watched her stride and found its measure, and now he counted aloud from the far bank like a metronome, like a prayer: *fourteen, fifteen, sixteen.* She realized distantly that he was doing it for the same reason Elias had told her to count. Because a number was smaller than a mile. Because terror could not fill a mind that was occupied.

*Twenty-one. Twenty-two.*

The shore-fast shelf rose ahead of her, the good ice, the ice frozen to the land itself, twenty yards of safety between her and the far bank. And between her and the shelf lay a stretch of young ice — the last floe-field, thinner, darker, formed late and never trusted, the river's final tollgate.

Osk had already crossed it. He stood at the shelf's edge now, holding out the birch pole.

*Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine.*

The young ice flexed under her like a trampoline. With every step it dimpled visibly, a shallow bowl of depression spreading out from her boot, and through the dark surface she could see the current running beneath — actual visible motion, the water sliding southward with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and knew she did not.

*Thirty-five. Thirty-six.*

A crack shot out ahead of her, dead straight, arrowing toward the shelf. She stepped over it without breaking stride — some part of her brain noting, calmly, that she had just stepped over her own death and it had missed — and then another, and then the surface under her left boot gave a soft wet sigh and dropped half an inch and held.

*Forty. Forty-one.*

Ten yards. Five. Osk's pole swung toward her, birch-bright against the grey. She lunged the last stretch — no technique now, no boatman's roll, just a woman running on the skin of a river — and her hand closed around the wood, rough bark biting through her mitten, and Osk hauled, and her boots came up over the shelf's lip onto ice that did not move, did not breathe, ice frozen to the earth, and she went down on her knees on solid ground and the sound she made was not a word in any language.

*Fifty-two,* said Halloway's voice, close now, real, and she looked up and he was running toward her along the shelf, hatless, his face wrecked with something he wasn't bothering to hide. *You made fifty-two.*

Behind her, out on the river, the young ice she had crossed began to go.

It did not shatter dramatically. It simply sighed — a long exhale of collapsing floes, plate after plate settling into the current with soft wet concussions, the tollgate closing behind her section by section, black water widening where she had walked. The whole middle of the river seemed to loosen at once, the great groan rising again, deeper than before, and downriver — where the bridge had stood, where the span had already fallen — something enormous shifted with a boom that she felt in her chest rather than heard.

Jude knelt on the shore-fast shelf with the ledger strapped to her chest and her brother's name in her pocket, and looked back across a mile of dying ice at the burning town.

The workshop would burn by afternoon. Elias and Osk stood on the far bank, two small figures against the smoke, and even at this distance she could see them waving — alive, upright, stranded. The river between them was opening. By nightfall there would be no crossing at all, no ferry, no ice road, nothing but water until the boats ran again, and no one knew how many weeks that would be.

Halloway reached her and pulled her up by both arms, checking her, counting her — eyes, hands, the ledger's straps — the way he'd once counted men off a casualty list, and his voice when he finally spoke was rough as gravel.

"You're across," he said. "That's one."

She turned back to the river. The ice was still talking — groaning, reporting, weeping black seams — and somewhere in that noise, faint under the wind, she thought she heard the far bank calling, two voices together now, and she could not yet tell what they were saying.

But the shelf under her knees shuddered, and the river's voice rose, and spring was coming up the valley like an army.
