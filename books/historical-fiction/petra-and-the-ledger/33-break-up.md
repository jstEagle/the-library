## 33. Break-Up

The ice spoke first.

It had been speaking all night — long groans rolling under her like the river clearing its throat, cracks that traveled in straight lines and then in none at all, a sound she had come, in the last hour, to think of as footsteps. Something enormous walking beneath her. She did not look down anymore. Looking down was a way of falling.

She kept her eyes on Halloway's voice instead. He stood on the far bank with his hands cupped around his mouth, and every few strides he called a number to her, steady as a metronome: *Forty. Forty-five.* She had stopped understanding what the numbers counted. Steps, yards, heartbeats — it didn't matter. What mattered was that he kept counting, because as long as he was counting, she wasn't dead yet.

The ledger rode against her chest in its oilcloth shroud, strapped tight, weighted so it would float if she went through — *when* she went through, some cold practical voice in her kept saying — and she could feel its edges through her coat with every breath, a small rectangular heartbeat of her own. Thomas's name was in there. Four hundred and eleven names were in there, plus Halloway's ninety-six, plus Bram's page from the chaplain, plus the ones she'd taken by candlelight from men who could no longer hold a pen but could still move their lips. The whole weight of them was maybe six pounds. It felt like carrying a cathedral.

*Fifty-five.*

Behind her — she allowed herself one glance, only one — Osk stood on the near bank beside Elias, both of them dark against the gray shore ice, and beyond them the eastern quarter of Vellmere had begun to smoke. Thin columns of it, four or five, leaning east on the wind like men hurrying somewhere. The burning detail had come down the far road before dawn, exactly as Halloway said they would. They were methodical. They started at the granary.

Elias's hands hung at his sides. Even at this distance she could see they were ruined — the frostbite had taken the fingertips on both hands, and the doctor in the workshop had wrapped them in linen until he looked like a man wearing two white flowers. He couldn't grip. He couldn't pull a lever. He couldn't have crossed ten feet of this ice, let alone eighty yards, and he knew it, which is why he had put the type block in her pocket an hour ago and closed her fingers over it without a word, pressing down once, twice, hard, the way you seal a letter.

*Sixty.*

The shelf under her feet changed pitch. She felt it before she heard it — a softness, a give, like walking on bread — and she did the thing Osk had drilled into her at the water's edge: spread out. Arms wide. Weight low. Keep moving. A walker's weight, Osk had said, measured in hours, and the hours were spent. She was on the last stretch now, where the river deepened toward the channel and the ice grew thin as window glass, black and webbed with white fracture lines that moved when she breathed too hard.

A crack opened ahead of her. Not a sound this time — a fact. Six feet of open water, steaming faintly, black as ink.

She stopped. Her body wanted to stop; every nerve in her screamed for stillness. But Osk's voice was in her head too, gruff as gravel: *Stillness is how they find your body in June.* She looked left, right, found the shelf continuing past the gap in a ragged tongue, and stepped sideways along it, arms out, a tightrope walker with no rope, the ledger pressing against her sternum like a second heart.

*Seventy,* Halloway called, and then, softer, almost lost in the wind, *you're nearly there, Jude.*

* * *

Later she would try to reconstruct those last twenty yards and fail. Memory gave her pieces: the smell of the water, mineral and ancient, rising through cracks too small to see. The moment her boot went through to the ankle and the cold clamped her leg like a manacle and she hauled it out and kept going because stopping meant freezing where she stood. The sound behind her — not a crack now but a *shift*, the whole shelf groaning as it began to move, the river taking back its floor inch by inch. And above all of it, threaded through everything, Halloway's voice, counting, always counting, never wavering, as if arithmetic itself could hold the ice together.

*Eighty.*

Her hand found rock.

Not ice — rock, the old stone footing of the bridge that wasn't there anymore, the pier base the masons had cut a hundred years ago, slick with spray and furred with frost, but solid, blessedly solid, and she grabbed it with both arms and pulled, and the ice chose that exact moment to let go.

She heard it go. The whole shelf, the entire road she had walked, calving away from the shore in one long sighing collapse, slabs tilting and sliding and turning under, the river closing over them with a sound like a door. She lay on the stone with her cheek against it and watched the place where she had been standing become open water, and she understood that the river had made the decision for everyone now. There was no going back. There was nothing to go back *to*.

"Jude." Halloway's hands were under her arms, hauling her up the bank, and she realized distantly that he must have waded in, that his coat was soaked to the chest, that he was saying her name over and over in a voice stripped of everything military. "Jude. Look at me. Are you hurt? Jude."

"The ledger," she said.

He laughed — one broken note of it — and touched the oilcloth bundle still strapped to her chest. "It's here. You're here. It's here."

Then the sky went orange.

* * *

She got to her feet because she had to see.

Across the river, the burning detail had reached the workshop quarter. Even at that distance she could pick out the buildings by their silhouettes — the granary already a skeleton of fire, the cooperage, the row of officers' stores — and then the workshop, Elias's workshop, the long low building with its north-facing windows where the light came in even and true, the building that had held the press, the type cases, the drying racks, the cot where the convalescents had slept, the table where Bram had wept and been forgiven and left and come back half-dead with a page of names carried against his heart.

Fire took it quickly. It had been primed for weeks — straw, lamp oil, the requisition order said *thorough destruction of unofficial records* — and the roof caught like paper. Heat rolled across the river in a wave she felt on her face, a hundred yards of winter air doing nothing to blunt it.

"Elias," she said. Not a question. His name, just his name, torn out of her.

"He's there." Halloway pointed. "Look. Look at the bank — do you see them?"

And she saw.

They had moved upriver along the near shore, away from the fires, two figures standing on the last shelf of shore-fast ice that hadn't gone yet — Osk broad and unmoving as a post, and beside him Elias, smaller, one arm raised. Waving. Not frantic. Slow, deliberate sweeps of the arm, the wave of a man who wants to be certain he is seen.

Osk raised his other arm too. Then both arms, and Jude realized with a jolt that went through her colder than the river had: they were alive. They had known. Osk had known his shelf, known his hours, known exactly when to walk himself and Elias back to the shore ice that would hold — the same instinct that had kept him poling the river for thirty years, that had lost him sons to unnamed graves and taught him, in exchange, to read ice the way priests read scripture. He had timed it. He had gotten them off.

"They're alive," she said. She said it twice more, testing each repetition like a plank. "They're alive. They're alive."

Halloway said nothing. He stood beside her, dripping, his jaw working, and after a while he lifted his own arm and waved back across the water — a major's salute transfigured into something plainer, a man waving to men — and across the distance Elias's answering wave came slower, and she knew, somehow knew, that he was laughing. That was the worst of it and the best of it. He was laughing over there on the burning side of the world, with his ruined hands and his drowned press and his life's work going up in orange light behind him, and he was waving at her like a man who had gotten exactly what he wanted.

Because he had. They both had. The press was ash. The original pages were ash. But the copies were here — against her chest, floating-weighted, oilcloth-sealed, four hundred and eleven names plus ninety-six plus Bram's page, carried over the ice by her own legs while the river tried to swallow them. He had smashed the press rather than leave it for the army, had burned his fingers doing it, and now he stood watching the army burn the rest, and his arm went up and down, up and down, patient as a metronome.

*Counting,* she thought. *He's counting my steps even now. From over there.*

* * *

The fires burned most of the morning. There was nothing to do but watch, so they watched — Jude and Halloway on the south bank, Osk and Elias on the north, four people arranged along eighty yards of open black water like punctuation marks on a sentence neither river nor war could erase. At some point Halloway draped his dry coat over her shoulders without asking. At some point she stopped shaking. The ledger stayed warm against her chest, warmer than she was, as though the names themselves were giving off heat.

Osk built a signal fire on the north bank — small, controlled, the fire of a man who respected flame too much to waste it — and laid wet branches on it, and the smoke rose in three deliberate columns. A ferryman's code, Halloway murmured. *All well. All here. Wait for the water.*

Wait for the water. That was the shape of everything now. The bridge was gone days early, taken by the ice before the engineers ever thought it would go. The ferry wouldn't run until break-up finished, a week, maybe two. The workshop was smoke. The chaplain was somewhere south with half the copies and a noose waiting if he was caught. And Jude Dunmore stood on a riverbank in a borrowed coat with six pounds of names strapped to her chest and nowhere in the world she needed to be except wherever those names could be read aloud.

"You did it," Halloway said quietly. He was looking at the water, not at her. "I want you to know that I understand what happened out there this morning. I stood on this bank and I watched a woman walk across a dying river carrying more than any convoy I ever commanded, and I will be watching myself remember it for the rest of my life."

"Halloway—"

"I struck forty names myself." He said it flatly, the way a man confesses to a debt. "Before I ever knew yours existed. Ninety-six are in that bundle because I wrote them down trying to sleep at night. Whatever inquiry comes — and it will come, when the rolls reach the Ministry and the numbers don't add up — I intend to stand in front of it and hand them that ledger and let it end my career where it should have ended years ago." He finally turned to her. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "You don't owe me anything for this morning. I want that said plainly. What I'm doing next, I'm doing for myself."

She studied him a long moment — the duty-bound major who had offered her safety and silence and then, stranded on the wrong side of a frozen river, had found something better to offer. She reached out and gripped his forearm, officer-fashion, and held it.

"When it's read," she said, "in the church, with the families there — you'll stand with me. Those ninety-six men get their names from your mouth as well as mine. That's not owing. That's sharing the work."

Something moved through his face like weather. He nodded once, unable to speak.

Downstream, the last of the workshop's chimney folded into the flames, and the smoke column bent east and thinned, and the wind coming off the river carried the smell of char and wet ash and, underneath it, faintly absurd, the clean mineral smell of spring. Break-up. The season turning whether anyone consented or not.

On the north bank, Osk raised one arm — not waving this time, pointing. Downriver. Where, if you knew what to look for, a mile below the ruined bridge, a flat-bottomed boat sat hauled up among the willows, tarped against the frost, waiting for the water.

Waiting for the water. Everything now waited for the water.

Jude put her hand inside her coat, over the oilcloth, over the type block in her pocket stamped with her brother's name, and watched the river run black and triumphant between her and the man she loved, and she made herself say it aloud, to the air, to the names, to the year:

"A week. Hold together a week."

The river answered with a long, grinding roar downstream, where the main floes had begun to move.
