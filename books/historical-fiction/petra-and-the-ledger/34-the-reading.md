## 34. The Reading

The church at Garriston Barracks had been built for eight hundred men and a regimental band. On the morning of the reading it held perhaps two hundred civilians, a handful of officers who had come out of duty or dread, and Chaplain Wren, who had spent the previous night in the vestry with the copied ledger open on his knees, reading names silently to himself until the candles burned down to pools.

He had carried the pages south in the lining of his coat, sewn there by his own hand with needle and thread borrowed from a laundress who asked no questions. Four hundred miles of railway checkpoints, three searches, one long night in a freight car between Vellmere Junction and the coast, during which he had pressed his palm flat against his chest and felt the paper's edge like a second heartbeat. He had told himself, then, that the hard part was the carrying.

He had been wrong. The hard part was this: deciding whose voice would say the names aloud.

* * *

The idea had come to him on the train, somewhere past the flooded orchards of Marrow Vale. A ledger read in silence was an archive. A ledger read aloud was a reckoning. He had written to Jude Dunmore — the woman who had kept the original, the woman whose handwriting he had traced and re-inked through sixty sleepless nights — asking whether she wished the families summoned by letter or left to hear of it by rumor. Her reply had taken six weeks and contained eleven words:

*Summon them all. Let no one learn it standing in a queue.*

So he had written four hundred and twelve letters. He had written them at the garrison desk after evening prayer, dipping the pen until his middle finger wore a callus like a small horn. *Dear Madam, your son, Private A—, reported lost in the action of October last, is recorded in an unofficial register as having died on the date following. This register will be read publicly at the garrison church on Sunday the ninth. You are invited.* He had not written "killed." He had not written "died." He had found, over the months, that the English language offered a dozen doors around a fact and that every family chose its own door, and so he had built them a corridor instead and let each walk down it at their own pace.

Some letters came back marked *addressee unknown*, returned to him like birds thrown against glass. Forty-one of them. He kept those in a separate folder and resolved, privately, to keep trying. A name that could not be delivered was still a name. It only needed more road.

* * *

On the morning itself, frost furred the churchyard yews and the bell rope was stiff as a fence post. Wren rang the hour himself because the sexton had refused — "I'll not toll for deserters' friends," the man said, and spat into the gravel, and Wren had looked at him for a long moment and answered, quietly, "Then stand outside, Mr. Hobbes, and count how many mothers walk past you."

The mothers walked past him. That was the thing Wren would remember afterward, when people asked what the day had been like: not the reading, but the walking. They came up the path in ones and twos, in black coats and good boots saved for Sundays, and they did not speak to one another, and they filled the pews from the back forward the way congregations do when they are afraid, leaving a moat of empty wood between themselves and the altar. In the third pew sat a woman with a baby who did not cry once the whole service. Wren learned later she was the widow of Sergeant Iliffe — a man struck from the rolls twice, once as dead and once, absurdly, as deserted, because a clerk had mislaid a page. His name was forty-seventh on the list. His widow held her daughter through forty-six other names before her own arrived, and Wren, watching her from the pulpit, saw her lips moving ahead of him, rehearsing, as though she meant to catch the name before it touched the floor.

He had chosen readers carefully. Not himself alone — no single throat could carry four hundred and twelve names without the words going thin, going liturgical, going dead. He had asked a schoolmistress from the town, Mrs. Anselm, whose brother was ninety-third on the list. He had asked a young lieutenant convalescing at the barracks, Lieutenant Pryce, who had hesitated three days and then appeared at the vestry door and said, "I signed some of those reports, sir. The false ones. I'd like to say them back." And he had asked Bram.

Bram had come south with him — half-dead still, coughing the river out of his lungs in wet rattles, insisting with the stubbornness of the recently forgiven. "I informed on a printer once," he'd told Wren on the train. "One name, one town, and a man went to the wall for it. I can't print. I can't fight. But I can stand up in front of God and a room full of grieving women and say names until my voice goes. Let me do that much." Wren had given him the last hundred names — the ones recovered latest, the unidentified dead whom Jude Dunmore had interviewed by candlelight, writing their fragments onto the page: *tattoo of an anchor, left forearm; called himself Danny; sang 'The Lass of Amiens' the night before.* Bram read those slowly, every fragment included, because a fragment might be the only mirror some family possessed.

* * *

The service began without music. Wren had considered a hymn and rejected it; hymns were for praising, and today nothing was being praised. He opened the great book — the fair copy Elias's rebuilt press had produced, bound in gray boards because gray was the color of the river ice in March — laid it on the lectern, and said:

"These names were removed from the official record of the war. They were removed by clerks, by officers, by men who believed the war would be kinder if it were tidier. It was not tidier. It was only quieter. Today we make it loud."

Then he read the first name. "Private Aldous Berrow, Fourth Rifles. Died the twelfth of September. Recorded in error as missing."

The silence afterward lasted four seconds. Wren counted them. Then, from somewhere in the back, a woman's voice — steady, unhurried, ordinary — said, "Thank you." Just that. And the next name came, and the next, and the room learned its rhythm the way a congregation learns a liturgy: name, date, correction, breath. Name, date, correction, breath. Mrs. Anselm took over at fifty. Pryce at one hundred and ten, his voice cracking only once, at number one hundred and thirty-eight, a boy he remembered signing away himself, and he stopped, and drank water, and started again, and nobody minded, because the stopping was part of the honesty.

Wren watched the pews the way a helmsman watches weather. He saw a man in a railwayman's cap stand when his son's name was called — stood straight through the next three names, as if attention could be paid like a debt — and sat again. He saw two sisters, seated apart, discover each other across the aisle at the sound of a shared surname, and move together without a word, folding into one silhouette. He saw the officer from the Ministry, the one who had attended uninvited in a gray suit, take out a notebook at the start and put it away, unused, twenty minutes in, and sit with his hands folded like a schoolboy caught at something.

At the interval — Wren had planned a rest at two hundred, knowing voices and hearts both tire — the baby finally cried, briefly, one outraged note, and a ripple of laughter went through the church, the first sound in two hours that was not grief-shaped, and the mother laughed too, wetly, and Wren understood that the room had turned a corner. Catharsis is not weeping, he thought. Catharsis is when the weeping makes room for something else to live in.

* * *

Bram read the final hundred. His voice was wrecked by the river and the winter and he read anyway, pausing at the fragments, giving each dying man's last described detail its full weight: the anchor tattoo, the song, the joke repeated by a stretcher-bearer who couldn't remember the man's real name but remembered the joke. When he reached the very last entry — *unidentified, buried near the depot sluice; carried a photograph of a woman outside a bakery; called her Petra* — a sound went through the church like wind through wheat, because everyone in that country knew the bridge, and everyone knew the stone it was named for, and everyone understood that even the unnamed had named things.

Wren closed the book.

He had prepared a closing address, three pages of it, drafted and redrafted in the vestry. He left it on the lectern, unread. Instead he said: "There will be copies. Printed properly, bound, one for every family that wants one and every parish that will shelve one. No one will need to depend on rumor again. That work begins tomorrow. Today—" and here his own voice failed him for the first time, and he let it, "—today you may go home having heard it said out loud, in front of witnesses, under God. Your sons existed. The record now agrees."

They filed out slowly, past the lectern, past the open book. Many touched it. Some wept over it. One old father — Private Berrow's father, the first name of the day — stood at the lectern for so long that Wren thought he would have to guide him out, but the man only laid his hand flat on the gray boards and said, "Twelfth of September. We were told the eighth. We lit his candle four days early for a year." And walked out into the frost with his back straight.

The gray-suited official waited until the church was nearly empty. Then he approached the vestry where Wren was gathering candle stubs and said, without preamble: "This was irregular. The register was never authorized. There will be questions."

"Yes," Wren agreed, blowing out a candle. "There already have been. For years. That was rather the point."

"And if the Ministry orders the copies destroyed?"

"Then the Ministry will be destroying four hundred and twelve letters home," Wren said, "each one already opened and read by the family it concerns, and I should very much like to watch them try to explain that at a parish tea." He set the candle stub in its box and looked up. "Major Halloway has already submitted himself to inquiry over the falsified rolls. He gave us his evidence freely. You may collect it from him — I believe he is expecting you."

The official left without another word. Outside, the frost was melting off the yews in bright drops, and Wren stood a moment in the churchyard watching the families disperse down the road toward the station — the two sisters arm in arm, the railwayman walking alone but upright, the widow with the silent baby — and he thought about the woman in Vellmere who had begun all this with a notebook and a stolen pen in a flooded field hospital, recording names while the water rose around the cots. She had said once, in a letter, that she did not feel brave, only *unable to stop*. Wren had spent the whole journey south believing he was delivering the ledger to the world.

Standing in the emptying churchyard, he understood it had been the other way around. The ledger had delivered him.

He went back inside to lock up, and found on the lectern, tucked into the great book where someone had left it, a small envelope bearing no name — only the words *For the keeper of the names*. Inside was a pressed flower, flattened and brown at the edges, and a slip of paper in a handwriting Wren recognized from four hundred letters of correspondence: cramped, slanting, resolute.

It read: *Tell them Thomas says thank you. And tell Elias the press sings better than he remembers. — J.D.*

Wren smiled, closed the book gently, and began, that same afternoon, to draft the letter that would carry the news of the reading north — toward the river, toward the free town where a smaller press was rising, toward whatever remained to be done. Because four hundred and twelve names had been spoken. But the ledger's last pages, he knew, were still being written.
