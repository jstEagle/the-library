## 35. Reckoning

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Halloway found fitting. Tuesdays were for paperwork. Wars were fought on other days — Fridays, mostly, when the orders had a week to ferment — but Tuesdays were for the quiet machinery underneath: the requisitions, the rosters, the reports that decided who existed and who did not.

The letter was brief. *Major E. Halloway will present himself before the Board of Inquiry at Garrison South, concerning irregularities in casualty reporting, Third and Fourth Quarters, 1917.* No accusation. No charge named. Just that word — *irregularities* — doing its small, careful work, like a probe slid under a bandage to see what was still living beneath it.

He read it twice, then set it on the table of his quarters and looked at it for a long time while the garrison bell counted out the hour.

He had expected this since the ice broke. Since Jude Dunmore crossed the river with the ledger strapped to her chest like a child, since the workshop burned and Elias Vane's press went into history as so much scrap iron, since Chaplain Wren's copies began arriving in southern offices like letters from the dead. The ledger existed now outside the river town. It existed in church reading rooms and provost files and the hands of families who had spent a year believing their sons were alive because an officer's pen had said so.

His pen. Some of them, anyway. His pen more than most.

He had struck names himself. That was the sentence he kept returning to, the one he could not revise no matter how many drafts he wrote in his head. In the autumn of 1917, holding a list of the dead from the Vellmere advance, he had been told — reasonably, even kindly, by a colonel who believed he was sparing everyone pain — that certain entries would be held back. Men missing rather than dead. Men whose deaths would complicate supply figures, or whose units were being reorganized, or whose families lived in districts where morale was already thin. The word used was *deferred*. As if death were a payment that could be postponed.

He had signed the amended roll. He had done it standing up, in a warm room, in less time than it took to drink a cup of tea.

That was the part that shamed him most, when he let himself examine it honestly — not the act but the ease of it. Not that he had been coerced, but that he hadn't needed to be. The machinery had asked nothing of him he wasn't already willing to give. He had been a good officer. Good officers kept the rolls clean.

Jude Dunmore had taught him what a clean roll actually cost.

---

He dressed carefully that morning. Not in his best uniform — there was no vanity left in him worth feeding — but in a correct one. Buttons seen to. Boots polished. If he was going to end his career, he would do it looking like the officer he had tried to be, not the man he feared he'd become.

In the bottom of his kit bag, wrapped in oilcloth, was his own ledger. Smaller than hers. Kept in a locked drawer for two years, written in at night, in a hand he barely recognized as his own — smaller, faster, guiltier. Forty-one names. He had counted them a hundred times. Forty-one men whose deaths he had signed away into silence, each entry dated, each with whatever detail he could recover: unit, wound if known, last reported position, next of kin where the records allowed.

Some entries were nearly blank. *Name unknown. Private, Second Battalion. Died of wounds, night of the 14th, field hospital near Vellmere. Body unclaimed.* Those blanks were the worst. They were the ones that had driven him, eventually, across the frozen river to a workshop that smelled of ink and lamp oil, to stand in front of a woman who looked at him like she already knew everything and was deciding whether to hate him for it.

She had decided, in the end, not to hate him. He still didn't understand why. He suspected he never would. Mercy rarely explained itself.

He had given her his ledger in February, before the thaw patrols came, before the burning. She had merged it into hers without ceremony — just her pen moving down the page, adding his forty-one to her hundreds, as if the two lists had always belonged together. Maybe they had. Two keepers of the same shame, keeping it differently.

Now the Board wanted to know about *irregularities*, and he intended to tell them everything.

---

Garrison South had been a seminary once. Halloway remembered it from before the war — he had passed through on maneuvers years ago, a young lieutenant full of theories about logistics, and the chapel had struck him then as too large for the number of men who prayed in it. Now the chapel was a hearing room. The pews had been pushed aside. A long table stood where the altar should have been, and behind it sat three officers and a civilian solicitor, and above them the empty niche where a statue had presumably stood before someone decided statues were expendable.

There was a symmetry in that, Halloway thought, taking his seat. Everyone deciding what was expendable.

"Major Halloway." The president of the board was Brigadier Corliss, a grey man with a grey voice, the kind of officer who had survived four years by ensuring nothing memorable ever happened on his watch. "You understand the purpose of this proceeding?"

"I understand you're investigating discrepancies between official casualty returns and a document that has come to be called the Vellmere Ledger."

Corliss's mouth tightened slightly. So the name had traveled. Of course it had. You could burn a workshop; you couldn't burn a phrase once families started using it in letters.

"You've read the ledger?" Corliss asked.

"I've read my portion of it."

"Your portion."

Halloway reached into his kit bag and set the oilcloth bundle on the table. He unwrapped it slowly, deliberately, letting the board see the worn cover, the water stain along one edge from a winter it had spent too close to a stove, the corners soft from handling.

"My own record," he said. "Forty-one names. Men whose deaths I omitted from official returns between August and December of 1917, pursuant to instructions I received and did not question as thoroughly as I should have. Dates, units, circumstances, next of kin where ascertainable. I compiled it beginning in January of 1918. I gave a copy of the contents to Miss Judith Dunmore in February of this year, and she incorporated them into the master ledger. This is my original."

The room went very quiet. The civilian solicitor — a thin man named Pryce with the patient face of someone who had heard every kind of confession — leaned forward and turned the ledger toward himself without touching it yet.

"You're offering this voluntarily?"

"I carried it here to offer it voluntarily, yes."

"You understand," Corliss said, and his voice took on the flat caution of a man walking toward something he suspected was wired, "that this document constitutes evidence against you. That you are, in effect, submitting your own confession."

"Yes, sir."

"And you wish the record to show that no pressure was applied to obtain it."

"The record may show that the only pressure applied was applied by me, to myself, over approximately fourteen months of sleepless nights." Halloway kept his hands flat on the table. Steady. He had learned steadiness in triage tents and burning quarters, and it served him now. "I'm not here because I was caught, Brigadier. I'm here because the ledger exists, and a ledger that exists ought to be answered for. By everyone whose pen is in it. Mine is in it forty-one times."

Pryce opened the cover and began, silently, to read. Halloway watched him and thought: *read all of it. Read the blank ones. Read the one that says* body unclaimed *and tell me what word we should have used instead.*

---

They questioned him for six hours.

They broke for a cold lunch he didn't touch, and then they questioned him for three more. Corliss wanted dates. The second board member, a Colonel Ashe with a wounded shoulder he favored like a talisman, wanted context — the operational pressures, the chain of instruction, the colonel who had first suggested that certain deaths might be *deferred*. Halloway gave the name. He watched something flicker in Ashe's face and understood that the colonel in question was either Ashe's friend, Ashe's patron, or Ashe himself, and he gave the name again, slower, so there could be no ambiguity in the transcript.

Pryce wanted details. Pryce was the only one who seemed to want the truth rather than the shape of a truth that would fit a predetermined conclusion, and Halloway found himself answering Pryce's questions at greater length than required — telling him about the field hospital at Vellmere, the flooded ward, the woman with the notebook who moved among the cots writing down what the officers' clerks were ordered not to write.

"She was there under what authority?" Corliss asked.

"None. That was her authority. She had none, and so she answered to no one, and so she recorded everything." He paused. "It took me until March of this year to understand that this made her the only person in that quarter doing the job correctly."

"You're fond of her," Ashe said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't relevant, and both of them knew it was meant to unsettle him.

"I'm in her debt," Halloway said. "Which is a different thing, and a larger one. She crossed the river ice carrying seven hundred names because the army I serve had decided those men were easier as rumors than as facts. Whatever happens in this room today, that fact doesn't change. You can strike my rank. You can't strike her crossing."

"We're not here to judge Miss Dunmore."

"No," Halloway agreed. "You're here to decide whether the falsification of casualty rolls was a system or a series of errors. And I'm here to tell you it was a system. I was a component of it. There are others. Some of them outrank me. Some of them are in this building."

The silence after that had weight. Corliss looked at him for a long moment, and Halloway saw the calculation move behind the grey eyes — not moral calculation, procedural. How far does this go. Who else does this touch. What does containment cost versus exposure.

"You could withdraw that statement," Corliss said quietly. "Amend it. Characterize the omissions as administrative failures under extreme conditions. The board would view such a characterization sympathetically. Your service record is otherwise—" he glanced at a folder, "—exemplary."

"I know what my record is. I wrote half of it."

"Then you understand what you're forfeiting."

Halloway thought about the bridge. He thought about standing on the south bank in March, counting Jude Dunmore's steps across the black ice, his voice going out over the cracking sheets — *eleven, twelve, keep your weight forward* — knowing that if the river took her, the last thing she would have heard was his voice, and finding that he could live with that, and then finding, when she reached the shore, that he couldn't live with anything less than what she'd done. She had carried the names. Someone had to carry the consequences. It was the same work, done from different banks.

"I understand exactly what I'm forfeiting," he said. "I've been forfeiting it since January of 1918, one sleepless night at a time. Today I'm simply making it official."

---

They adjourned at dusk. The verdict would take days — these things always took days, the machinery grinding through its necessary forms — but Halloway could read a room, and he had read this one. Corliss wanted it contained. Pryce wanted it true. Ashe wanted it buried, and Ashe had friends.

Whatever came out of the grinding, Halloway already knew the shape of his own sentence, because he had written it himself: the end of his career. Not disgrace, perhaps — the board might soften it, might grant him medical retirement, might let him resign before the findings published — but the end. No further commands. No further rolls bearing his signature. And that was right. That was, in fact, the entire point. A man who had erased forty-one names should never again hold a pen over a roster. It was not punishment. It was engineering.

He walked out through the chapel's side door into the cold evening, past pews stacked like cordwood, and stopped when he saw who was waiting in the vestry corridor.

Chaplain Wren looked older than he had in the river town. The smuggling run south had cost him something visible — a stiffness in his left hand, a new caution in his eyes — but the smile was the same, tired and unarmored.

"They told me a major was giving evidence today," Wren said. "I confess I assumed they'd dragged you in. It took me an hour to believe you'd walked in on your own feet."

"It took me fourteen months to walk in on my own feet. The actual walking was simple."

Wren studied him. "The readings continue, you know. Every Thursday, the garrison church. We're up to the D's. Families come from thirty miles. Last week a mother found her son's name — he died in October, she'd been told he was missing. She thanked me." His voice caught slightly. "She thanked *me*. And I stood there thinking, I didn't write a single name in that book. I only carried it. There's a woman across the river who wrote them, and a major who — what did you call it? — kept them differently."

"How is she?" Halloway asked. He hadn't managed to ask anyone else. It felt like a privilege he hadn't earned, asking after her.

"Well. Working. She and the printer correspond — the ferry runs again now, did you know? He sends letters with little printed ornaments in the margins. Flowers, mostly. She showed me one." Wren smiled. "She's well."

Good, Halloway thought. *Good.* The word was insufficient and he used it anyway, privately, the way you press a coin into a dead man's hand — not because it buys anything, but because the gesture is all you have.

"There's a copy of the published ledger coming off the printer's press," Wren said. "First edition. He's dedicating it. Would you like to know to whom?"

Halloway shook his head. "Tell me when it comes. I'd rather read it in his words."

"He'll want you to have one. He asked after you, actually — through Bram, who carries the post now. He said—" Wren hesitated, then delivered it plainly, the way you deliver a verdict: "He said the major paid his debt better than most men pay their honest bills. I believe that was a compliment."

Halloway laughed — short, surprised, real. "From a deserter-printer, I'll take it as a citation for valor."

They stood a moment in the cold corridor, two men who had carried the same book by different roads.

"The board resumes Thursday," Wren said. "You know they'll end you."

"I'm counting on it."

"And you're at peace with that?"

Halloway considered the question seriously, because Wren deserved a serious answer and because he had promised himself, somewhere on the ice in March, that he would stop lying in small ways if he ever stopped lying in large ones.

"No," he said. "But peace isn't the thing I'm after. Peace is what the falsified rolls offered everybody — a quiet war, a tidy column, nobody disturbed. I'm after something else. I'm after getting it *right*, finally, at whatever price the right version costs." He pulled his coat collar up against the wind coming through the broken window. "Ask me again in ten years. If I've built anything worth building out of what's left, ask me then."

Wren put out his stiff left hand, and Halloway took it.

"Thursday, then," the chaplain said. "I'll be in the gallery. Someone should be sitting behind you who knows what's in that book."

---

That night, in his quarters, Halloway unpacked his kit bag and laid out what remained of his possessions on the narrow bed, the way Jude had once inventoried a satchel against the end of the world. Uniforms. A photograph of a house in Kent he no longer expected to live in. A ration tin that had held, for one strange week in a burning quarter, a community. And at the bottom, loose, a single piece of printer's type — a lead block stamped with a letter, given to him by Elias Vane the day the press came apart, pressed into his hand with frostbitten fingers and no explanation except a look.

He turned it over. An asterisk. The footnote mark. *See below,* it said, in the grammar of printers. *This is not the whole story. Refer to the bottom of the page.*

He set it on the sill where the morning light would find it, and he understood, finally and completely, what he had chosen: not ruin, but reference. He would be the footnote to Jude Dunmore's main text — the officer who proved the ledger true, who authenticated every page with his own surrender, who made it impossible for the board to dismiss seven hundred names as sentiment or forgery. They could take the rank. They could not take the corroboration.

Thursday he would stand again in the chapel where the altar used to be, and he would say the forty-one names aloud, one by one, into the record, so that they entered the proceedings not as evidence but as testimony — men speaking at last, through him, in the only courtroom that would ever hear them.

Outside, far off toward the river, the evening train whistled — the ferry line running again, the crossings restored, the world cautiously stitching itself back together. Somewhere north of the water, a woman was adding names to a book. Somewhere in a free town, a press was turning.

Halloway blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, waiting for Thursday, lighter than he had been in two years — the way a man feels after setting down something heavy, not because the weight is gone, but because he has finally put it where it belongs.
