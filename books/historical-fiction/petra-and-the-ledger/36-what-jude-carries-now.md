## 36. What Jude Carries Now

The room I rented in Vellmere's south quarter had one window, one chair, and a floor that sloped toward the river the way old men slope toward the fire. It cost four francs a week and smelled of tallow and other people's cooking, and it was the first place since the workshop burned where nobody was dying.

I unpacked on the third morning, after I'd slept enough to stop flinching at carts.

I had learned in the war to inventory things properly — not the way quartermasters do it, all columns and totals, but the way you do it when everything you own fits on a blanket spread across a floorboards, and you need to know what you have before the world comes to take it again. So I laid it out. All of it.

The blanket itself. Two dresses, one of them Elias's mending — he'd stitched the hem of the blue one with the crooked, patient stitches of a man who sets type for a living and sews like one too, each stitch placed as if it might be read later by strangers. A comb with three teeth gone. The ration tin I'd carried over the ice, dented where I'd dropped it on the far shore because my hands were shaking too hard to hold anything. A photograph of Thomas at fourteen, squinting into a sun that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

And then the three things that were not simply things.

I'll take them one at a time, because that is how I have come to hold them, and because an inventory is a kind of prayer if you do it slowly enough.

* * *

The ledger first. Always the ledger first.

It weighed less than a loaf of bread. That was the thing about it that never stopped astonishing me — that two years of names, four hundred and eleven names by the last count Halloway and I had made together on the near bank while the smoke rose behind us, could be carried in one hand. Four hundred and eleven men who had existed hard enough to bleed and die, and whom the army had decided did not exist at all. The whole weight of that decision fit inside my satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was lighter than my ration tin.

The original was water-stained along one edge from the crossing. The ice water had gotten in despite the oilcloth, despite the wax, despite everything, and the bottom corner of the first thirty pages had gone soft and brown, the ink feathering outward like frost on glass. Three names were half-lost to it. I had sat up most of one night in Wren's tent trying to recover them, holding the pages at an angle to the lamp, reading the ghost-grooves the pen nib had left in the paper even where the ink had bloomed. I got two back. The third — a corporal, initial J., surname beginning with what might have been an M or might have been a W — stayed lost. I wrote *illegible, drowned* beside the space, which was true twice over, and hated myself a little every time I read it, and left it there anyway, because a book that lies to comfort its keeper is worth nothing.

The rest of the pages had survived clean. I turned through them now, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the rented room, and the smell of them came up — river water and lamp oil and under it, faintly, still, the iron-and-rag smell of the flooded hospital at Vellmere where the first entries had been made. Memory lives in paper longer than it lives in us. I could open to page nine and be nineteen again, kneeling on a wet plank floor, writing *Pvt. Aldous Renner, 2nd Btn., died 4 March, fever* in a hand that shook only a little, while a doctor ten feet away told me to put the pen down and bring bandages instead.

I had not put the pen down. That was the whole story, really. Everything else — the bridge, the ice, Elias's hands, Halloway's ruined career, the burning of the eastern quarter — all of it grew out of the single small refusal of a girl who would not stop writing.

Four hundred and eleven names. And now, because of Wren's copies and Bram's page smuggled south and Elias's press turning in a free town, the names existed in more places than one. That was the arithmetic I had wanted from the beginning, though I hadn't known how to say it: not that the ledger should survive, but that it should become un-killable. A thing written once can be burned. A thing written forty times, carried by a chaplain and a deserter and a runaway orderly and a major and a woman who crossed a breaking river with it strapped to her chest — that thing has roots. You would have to burn the whole country to get it out of the ground.

I closed the book and held it against my sternum for a moment, the way you'd check whether a heart was still beating.

Then I set it down and picked up the next thing.

* * *

The type block was smaller than my thumbnail's width of lead and oak, and it was broken — snapped clean across the middle sometime during the dismantling of the press, or during the smashing of it; neither of us ever knew exactly when, because neither of us was watching the little pieces while the big ones were dying. Elias had pressed it into my hands on the near bank with his ruined fingers, those swollen white sausages the frost had made of the hands that had set four hundred and eleven names in metal, and he'd said, *So you don't forget how it sounds.*

How what sounds.

The block falling into the composing stick. The small iron click of a letter taking its place among letters, the sound that meant someone was about to be said, permanently, in ink. He knew I knew the sound. I'd spent enough nights in that workshop, setting type under his elbow, him correcting my grip without looking up from his own stick, that the click lived somewhere below memory in me, in the hands themselves. But he gave me the block anyway, because Elias understood something I was only beginning to learn: that we carry grief better when it has a weight and a shape, when it can sit in the palm and be looked at rather than merely felt.

The block bore a T. Thomas's T — the same face of type Elias had used to print Thomas's name in the first fair copy, and in every copy after. When I held it up to the window light I could see the fracture running through it, and I thought, not for the first time, that there was a sermon in that if anyone wanted one. The letter that named my brother was itself broken. Everything we used to say the dead with had been damaged by the saying. And yet the impressions stood. The name had gone out into the world whole, however cracked the instrument.

Some mornings I woke and reached for it before I was fully awake, the way you reach for a person in a bed that has gone cold, and finding it, remembering where I was and why, I would lie still and let the morning come to me slowly, the way ice lets go of a shore.

* * *

The flower I almost didn't count as mine.

Elias had pressed it between two sheets of blotting paper the autumn before the burning — a late aster, purple going gray at the edges, picked off the embankment above the drowned depot on one of the few days the cold relented and let us pretend the season was ordinary. He'd given it to me without ceremony, sliding it into the ledger at the page where Thomas's name stood, and said only, "For the margin. Books should keep one living thing."

I had forgotten it was there until the night after the crossing, in Wren's tent, when I opened the oilcloth to check the damage and found the flower flat and papery and perfect against the page, holding its color the way pressed things do — a memory of purple rather than purple itself, but stubbornly, unmistakably itself.

A man who makes books had known, before I did, that a record of the dead needs a proof of life tucked into it somewhere, or the reader drowns in it. That was Elias. He thought in structures I didn't have words for. Where I saw a list, he saw a book; where I saw duty, he saw craft; where I saw an ending, he saw the last line before the colophon, and a colophon means somebody intends to print more.

I kept the flower in the ledger still. It seemed right. But I took it out sometimes, just to look at it, just to remember that the winter had contained one warm afternoon, one gray-purple aster, one man's hands — whole then, both of them, quick and sure — touching mine as he passed me the blotting paper.

* * *

That was what survived. I want to be honest about what didn't, because gratitude that ignores the losses isn't gratitude; it's arithmetic done by a coward.

Thomas hadn't survived. That went without saying, had gone without saying for two years, and yet I found I had to say it anyway, to myself, in the new quiet of the rented room, because the inventory was his as much as mine. Every item on the blanket traced back to him. The ledger began with his name. The type block spelled its first letter. Even the photograph — that was his, though the boy in it couldn't have imagined becoming the reason for anything.

The workshop hadn't survived. The press hadn't. Elias's hands — the hands, specifically, the particular instruments that had made everything else possible — had come out of the river and the frost as things that would never again set type, never again pull a lever, never again do the one work he had chosen for himself when he deserted rather than print lies. I had seen him three days after the crossing, before Osk ferried him north, and his hands had been wrapped in the same kind of bandages I'd spent a war putting on other men, and when he'd tried to joke about it his voice had cracked in the middle, and I had understood that I wasn't looking at a wound. I was looking at a changed life.

Halloway's career hadn't survived — but that one he'd spent himself, deliberately, like a coin he'd finally decided was good for something, and I refused to mourn it. Wren was still walking south somewhere with his copies and his death sentence if caught. Osk had lost his boat's season and nearly his boat. Bram — Bram had vanished south with the chaplain, forgiven by everyone except perhaps himself, carrying a guilt I hoped the printing of the book would eventually burn down to something livable.

And the bridge hadn't survived, though they were already rebuilding it. They'd started within a month. There was stone coming up the river on barges, and scaffolds rising at the old footings, and men in shirtsleeves arguing about mortar in voices that carried all the way to my window. Petra, the town had always called the crossing — the rock — and the rock, it turned out, was not the stone at all. The rock was whatever refuses to be washed away. I'd carried some of it across the ice myself.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the whole inventory spread on the blanket, and I did the accounting honestly, the way I'd promised myself I would.

What survived: the names, all of them but one illegible ghost. The love — changed in shape, tested at a distance, but alive; I had his last letter in my coat pocket and I would answer it tonight. The friendship of a major who had traded his uniform for his conscience and called it a fair price. A community scattered but unbroken: Wren walking, Bram walking, Elias printing, Osk ferrying. My own life, which I had offered to the river and which the river, in the end, had declined to take.

What didn't: my brother, the workshop, the press, the ease of Elias's hands, the version of myself who had knelt on a wet floor two winters ago believing a private list could change nothing.

She'd been wrong, that girl. It was the only belief of hers I was glad to have lost.

* * *

Toward evening I packed the inventory away again — dresses folded, photograph propped on the sill where the last light could find it, ledger and type block and flower each returned to their places. The ledger under the loose floorboard by the bed, because habits outlive their reasons. The block in my breast pocket, where I could feel it click faintly against my ribs when I moved, a small iron heartbeat. The flower staying where flowers belong: in the book, keeping Thomas company.

I lit the lamp and took out Elias's letter to answer it.

His handwriting had suffered with everything else — the strokes came out crabbed now, laborious, each word visibly fought for — but he had told me once that a printer judges a page by what it says, not by how easily it was set, and he signed his letters the old way, with a tiny ornament printed from a scrap of type he'd salvaged: a fleuron, a little leaf, the printer's mark for *something beautiful grown here.* This letter's leaf was smudged, printed by a hand that couldn't press evenly anymore. I loved it more than any clean impression he'd ever pulled.

He wrote that the new press was small — a hand press, secondhand, bought from a widow — and that it groaned in a key the old machine had never used, and that he was learning its temper the way you learn a horse's. He wrote that his hands were improving, or that the doctors said so, though the sentence after that one was crossed out entirely, struck through so hard the paper had thinned, and I let it stay unread, because some sentences a man is allowed to strike from his own reports.

And then, at the bottom, beneath the smudged fleuron, a single line I must have read six times already:

*I have begun to set the names. Your brother is the first word on the first page. Come north when the ferry runs steady, and bring the flower — I want to see what the winter did to its color.*

I sat down at the one chair, by the one window, with the sloping floor and the tallow smell and the trains whistling past on the restored line, and I dipped my pen, and I began:

*Dear Elias —*

Outside, far down the darkening street, the evening boat sounded its horn at the landing — the ferry, running again, crossing and recrossing the water that had tried to keep us apart and failed. In the morning I would buy a ticket. Tonight there was ink, and paper, and a letter to finish, and somewhere north of the river a press waiting to be fed the only crop it would ever need to grow.

I wrote his name, and under it mine, side by side on the page the way they would stand together on the title page of the book — and outside, the horn sounded again, long and low, like something answering.
