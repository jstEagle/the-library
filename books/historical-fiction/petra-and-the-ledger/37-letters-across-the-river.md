## 37. Letters Across the River

The ferry ran again on the third week of April.

I learned it the way I learned everything that spring — by standing in a queue and listening to strangers talk. The ice had gone out of the river in a single violent night, taking the pilings of the old stone bridge with it, and for six weeks the two banks had been countries unto themselves. Then a flat-bottomed ferry named the *Mercy* began making crossings at slack water twice a day, and the war's paperwork started flowing again, and so did the smaller traffic: letters, parcels, the occasional coffin.

I was fourth in line at the ferry office in Vellmere when the clerk called out my name. Not my name — a description. "Woman who came asking after printers." He held up an envelope as if it might bite him. "Came over on the morning boat. No return address worth reading."

It was addressed in Elias's hand, which is to say it was addressed beautifully, every letter set as though he had composed it rather than written it, each *d* and *h* standing straight as soldiers who had deserted their post but kept their posture. My name across the front. Beneath it, small and precise, a printer's ornament: a fleuron, the little leaf-shape compositors tuck into gaps, stamped in faded violet ink.

I stood there in the ferry office holding it and could not make my hands open it. The clerk cleared his throat. A soldier behind me shifted his pack. I stepped outside into the cold bright morning, sat down on the ferry office steps with the river running loud and brown and free below me, and opened it.

Inside, one page, printed — actually printed, set in type and pulled on some small press, not written at all. He had composed a letter the only way he knew how to say anything true.

*Jude.*

*I have hands again, or something like them. The left works entirely. The right works on its own schedule and holds grudges. The doctors here call the frostbite damage "moderate," which tells you what they have seen this winter that they can call mine moderate.*

*I am in Aldenmoor. Free town, whatever that means now. There is a room above a cooperage with a north window and enough headroom for me to stand up in, which I count among the year's great luxuries. I have begun to build.*

*Not the press. Not yet. First the type. I have been casting sort from whatever lead I can beg off the plumbers, and my right hand objects, and I do it anyway. You will understand why. Some things you cannot let other people's hands be the last hands to touch them.*

*I set your name first. It seemed correct. It is sitting in the composing stick now, waiting for a sentence worthy of it. I have not found one yet. I have been at it nine days.*

*The cooper says I hum when I work. I told him it was a habit from a workshop where humming meant the work was going well and silence meant the provosts were coming. He laughed. I did not tell him it was true.*

*Osk sends his regards and wants me to tell you he has renamed the ferry. You will see it when you come down to the landing. He is insufferable about it. He has earned the right.*

*The river between us is a river. That is all it is. I keep reminding myself. I have watched it freeze and break and burn things and carry things, and underneath all of that it is only water doing what water does, going somewhere it has decided to go.*

*I decided a long time ago where I was going. It took me until this winter to admit it had nothing to do with geography.*

*Set your name in type for yourself sometime. You will see what I mean. Some words hold themselves differently when they are made of metal and meant to last.*

*E.*

I read it four times. Then I folded it along his creases, put it in my coat pocket against my heart like an idiot, and went home to answer it.

The problem was that I wrote. I did not print. My reply would arrive as ink on paper, ordinary, human, crooked — while his came to me typeset, deliberate, every word weighed before it ever touched the page. Writing back felt like shouting into a cathedral.

I wrote anyway. It took me most of a day, because everything worth saying kept turning out to be either too large or too small, and the space between those two sizes is where most of my life now lives.

***

His second letter came eleven days later. The fleuron on the envelope was different this time — a pointing hand, the little manicule that old books use to say *look here.* Look here. As if I needed telling.

*Jude.*

*The sentence for your name remains unfinished. This is no longer a compositional problem. I have accepted that it may never be finished, and that some sentences are meant to stand open the way doorways stand open.*

*Progress report. The press exists. Do not congratulate me yet — it exists the way a foal exists, all legs and bad decisions. I found the frame iron in a scrapyard outside town, and the screw I bought from a man who swore it came off a cider press and I have chosen to believe him because the alternative is that it came off something worse. The platen is oak. The bed is slate from a demolished schoolhouse. Children learned their letters on that slate. Now it will learn mine.*

*I pulled the first proof yesterday. It was terrible. The impression was uneven, the ink was too thick, the whole thing looked like it had been printed by a man with one working hand in a room above a cooperage, which is accurate reporting. I hung it on the wall anyway. Osk came up uninvited — the man has never once been invited anywhere in his life — and stood looking at it for a long time.*

*"That's her name," he said.*

*"Yes."*

*"You printed her name before you printed anything else."*

*"Yes, Osk."*

*He nodded like a doctor confirming a diagnosis and went back downstairs. I could hear him laughing to himself the whole way.*

*Tell me what Vellmere is doing. Tell me the small things. I mean it — the small things. What the market smells like now that the thaw has rotted the winter stores. Whether Mrs. Halloway — no. Strike that. Whether the major writes to you. Strike that also; I set it in type before I thought, which is the danger of thinking in metal, and then I struck it, which is the mercy of it. Consider both lines officially deleted. Consider them not-deleted. Consider that I am forty-one years old and composing like a boy and I do not care.*

*Tell me the small things.*

*E.*

So I told him the small things. I told him the market smelled of river mud and horse and the particular sweetness of turnips going soft in the warm weather, and that nobody minded because sweetness was sweetness. I told him the garrison band played badly on Sundays and that everyone applauded anyway because applause was cheaper than bread and easier to give. I told him the ferry office clerk had started wearing a paper flower in his cap, and no one asked why, and he never said, and it had become the kind of fact that makes a town bearable.

I did not tell him whether Halloway wrote. Because he did, and because the answer would have taken more honesty than a letter about turnips deserved, and because I was a coward in exactly one direction and I knew which direction it was.

Halloway's letters were different creatures. They arrived in official envelopes with his rank stenciled in the corner even on private correspondence, as though the army had gotten into the habit of him and couldn't break it. They were careful, generous, and sad. He wrote about the inquiry — his inquiry, the one he had invited onto himself by walking into the provost's office with our merged ledger under his arm — in the tone of a man describing weather in a country he'd decided to move to. *The tribunal sits in June,* he wrote. *I expect to lose everything except the part of myself I spent the winter trying to find. That part they cannot court-martial, though several men are eager to try.*

He always ended the same way: *If you need anything, Jude, anything at all, you know how to reach me. I am, as ever, yours in the ways that matter and absent in the ways that don't. — J.H.*

I answered his letters too. Duty, affection, gratitude — all real, all present, all insufficient in a way he understood better than anyone. He had carried me across a river in his voice, counting my steps from the far bank, and we both knew what that was worth and what it wasn't.

But it was Elias's letters I read standing up, and Halloway's I read sitting down, and I have never needed a psychologist to explain the difference to me.

***

The third letter came with a parcel.

The parcel was small, wrapped in oilcloth — our oilcloth, the same stock we'd sealed the ledger in, and I recognized it instantly the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Inside, packed in sawdust, was a block of type. Not a single sort. A full block, maybe two inches square, mounted on wood, and cut into its face in letters deep enough to bite:

**THOMAS DUNMORE — 1891–1916**

And beneath, in smaller type, the line I had spoken over his empty grave with no stone to mark it: *HE WAS HERE.*

I sat on the floor of my room with the block in my lap for a long time. Outside, the ferry horn sounded its crossing. Somewhere under all that running water was the shelf of shore-fast ice I had walked across in February with the names strapped to my chest, and it was water now, just water, just the river being the river.

The letter that came with it was short.

*Jude.*

*I cast this from new lead, but the letters are copied from your ledger's hand — I measured your pen strokes from the pages you sent the chaplain and cut the pattern to match. So it is his name in your handwriting in my metal. Three hands made this thing and none of them is the hand it honors, which seems right. That is how memorials work. The dead get the name; the living get the labor.*

*Keep it or bury it. Set it in a page someday or set it on a shelf. It is yours either way. It was always going to be yours; I just hadn't met you yet when I started learning the trade that would let me make it.*

*Aldenmoor report: the press prints. Properly now. I pulled a clean sheet last Tuesday and had to sit down afterward. Osk pretended not to notice, which from Osk is a eulogy of tact.*

*First job is small — playbills for a music hall, business cards, a wedding invitation for the cooper's daughter. Honest work for honest money. But I have begun setting something else at night, after hours, page by page. You know what it is. You have known since the fleuron on the first envelope.*

*I am printing the names, Jude. All of them. Yours, mine, Wren's copies, the lot — set as a book, so that no family in this country ever again has to learn their son died from a rumor in a market queue. Wren has written to me from the south. He says families write to him asking where they can buy such a book, and he has had to write back saying no such book exists. I intend to make him a liar.*

*It will take a year. Possibly two. My right hand and I are negotiating terms.*

*I am not asking you to come. I want you to know that. I am not asking anything. I am telling you what I am building, because you are the reason it is being built, and a builder should tell the architect.*

*Though if you happened to be passing through Aldenmoor, the cooper's wife makes a pie that would justify the journey on its own merits.*

*E.*

I laughed, alone in my room, until I cried, and then I laughed some more, because grief and joy had long since stopped pretending to be separate things in me. They shared a house now. They split the rent.

***

We fell into a rhythm, the letters. Every ten days or twelve, sometimes three weeks when the river misbehaved, the *Mercy* brought me an envelope with an ornament on it, and no two ornaments repeated. A fleuron. A manicule. An asterisk, a dagger, a double dagger, a section mark — the whole quiet arsenal of punctuation that printers use to say *something matters here* without saying what. Once he sent an asterism, the little triangle of three stars, and beneath it in tiny type: *this one means a passage of unusual beauty. I put it on the outside because the inside didn't deserve it. The inside is just me missing you at length. You're welcome.*

I wrote back in ink, in my own crooked hand, and I began to suspect he preferred it that way — that my letters' imperfection was a relief to him, proof that not everything needed to be perfect to be kept. He never said so directly. He said it in type once, though. The seventh letter bore a single line of ornament above the address, a row of deliberately damaged sorts — letters with chipped faces, cracked shoulders, one upside-down *n* — and beneath them:

*Flawed sorts still print. Ask any compositor. Ask any woman who walked a river in February.*

I kept every letter. All of them, in the order they arrived, tied with string in a bootbox that had once held ration tins. On nights when Vellmere got loud — and it got loud that summer, with the tribunal approaching and the town full of officers who remembered the eastern quarter burning and had opinions about who was to blame — I would take the box down and read one letter, just one, chosen at random, and go to sleep.

Once, drunk on two glasses of the ferryman's terrible cherry brandy, I read them all in a single night and then sat until dawn writing a letter I never sent, in which I told him everything — about Halloway, about the tribunal, about the specific weight of loving two men when one of them taught you to count your own footsteps and the other taught you what your footsteps were for. In the morning I burned it in the stove and wrote instead:

*Elias. The small things: the market smells of strawberries now. The clerk's paper flower has been joined by a second flower, and the town has agreed without discussion that this means something happy. The band played "Home, Sweet Home" so badly on Sunday that three people wept and it was unclear from grief or from the trombone.*

*The larger thing, since you asked in every letter and stopped asking in the last one, which was the loudest way you could have asked: I am coming. Not passing through. Coming. When the tribunal ends in June — whichever way it goes, and I will tell you about that when it's done, because Halloway deserves that much discretion and I owe him more than that — I will come south on the Mercy with one satchel. You know what fits in a satchel. You helped me pack it once on the worst night of my life, and you argued for the press, and I argued for the names, and we were both right and we were both wrong, and I have had a year to think about what fits.*

*What fits is this: the ledger. Thomas's block. The bootbox. And me.*

*Save me a place at your table. Save me work for my hands. I can learn to cast type. My hands are steady — you've seen why. Teach me the trade that let you make what you made. I want to spend whatever years remain setting names in metal beside you, and I want the pie.*

*Jude.*

I sent it on the morning boat. Then I stood at the landing watching the *Mercy* — renamed, as promised, the *Dunmore*, because Osk was exactly that kind of sentimental and exactly that kind of stubborn — grow small on the brown water, carrying the first sentence of the rest of my life toward a room above a cooperage in Aldenmoor.

Three days later, a letter came that broke the rhythm. No ornament on the envelope. Handwritten, hurried, the letters leaning like fence posts in wind — his actual hand, not his composed one, and my stomach dropped before I broke the seal.

*Jude.*

*Something has happened. Come sooner than June if you can. The press is fine, I am fine, Osk is insufferable as ever — but the first copies of the book came off the press yesterday, and there is a man here asking questions about where the type was cast, and I think he is from the tribunal, and I think he is not asking for Halloway.*

*Come soon. Bring nothing you cannot run with.*

*E.*
