## 38. The Workshop Rises Again

The town was called Ferrowden, and it smelled of cut pine and horse lye and nothing burning at all, which took Elias three weeks to stop noticing.

He had arrived in October with a crate of salvaged type — the same crate that had once hidden a ledger of dead men's names — and forty-one marks borrowed against a promise he hadn't known how he'd keep. He rented the back half of a cooper's shop on Tanner's Lane because the cooper worked days and the rent was cheap and because the building had stood since before the war, its beams dark with age and smoke that belonged to ordinary fires, hearth fires, honest ones.

The first thing he built was not a press. It was a table.

He built it from pine offcuts, four legs and a top planed smooth, and when it was done he set the type case on it and stood looking at the compartments for a long time without touching them. The letters were all there, or nearly. He'd lost some of the small e's to the river, and an entire row of ampersands, and somewhere between the workshop and the ferryman's shed a drawer of figures had gone missing so that his numbers ran out past seven. But a press is not a machine, he reminded himself, running his thumb along the dividers. A press is a decision made wooden.

His hands were the problem. The frostbite had taken feeling from the first two fingers of his right hand and left them stiff as type metal, and the surgeons at the garrison hospital had talked about amputation in the way men talk about weather — regretfully, as though it were simply coming. It hadn't come. Instead the fingers had stayed, blackened at the tips for a month, then pink, then merely useless, curled like question marks. He learned to grip the composing stick between his remaining fingers and his palm, awkwardly, the way a man carries water in cupped hands, spilling some, but carrying it.

He set his first word in December. It took him eleven minutes.

The word was *Petra*.

He chose it deliberately, one letter at a time, sliding each piece of type into the stick until the name of the bridge sat solid in his hand — p, e, t, r, a — five characters that weighed almost nothing and meant everything: the rock the bridge was named for, the rock the new bridge would be named for, the stone under everything. He inked it with a brush because he had no roller yet, pressed it to a scrap of the cooper's wrapping paper, and pulled back something faint and grey and true.

Then he sat down on the floor of the empty shop and wept, quietly, the way men do when no one has taught them another way, until the candle burned down and the cold drove him up the stairs to his room.

* * *

By January he had a press.

Not a real one — not the great iron beast of Vellmere with its flywheel and its treadle and the lever you could pull all day, that heart of oiled steel he had loved the way other men love horses or wives. That one lay in pieces under the ice-melt of a burned quarter, or sold for scrap, or standing rusted in some army yard as a monument to nothing. He had smashed it himself rather than let them have it, and his hands still remembered the weight of the maul, and sometimes at night they ached with it worse than the frostbite ever had.

So he built a common press. Oak frame, iron screw bought secondhand from a bookbinder who swore it had come off a church press in a town whose name Elias pretended not to recognize, platen and coffin assembled by lamplight over nine nights with his ruined hand bracing what his good one turned. The cooper watched from the doorway one evening, arms folded, sawdust in his beard.

"My grandfather had one of those," the cooper said. "Printed funeral notices. Whole town's deaths in a column, week after week, through the last war." He scratched his jaw. "You printing funerals too?"

"No," Elias said. "Names. But not for mourning."

The cooper looked at him a long moment, then nodded once and went home, and never asked again, which Elias learned was the Ferrowden way. A river town. They knew what it was to lose men to water and paperwork both.

The press printed its first clean sheet on the seventeenth of January. He hung the proof on the wall above the table, where he could see it from the door every morning: a single line, set large.

*In this book are the names of 1,204 soldiers struck from the official reports of the winter campaign, 1917–18.*

Beneath it, smaller: *Read them aloud.*

* * *

Jude's letters came irregularly through the winter, carried south by chaplain's post and north again by whatever channel Wren had built out of cassocks and favors. She wrote in her ledger-hand, upright and unhurried, and she wrote about the work: the families writing back, the woman in Corvelin who had spent two years believing her son a deserter and learned from a printed page that he had died holding the ford; the old man who walked forty kilometers to the garrison church to hear the reading and asked Wren afterward whether names could be added still, because his boy had gone down at the weir and no report had ever mentioned him.

*Yes,* Jude had written. *That is the whole point of a living document. Send me his name.*

Elias read that letter four times and then set it in type.

That became the arrangement, unspoken and then spoken and finally simply true: everything she sent him, he printed. Not the private things — those stayed between them, folded into oilcloth packets tied with the same string she used for the ledger — but the public things, the corrections, the additions, the small dispatches from the country of the counted. He printed them as broadsheets, fifty copies, a hundred, and Wren's people distributed them through garrison towns and market squares, and slowly, in the way ice goes out of a river — invisibly, and then all at once — the official story began to crack along lines someone had scored into it years before.

An army auditor came in March. Elias saw him before the man saw the shop: a neat figure in civilian grey asking the farrier questions, taking notes. For two days Elias kept the press cold and the type distributed and answered the door as a jobbing printer with nothing but a commission for seed labels and a hand too ruined to do much of it. The auditor looked at the proof hanging on the wall — he could hardly not look at it, it was the size of a window — and read the line twice.

"One thousand two hundred and four," he said. "Is that a claim?"

"It's a count," Elias said. "Counts aren't claims. Claims can be false."

"And who verified your count?"

"A major," Elias said. "Who will be verifying himself shortly, if I understand the correspondence correctly. You may wait for him."

The auditor wrote something down, thanked him with a face that gave away nothing, and left. That night Elias printed nothing. He sat with the letter Jude had sent in February, the one that said only *Halloway has requested the inquiry himself. He says he wants to be cross-examined by people who hate him. He says that's the only kind of examination worth having.* And beneath it, smaller, in the hand she saved for herself: *I think he is trying to become the man he told me he couldn't carry across the ice. I think it might be working.*

* * *

The book itself took the spring.

They had argued about it first — Jude and he, across three letters' distance, the quarrel arriving and departing by chaplain's post at the speed of grief. She wanted the ledger printed exactly: every name in ledger order, with the annotations, the fragments of testimony from the dying, the mass of unidentified dead given whatever identity candlelight and patience could wring from them. He wanted more. He wanted prefaces and sources and the auditor's seal and a title page that dared the army to burn it — because a broadsheet burns, but a *book*, a thousand books, books in parish libraries and widows' parlors and the satchels of traveling priests, a book is a thing with too many bodies to kill.

"You're talking about a weapon," she wrote.

"I'm talking about a reprint," he wrote back. "You've seen what rumor does. Rumor is a name heard thirdhand, doubted at every hand. Print is a name a mother can hold. Put it in her hands."

Her next letter contained the answer and nothing else: Thomas Dunmore's full entry, transcribed fair, ready for page one. And below it: *Set this first. If the type fails anywhere, let it fail everywhere else.*

So he began there, in April, at the long table by the window where the light lasted until eight. His hand had learned its new grammar by then — the stiff fingers bracing the paper, the good fingers doing the fine work, the whole grip slow but sure, a hundred and twenty words an hour where he'd once set six hundred. It didn't matter. Speed was for newspapers and falsified rolls, for the machines of men who needed names to move faster than truth. This work could take all year. It could take ten years. Every hour of it was an hour in which the names existed in metal, independent of any officer's pen, any requisition order, any fire.

He set Thomas's entry and pulled a proof and held it up to the window light, and for a moment he was back in the Vellmere workshop with the ice groaning on the river and Jude asleep over the ledger with ink on her cheekbone, and his chest did the thing it did now whenever he thought of that winter, which was to ache and be grateful in the same motion, grief and gratitude sharing a body the way his two hands shared a task.

He printed five hundred copies of the signature sheet that night. By June he had a bindery — two sisters from the milliner's who sewed signatures for wages and, after the first week, for nothing, because their brother's name was number 317. By July the first finished copies existed: grey boards, plain title, no ornament except the small device he cut himself from a scrap of boxwood and printed in the corner of every page.

It was a type block. The letter T.

* * *

The dedication went on the page facing the title, and he set it himself, of course, though Jude had written it:

*To those who carried the names —*

*over bridges, over ice, over the silence of official paper.*

*And to Petra, which is stone,*

*and to what is written, which endures longer than either.*

The orders came slower than the copies. That was the miracle nobody had planned: the book outran its suppression. An auditor's inquiry moved at the pace of committees; a printed book moved at the pace of daughters mailing copies to mothers, chaplains slipping copies into knapsacks, a colonel's widow buying twelve and giving them away at church. By August there were copies in three provincial capitals, and the Ministry issued a statement calling the ledger "unverified," and the statement was printed in newspapers *alongside the book's title and where it could be bought*, which was the finest advertisement money couldn't buy, and Elias laughed alone in the shop until the cooper knocked on the wall to ask if he was quite well.

He wasn't well. He was better than well. He was a man rebuilding his trade around the only job that had ever mattered, and on the evenings when the light failed and his hand cramped and the type seemed endless, he would pull one finished book from the stack and open it to a random page and read a name aloud to the empty room — *Aldous Merrin, private, drowned at the weir, survived by a sister who thought him a thief* — and the cramp would loosen, and the type would seem less endless, and he would go on.

In September, a woman came to the door.

She was perhaps sixty, dressed in black gone grey at the seams, and she stood in the doorway of the print shop holding a copy of the book wrapped in a shawl as if it were an infant, which, Elias thought, in every way that signified, it was.

"I'm told you're the printer," she said.

"I'm the printer."

"My son is number 88," she said. "I've read it forty times. But reading isn't—" Her voice caught and restarted. "I wanted to see where it was made. I wanted to shake the hand."

Elias extended his right hand, the ruined one, the two dead fingers curled against the palm, and she took it in both of hers without flinching, without pausing, and held it the way you hold something you've been told is fragile, and said: "Thank you for what these cost you."

After she left he stood a long time at the window watching Tanner's Lane fill with evening. Then he went to the type case, to the compartment at the bottom where he kept the things that weren't for sale — Halloway's list in its original hand, the oilcloth scrap from the ledger's wrapping, and the type block stamped with Thomas's name, which Jude had pressed back into his hands on the near bank of a breaking river and which he had refused to melt down or sell or spend, because some letters are not for setting.

He took it out and held it against the fading light. *Thomas*. Fourteen pieces of lead that had crossed the ice in a dead man's name and come out the other side.

The knock came at dusk, as knocks did. He expected a customer, a courier, another grieving parent — the shop had become a place of pilgrimage, unofficially, and he kept the kettle hot for it. What he found instead was a boy of fifteen, river-mud to the knees, breathing hard from a run, holding out a letter sealed not with wax but with the string-and-oilcloth fold he knew better than his own signature.

"From across the water," the boy gasped. "Chaplain's man said you'd want it quick. Said there's news about the bridge."

Elias broke the fold with his thumbs.

The handwriting was Jude's, steady as always. But the first line was not about families, or the inquiry, or the reading. The first line said: *They've approved the reconstruction. Petra will stand again by summer — and they've asked me to read the names at the laying of the cornerstone. All twelve hundred and four.*

*I need my printer there.*

Elias looked up at the boy, at the lane, at the press waiting in the lamplight behind him with tomorrow's forms already locked in the chase.

"Tell the chaplain's man," he said, already reaching for his coat, "that the printer is coming."
