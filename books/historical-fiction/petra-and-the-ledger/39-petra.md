## 39. Petra

The bridge took eleven years to build and one afternoon to walk.

Jude had counted on it being harder than that. She had imagined standing at the near end for a long time, hand on the cold stone parapet, gathering herself the way you gather yourself before a cold river. She had rehearsed the trembling. What she had not rehearsed was the ordinariness of it — carters passing her with nods, a boy fishing off the second pier, gulls working the shallows where the ice used to speak. The bridge did not care what had happened beneath it. That was, she supposed, the whole argument for building things out of rock instead of memory.

*Petra*, they called it. The old ferryman's word, Osk's word, the word the masons carved into a bronze plate at the southern approach: **PETRA — THE ROCK BRIDGE — ERECTED BY THE TOWNS OF VELLMERE AND ASHCOMBE, 1926**. She had read somewhere in the gazette that the name came from an older tongue, some engineer's Latin, *from the stone we cross*. But Jude knew better, or believed she did. Osk had said it once, on the last night of the ice road, when she asked him why he'd risked the shelf of shore-fast ice for three passengers and a satchel of names. He had shrugged inside his coat and looked out at the black water showing between the floes.

"Stone," he said. "Everything else goes downriver."

She stood now at the bronze plate and put her palm flat against it. Eleven years since the crossing. Nine since the first letter from Elias arrived in Ashcombe with a printed ornament folded inside — a small fleuron, an inked leaf, proof that his hands still made things even when they couldn't hold a pen without aching. Seven since the book came off his press in Correnvale: *A Ledger of Names, Being a True Roll of the Wounded and Dead of the Winter Campaigns, 1917–18, As Recorded by Those Who Kept Them.*

Her copy sat in the satchel on her shoulder now, wrapped in oilcloth out of habit. Old habit. You wrapped things in oilcloth or you watched them drown. She unwrapped it here, at the head of the bridge, in the flat gold light of an October afternoon, because this was the place the dedication belonged.

She turned past the frontispiece — Elias had insisted on a woodcut of the workshop, smoke rising from its chimney, though the workshop itself was ash and cinder within a month of the thaw — and found the page she always stopped at. Not the first name. Not Thomas. She would come to Thomas later; she always came to Thomas later, after her legs had done their work and her heart had settled like sediment in a glass. First she came to the dedication, set in type so small and clean it looked like something grown rather than made:

***To those who carried the names — across water, across fire, across the years — so that no family need depend on rumor.***

Eleven words. It had taken him four months to settle them. He wrote to her about it the way other men wrote about weather or crops: *I have tried "so that no mother need wait," but it excludes too many. I have tried "against forgetting," but it sounds like a monument, and monuments are for men who want to be forgiven cheaply.* In the end he sent the line as it now stood, and added: *It is yours as much as mine. Strike anything you cannot live under.* She had struck nothing. She had lived under it nine years and it held her weight better than the shore-fast ice ever had.

A carter rattled past, wheels knocking on stone. The boy on the pier pulled up a fish the size of his forearm, whooped at it, and threw it back. Gulls screamed their one opinion. Jude tucked the book under her arm and walked out over the river.

* * *

The river was lower than she remembered, or the bridge made it look lower — raised her above it, gave her the long view downstream that no one standing on ice ever gets. From up here she could see the whole reach of it: the bend where the drowned supply depot had gone under, the shingle spit where Osk's boat used to winter, the far bank where the workshop had stood. Nothing marked the spot now but a difference in the grass — greener there, the ferrymen said, because ash is kind to ground. Elias had laughed at that when she told him. *Good,* he wrote. *Let the only thing it grows be paper.*

She reached midspan and stopped where the ice had been thickest, where the shelf of shore-fast ice had run from bank to bank like a road laid by something patient and cruel. Below her the water moved green and slow, carrying leaves, carrying light. On the night of the crossing it had carried slabs the size of chapel roofs, grinding, groaning, opening seams of black water faster than she could count steps — except someone had counted them for her. Halloway's voice, carried thin across the breaking river: *Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Keep your eyes on me, Dunmore. Twenty-four.*

He had died five years ago. Not on the ice, not by firing squad, though both had wanted him. He had submitted himself to inquiry over the falsified rolls, using the merged ledger as evidence against his own signature, and the inquiry had stripped his rank and very nearly jailed him, and then the war had ended and everyone had wanted very much to stop counting, and so they let him go with his career broken and his name quietly ruined. He had spent the last years of his life answering letters from families. Thousands of them. He kept a room in Ashcombe with nothing in it but a desk, a lamp, and files. When she visited him in the spring of '23 he showed her his system: each letter answered in his own hand, each answer citing the page and entry number of the published ledger, each file closed with the same sentence written at the bottom — *Now you know.* He had said it without pride. He had said it the way a man sets down something heavy and finds his arms still shaped around the weight.

"You could have crossed," she told him once, on his narrow porch, watching the light go off the water. "That night. There was room on Osk's shelf of ice for two walkers if the second one was brave."

"I know," he said.

"That's all? *I know?*"

He considered the question honestly, which was the most decent thing about him — he considered everything honestly, late, and at cost. "If I had crossed with you," he said, "I'd have spent my life believing I bought my way back onto the right side of the ledger with your courage instead of my own." He looked at her then, and there was nothing left in his face of the major who had once offered her protection in exchange for silence. "I struck names, Jude. A man like me has to carry at least one thing all the way across himself. This was mine. You were already carrying enough."

She had wanted, for eleven years, to say something to him at the end — some accounting between them, love or gratitude or grievance, some settling of what they had been to each other on either bank of that river. What she had managed was to sit with him through the last evening while he filed his final letters, and to tell him that his list was in the book, every name, merged with hers and Bram's page and Wren's copies, and that no one reading it could tell which keeper had written which line. He had nodded slowly, like a man hearing a verdict he'd hoped for.

"Then it's done properly," he said. "Anonymous. The way the work should have been from the start."

At the railing of the new bridge, Jude rested her hand where his voice had come from — that stretch of air, that span of water. The stone was warm from the day. Somewhere below, a barge sounded its horn, twice, heading upriver with coal.

* * *

Bram met her at the Ashcombe end, as arranged, walking slowly the way he always walked now, favoring the leg the fever left him. He was gray at the temples and ran a laundry in town that employed four men, two of them veterans no one else would hire, and he still could not pass a burning barrel in the street without stopping until the smell passed. They had never spoken the whole truth between them. He had taken the page, vanished, nearly died getting it to Chaplain Wren, and come back half-dead to a workshop that had every reason to turn him out — and she had chosen to believe him, and the choosing had turned out to be the truest act of faith she ever performed, because the evidence had never once cleared him. Only the outcome had.

"You'll stay for supper," he said. Not a question. He had learned that from her, she suspected — the flat certainty that people must be fed whether they consent or not.

"After the bridge," she said.

"You've been on the bridge an hour."

"The bridge takes as long as it takes." She touched his arm. "Walk it with me. You've never walked it."

"I've no reason to cross," Bram said. "My life is on this side now."

"Neither have I. Come anyway."

They walked it together, the deserter's orderly and the keeper of the ledger, past the third pier where the boy had given up fishing and gone home, past the plaque with its Latin nobody read. At midspan Bram stopped and looked down at the water a long time.

"I knew a printer in Marlowe," he said. "Before. Before I informed. They hanged him for the rolls he printed." His voice was level, worn smooth like a stone he'd handled ten thousand times. "I've read the dedication page forty times. Forty times, Mrs. Dunmore. And every time I get to *those who carried the names*, I think: does it mean me? Can a man inform on a printer and then carry names and call it square?"

"No," Jude said.

He flinched. She had meant it to land.

"No," she said again, gentler. "Nothing squares it. That's not what the book is for. The book isn't forgiveness, Bram. It's just the names. Forgiveness is a different labor and you're the only one who can do yours." She looked at the water, where the leaves went by without hurrying. "But the carrying counts. It counts alongside everything. I won't let you strike yourself from the record any more than I'd let an officer do it."

Bram said nothing for a while. Then he laughed — short, surprised, wet-eyed. "You know what you are? You're the only quartermaster I ever obeyed."

"Supper," she said. "And you'll set the table, since you're so fond of orders."

* * *

She stayed in Ashcombe three days. On the second evening she wrote to Elias, sitting at Bram's kitchen table under a lamp while Bram washed dishes behind her, and told him about the bridge — the carter, the boy, the bronze plate, the way the river looked from midspan, low and green and utterly uninterested in what it had done to them. She told him she had read the dedication aloud at the head of it, alone, quietly, the way you read a thing into the place it belongs. She told him about Halloway's porch and Bram's question, though he knew both stories already; some stories you retell not for information but because saying them again in a new place proves they survived the move.

His reply came by the week's end, and as always it carried printed ornaments — this time a border of acorns along the margin, inked from a block he'd cut himself, because his frost-scarred hands couldn't manage fine engraving anymore but could manage acorns. The letter said, in part:

*I walked our bridge too, in August. Did I tell you? Probably I told you. Forgive me if I repeat myself; at fifty-one I am becoming a man who repeats himself, which I understand is the fate of all printers and most husbands-to-be.*

(They were not married. They had discussed it every year for nine years, usually by letter, usually in autumn, and every year the distance had argued its case and they had let it win — not from doubt, but because what they had built between Ashcombe and Correnvale seemed to require the river between them the way a press requires its platen. Something to press against. Something to make the impression deep.)

*I stood at the middle and thought about the night the ice spoke. Do you remember the sound? Everyone says artillery, but it wasn't. Artillery wants something. The ice didn't want anything. It was just enormous and indifferent and going to pieces, and we were on it, and we went across anyway. I have decided that is the whole of what I believe about anything: the ice will break; cross anyway; carry what matters strapped to your chest.*

*The second edition is nearly set. Wren's people want five hundred more for the southern parishes. My hands complain but they complain the way old workers complain — faithfully. Send me your corrections by the feast of St. Martin and I'll pull the first sheet the week after. And Jude —*

Here the pen had paused; she could see the hesitation in the ink, a small pooling where he'd rested the nib.

*— I have been thinking about last pages. Books want endings the way rivers want the sea. Yours doesn't have one yet. I noticed it at the proof stage: the roll ends with the last name entered and nothing after. No colophon. No closing line. As though the book is still waiting for something. I don't know what. But I think you do, and I think you've known for years, and I think you should write it before either of us is too old to hold the pen steady. Whatever it is. Whoever it's for.*

She read that paragraph four times by Bram's kitchen window, with the acorn border catching the lamplight, and felt something open in her chest the way the seams opened in the ice — not dread, exactly. Recognition. Because he was right. She had known for years. There was a line she had been carrying the way she'd carried the ledger over the black water: close to the chest, weighing more every year, waiting for solid ground.

* * *

On the morning she left, she went back to the bridge alone, early, before the carts. Mist stood on the river in columns. The bronze plate wore beads of condensation like the whole structure was breathing. She knelt — her knees objected, fifty-six years old and entitled to — and read the dedication once more, and then she took out the book and did what she had come all these miles to do.

She turned to the last page. The roll of names ended where it had always ended, with a soldier whose name she'd taken by candlelight in the flooded ward while the provosts' boots came down the corridor — Private Aldous Merrin, aged nineteen, who had asked her twice whether his mother would be told, and whom she had promised, and whose promise had held for eleven years because she built a book around keeping it. Beneath his entry, blank space. Beneath the blank space, nothing.

She uncapped her ink — the same iron-gall recipe Elias mixed, the same brown-black that had outlasted fire, flood, requisition, and time — and she dipped the pen, and she held it over the page, and her hand was steadier than it had any right to be.

Because she finally understood what the last page was for. Not the dead. The dead were done; the dead were carried; the dead had their rock now, cut and dressed and laid bank to bank. The last page was for the living — for the ones who stayed on the ice when it began to sing, who fed strangers at gunpoint of hunger, who chose the heavier burden and called it love. The record could not end with the ones who were lost. It had to end with the proof that some of them made it, and that what they made it *with* was worth writing down.

Elias's voice came back to her across eleven years and one wide river: *Whatever it is. Whoever it's for.*

She set the nib to the paper, and outside the mist began to lift off the water, stone by stone, pier by pier, the whole bridge emerging into the light as if it had always been there — and she wrote the first word.
