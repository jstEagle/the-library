## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The summons came at half past four in the afternoon, delivered by a boy whose ears had gone the color of raw meat in the cold.

"Keeper Voss wants you," the boy said, breath steaming through the gap where his front teeth should have been. "The master clock's stopped. Stopped dead, he says, like something killed it."

Sebastian Marchetti set down his loupe and looked at the boy for a long moment, because that was what you did with news like that. A clock did not stop dead. A clock slowed, faltered, drifted — it grew old and honest about it. But stopped dead was another thing entirely, and the master clock at the observatory had not stopped in forty years, not through the flood of '79, not through the fire that took the east wing of the customs house, not even on the night Sebastian's parents' boat went down and every bell in the city rang out of grief or habit or some failure no clockmaker ever found.

"Tell Keeper Voss I'll come tonight," Sebastian said. "After dark. The cold is bad for the works if I open the case while the sun's still on the tower."

The boy nodded and ran, and Sebastian stood a moment longer in the warmth of Marchetti & Nephew — though the sign said only *Marchetti*, Aldo having never gotten around to repainting it after taking Sebastian in eleven years ago — and listened to the small chorus of the shop. Forty-one clocks ticking in imperfect agreement, each one a little stubborn, a little off from its neighbor, so that the sound came to the ear not as time but as weather. He loved that sound more than he could say, which was why he never said it. Some things went thin when you spoke them aloud, like ice underfoot.

He packed his case the way he did everything: carefully, invisibly, without flourish. Screwdrivers wrapped in flannel. Tweezers, two kinds. The brass oiler with the needle tip. A lamp, spare wicks, a tin of tallow candles because the observatory's electricity was a rumor more than a fact. Last, almost as an afterthought, he wrapped a heel of bread and a wedge of cheese in paper and tucked them beside the tools, because he had learned long ago that repairs kept their own hours, and a man who worked through the night ought not to arrive home starving at dawn and wake the whole house with his chewing.

"Aldo!" he called toward the back room. "I'm wanted at the observatory."

His uncle appeared in the doorway wiping his hands on a rag, a big man gone soft at the middle, with forearms still like dock rope and a face that seemed permanently amused by some joke told twenty years ago. That face changed now. Not to worry, exactly. Sebastian watched it happen and could not have named what it became — something quickened, something attentive, the look of a man hearing his own name called from a crowd.

"The master clock?" Aldo said.

"Stopped dead, they say."

"Hm." Aldo folded the rag into quarters, then eighths, his fingers working without instruction. "You'll want your heavy coat. And take the river road, not the hill — the wind up there will strip the paint off you." He paused. "And Sebastian. If anything should seem... irregular up there. Anything at all. You come home first and tell me before you touch it."

"Before I touch it?"

"Some mechanisms are like patients. You don't go cutting until you know what's underneath." Aldo smiled then, the old easy smile, and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller man. "Go on. Make us proud. It'll be the escapement. It's always the escapement."

But his uncle's eyes stayed on him as he left, and Sebastian felt them between his shoulder blades all the way down the street, warm as a hand.

* * *

The river road was a corridor of blue dusk and groaning ice. The cold had come down off the mountains three days ago and settled over the city like a lid on a pot, and now the river lay locked from bank to bank, white and ridged, with here and there a dark seam where the current still argued beneath. Men crossed it on foot with lanterns, small and careful as figures on a painted plate. Sebastian walked with his head down and his collar up and thought, as he always thought on this road, of his parents.

Eleven years, and the memory had worn smooth as a river stone. His father laughing in the boat. His mother's hat blowing off and his father catching it one-handed without looking, because he'd done it a hundred times. Then the storm, the week after, the one nobody predicted — and the river giving back only one oar and half a hull. Sebastian had been nine. He remembered less of the drowning than of the afterward: the house going quiet, the neighbors' casseroles, and then Uncle Aldo arriving with two trunks and a voice too loud for the rooms, filling the silence the way a man fills a hole in a wall — quickly, roughly, with whatever is at hand.

Aldo had fed him. That was the plain truth at the bottom of everything, the bedrock under all the rest. When Sebastian woke screaming in the night, Aldo was there with cocoa he'd burned but brought anyway. When the boys at school said orphan like a disease, Aldo taught him to say *apprentice* instead, and made the word mean something. When Sebastian showed his first gift — the uncanny patience, the fingers that could feel a bent tooth in a gear train by touch alone — Aldo had wept openly in the shop and told every customer within earshot for a month.

So yes. Sebastian owed him. He knew the exact weight of that debt the way he knew the weight of any mechanism: precisely, to the grain. And he paid it gladly, in long hours and unasked questions, in being the nephew who asked nothing and noticed nothing and wanted nothing beyond the bench and the work and the ticking chorus of the shop. It was a good arrangement. It was a contented life. He had told himself this so many times, in so many identical words, that the sentence had worn smooth too, and lately — he could not say when it started — the words rang slightly false against his ear, the way a cracked bell rings.

He pushed the thought away and climbed the observatory hill by the back stairs, where the wind did indeed try to strip the paint off him, and let himself in through the tradesman's door with the key Keeper Voss had sent.

* * *

Inside, the tower breathed around him. He knew that breath; every clockmaker knows it. A great clock is not furniture, it is an animal — it inhales through the weights descending, exhales through the escapement's tick, and its blood is oil and gravity. But tonight the animal was silent, and the silence had a texture to it, dense as felt. Sebastian stood in the dark of the stairwell and felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

Lamplight spilled from the clock room above. He climbed toward it.

Keeper Voss was a small man shaped like a question mark, seventy if he was a day, wrapped in so many coats he seemed assembled rather than dressed. He sat on a stool beneath the great clock case with a mug of something steaming, and he looked, Sebastian thought, like a man keeping vigil over a corpse.

"There he is," Voss said, with relief he didn't bother hiding. "Marchetti's boy. Your uncle said you'd come."

"My uncle spoke to you?"

"Sent word ahead, didn't he? An hour ago. Boy on a bicycle, half-frozen." Voss shrugged inside his coats. "Said his nephew was the best hands in the city and I was to give you anything you needed and ask no questions."

That sounded like Aldo — generous, commanding, and just slightly strange all at once. Sebastian filed it away without examining it, the way he filed everything, and turned to the clock.

She was a beauty even in death. Nine feet of walnut case, glass-fronted, the dial above pale as a moon. Behind the glass, the pendulum hung motionless, and the great brass bob — polished by a century of admiring hands — held the lamplight like a closed eye. Sebastian opened the case door and put his ear to the movement, though he already knew what he would hear. Nothing. Still, you listened. Manners mattered, even to machines.

"Dead since when?"

"Quarter past midnight last night. To the minute — well." Voss huffed. "To the minute according to her own last testimony. She simply quit. No slowing, no stutter. One tick and then none, like a man mid-sentence."

Sebastian began at the top and worked down, the way his father had taught him before he'd taught him anything else: *the head first, boy, always the head, because trouble rolls downhill.* The going train was clean. The escapement — Aldo would be pleased — was sound, jeweled bearings bright, pallets true. He checked the suspension spring, the crutch, the beat. All correct. All correct. He descended through the gear train tooth by tooth, feeling each wheel turn under his fingertips, listening with his skin the way other people listened with their ears.

It took him two hours to find it, and when he found it he sat back on his heels and stared.

Someone had been inside the clock.

Not broken in — *let in*. The case door's hinge screws bore fresh scratches, bright metal under old patina, and the movement's retaining clip had been removed and replaced by someone competent, someone who understood clocks, someone who had taken out the third wheel of the striking train and — this was the part that made the hair rise on Sebastian's arms — put it back. Almost perfectly. Except for one tooth, filed down by a fraction of a millimeter, enough to let the wheel slip its engagement once every revolution, enough to stop the train at exactly quarter past midnight and never before and never after.

Sabotage, done beautifully. Done by a craftsman.

"Found it?" Voss asked behind him.

"Yes," Sebastian said slowly. "Worn tooth. Third wheel. I can cut a new one tomorrow — I'll need to take the old one for a pattern."

"Take what you like." The keeper drained his mug and stood, joints crackling like green wood in a fire. "I'm for bed. Lock the door when you go — and lad." He paused at the stairwell, silhouetted against the light. "Your uncle said you were the best hands in the city. He also said—" the old man frowned, searching for the words, "—he said you were a boy who knew how to see a thing and say nothing. His words. *Say nothing.* I took it for modesty."

Voss thumped away down the stairs. Sebastian knelt alone before the open clock, the dead wheel cupped in his palm like a coin, and told himself there was a simple explanation, there was always a simple explanation, and reached for his tools.

Outside, far below, the ice on the river boomed once — a long, deep report, like a door closing in an empty house — and the sound climbed the tower and faded, and the night went on being the coldest night of the year.

He worked until the candles burned low. Toward dawn, stiff and salt-eyed, he gathered his case and doused the lamp, and was halfway down the dark stairwell when he heard it: a sound from the direction of the observatory's front door, faint under the wind.

A knock. Small. The kind of knock a very small fist makes.
