## 2. A Knock at the Observatory Door

The sound came again — two small taps, then a pause, then nothing, as though whoever knocked had spent their whole courage on the second try and now stood listening to see if the mountain of stone above them would answer.

Sebastian stopped on the stairs with his case in his hand. The wind pressed against the tower's flanks and moaned through the louvres, and somewhere below him the city slept under its crust of snow, chimneys smoking, dogs dreaming, all of it ticking along like a movement in a watch that no one remembered winding. Nobody climbed this hill at dawn in January. Nobody climbed it at any hour without a summons, and summons came by day, by messenger, in envelopes.

He went down anyway.

The observatory door was oak older than his grandfather, iron-banded, swollen shut with cold most winters. He unhooked the lantern from its bracket, thumbed the striker until flame caught, and pulled the door open against its own reluctance.

Cold poured in like water finding a crack.

On the step, curled into the shallow lee of the doorway where the stone turned just enough to blunt the wind, was a child.

She could not have been more than six or seven. She lay on her side, knees drawn to her chest, one mittened hand still raised as though the knock had cost her everything she had left. Snow had gathered along her back in a thin white ridge, the kind that forms only on things that have been still for hours — on fence rails, on sleeping birds, on things that do not move. Her breath came slow and shallow, each exhale blooming faintly silver and dying.

She wore a coat too large for her, wool gone grey at the seams, and boots with one lace missing. Pinned to the front of her coat, high on the chest where it could not be missed, was an envelope.

Sebastian crouched. The lantern light found her face: chapped lips, lashes furred with frost, a smudge of soot on one cheekbone. A girl. Small enough that he could have carried her in one arm and his tools in the other.

*Forget her face.*

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and made no sense at all, and yet it sat in him with the certainty of something read rather than imagined. He shook it off. His fingers were already moving, professional, checking — pressing two of them beneath her jaw where the pulse runs thin in children. It was there. Slow, but there. Her skin felt like paper left out overnight.

"Hello," he said. Nothing. "Can you hear me?"

Nothing again, except the wind, which chose that moment to shove past him into the entryway and scatter the snow at the threshold in a fine hissing veil.

He should fetch someone. The keeper's cottage was down the hill; Father Anselm kept a stove going at all hours and had raised four daughters of his own. There were proper channels for found children. There were constables. There was an order to things, and Sebastian Marchetti had built his entire life inside that order — the summoned repair, the completed work, the payment, the walk home, the kettle, the bed. He did not deviate. Deviation was how people drowned, his uncle liked to say, tapping the table twice. Rivers freeze flat and honest, boy, and then they don't.

But the keeper's cottage was down a quarter mile of drifted path, and the child had been here long enough for snow to build a spine along her shoulders, and every minute the argument took was a minute her blood got slower and thicker in her veins.

He looked up the stairwell behind him, toward the clock room, where the great regulator ticked with the patience of geology and the air was warm from a night of lamps and labor. Then down at the child.

"Oh, hell," he said quietly, because Uncle Aldo was not there to hear him say it, and lifted her.

She weighed almost nothing. That was the thing that undid him first — not the frost, not the envelope, but the weight of her, or the absence of it, like lifting a bundle of kindling that turned out to be feathers. Her head lolled against his shoulder and her frozen mitten bumped his collarbone and she made no sound at all, and Sebastian Marchetti, who had not held anything living in longer than he cared to count, carried her up the dark stairs two at a time with his heart going hard against his ribs.

The clock room held its warmth like a held breath. Stone walls a yard thick, the regulator's brass pendulum swinging in its long slow arc, the smell of oil and hot metal and candle stubs — a smell Sebastian loved the way other men loved bread baking. He cleared the workbench with one sweep of his forearm, screwdrivers ringing onto the floor and being ignored, and laid her down on the folded canvas drop-cloth where the big escapement usually rested.

Then he worked.

Not on clocks, for once, but the principles were the same. Something seized is not broken; it is starved. Warmth first, gently — he fed the little iron stove until it glowed, dragged it close but not too close, the way you'd warm a frozen mainspring over a flame and never *in* it. He unwound the frost-stiff scarf from her neck. He chafed her hands between his own, those hands trained to a delicacy that could balance a pallet fork on a pinhead, and felt the cold recede out of her fingers degree by degree like tide going out.

Her eyes opened once, partway. Grey, he thought, or blue-grey, the color of the river ice at dusk. They found his face and considered it without fear and without recognition, the way one considers a piece of furniture in an unfamiliar room, and then closed again.

"You're all right," he said, not knowing if it was true, saying it because the words needed saying aloud in that room. "You're all right."

She slept. Really slept now, the rigid sleep of cold giving way to the loose sleep of safety, her breathing deepening, one hand curling shut around the fabric of the drop-cloth.

And the envelope stayed pinned to her coat, and he could not stop looking at it.

It was good paper. That was the first wrong thing. Cheap paper tears, yellows, goes soft with damp — he handled letters daily, invoices and parts lists and the observatory's correspondence, and he knew paper the way a farrier knows hooves. This was heavy stock, cream-colored, cut clean. The seal was wax, dark red, stamped with a device he couldn't make out from across the bench. And pinned through both flap and coat with a steel pin, deliberate, angled so the letter rode over her heart like a small breastplate.

A letter that traveled with a child. A letter that mattered more than the child's comfort, since whoever dressed her had pinned it where it wouldn't be dropped, wouldn't be lost, wouldn't be left behind even if everything else about her was.

Sebastian wiped his hands on his apron and stood over her for a long moment.

He should read the address at least. If it named some house in the city, some street, some family waiting and searching, then every hour he delayed was an hour of someone else's grief. That was the decent logic of it, and he clung to the decent logic the way a man on ice clings to the center of the pond.

He bent close and tilted the envelope toward the lamp.

No address. No name. No direction at all on the face of it — only, written small and level across the seal-side fold, in ink gone brownish with cold, five words:

*For the apprentice who repairs.*

His breath stopped.

There was exactly one apprentice who repaired, on this hill, on this night, in this room. Which meant the letter had been meant for him before he had ever seen her, which meant the child on his workbench had been sent — addressed, delivered, *posted*, like a parcel requiring signature — and the sender knew his trade, knew his post, knew the coldest night of the year would find him alone in this tower with the master clock.

*Forget her face.* There it was again, that phrase surfacing from nowhere, and this time it brought a voice with it, low and familiar, and Sebastian pushed it down hard.

He straightened. He paced once to the window, where the sky was going from black to bruised grey over the eastern ridges, dawn coming up like a decision being made slowly. Below, the river ran white and silent between its banks, holding its breath the way rivers do before they speak. Somewhere under that ice moved the current, patient, carrying whatever it carried.

When he turned back, the girl had not moved, and the envelope had not changed, and neither had he — and yet everything in the room had shifted a half-degree, the way a whole mechanism shifts when one tooth, one single tooth, wears wrong.

He fetched the blanket from the keeper's chair by the transit instrument, the one Aldo had knitted nothing of and the keeper used when napping through the small hours, and tucked it around her. He set water on to warm. He told himself he would wait until she woke, ask her name, ask where home was, hand her and her letter over to the proper authorities by noon with the whole affair concluded neatly, filed away, forgotten.

*Forget her face.*

He caught himself rubbing at his own chest, as if the words had been pinned there too.

While the water heated, he did what he always did when his mind refused to sit still: he worked. There was a repeater carriage from the west turret clock on the side bench, its snail cam worn to a shadow, and he took it up and began to dress the worn face with a fine file, letting the rhythm of it steady him — stroke, turn, stroke, turn — the oldest prayer he knew.

The light strengthened. The pendulum swung. The child breathed.

And at the bottom of the envelope, where he hadn't looked before, where the lamplight had finally reached at the right angle, there was one line more, smaller than the rest, written in a different hand entirely — hurried, slanting, pressed so hard the nib had torn the paper:

*Open it, and you will wish you hadn't.*

Sebastian set down the file.

He looked at the sleeping girl, at the frost melting out of her lashes now, at the soot-smudge cheek and the too-large coat and the fist still curled in the blanket. Outside, far down the hill, the city was beginning to wake; he could hear the first tram bell, faint, like something calling from another country.

He picked up the letter-opener from the bench — bone-handled, a gift from Uncle Aldo on his fourteenth birthday — and slid it under the seal.

The wax cracked with a sound very like ice breaking.
