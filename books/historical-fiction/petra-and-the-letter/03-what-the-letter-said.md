## 3. What the Letter Said

The letter weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing Sebastian noticed, turning it over in his hands at the workbench while Petra slept on in the nest of blankets he'd made her among the gear cases — a child no heavier than a clock pendulum, and a letter lighter still, and yet the two of them together had made the whole observatory feel suddenly, dangerously occupied.

He set it down. Picked it up again. Set it down once more, as if it might behave like any other object he understood — as if weight were the only honesty an object ever told you.

Outside, the dawn came up grey and thin through the frost-ferned windows. The master clock ticked above him with its repaired heart, steady as a conscience, and Sebastian found himself listening to it the way a drowning man listens to a bell buoy: for proof that some things went on being true.

*Forget her face,* the pinned note had said. Well. He had already failed that instruction thoroughly. The girl's face was the kind that stayed — chapped cheeks, lashes crusted with ice-melt, a small stubborn crease between her brows even in sleep, as though she dreamed of arguing with someone and intended to win.

But the letter was another matter. The letter had been addressed not to the observatory, not to the city, but to him. To *Sebastian Marchetti, apprentice, son of Elias Marchetti.* Someone out there in the frozen dark had known his name, his father's name, and had carried a sleeping child across half the city to lay both at his door.

You did not forget a thing like that. You opened it.

He worked his thumbnail under the seal — black wax, pressed with a stamp he didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a single vertical line, like a coin seen edge-on, like a door closed. The wax cracked with a sound very small and very loud.

* * *

Inside were two sheets. The first was written in a fine, hurried hand, ink blotted where the writer's pen had stabbed down too hard, and Sebastian read it standing, then sitting, then standing again without any memory of moving.

*If you are reading this, then I am past helping anyone, and my daughter is yours by default, which is more than she deserves and less than she needs.*

*Her name is Petra. She is seven years old. Her mother put her on the ice road before midnight with instructions to follow the river until she saw the observatory light, because your tower is the one place in this city certain men will not look — they think it holds nothing but time, and time has never been worth stealing.*

*I was a partner, once, in something I will not excuse. So was your uncle. Understand me clearly, boy: Aldo Marchetti was never merely kind to take you in. He took you because your father knew too much and drowned anyway, and because a dead man's apprentice nephew asks no questions about money that arrives wrapped in oilcloth.*

*Aldo is the paymaster. Not a courier, not a lookout. The paymaster. Every contraband that moves beneath this city moves through the crypts below the clock towers — brandy under the bell chambers, silk in the weight shafts, ledgers kept in a language of gear-ratios only clockmen can read. Your uncle wrote that language. Your uncle taught it to me. And when the magistrates began asking questions last autumn, Aldo sold my name to save his own, and the men he sold it to came to my workshop on a Tuesday night and I have not slept since.*

*I do not ask you to believe me. I ask you to read what follows and remember that your father died owing three months' wages from a man who could buy and sell the street he lived on.*

*Forget her face, I said on the note outside. That was her mother's instruction, and I have broken it already by writing to you at all. But faces are the least of it now. If you are half the clockman your father was, you will understand what I am sending you: not a girl. A record. Everything she saw that night is in her head, filed and ticking, and there are men in this city who would stop every clock in the harbor to keep it from striking.*

*Protect her or don't. But do not give her back to anyone who comes smiling.*

It was signed simply: *Tomas Vey.*

Sebastian read the name four times. Tomas Vey. His father's name was in the second line of the letter and this stranger's name was in the last, but it was the middle of the letter that had stopped his breath — *Aldo is the paymaster* — and he stood in the cold observatory with the words ringing in him like a cracked bell, off-true, impossible, and yet somehow, horribly, resonant. Because some part of him — some small, long-buried part that had counted Uncle Aldo's coins over the years and always found a few more than an honest repair shop should yield — that part was not surprised at all. That part had been keeping its own silent ledger for fifteen years.

The second sheet was shorter. Older paper, different hand — rounder, more careful, the hand of a man who had learned to write numbers before letters.

*Boy,* it began, and Sebastian's stomach turned over, because he knew that hand. He had watched it move across repair tickets his whole childhood. *Boy, if you are reading this, then Vey got his letter out and I am too late to burn it.*

*Whatever he told you, hear the truth from the only family you have left. Yes, money moved through the towers. Yes, I kept the books. I did it for the shop — for YOU — after your parents' boat went down and there was nothing but debt and a hungry nephew. One compromise, then another. That is how it goes; no one decides to be wicked, they only fail to decide otherwise.*

*Vey betrayed us all before the end. Whatever he claims I did to him, ask yourself who is writing from hiding and who is writing from his own bench.*

*Burn this. Burn his letter. Take the child to St. Brigid's on Foundling Lane and say nothing to anyone, and come home, and we will speak of it never.*

*— A.*

Two letters. Two truths, each claiming the other was the forgery. Sebastian laid them side by side on the bench and looked from one hand to the other and felt the ground shift beneath everything he believed — not collapse, nothing so clean as collapse, but that slow lateral sliding a man feels when the floor of a tower sways in high wind and he must trust the structure or fall.

The flashback came on him unbidden, sudden as a struck match:

* * *

He was eleven again, and it was the winter after the drowning, and Uncle Aldo had woken him past midnight with a lamp and a coat held out.

"Come along, Seb. Can't sleep either, eh?"

They had walked to the harbor through falling snow, and Aldo had talked the whole way — Aldo always talked when he was nervous, filling silence the way a careless workman fills a joint with too much glue.

"Your father," Aldo said, "was the finest hand in this city, and don't let anyone tell you different. Finest, and the most stubborn. A man like that sees a ledger that doesn't balance and he won't rest till he knows why." He had stopped at the rail then, looking down at the black water, the ice forming in plates along the pilings. "I tried to warn him, Seb. I want you to know that. Whatever people say — and people say things, they will, they always do — I tried to pull him back aboard before the weather turned."

The boy Sebastian had taken his uncle's hand, which was trembling, and had thought: *he is cold.* He had thought: *grief shakes a man like that.* He had stood beside Aldo at the rail and loved him so much it hurt, because this man had taken him in, fed him, taught him escapements and patience, and asked nothing in return except that Sebastian become invisible — careful, quiet, unremarkable, a boy who noticed everything and repeated nothing.

"You'll mind what I tell you now," Aldo had said, crouching to his eye level, snow gathering on his shoulders, his eyes very bright and very tired. "In this trade, the honest ones drown and the careful ones float. Be careful, lad. Be careful, and you'll live to be old, and old men get to be forgiven anything."

*Be careful, and you'll live to be old.*

Sebastian, twenty-six years old, stood in the observatory holding two letters and finally heard the sentence whole.

* * *

Behind him, among the gear cases, a small voice said: "Is it morning?"

Petra sat up in her blanket nest, hair wild, one cheek printed red with the weave of the wool. Her eyes went straight to the letters in his hands with a child's terrible directness, the way birds go to glass.

"That's Papa's handwriting," she said. "The slanted one."

Sebastian's throat closed. "You can read?"

"Mama taught me. Papa says — said — reading is the only lock nobody picks." She slid off the gear case and came toward the bench, bare feet silent on the stone, and stopped just short of touching the pages, as if she too sensed their weight. "Did Papa write to you? Did he say when he's coming?"

The question went through him like a drift pin through soft wood. Sebastian crouched to her level, folding the letters away behind his back, and lied — badly, he suspected, but children forgive bad lying when the alternative frightens them.

"He wrote to ask me to take care of you," Sebastian said. "And I mean to. That's the truest thing in either of these pages."

She studied him with her father's directness, whatever else her father might have been. Then, gravely, as though concluding a negotiation: "I can wind things. Mama says I'm good at small mechanisms."

"We'll see about that." He straightened, and the master clock above them struck six, the note rolling out over the frozen river, over the city, over the crypts beneath the towers where, if one word of Tomas Vey's letter was true, contraband slept in the dark beside the bones of the men who built the bells.

Six strokes. Somewhere across the water, Uncle Aldo would be lighting his stove, unaware — or entirely aware — that his careful, invisible nephew now held his confession in two hands and a witness in his house.

Sebastian looked at the letters, and at the girl, and at the frost creeping silver across the window where the river ran black and locked beneath the ice.

He could burn them. Both of them. Take her to St. Brigid's on Foundling Lane, exactly as instructed, and go home, and speak of it never.

Or he could walk down the hill to the Marchetti bench and ask his uncle one question, face to face, and watch his eyes when he asked it — because Aldo Marchetti could forge any hand in the city, but Sebastian had been reading that particular face since he was eleven years old, and there was one thing it had never once managed to hide.

He put on his coat.

"I'm going out for an hour," he told Petra. "There's bread in the tin, and the big clock likes to be listened to. Will you be all right with the gears?"

"They're better company than most people," she said solemnly, and he almost laughed, and did not, because laughing would have loosened something in him that needed to stay tight for what came next.

At the observatory door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back once — at the small figure already climbing onto the workbench stool, at the two letters lying on the bench like a split rail over a chasm — and then he stepped out into the cold and shut the door behind him.

Down the hill, through the waking streets, toward the shop where the man who raised him was surely counting money wrapped in oilcloth.

One question. Watch the eyes.
