## 4. The Face He Was Told to Forget

Petra woke inside a heart.

That was her first thought — that she was small enough now to sleep inside a great brass heart, and it beat all around her, *tick-tick-tick*, steady and patient, warming the dark the way a stove warms a room. She kept her eyes shut a long while and listened, because Papa had taught her that when you wake somewhere strange, you listen before you look. *Ears are honest, Piccola. Eyes will lie to you if you let them.*

Her ears said: gears. Hundreds of them, turning somewhere above and below and beside her, wrapped in oil-smell and dust and the faint sweet ghost of apples.

Then she remembered, all at once, the way cold remembers itself in your bones: Mama's hands on her shoulders in the dark of their own hallway. Mama's breath against her ear, fast as a bird's wing. *Run. Don't stop running until you see the tower with the round white face. Give this to whoever opens the door. Then forget my face.*

*Forget my face.* As if she could. As if anyone could.

Petra opened her eyes.

She was lying on folded sacking behind a wall of wheels — no, not a wall, a *cage*, a lattice of brass rods and gear teeth taller than her father's workbench at home, and beyond it a gray window high up near a ceiling lost in shadow. She was warm. Someone had put a coat over her — a man's coat, too big, smelling of metal polish and lamp smoke. Her stomach growled, loud in the ticking hush, and she remembered: bread. A boy had given her bread last night, or what she'd thought was last night. A boy with flour still on his sleeves even though he wasn't a baker, and eyes that looked at everything except her face, very carefully, the way people look away from something hot.

The boy had fed her by lamplight and then made her a bed among his machines, and Petra decided, with the serious gravity of seven years old, that she trusted him. Not entirely. But the way you trust a bridge — enough to cross.

She sat up. The coat slid off her shoulders. And there he was, coming around the gear-cage with another piece of bread and an apple cut into slices, because — she would learn this about him later, and it would matter — Sebastian Marchetti never handed anyone a whole apple. He always cut things into pieces small enough that they couldn't be taken away from you all at once. He had learned that from losing things.

"You slept through the bells," he said. "All four quarters. Most people can't."

"I wasn't most people," Petra said. "I was asleep." She took the apple slices first, counting them — five — then the bread. "Your clock is loud."

"He's not mine." Something moved across the boy's face, quick as a fish under ice. "He belongs to the observatory. I only keep him alive."

"Is he sick?"

"He was. Badly. Like a fever in his escapement." He said it plainly, the way other boys might say *my dog had worms*, and Petra understood suddenly that this boy loved the clock the way she had loved her father's lathe — not a thing, a creature. That made him safe. People who loved machines were careful people. Papa used to say careless hands broke careful hearts.

"What's an escape-ment?" She chewed, watching him.

"It's the part that lets the time out. A little bit at a time." He crouched down across from her, folding his long arms on his knees. "Otherwise it all comes at once and ruins everything."

Petra thought about that for longer than she meant to. "Like screaming," she said finally. "Mama says grief is like that. You have to let it out in little pieces or it comes all at once and ruins everything."

The boy went very still. Somewhere above them a gear changed its mind about something, and the whole heart-of-brass shifted its rhythm, and in that shift Petra heard the boy breathe again.

"My name is Petra," she offered. "I know you're not supposed to tell strangers your name. But you gave me your coat, so you're only half a stranger."

"Sebastian," he said. Then, quickly, as if he'd been warned against it: "Marchetti."

The name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash at the bottom, listening, and when it came it was only a small cold sound: *Papa's letter is for a Marchetti. Papa wrote the name twice and pressed hard on the pen.*

"Mama told me to find the observatory," Petra said. "And give a letter to the person who opened the door. But—" She stopped. Here was the trouble, the snag her thoughts kept catching on, and she picked at it aloud because keeping secrets alone was heavy work. "The person who opened the door was you. But the note pinned to my coat says to give it to a grown-up. It says *the apprentice must not read it*. It says—" she screwed her eyes shut and recited, because Mama had made her say it eleven times on the walk, until it lived in her mouth like a prayer, "*forget the girl's face. Forget where she came from. That is how she lives.*"

When she opened her eyes, Sebastian had gone the color of candle wax.

* * *

They sat a while with only the clock talking. Petra finished her apple, core and all, because Mama said waste was a sin against whoever grew it, and studied the boy while he pretended to study a wrench.

He was older than her — twelve, maybe thirteen, though he held himself like someone much older trying to be smaller. His hands were nicked all over, little white scars crossing each other like the lines on a map of somewhere difficult. There was oil under his fingernails and a burn mark on his wrist shaped like a crescent moon. When he thought she wasn't looking, he touched his breast pocket, where the letter lay, over and over, the way you touch a tooth that aches.

Finally Petra said, "You've read it."

It wasn't a question. He didn't insult her by pretending otherwise.

"Some of it," he said hoarsely. "Not— Petra, some of it isn't for children."

"I'm not most children either." She brushed crumbs from her skirt with great dignity. "I carried a letter across the whole city in the snow. Nobody carried me."

"That's exactly why you shouldn't have had to."

"Well, I did." She lifted her chin. This was the fierce part, the part Mama had built into her without meaning to, all those nights of *stand straight, Piccola, stand straight*. "So you can't put me back outside it. Whatever's in the letter, I walked here carrying it. That makes it partly mine."

Sebastian looked at her then — really looked, straight at her face, breaking whatever rule the note had set — and Petra saw him decide something. She saw it happen, the way you see a scale tip. His jaw set. His hands stopped touching the pocket.

"Tell me about your father's workshop," he said quietly.

And so she did, because that was the trade: his truth for hers, and hers came out in fragments, the way Mama had left it with her — broken on purpose, packed small for running.

* * *

"There was a bench under the north window," Petra said, "with the good lamp. Papa let me sweep the brass filings, and they smelled like— like pennies and rain. He made instruments. Beautiful ones. Barometers with ships carved on them, and a telescope once, a real one, for the university men. He said—" her voice wobbled and she pressed on, fierce, "he said a well-made thing tells the truth about its maker. That's why bad men liked his workshop. Because everything in it was honest, so nobody watched the dishonesty going on underneath."

Sebastian didn't move. But she felt him lean in anyway, the way you feel warmth come nearer to a fire.

"The cellar had a false floor. I wasn't ever supposed to go down, but I did once, and Papa caught me, and he didn't scold. He just went white and said, *Petra, some doors are load-bearing. You open them, the whole house comes down.*" She hugged her knees. "I thought he meant the actual house."

"And then?"

"Men started coming at night." Her voice dropped to match the memory, which had always been whispered, which had never once been spoken at full volume. "After the lamps were out. Three knocks, then two, then one. Papa would go down and be gone hours and come up smelling of river water and other men's cigars. Mama cried in the kitchen where she thought I couldn't hear. She cried with her back straight, standing up, so I'd think she was just washing dishes badly."

The gears turned. The gears always turned; that was the mercy of this place. Nothing in here demanded she finish.

"One night in autumn," Petra said, "a man came who didn't do the knocks right. Four knocks. Papa went down anyway, and there was shouting, and a crash like the lathe falling, and Mama grabbed me out of bed with no coat and stood me in the coal cellar and said, *whatever you hear, you're asleep, you're dreaming, you're asleep.*" Petra's breath fogged faintly in the cold air of the tower. "But I heard Papa say a name. Through the floor. He said it twice, like he couldn't believe it, like the word was a knife somebody had handed him handle-first."

She looked at Sebastian.

"Aldo," she said. "He said *Aldo*."

The boy flinched as if she'd struck him. The wrench slipped from his fingers and rang on the iron plate below, one bright wrong note in the clock's patient music, and for a moment Petra saw him not as the tall careful boy who fed her, but as a machine with something jammed in its works — a gear with a splinter of truth wedged between its teeth, grinding, unable to stop and unable to turn.

"That's my uncle's name," he said at last. Barely a whisper.

"I know," said Petra. "It was on the letter too. I read it upside-down while you were sleeping, before you woke up and hid me. Papa's handwriting was on the front. Papa's *seal* was on the back — the ship with the broken mast, pressed in red wax. I'd know it anywhere. He let me press it once, for practice." Her chin trembled and she locked it still. "So you see, it isn't just a letter. It's Papa's hand. Papa's seal. Papa's voice. He's dead now — Mama said so, or the way she said *run* said so — and a dead man's letter doesn't lie, Sebastian. Dead men can't afford to."

Sebastian stared at her, and Petra watched the ground shift under him; she knew that exact feeling, had worn it for three days now like a coat that didn't fit. Everything he believed, tipping over sideways, slow as the big pendulum swinging above them both.

"The men who came after," she said. "There'll be more. One of them I'd know anywhere — he used to bring us oranges at midwinter, Papa's partner, with a laugh like a dog barking. If he's looking for me, it isn't because he misses oranges."

Somewhere below them, far off across the frozen city, a bell began to ring — not the observatory's own measured voice but a stranger's, urgent, repeated. Sebastian stood up so fast he knocked his head on a gear-shaft, and didn't seem to feel it.

"Someone's at the yard gate," he breathed. "That's the keeper's bell. Nobody rings it at this hour unless—"

He looked at Petra. Petra looked at the letter-shaped lump in his pocket.

"Hide me," she said, and her voice didn't shake at all, which surprised her more than anyone.
