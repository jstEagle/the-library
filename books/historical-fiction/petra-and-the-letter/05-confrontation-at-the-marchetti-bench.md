## 5. Confrontation at the Marchetti Bench

Sebastian did not sleep. He sat on his stool behind the workbench with the letter open before him, reading it by lamplight until the words wore grooves in his mind, the way water wears stone. *The paymaster is Aldo Marchetti. The crypt beneath the Clockmakers' Guild hall. The girl is the daughter of Tomas Reiner, whom Marchetti sold to save his own skin.*

Outside, the observatory's great dome ticked against the cold, iron contracting, and beneath it the master clock kept its patient count. Sebastian had spent four years learning to hear that clock the way other men heard their own hearts. Tonight it sounded like a stranger.

Petra slept in the narrow cot behind the tool wall, buried under two of his own blankets, her breath small and even. He had told her the letter was a bill of lading. He had lied to a child with a face like a struck bell, and the lie had come easily, which frightened him more than the letter did.

At first light he banked the fire, pinned the blankets close around her, and set the letter inside his coat, over his heart, where it sat like a coal.

"I'm going out," he told her. "You don't open the door. You don't come down. You hear the bells and count them, and if there are more bells than there should be, you hide in the escapement housing like I showed you."

"Are you coming back?"

The question was so plain it went through him like a drill through brass. "Yes," he said, and found that he meant it, and that meaning it changed the shape of the whole day.

* * *

The Marchetti shop sat on Threnody Lane, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the gutters ran brown with snowmelt. Sebastian had walked this street a thousand times — to fetch solder, to deliver a repaired carriage clock to the widow Hessel, to come home. He knew which flagstone rocked underfoot and which window shutter was painted green on one side only because Aldo had run out of paint. He had thought he knew the street. He had thought he knew the man.

The bell above the shop door gave its familiar cracked note. Inside, the smell of oil and pine shavings and the particular sweetness of Aldo's pipe tobacco, and beneath it all — now that he was looking for it — something else. River water. Cold storage. The smell of things kept in the dark.

His uncle stood at the long bench with his back to the door, a loupe screwed into his eye, working on a regulator with hands that had not trembled in forty years. Sebastian watched them now. They were steady. Of course they were steady. A man who moved contraband beneath the city for a decade did not tremble over a balance spring.

"Sebastian." Aldo did not turn. "You're early. The observatory clock again?"

"No."

The loupe came out. Aldo set down his tweezers with the exaggerated care of a man buying himself a moment, and turned. He was a broad, grey man, built like a bracket, with the deep-lined face of someone who had spent his life squinting at small things. He looked at Sebastian the way he looked at a movement that had been brought in by an amateur — kindly, wearily, already knowing what was wrong.

"You've been in the letter," Aldo said. "You've had it open."

Sebastian's hand went to his coat before he could stop it. "You knew there was a letter."

"I knew there would be one, someday, from someone." Aldo pulled off his apron and hung it on its hook, a domestic gesture so ordinary it made Sebastian's throat hurt. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down, boy. You'll want to be sitting."

Sebastian did not sit. He stood at the customer's side of the bench, the side where strangers stood, and the geography of that — the width of oak between them — felt suddenly like the whole distance of his life.

"Say it isn't true," Sebastian said. "Say it, and I'll believe you. I've believed you since I was nine years old. You pulled me out of the river the night my parents drowned. You fed me. You taught me everything I know. So say it."

Aldo's face did something complicated and brief, like a clock striking the hour — many small motions resolving into one sound. "I pulled you out of the river," he said. "That's true."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Aldo said. "It isn't."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs the shop cat moved across a floorboard. Sebastian heard his own pulse in his ears, faster than any escapement he'd ever regulated.

"The letter says you were the paymaster," Sebastian said. "That the crypts under the guild hall move more than bones. That men paid you, and you paid men, and the clocks above it all kept honest time so no one would look below." His voice was steadier than he felt. "It says you sold Tomas Reiner to the customs men to keep your own name clean. That he's dead. That his daughter was at my door with a note telling me to forget her face."

"And did you?" Aldo asked quietly. "Forget it?"

"No."

"No," Aldo said. "I didn't think you would. You never could forget a face. It's your worst quality as a tradesman and your best quality as a man, and I have never known which one would kill you first." He came around the bench, and Sebastian stepped back without deciding to, and Aldo stopped, and something in his uncle's eyes went old and tired and very far away. "I'm not going to deny it. I'm not going to confess it. I'm going to tell you what you need to know to stay alive, and you're going to hate me for the way I have to tell it."

"Then tell it."

"The men in that letter — the ones who wrote it, the ones it names — they are not magistrates. They are not reformers. They are the other half of the ring, and the half that does the drowning." Aldo's voice was flat and even, the voice he used to explain a difficult repair. "Tomas Reiner found out what I was and tried to buy his way out with what he knew, and the men he tried to sell it to were the men I answered to. I did not put him in the river, Sebastian. But I opened the door he was pushed through, and I have paid for that door every night since, in a currency you can't see. That is my confession, and it is all you will get, and it is worth exactly nothing in a court and everything in this room."

Sebastian found he had gripped the edge of the bench. The oak was cold. "The girl—"

"Is the letter's last living seal." Aldo said it without cruelty, which was worse. "As long as she can be produced — as long as someone can point at her and say *she saw, she remembers* — she is worth more to them alive than dead, and the moment she can't be, she's worth nothing at all. They will come for her. Not today. Not with knives. They'll come with questions and badges and soft voices, and they'll ask the observatory keeper who's been sleeping in the clock room, and they'll ask the widow Hessel who her nephew's boy has been visiting, and they will be so reasonable that you'll want to help them." He leaned on the bench, and for a moment Sebastian saw his uncle's hands clearly — the burn scar on the left thumb, the one from the night he'd taught Sebastian to sweat a joint, the hands that had made him. "And anyone found shielding her — anyone — will be handled the way loose ends are always handled. You know how loose ends are handled. You've read the same letter I'm describing."

"You're warning me off her."

"I'm warning you toward your own life."

"The same thing," Sebastian said, "from where I'm standing."

Aldo straightened. For a long moment the two of them looked at each other across the bench, across the scattered brass and the oil stone and the half-finished regulator with its pendulum hanging still, and Sebastian understood that this bench was the altar of his entire childhood — every lesson, every supper, every winter night when Aldo had sat with him by this very stove after the nightmares about the river — and that the man on the other side of it had built all of it on a floor of moving contraband and a drowned man's name.

"You were nine years old," Aldo said at last, and his voice cracked on it, one hairline fracture in forty years of steel. "You were blue when I pulled you out. You didn't cry. You just looked at me. And I thought — this one, this one I can make into something clean. Everything I did after, I did so there would be one clean thing at the end of it. You were supposed to be the clean thing, Sebastian. Don't make yourself into the payment."

The words went into him like a blade going into a clock case — precise, intended, devastating. Sebastian made himself let go of the bench. He made himself straighten.

"I'm not your clean thing," he said. "I'm a man. And there's a child asleep in my clock room who was told to run in the night by her dying mother, and every hour I spend here deciding whether to protect her is an hour she's spending alone, so I'm going to stop spending them."

"Sebastian—"

"You said the men will come." He was moving toward the door now, and his legs felt like someone else's. "Then they'll come. And when they ask me, I'll have to decide what I am. Because I can't be your nephew and a liar at the same time. I've tried it for two days and I'm no good at it."

He had the door open. The cold came in off Threnody Lane like a verdict.

"Boy." Aldo's voice behind him, low and terrible. "There is a berth on the last ferry before the thaw. One. It's spoken for. Whatever you're thinking of doing — whatever foolish, decent, impossible thing is forming in your head right now — know that there is exactly one way off this side of the river when the ice goes, and it is not yours, and it is not hers."

Sebastian stood in the doorway with the bell cracked and silent above him.

"Whose is it?" he asked, though he already knew, though the letter had told him, though he had known since dawn.

Aldo did not answer. That was the answer.

Sebastian walked out into the white morning and did not look back, because he was afraid that if he did, he would see the man who raised him instead of the man in the letter, and he could not afford, today, to see the first one.

* * *

The climb back up to the observatory took longer than it ever had. The snow had begun again, fine and dry, hissing against the dome. Twice Sebastian stopped on the stair and pressed the letter flat against his chest just to feel that it was still there, still real, still the heaviest thing he had ever carried.

He let himself in. The clock room was warm where he'd left the fire banked, and the smell of oil and iron wrapped around him like the shop used to, like home used to, and for a moment he stood in the doorway and let himself grieve — quickly, privately, the way a workman grieves a movement too cracked to save.

Then he heard it. Small feet on iron. Petra came around the escapement housing with his blankets still around her shoulders like a cloak, and her eyes went straight past him to the empty doorway behind.

"You came back," she said.

"I came back." He knelt so they were level. "Petra. I have to ask you something, and it may be the most important question anyone ever asks you. When the men came to your father's workshop that night — the men your mother was running from — did you see any of their faces?"

Petra's chin came up. Under the fear in her eyes there was something hard and bright, a gear that would not strip.

"One," she said.
