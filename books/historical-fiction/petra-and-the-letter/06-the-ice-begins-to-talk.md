## 6. The Ice Begins to Talk

The river spoke first.

Sebastian heard it on his way up the observatory path in the grey of a Tuesday morning — a low, groaning complaint from under the ice, like a door being forced somewhere far away. He stopped at the railing above the embankment and watched the frozen surface shudder once, a long ripple running beneath the snow the length of the channel, and then go still again. Three weeks ago that ice had been silent as a church floor. Now it talked all night, he'd been told. Cracked and muttered and shifted, testing its own strength.

The ice-wardens had posted their notice at the ferry landing two days past: *THAW EXPECTED WITHIN THE FORTNIGHT. NO CROSSINGS AFTER THE TENTH.*

He counted without meaning to. Six days.

He climbed the rest of the way to the observatory with his collar up and Petra's secret riding against his ribs like a second heartbeat. She was still asleep among the clockworks when he let himself in, curled in the nest of blankets behind the escapement housing where the gears kept the air moving just enough to keep her warm without ever quite warming her enough. He had begun to think of it as her room. That frightened him more than the river did.

* * *

The trouble came at noon, and it came politely.

Sebastian was at the bench, fitting a new pallet stone to the observatory's sidereal regulator, when he heard voices on the stairs — the keeper's voice, Mr. Halloran, pitched higher than usual, and beneath it a voice Sebastian did not know. Smooth. Unhurried. The voice of a man who had never once been made to wait.

"—certainly understand the need for discretion," Halloran was saying as they came through the door. "But I'm afraid I can't help you. The Marchetti workshop holds the maintenance contract. All repairs are logged there."

"All repairs," the smooth voice agreed. "And yet we've been to the Marchetti workshop, and their ledger shows no entries for this establishment since November." A pause, delicate as a jeweler's probe. "Curious, given that the master clock has been failing since December. Your own letters to the city works office say so."

Sebastian did not turn around. He kept his loupe at his eye and his tweezers on the pallet stone and listened to the cold walk into the room behind him.

There were two of them. He knew that much by the sound of their boots — one heavy, one light, the heavy one staying by the door the way heavy men do. The smooth one moved closer. Sebastian could smell him now, something resinous and foreign under the wool of his coat, camphor maybe, or the tar they used to preserve rope.

"Mr. Marchetti," the voice said.

Sebastian set down his tweezers. He turned, slowly, and lifted the loupe from his eye.

The man was perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples, dressed too well for the weather and not at all for the neighborhood — a charcoal coat with an astrakhan collar, gloves of kid leather, a stick with a silver head tucked under one arm though there was nothing wrong with his legs. His face was pleasant the way a well-cut gear is pleasant: precise, purposeful, and utterly without warmth. Behind him, filling most of the doorway, stood a broad man with a broken-knuckled hand resting on the jamb.

"Forgive the intrusion," the pleasant man said. "My name is Corvus. I represent certain commercial interests along the river." He smiled. "We're compiling a survey of the clock towers. Their condition, their access, their — cellars. You've worked here some months, I believe."

"Apprentice," Sebastian said. "I repair what breaks."

"And what breaks, Mr. Marchetti?" Corvus wandered toward the master clock, trailing one gloved finger along the case without touching it, the way a man strokes a horse he intends to buy. "In your professional opinion. What breaks?"

"Gears wear. Pivots gall. Oil dries out." Sebastian kept his voice flat as a spirit level. "Clocks are simple. They do exactly what you build them to do."

"Mm." Corvus turned. For a moment his eyes went past Sebastian — up, along the gallery rail, toward the shadowed housing behind the escapement where a child might be hidden if a child were hiding — and Sebastian felt the hair rise along his arms. Then the gaze came back, mild and empty as a dial with no hands. "Yes. Simple things. And yet so often used for complicated purposes." He drew off one glove, finger by finger. "You'll forgive me. I have a poor memory for faces myself. It's a family failing. We rely instead on — other records." From inside his coat he produced a small notebook, licked his thumb, and turned a page with theatrical slowness. "Marchetti. S. Apprentice. Nephew of Aldo Marchetti, clockmaker, Vintner's Row." He looked up. "Aldo is your uncle."

"He raised me."

"A generous man." Corvus closed the book. "Generous men keep generous ledgers. You might tell him we called. Tell him—" and here the smile returned, wider, showing teeth this time, "—tell him the survey is nearly complete. There are only so many towers left, and only so many crossings before the thaw, and my principals dislike unfinished business almost as much as they dislike cold water. Do you follow me, apprentice?"

"I follow clocks," Sebastian said. "Not threats."

"Then we shall speak plainly another day." Corvus replaced the glove, settled his stick under his arm, and paused at the door long enough to add, softly, "Give the girl our regards."

Halloran shut the door behind them. The keeper was gray-faced, his hands unsteady as he threw the bolt. "They were here Sunday," he said, without turning. "While you were at your uncle's. Asking after the same things. I told them nothing because I know nothing, boy, but they didn't believe me, and I don't think they'll believe you either." He finally looked at Sebastian, and his old eyes were frightened and kind at once. "Whatever you're keeping up here behind my clockworks — whatever it is — the river's going to break soon, and everything the ice has been holding down comes floating up."

* * *

The note was waiting on his workbench that evening.

He found it wedged under the brass weight of his bench lamp, folded twice, sealed with no seal at all — just a smear of black wax pressed flat with a thumbnail. The shop was dark around him; he had come back after supper to finish the pallet stone, and for a long moment he stood in the doorway of his own workroom feeling the way a man feels when he realizes the lock on his door has been picked by someone who wants him to know it.

He lit the lamp. He opened the note.

The handwriting was small and exact, the hand of someone trained to letter dials:

*Your uncle sails the night of the tenth. Last berth before the thaw — the *Vrouw Maria*, north landing, midnight tide. One berth. His name is already written in the book. If the girl is still in this city on the eleventh, she will be found, and so will you, and so will he. Corvus keeps accounts. So do we. Bring us what she carries and there may be three names in the book instead of one.*

*— A friend of your father*

Sebastian read it four times. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river groaned again, longer this time, a note held and then released.

*A friend of your father.* His father had been dead nineteen years, drowned within sight of the harbor with Petra's story hanging unspoken over the whole city like fog. But the words did their work regardless; they slipped under his skin and sat there, cold. Whoever wrote it knew his history. Knew which wound to press.

And the rest of it was worse than any threat. He read it a fifth time and let it settle into him piece by piece.

The night of the tenth. Midnight tide. *One berth.* His name is already written in the book.

Aldo had bought passage. Aldo — who had told him only days ago, standing at the bench with his back half-turned and his voice like gravel in a sack, *the men in that letter will come for the girl, and for anyone fool enough to stand between them and her* — Aldo had already arranged to be gone before they came. One berth. Not two. Not three. His uncle had counted the cost of leaving and decided that the count included one man only, and Sebastian understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than anger, that Aldo had probably purchased the berth before he had ever summoned Sebastian to fix the master clock. Before the girl. Before the letter. Perhaps even before the ice began to talk.

His uncle had been packing to run since winter began, and had said nothing, and had let Sebastian stand in front of Corvus today with nothing but an apprentice's flat voice and a straight spine while the older man's escape sat bought and paid for at the north landing.

Sebastian put the note in the lamp flame and watched it curl. The paper burned greenish at the edges, then black, then gone. Ash drifted onto the bench among the brass filings, indistinguishable from them.

Six days. Five, really — the tenth would arrive whether he counted honestly or not. And in those five days he had to decide what he was going to do about a child asleep behind a clock escapement, a letter sewn into his coat lining, a smuggler's arithmetic of berths, and an uncle whose silence had been the only fatherhood Sebastian had ever known.

He thought of Petra's face when she had said *one* — the hard bright gear of her, refusing to strip. He thought of Aldo's hands teaching his smaller ones to file a pivot true, twenty years ago, the first kindness he could remember after the water took his parents. Both memories were true. That was the unbearable part. They could both be true, and still he could keep only one of them.

Outside, the river cracked — a sharp report like a pistol shot, then a long grinding sigh, the ice shrugging loose of the banks. The wardens would be out with their lanterns, marking the leads. By Friday there would be open water at the mouth of the channel. By the tenth, the *Vrouw Maria* would take on her last passenger before the thaw closed the crossing for a season.

One name in the book.

Sebastian banked the lamp, climbed the tower stair in the dark, and stood a moment outside the little nest of blankets, listening to Petra breathe among the ticking wheels.

On the tenth, he decided, he was going to the north landing. He needed to see the book with his own eyes — and he needed to ask his uncle, one final time, face to face, why the count had never included anyone but Aldo Marchetti.

From behind the escapement housing, in her sleep, the girl said a name he didn't recognize, very quietly, the way children do when they are dreaming of a door they cannot open.

The river answered her, cracking again beneath the ice.
