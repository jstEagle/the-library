## 7. What Petra Remembers

The clock tower was warm in the way that machines are warm — not like a hearth, but like something alive that had been working too long. Petra sat cross-legged on a plank platform between two great gear wheels taller than she was, and she listened to the tower breathe.

It breathed in ticks. It breathed in the slow groan of the pendulum overhead, swinging its iron arc through the dark, and in the whisper of chains, and in the small metallic sighs of springs settling and releasing, settling and releasing, all through the night. Papa's workshop had sounded like this, she thought. Smaller, yes — his clocks had fit on shelves and mantelpieces instead of filling a whole stone throat of a building — but it had the same heartbeat underneath. She closed her eyes and let the sound hold her, the way a current holds a leaf.

She was not going to cry. She had decided this on the second night, after the boy with the oil-black fingers — Sebastian — had carried her up here wrapped in his own coat because the observatory keeper had come asking questions about strangers. *Up in the tower no one looks for you,* he'd said. *They look at clocks. They never look inside them.* He'd been right. For three days now she had lived among the works of the great clock, eating bread he smuggled up in a tool bag, sleeping under an old sailcloth that smelled of machine oil, watching through the slats as the city went about its business below without her.

Mama had said: *Run, and don't stop running until you find the man who fixes the big clock.* Mama had said it with her hands on Petra's shoulders, hard enough to leave the shape of fingers in the memory. Mama had pressed the letter into her coat lining and sewn it shut while the men were still hammering on the door downstairs, and her needle had gone through the cloth so fast it barely made a sound, and then she had kissed Petra's forehead and said, *Forget my face if you have to. Forget everything except the letter.*

But Petra had not forgotten anything. That was the trouble.

* * *

She remembered the workshop at night the way you remember a room where something broke.

Not the lamps — the lamps had been ordinary, yellow, the same as any evening. Not Papa at his bench, either; he had been ordinary too, hunched over a carriage clock with his loupe screwed into his eye, complaining under his breath about a mainspring the way he always did, muttering *stubborn little beast* to a mechanism that could not answer him. It was what came after the ordinariness that stayed.

Three knocks. Not loud. A particular rhythm — two quick, one slow — and Papa had gone still in a way Petra had never seen him go still. Still like a stopped clock. He had set down his tweezers very carefully, as though they might make noise, and looked at the door, and Petra had watched him become someone else. His shoulders came forward. His chin came down. He became a smaller man wearing her father's body.

"Petra," he said quietly. "Go up to bed."

"I'm not tired."

"Petra." And there was something in his voice she had never heard before, and being six years older now than she had been on that night, she finally had a word for it. It was fear. Not the fear of thunder or of falling — the fear a man has when he knows exactly what is coming through a door and cannot stop it.

She had climbed the stairs, but she had not gone to bed. She had lain on the landing with her cheek against the cold boards, looking down through the balusters into the lamplight below, because children always watch when they are told not to.

The man who knocked twice-quick-and-once-slow had a voice like gravel rolled in honey. She remembered that voice better than his face, though she knew his face too: heavy brows, a beard trimmed close and gray at the edges, a scar splitting one eyebrow into a pale fork. He had called her father by a name that wasn't quite his name — *Emil, Emil, you stubborn fool* — and Papa had answered in a low, flat voice, "That's not my name anymore and you know it."

"Names," the scarred man had said, laughing softly, "are the cheapest thing a man can change. Cheaper than coats. Cheaper than towns." Then, quieter, in a different tone entirely, the tone of a ledger being read aloud: "Four shipments, Emil. The winter consignments. They passed through your crypt and they never reached the river. Where are they?"

"There is no crypt under my shop."

"There is a crypt under every shop on Clockmakers' Row. That was the whole design of the Row, old friend. You think the towers were built so high for the sake of astronomy?"

And Papa had laughed — a terrible laugh, short and cracked, the laugh of a man who has just understood the shape of a trap he has been standing inside for years. "You want to know where the goods went? Ask Marchetti. Ask Aldo Marchetti where the winter consignments went, since it was his hand on the ledger, his key on the crypt gate. I only ever kept the time. I never touched the cargo."

Silence, below. The kind of silence that has weight.

"You signed for them," the scarred man said at last, gently, almost kindly. "Your mark is on the chits. Because Marchetti asked you to sign, and you trusted him. That's what trust costs, Emil. It cost you your name, and now it will cost the rest."

Then the other sounds began — drawers pulled out, the scrape of the bench, wood splintering somewhere — and Petra had pressed her face against the boards and shut her eyes and listened anyway, because not listening was worse. And the last thing she heard before her mother's hand closed over her mouth from behind, before the whispered *run*, before the back stairs and the frozen alley and the long white terror of the river road — the last thing she heard was the scarred man saying, in a voice almost tender:

"And tell Marchetti his account is open. We always collect."

* * *

She must have slept, because the light through the slats had changed from blue to gold when she heard the hatch lift and Sebastian's head appeared, haloed in dust motes.

"You're awake," he said, relieved, climbing through with the tool bag. "I've been listening for you for ten minutes. There's bread. And — " he hesitated, and she saw that his hands had gone careful around whatever else was in the bag, the way people handle things that might break — "and there were men at the observatory this morning. Asking the keeper questions. About a girl. About a letter."

Petra took the bread and did not eat it yet. "What did they look like?"

Sebastian blinked. "One was young. Nervous. Kept looking at the floor like the answers might be written there. The other — " He stopped. Something moved across his face, some reluctance.

"The other," Petra said.

"He was older. Heavyset. Gray in his beard, cut close. He had — " Sebastian's hand rose halfway toward his own face, toward his brow, and faltered. "A scar. Through one eyebrow. Like a fork."

The bread went very still in Petra's hands.

"It's him," she said.

"Who? Petra, who—"

"My papa's partner." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and she was distantly proud of that, the way she'd been proud the night she ran and didn't stop. "He came to our house the last night. He knocked two quick and one slow, and Papa went still like a stopped clock, and he said — " she closed her eyes and let the words come back up out of the deep place where she had buried them, whole and shining and terrible — "he said, *ask Marchetti where the winter consignments went, since it was his hand on the ledger.*"

Sebastian had gone the color of the sailcloth.

"He said my papa signed the chits because *your uncle asked him to*. He said—" and here the steadiness cracked, one hairline fracture straight through, "—he said, *tell Marchetti his account is open. We always collect.* And now he's here. In this city. Looking for me." She looked up at Sebastian, at the boy who fed her and hid her and had not once told her to forget her face. "He's collecting."

For a long moment Sebastian didn't move. The tower ticked around them both, patient as ever, keeping a record of every second that passed. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"When I found you, there was a note pinned to your coat. It told me to forget your face. And the letter inside — " He swallowed. "The letter names my uncle. It says things I didn't want to believe. Things I *still* don't want to believe, because Aldo Marchetti raised me when nobody else would touch me." He knelt down across from her, so their eyes were level, and his were bright and wet and furious all at once. "But you remember his face. You remember his voice. You remember what he said in your father's house the night your family was destroyed. That isn't paper, Petra. Paper can be forged. Paper can be stolen or burned or denied. What you carry in your head — "

"Is true," Petra said.

"Memory is the only evidence that matters," Sebastian said slowly, as if the words were gears he was fitting together for the first time, testing each tooth against the next. "And they know it. That's why they're searching. Not for the letter. For *you*."

Above them the great pendulum swung through its iron arc, unhurried, indifferent, counting down seconds neither of them could see being spent. Petra hugged the uneaten bread to her chest and looked out through the slats at the city below, where somewhere a man with a forked scar was walking the streets with her name in his pocket.

And far off, faint under everything, she heard the river ice begin to talk.
