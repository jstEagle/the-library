## 8. The Confession in the Clockwork

The knock came at half past eleven, three blows struck low on the observatory door, soft as a man asking pardon before he commits a sin.

Sebastian knew the rhythm of that hand. He had known it since he was six years old, since the night Uncle Aldo carried him up four flights of stairs in a coat that smelled of pipe smoke and river water, and set him down in a warm room and said, *You are a Marchetti now, boy, and Marchettis keep time.* Twenty years of that knock lived in Sebastian's knuckles. He could have answered it blind.

He did not answer it at all.

He stood instead in the dark of the gear room with Petra's small hand pressed into his, feeling her pulse flutter against his palm like a moth against glass, and listened to the door open below — because Aldo had a key, Aldo had always had a key, and there was no lock in this city his uncle could not persuade.

"Sebastian." The voice climbed the stairwell, flattened by stone. "I know you're up there. The keeper told me which way the wind was blowing, and the wind smells of a frightened child."

* * *

They had moved Petra an hour earlier, when the first stranger came asking questions at the gate. She had not cried. That was the thing about her that undid him — she went where he pointed her, folded herself into the narrow gallery behind the great escapement wheel, and pulled a scrap of canvas over her knees like a girl settling into a pew. Only her eyes gave her away. They tracked every sound the tower made, cataloguing it, filing it away the way her father must have taught her to catalogue the parts of a clock.

Now Sebastian eased her hand free and stepped out of the shadow of the works.

Aldo stood at the top of the stairs with his hat in one hand and a lantern in the other, and the light did something cruel to his face. It found all the years at once. Sebastian had never thought of his uncle as old; Aldo Marchetti was the man who could carry a case of brass up six flights without losing breath, who danced at weddings until the fiddlers quit. But the lantern showed him stooped now, gray to the roots, with a bruise blooming yellow-green along his jaw like a stain spreading through watercolor.

"They hit you," Sebastian said.

"My partners have developed opinions." Aldo set the lantern on the workbench, carefully, the way he handled everything, as if even fire deserved respect. "Where is she?"

"You know I won't tell you."

"I know you won't tell me *where*. I'm asking you to tell me *why*." He took off his coat and laid it over the bench, and for one absurd moment he looked like any evening of Sebastian's childhood — Aldo come home from the shop, hanging up his things, ready to talk about gears over supper. "Sit down, boy."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes." Something moved across his uncle's face — pride, or its corpse. "You always would."

The master clock ticked above them, vast and patient, the escapement letting the great wheel fall forward one tooth at a time, one second at a time. Sebastian had spent three nights listening to that rhythm while he worked. Tonight it sounded like a countdown.

"The letter," Aldo said. "I want it."

"No."

"It's a death warrant, Sebastian. Not for me — I've earned whatever comes for me. For *her*. Do you understand what's written in that ink? Names. Dates. Her father's ledger arithmetic. If the magistrates read it, she becomes the daughter of a smuggler. If my partners read it, she becomes a loose thread. Either way—" he spread his hands, and they were shaking, Sebastian saw, actually shaking— "either way, that paper will hang her. Give it to me and I'll burn it tonight, and we'll find a boat, and—"

"And what? You'll take her where? On whose berth?" Sebastian heard his own voice go flat and cold, a voice he didn't recognize. "I know about the ferry, Uncle. I know the last berth before the thaw has your name on it. Has had for weeks, hasn't it? While you sat at our table eating my bread."

Aldo closed his eyes. When he opened them, the performance was gone — the wounded dignity, the uncle-of-the-weddings. What remained was tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"Your bread," he said quietly. "Yes. Let's speak of what I've eaten at your table, boy, since you've decided to keep accounts."

* * *

He talked for a long time. Sebastian made himself stand still and let him.

It began, Aldo said, the winter after the drowning — after Sebastian's parents' boat went down in the ice with nine others, after the city rang its bells and then forgot. The shop was failing. There was a man named Corvi who came in to have a watch repaired and paid triple and asked no questions, and then asked only one: *You have hands, Marchetti, and a cellar under your shop, and the crypts beneath the towers run the length of the old quarter. How good are your locks?*

"I told myself it would be once," Aldo said. "Every man in this business tells himself that. Then it was twice, and then it was the arrangement, and then it was fifteen years, and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped telling myself anything at all. That's the trick of it, Sebastian. Not greed. Sleep. You learn to sleep."

"And her father?" Sebastian asked. "Petra's father. The letter says you betrayed him."

The silence stretched so long the clock filled it, tooth by tooth by tooth.

"Tomas Veyra kept the books," Aldo said finally. "Not our books — *his* own. He was the honest one among us, God forgive the word. He kept a true ledger of everything that moved through the towers, every payment, every name, because he told himself he was protecting himself. And he was, for a while. A man with the whole truth written down is hard to kill." Aldo's laugh was terrible, small, dry as a broken mainspring. "So we didn't kill him. We drowned him. There was a shipment in November that went wrong — magistrates at the north gate, someone talked, and Corvi decided it was Tomas. And I—" His hand rose halfway and fell. "I confirmed it. I knew it wasn't true. I knew who *had* talked, because it was me, it had been me for two years, feeding the customs men just enough to keep them off our true route. And I let them believe him. I watched them take his workshop apart, and I watched his wife carry that child out the back door in the dark, and I said nothing. Because saying something meant giving up the berth. My berth. The boat I'd already paid for with fifteen years of sleeping."

Sebastian discovered he had gripped the edge of the workbench hard enough to hurt. He made himself let go.

"She was at your table too," he said. "That night. Petra. She remembers your face, Uncle. She remembered it before she ever saw you here."

"I know." Aldo looked at the floor. "She was four. She sat on my knee while I wound the regulator. She called me the clock man." He straightened, and the exhaustion in him seemed to calcify into something harder. "So now you have it. All of it. Are you satisfied?"

"No."

"Good. Because I haven't finished begging." He crossed the room and stopped close, close enough that Sebastian could smell the river on his coat, the same river-smell from twenty years ago, from the night he'd carried a orphaned boy up the stairs. "Corvi knows the letter exists. He knows the girl has it. They gave me until dawn to bring them both, or they come themselves, and Sebastian — they will not be gentle, and they will not stop at her. They'll burn the shop. They'll burn the *observatory*. Everyone who ever sat at my table." His voice cracked down the middle. "Give me the letter. Give me the girl. Let me do one decent thing at the end and put her on that boat myself, and you come with us, and we vanish, and no magistrate ever learns what a Marchetti did. That's all I ask. Silence. One winter of silence, and then the world can think what it likes."

"That's not silence," Sebastian said. "That's you asking me to drown the truth the way you drowned her father."

"Family is not truth!" It burst out of him, loud enough to startle pigeons from the belfry, loud enough that above them, faintly, Petra shifted behind the canvas. Aldo lowered his voice to a rasp. "Family is the only thing that holds when the ice goes out. Your father held my hand in the water, boy. They pulled bodies out of that river for a week and I identified my brother by his wedding ring, and I swore on that ring I would raise you clean. Clean! And look what raising you clean has bought me — a nephew who stands in a clock tower and calls me a murderer to my face."

"You called yourself one first."

The words landed like a dropped weight. Aldo flinched as though struck, and for a moment — one long second, one full swing of the pendulum — Sebastian saw him truly: an old man in a cold tower, cornered between his partners and his sins, holding out his hands to the only person left who might still catch him.

"Aldo," Sebastian said, more gently than he felt. "Go home. Lock your door. When the magistrates come — and they're coming, the letter is already beyond either of us — tell them everything. All of it. Tomas Veyra. Corvi. The crypts. Tell the truth and maybe, *maybe*, what's left of your life belongs to you again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then at dawn I walk to the customs house myself."

His uncle looked at him for a long time. Whatever he searched for in Sebastian's face, he apparently found, because something in him settled — not peace, but surrender's grim cousin.

"You have her eyes," Aldo said at last, moving toward the stairs. "Did you know that? Tomas Veyra's eyes. Stubborn past all sense." He picked up his hat. At the top of the stairwell he paused, his back turned, and the lantern light caught the tremor in his shoulders. "The berth leaves at first ice-break. Two days, perhaps three. Whatever you mean to do with that paper and that child, boy — do it fast, and do it far from me."

Then he was gone, his footsteps descending into the dark, and the great door boomed shut behind him.

* * *

Sebastian stood alone in the ticking dark for a long while before he climbed to the gallery.

Petra was awake, of course. She had heard everything — children always hear everything, that was their curse and their education. She looked up at him from the canvas with Tomas Veyra's stubborn eyes and said, in her small precise voice:

"He lied about one thing, Sebastian. About the November shipment." She pulled something from inside her coat — not the letter, but a scrap of paper, soft with handling, covered in a child's careful copy of figures. "Papa made me memorize these. He said if anything ever happened, numbers couldn't be burned. Numbers couldn't be drowned." She pressed it into Sebastian's hand. "There's a name at the bottom I never understood until tonight. It isn't Papa's partner. It's the man Papa trusted most."

Sebastian looked down at the page. At the bottom, in a dead man's hand, was a single line of figures — and beside them, unmistakable, a name he had known his whole life.

Not Aldo's.

The observatory keeper's.

Above them, out in the frozen dark, the river groaned like something waking.
