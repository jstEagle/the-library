## 9. The Last Berth

The ice began to talk at midnight, and by dawn it was screaming.

Sebastian stood at the rail of the ferry landing with the letter inside his coat, pressed flat against his chest like a second heartbeat. Below him the river was tearing itself apart — great plates of ice heaving up and grinding down, groaning in voices that sounded almost human, almost pleading. Chunks the size of church pews rode the current past the pilings, colliding, splitting, spinning away into the gray water that had finally won its argument with winter.

Three days since he'd found the name on the confession page. Three days of not sleeping, of winding the master clock with hands that wouldn't steady, of watching Petra copy her mother's instructions into memory the way he'd once copied gear teeth into his notebook — carefully, completely, because it was all she would ever have left.

*The observatory keeper.* Not Aldo. The man who had signed the ferry manifest, who held the last berth before the thaw, who had smiled at Sebastian across the ticket desk every winter for six years and asked after his uncle's health.

Aldo had been the paymaster, yes. The letter said so, and Aldo's face — when Sebastian had cornered him among the gears, when the confession had finally come out of him in the dark like oil from a cracked reservoir — had confirmed it more thoroughly than any ink could. But the keeper had been the one who moved the money out. The one whose name sat at the bottom of the page in figures only a magistrate could love. And the keeper had booked the berth for himself.

*One berth,* the harbor master had told him yesterday, not meeting his eyes. *One passenger before the river opens. It's spoken for. You know how it is.*

Sebastian knew exactly how it was.

* * *

Petra found him at the rail just as the sky went the color of pewter. She was wearing the coat she'd arrived in, the one with the pin-holes where the letter had been fastened, and she had insisted on wearing it today despite the cold, as if the holes themselves were proof of something. She was seven years old and she stood like a clockmaker at his bench: spine straight, hands still, watching everything.

"Is it time?"

"It's nearly time."

"The boat comes at the ninth bell." She said it without inflection, the way she'd learned to say everything since the night men came to her father's workshop. Children learned arithmetic from grief faster than from schoolmasters. "You told me."

"I told you."

She looked out at the breaking ice. Her breath came in small white ghosts. "Are you coming too?"

And there it was. The question he'd been carrying for three days like a stone in his boot, the one he'd hoped she wouldn't ask because he hadn't yet found an answer he could survive saying aloud.

He crouched down so they were eye to eye. Up close her chapped lips and wind-burned cheeks made her look older than seven, older than anyone ought to be, and he hated the world a little more for it.

"Petra," he said. "I need you to listen very carefully, the way you listened in the tower. Can you do that?"

Her chin lifted a fraction. A nod.

"The letter goes with you. Not the confession — my uncle's part is finished, whatever happens to him now. Your letter. Your testimony. When you get downriver, there will be a woman waiting at the Verrone dock — a widow named Sartori, with a blue door. My uncle told me her name and I believe he told me true, because it was the one thing left in him that wasn't rotten. You give her the letter. You tell her what you saw. The man with the gray gloves, the one who was your father's partner. You tell her everything you told me."

"I remember it all," she said. "I practiced."

"I know you did." His throat closed. He worked past it the way he worked past a seized spring — slowly, with pressure, without letting it snap. "The letter is your protection. Anyone who asks why you're on that boat, you show them the seal and you say nothing else. Words can be argued with. Paper with a magistrate's stamp cannot."

"And you?" she said. Because children cut straight through, always; because she had spent one winter learning that adults lied with their mouths while their eyes counted something else. "You're coming too. There's room if we squeeze—"

"No."

The word fell between them onto the wet planks, and the river seemed to swallow it.

"There is one berth," Sebastian said. "It was bought with stolen money by a man who thought no one would stop him. I stopped him. That leaves one berth, and one person on this landing who needs it more than anyone else in this city needs anything." He took her small mittened hand and pressed the letter into it, folding her fingers over the seal. "It isn't me, Petra. It was never going to be me."

* * *

Aldo Marchetti came to the landing at the eighth bell.

Sebastian heard him before he saw him — the uneven tread, right foot heavier than left, the sound of a man who had climbed observatory stairs and workshop stairs and smugglers' crypt stairs for forty years and was now climbing toward the worst morning of them. He came down the ramp in his good coat, the black wool one he saved for funerals and christenings, and Sebastian understood with a dull ache that his uncle had dressed for his own.

Two men followed him. Not magistrates. Not yet. Men in gray gloves, moving with the unhurried confidence of creditors, keeping Aldo between them the way a wall keeps a prisoner.

"You wrote to them," Aldo said. No greeting. His voice was hoarse, scraped raw from the night in the tower, from the confession, from the begging that had come after it. "You sent the letter to the harbor office. I raised you better than to write letters."

"You raised me to keep clocks running," Sebastian said. "Everything else I taught myself."

The taller of the gray-gloved men stepped forward and produced papers — manifests, seals, the dead man's figures transcribed into an official hand. He read the charges in a voice pitched for the record rather than for mercy. Paymaster of the ring. Conduit beneath the towers. Complicity in the death of one Emilio Verrone, clockmaker, deceased these four months. Aldo stood through all of it without flinching, the way he stood through the striking of the hour, and only once did his eyes leave the papers — only once did they find Sebastian over the gray shoulders, and what passed across his face then was not anger.

It was something worse. It was pride.

*You did what had to be done,* that look said. *I only wish I'd been strong enough to do it first.*

The gray-gloved men turned him toward the shore road, toward the magistrate's office and the cells beneath it, and Aldo went without being pushed. At the top of the ramp he stopped. He didn't turn around. He spoke to the frozen air in front of him, but every word was aimed backward, over his shoulder, at the boy he had taken in when there was no one else to take him.

"The Verrone woman has a blue door," he said. "Tell the girl to knock twice. Once means strangers."

Then they walked him away, down the white road, getting smaller, until the falling snow took him the way the river takes everything eventually, and Sebastian stood at the rail with his fists clenched on the cold iron and let himself feel all of it at once — the love, the betrayal, the grief, the terrible rightness — and discovered that feeling it all at once does not kill you. It only makes you into someone new, someone who has to be built again from the ground up, gear by gear, tooth by tooth.

* * *

The boat came at the ninth bell.

It was a river packet, low and black-hulled, riding deep with the season's last cargo, and it came down the channel where the ice had opened like a wound, throwing sheets of spray off its bow. The deckhands caught the mooring lines. The gangplank went down. Somewhere behind Sebastian, along the shore, the city was waking to the sound of the thaw, bells ringing the hour in towers he would never climb again without hearing his uncle's voice in the escapement's tick.

He walked Petra to the plank.

At the bottom of it, the mate held out his hand for the ticket, and Sebastian put the keeper's forfeited passage paper in it — reclaimed at dawn, with the harbor master sweating and cooperative, from a man who no longer needed to flee anywhere. The mate glanced at it, glanced at the girl, glanced at Sebastian.

"One berth," the mate said. "That's the rule. One."

"She's traveling alone," Sebastian said. "There's a woman waiting. Blue door, Verrone dock. Knock twice."

The mate shrugged the shrug of men who move other people's lives on schedules. "Alone, then. If she can manage the plank."

"I can manage," Petra said.

She went three steps up the gangplank and stopped. Turned around. The wind off the river pulled at her hair and she stood against it the way she'd stood against everything since the night her mother whispered *run* — small, and fierce, and absolutely unwilling to be pitied.

"You gave me the berth," she said.

"Somebody had to take it."

"That's not what I mean." She dug in her coat pocket and came out with something small, holding it up between two mittened fingers so he could see it catch the light. A brass escape wheel, no bigger than a thumbnail, polished to a shine that could only come from years of handling. His — the one she'd taken from his bench the first week, the theft he'd pretended not to notice because noticing would have meant asking questions he didn't want answered. "I'm giving it back. So you'll know I kept it safe. Like you kept me."

Sebastian took it. His hands were shaking now, finally, now that it was too late for shaking to change anything, and the little wheel trembled in his palm like a living thing.

"Go," he said. "Before I make the wrong choice."

"You won't," Petra said. "You already made the right one. I watched you do it."

Then she turned and ran up the plank, light-footed, sure-handed, a girl who had learned to move through dangerous places without making a sound, and the mate swung the board in, and the lines came off the bollards, and the packet backed out into the channel with the ice grinding applause on either side of it.

Sebastian watched until the gray water took her too. But this taking was different. This taking was a promise.

He stood at the empty landing a long time, long after the boat was gone, turning the escape wheel over in his fingers. Then he noticed the paper folded into his coat pocket that hadn't been there before — slipped there, he realized, somewhere between the rail and the plank, by a child with quick hands and a clockmaker's patience for small precise work.

He opened it. One line, in careful pencil:

*Knock twice. I'll be listening.*

Downriver, the river ran open all the way to the sea. And back up the hill, above the waking city, the observatory clock struck ten — true, and clean, and waiting for him to come home.
