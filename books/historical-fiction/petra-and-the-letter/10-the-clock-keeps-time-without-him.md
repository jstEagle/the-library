## 10. The Clock Keeps Time Without Him

The thaw came all at once, the way spring always did in that city — not gently, but like a decision. One morning in late March Sebastian walked up the observatory hill and found the river running brown and fast and free, dragging the last pans of ice out toward the sea, and the whole waterfront shouting about it: gulls, dockhands, children throwing sticks into the current to watch them race. The winter that had held everything — the clock, the girl, the letter, his uncle — was simply gone downstream, and the world had the nerve to look ordinary again.

He stood a while at the rail of the observatory terrace, hands in his pockets, listening.

Behind him, through the stone wall, came the sound he had spent three months learning to hear properly: the master clock, striking nine. Not the sick, halting stagger it had made in January, when each hour arrived like a man climbing stairs with bad lungs. This was clean. Nine strokes, evenly spaced, the escapement releasing each second as if seconds were something to be generous with. Herr Voss, the observatory keeper, had stood beneath it the first morning after Sebastian's repairs held and said nothing for a full minute, which from Voss was a eulogy and a coronation both.

"Marchetti." The keeper's voice now, behind him on the terrace, gravel over gravel. "You're going to stand at that rail until you grow moss. Come inside. There's weather coming off the mountains and I want the barograph wound, and there's a letter."

Sebastian turned. "A letter?"

Voss's face did its usual work of arranging itself into disapproval before anything else could get there. But his eyes, pale and quick as a wren's, were watching Sebastian with something gentler underneath. "Downriver," he said. "Marked with your name and mine both. Came up on the packet boat this morning." He paused at the door, one hand on the iron latch, and added, in the tone he used for readings he considered self-evident: "It's been four months. She'd be taller."

* * *

Inside, the observatory smelled of oil and cold brass and, faintly, of bread — Voss's housekeeper had taken to leaving loaves on the warming shelf above the clock room, an indulgence she pretended was accidental. Sebastian climbed the spiral stair to the clock chamber, where the great pendulum swung in its long patient arc, and where, in a corner behind the gear train's housing, a small blanket still lay folded. He had not moved it. He was not sure he ever would.

Petra had slept there eleven nights. He had fed her bread and honey and taught her the names of the wheels; she had learned faster than any apprentice he'd known, her small fingers finding the escape lever by touch alone, and on the last night, the night the ice broke, she had looked up at him from under the wool cap he'd bought her and asked, with terrible directness, whether he was coming too.

*The boat takes two,* he had told her. *And the letter goes with you.*

She had argued. Eleven years old, exhausted, hunted, and she had argued with him like a barrister — that he should come, that they would find somewhere together, that she didn't want to arrive among strangers knowing no one. And he had knelt on the frozen jetty and held her by the shoulders and told her the only true thing he had: *You carry the truth, Petra. Somebody has to be alive to tell it. That's the whole job now.* Then the ferryman had called the last call, and she had pressed something into his hand — folded small, sealed with candle wax because she had no seal of her own — and said, "When it's safe. You'll get my letter. I promise on my father's workshop." And then she was a shape in the boat, and then a lantern, and then nothing but the river.

The magistrates had come for Aldo three days later. Sebastian had not gone to watch. He had heard enough secondhand: the uncle who neither denied nor confessed even at the end, standing in the magistrate's hall with his coat buttoned wrong, saying only, "My nephew keeps the clocks true. Whatever else is said in this room, that much is true." It was not an apology. Sebastian had stopped waiting for it to become one. Grief, he had learned that winter, does not resolve like a gear train; some meshes simply never come right, and you build around them.

He sat down on the clock-room stool, broke the wax with his thumb, and read.

*Dear Sebastian,*

*We are at my aunt's, past the third lock, where the hills start. The people here are kind and ask few questions, which the letter explained why. Magistrate Brenner wrote ahead himself — he says testimony given freely counts more than testimony taken, and that I gave it well. I practiced on the boat. A woman with a fur muff let me practice on her.*

*I am going to school. They say I am quick at numbers. I told them it was because a clockmaker taught me that every wheel is honest even when the hands lie, and the teacher wrote it down, so maybe I will be a writer instead.*

*Is the clock still true? Please check the third wheel bearing, it always ran hot in spring, Papa said so.*

*Your friend,
Petra*

At the bottom, in a different, adult hand — the aunt's, presumably — a single line: *She reads yours aloud every time. There have been four.*

Sebastian sat very still. The pendulum breathed behind him. Four letters, and this was the fifth, which meant she had counted his the way he had counted hers, which meant — he made himself hold the thought without flinching — which meant he had not been forgotten. He had been told once, in ink on a pinned note, to forget a face. He had failed at it thoroughly, on purpose, and it was the finest failure of his life.

* * *

Herr Voss found him an hour later, still on the stool, the letter open on his knee, doing nothing that could be called work and everything that could be called keeping time.

"The barograph," Voss reminded him, without heat. Then he crossed the room, which he almost never did, and stood under the great dial looking up at the hands as though consulting them. "I've had a communication from the Observatory Board," he said. "Regarding the assistant position."

Sebastian's stomach performed a small, traitorous drop. "The position they gave to Kessler's nephew."

"The position Kessler's nephew resigned from in February, having discovered he disliked waking before noon, working with his hands, or being useful." Voss turned. He was not a tall man and he did not attempt to be imposing; his authority lived entirely in his precision. "The Board asks whom I recommend. I have told them the truth, which took them aback considerably. I told them I recommend the young man who repaired a century-old master clock alone, in the worst cold in thirty years, without instruction, without complaint, and without once letting it stop — including the week he was being watched by smugglers, questioned by magistrates, and grieving his family." He paused. "They will offer you the post. Permanent. Rooms included. You will accept, presumably, after making me wait an appropriate interval so that you seem to have other prospects."

Sebastian looked down at his hands. They were steady. All winter they had been steady when everything else wasn't, and now, offered the one thing he had wanted since he was eight years old and first allowed to hold a loupe, they trembled.

"Herr Voss," he said carefully, "you know what my name is worth in this city now. Marchetti. Every clerk and customs man knows it."

"I know exactly what your name is worth," Voss said. "That is why I recommended you by your work. Names are inherited, boy. Work is earned. I have kept this observatory for twenty-six years, and I will tell you what I have learned about clocks and about men: you cannot choose what you descend from. You can only choose what you keep running." He straightened his cuffs. "The rooms are small. The pay is modest. The view is the best in the city, and the company—" he glanced, dry as dust, at the folded blanket in the corner, "—the company has been known to improve."

Sebastian laughed. It came out rough, half-swallowed, the way his laughs had done since January, but it came out. "I accept," he said. "Now. Before you revise the recommendation."

"Too late. I already revised it upward." Voss was already moving to the door, because sentiment, in his economy, was like lubricant — necessary, but applied sparingly. At the threshold he stopped. "There is one condition," he said. "The Board requires the assistant to maintain correspondence records. Scientific correspondence. Meteorological societies, instrument makers, that sort of thing." His wren-quick eyes flicked to the letter on the stool. "I trust you'll manage the volume."

* * *

Spring settled in the way it does in cities built on rivers: mud first, then green, then the sudden extravagant insistence of chestnut blossoms along the embankment. Sebastian learned the rhythm of the observatory as a home rather than a workplace — the dawn readings, the afternoon when sunlight struck the meridian marks, the evenings when he stayed late not because the work required it but because the pendulum's swing sounded, to him now, less like machinery and more like a heartbeat he had permission to keep.

In May he received a parcel, postmarked past the third lock. Inside was a drawing in pencil — a clock tower on a hill above a river town, rendered with startling care, every wheel suggested correctly beneath the dial — and at the tower's base, small and unmistakable, two figures. One wore an apron. One wore a wool cap. Beneath, in her careful new schoolhand: *So you don't forget either.*

He had it framed and hung it in the clock room, beside the blanket he had still not moved, and Voss, passing it daily, remarked on nothing and dusted around it carefully, which from Voss was a blessing.

On midsummer's eve, the city held its festival, and the observatory hill filled with lanterns and fiddlers and couples leaning on the terrace rail, and the master clock struck ten over all of it, true to the second, and Sebastian stood in the doorway of his own small room above the gears and felt the strangest thing settle over him — not happiness exactly, something quieter and sturdier than that. Belonging, he thought. Not inherited. Earned. Like a good fit between wheel and pinion: invisible when done right, holding everything up.

Then the night porter knocked, apologetic, holding out a tray. "Brought up with the last packet, sir. Marked urgent."

On the tray lay a single envelope. The handwriting on it was a child's, grown more confident since May. Sebastian broke the seal, expecting another drawing, another report of arithmetic triumphs.

Instead, six words, underlined twice:

*Papa's partner was seen here. Asking questions.*

Below it, smaller:

*Come quickly. — P.*
