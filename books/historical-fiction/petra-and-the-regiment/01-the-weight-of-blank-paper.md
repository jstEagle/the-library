## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The river froze in the third week of November, and after that the town of Greywater might have been a place on no map at all.

Beatrix Halloran liked it best this way, though she would never have said so aloud. When the ice came, the barges stopped, and the soldiers stopped, and the letters from the capital stopped, and the workshop became a small warm island of lamplight and linseed oil where nothing moved except her hand. For five winters now she had lived like this — sealed in behind the shutters Master Aldous had built double-thick, working by candle because daylight in December barely qualified as light at all, keeping the stove fed just enough to keep the ink from freezing in its pots. The townspeople called her the widow Halloran, which was not true in any way that could be proven or disproven, and she let them call her that because a widow was a shape the world knew how to leave alone. A widow grieved. A widow kept to herself. A widow was permitted a lifetime of silences without anyone asking what the silences contained.

The atlas lay open before her on the great drafting table, all four feet of it, weighted down at the corners with the brass sea-horses Aldous had brought back from a surveying voyage before Beatrix was born. Thirty-one years of his life went into those pages, and five years of hers. *The Complete Hydrography and Interior Survey of the Northern Provinces,* the title page read, in lettering so fine you needed a lens to count the strokes. *Engraved and Drawn by A. Halloran, Master Cartographer.* No mention of any other hand. There never would be. She had made her peace with that erasure long ago, or told herself she had, in the way one makes peace with weather.

She dipped the pen and touched it to the coast.

The final coastlines. That phrase had lived in her head for so long it had worn smooth, the way a stone wears smooth in a pocket carried every day. The northern coast — the grey teeth of it, the fjords and shingle bars, the little towns whose names existed nowhere else but here and in the memory of fishermen — was the last unfinished country in the whole vast work. Aldous had surveyed it in the summer of the year he died. He had come home with his notebooks full and his hands cracked from salt wind, and he had spread the charts across this very table and said, in the voice he used when he was about to make her do something difficult: *You'll cut these yourself, Bea. My eyes are going. Yours are better.*

That had been eight months before they found him at the bottom of the river stair with a split skull and an empty brandy bottle beside him, and the coroner — a coroner who owed his appointment to people in the capital — had written the word *accident* in a hand much steadier than a grieving man's should be.

Beatrix did not think about that. She had become very skilled, over five years, at not thinking about things. It was a discipline like any other, and she practiced it the way she practiced lettering: daily, precisely, without sentiment.

The pen moved. Cape Verren. The Skerries of the Nine Sisters, nine careful dots with soundings written between them in numerals two millimeters tall. The estuary at Cold Harbour, where the tide ran wrong twice a day and drowned a horse and rider every generation, and where Aldous had spent three weeks taking soundings in a leaking boat while swearing with an eloquence she had never heard matched before or since. She drew the water as he had taught her to draw water — not as a flat blue absence but as a living thing, hatched and feathered, shallow here and treacherous there, with its sandbanks shifting under the lines like thoughts under a face. A map lied if it pretended the world stood still. That was the first thing he had ever taught her, the week she arrived at his door at eleven years old, orphaned and furious, clutching a letter of introduction from a dead father. *A map is a promise,* Aldous Halloran had said, tapping the paper with a forefinger stained black to the second knuckle. *And the only honest promise is one that admits it will be broken.*

She worked until her shoulders burned, and the candle guttered, and she trimmed the wick and worked more.

* * *

At midnight she allowed herself the luxury of standing up.

Her back crackled. The stove ticked as the fire settled. Through the shutters the wind worried at the ice on the river, a long low moaning that the townsfolk said sounded like a woman crying and that Beatrix had decided, years ago, was simply physics — air moving under pressure through gaps in the frozen surface. Everything was physics if you refused to make it anything else.

She heated water for tea she mostly wouldn't drink, out of habit rather than appetite, and while it warmed she walked the length of the workshop as she did every night at this hour. It was a ritual, though she'd have denied the word. Ritual implied belief. This was inspection.

The engraving room, with its bed of copper plates wrapped in wool against the cold. The press itself, iron and oak, older than either of them, its screw greased and waiting. The drying racks hung with proofs like laundry. The shelves of Aldous's library — three hundred volumes, most of them annotated in margins so dense the printed text seemed merely a frame around his handwriting. The locked drawer in the map chest where the original surveys slept in their oilcloth. The false drawer beneath it, which no visitor had ever found, and which held —

Which held nothing now, actually. She had emptied it in October, burned the contents in the stove piece by piece, and told herself it was prudence. Letters were dangerous. Papers were dangerous. Anything written in a hand that could be identified was dangerous, and she had spent five years learning exactly how dangerous, in the slow way, the way you learn how deep a river is by wading in up to your chin.

Except one thing. One thing she had not burned, folded into oilcloth at the very back, and she did not permit herself to look at it now, and the not-looking took more discipline than everything else she did all day combined.

She drank her tea standing at the window gap between the shutters, watching the frozen river hold the moonlight. Greywater lay dark along both banks — two hundred houses, one bridge, one church, one tavern with a reputation, and perhaps six hundred souls who thought of themselves, when they thought of the empire at all, as being at the edge of it. The capital was eleven days' hard ride south, and might as well have been the moon. Out there past the ice, somewhere south, the empire was busy coming apart at the seams — she gathered as much from the almanacs and the occasional peddler — regiments redeployed, ministries reshuffled, ministers arrested, whole provinces changing allegiance the way ice changes to water. None of it reached Greywater except as rumor, and rumor froze out of the air by January.

Good, she thought, and then caught herself, and hated the smallness of the thought. Good that the world stayed away. Good that no one came. She had arranged her life into a fortress with a single gate, and the gate opened only for flour and lamp oil and the printer's boy collecting finished plates, and she had believed — had *needed* to believe — that this was enough. That a person could spend a life preserving a dead man's truth instead of living her own, and call it a life.

The tea went cold in her hands. She drank it anyway.

* * *

It was near three in the morning, in the deepest quiet, that the crack came.

Not in the ice outside. In her.

She had returned to the table and was cutting the final headland — Cape Verren's northern face, the last unfilled space on the last unfinished sheet, the true end of thirty-six years of another person's labor — and she paused, as she always paused at this point in the coast, to check the detail against Aldous's field notebook, which lay open beside her. And the notebook fell open, as notebooks do, to a page near the middle rather than the end, and she saw his handwriting, and something in her chest gave way with a sound she almost heard.

Because she could not remember his face.

She sat very still, pen lifted, ink gathering in a dark bead at the nib, and tested it again the way you probe a tooth you suspect has gone bad. His voice she had — that dry gravel, the way he said *Bea* with the *a* clipped short, the humming he did when measuring, tuneless and constant, a sound she had complained about for twenty years and would give her remaining sight to hear once more. His hands she had absolutely: broad, scarred at the thumb where a chisel had slipped in '41, always inked, nails bitten when a survey wasn't going well. The smell of him — tobacco and tallow and river water. The weight of his arm across her shoulders the day the first completed plate came off the press and he'd laughed, actually laughed, a rare event recorded in her memory like a comet.

But his face. She closed her eyes and reached for it and got — a beard, grey-brown, yes, and heavy brows, and eyes that she wanted to say were brown but might have been grey, and beyond that the portrait dissolved into a sketch of a face rather than a face, an outline waiting to be colored in by someone who remembered. Five years. Five years of living inside his work, breathing his air, sleeping in his house, wearing down his pens and sitting in his chair, and the face had quietly left her, the way faces do, without ceremony, without even a goodbye, while she wasn't guarding it.

The ink bead swelled and dropped onto the margin and she blotted it fast, heart hammering, absurdly guilty, as though the blot were a crime against him.

"You're forgetting me," she said aloud to the empty workshop, and heard how young her own voice sounded, and hated that too.

He would have had something for this. Some dry proverb. *Grief is a cartographer's error, Bea — you keep drawing the person from the old survey instead of walking the ground again.* Or worse, he'd have said nothing at all and put a pencil in her hand and pointed her back at the coastline, because in his theology work was the only prayer that counted, and feeling sorry for yourself was a form of idleness and idleness a form of lying.

So she did what his ghost demanded. She bent back over the cape and cut the northern face of Cape Verren, stroke by stroke, and she made it perfect, and she made it in his name, and the discipline closed over the crack like ice forming over a hole in a river — not healing it, never healing it, only covering it, so that anyone crossing could pass safely and never know the black water underneath.

By dawn the coastlines were done. All of them. Every mile of every shore in the northern provinces, drawn true, engraved fair, ready for the pull of proof sheets that would precede the printing of perhaps four hundred copies of the greatest atlas the empire had ever ignored. She signed the plate — *A. Halloran fecit* — in letters she had practiced for a year until no examiner alive could tell them from the dead man's hand.

Then she cleaned the pen, banked the stove, and stood a moment in the middle of her fortress with her arms wrapped around herself, listening to the river groan under its ice.

Five years of silence, finished. The work was done. In spring, when the boats came, the atlas would go south under a name that was not hers, and that would be the end of it, and she would go on being the widow Halloran until she was an actual one or until she died of nothing in particular in a warm room full of beautiful lies.

She blew out the candles and went up to bed, and slept without dreaming, which she counted as a mercy.

* * *

The knocking woke her while the sky was still the color of dirty snow.

Three blows, small ones, spaced too far apart — the knocks of someone who had to gather courage between each. Not the printer's boy, who hammered like a debt collector. Not a neighbor; neighbors didn't knock at her door at all anymore, having learned. Beatrix lay in the cold dark and listened and felt the fortress register the intrusion the way a spider feels a fly touch the outermost thread.

Again: three small knocks. Then a fourth, fainter, as if the knocker were falling asleep on her feet.

Beatrix rose, pulled her coat over her nightdress, took down the poker — old habits, old fears, and she no longer asked herself what she was afraid of, because the answer had a name and the name had a rank and thinking of it was forbidden — and went down the stairs through the freezing workshop, past the finished atlas gleaming faintly on its table like something holy, and unbarred the door.

Dawn stood grey behind the visitor. Snow had fallen in the night, an inch of it, unsullied except for one line of small footprints leading up from the river road, and at the end of the footprints stood a child.

A girl, seven years old perhaps, maybe less — half starved, hard to say. Frost in her eyebrows. Lips cracked and dark with cold. No coat, only a blanket that might once have been a soldier's greatcoat, pinned at the throat with a rusted pin. Bare feet gone past red into a purple that made Beatrix's stomach drop. And clutched against her chest with both arms, the way a child holds a doll, or a drowning sailor holds driftwood: half of a leather dispatch case, army-stamped, torn raggedly down the middle, the strap dangling.

The girl looked up at her. Her eyes were enormous and perfectly calm, the way eyes go calm when they have seen the end of calm forever, and she did not cry, and she did not speak, and when Beatrix stepped aside from the doorway — breaking, in that motion, every rule she had built her life upon — the child walked past her into the workshop as though she had been expected, leaving small bloody prints on the clean floor, and stood in the warmth looking up at the hanging maps with an expression that Beatrix would spend the rest of her life trying to describe.

Tucked into the broken strap of the case was a note, folded once, cheap paper, a single line in a stranger's hurried hand:

*Forget the face.*
