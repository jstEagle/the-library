## 2. A Knock at the Ice

The knock came at the hour when the river was still deciding whether it was ice or water.

Beatrix heard it as a sound she had trained herself never to hear: three small blows against the workshop door, soft, uneven, spaced too far apart to belong to any adult hand. She was standing at the drafting table with a pen full of Prussian blue in her fingers, inking the last of the northern coastline — the headland where Aldous had once written *here be honest rocks* in his marginal hand before scratching it out and drawing instead the careful, official lie that the survey demanded. The blue trembled and put down a blot the size of a currant on the water just off the point.

She did not move. That was the first rule of the workshop, the one she had carved into herself five years ago with all the force of grief turned inward: *no one comes here; no one is let in; nothing is said.* The town of Verrin-on-Aldwater knew the Halloran workshop as a place where an old man had died and where his widow — they assumed widow, they assumed daughter-in-law, they assumed anything except the truth — kept up the map trade alone. They brought her commissions through the letter slot. They collected finished plates from the stone step. In five years she had spoken aloud to perhaps forty people, and never for longer than the time it took to name a price.

The knock came again. Three blows, then a pause, then two more, weaker.

It was not a creditor. Creditors knocked like fists. It was not the colonel's men — she did not know what their knock would sound like, but she knew it would not come at dawn, and it would not be patient, and there would be more than one pair of boots on her step. This was something small. Something that had walked a long way and was now leaning its whole weight against the door because it could not lift its arm properly anymore.

Beatrix set down the pen. She wiped the blot away with the corner of her sleeve and ruined the sleeve doing it, and stood listening to her own heartbeat, which was behaving disgracefully, quick and loud, like a girl's.

*Do not answer it.*

That voice was Aldous's voice — or rather it was the voice she had built out of everything he'd taught her, the discipline he had worn like armor and died without. *A cartographer keeps clean margins,* he used to say. *Whatever bleeds over the edge of the frame ruins the whole sheet.* She had taken that literally. She had made her life a margin. No bleeding edges. Nothing living inside the frame but her work.

The knock came a third time, and this time there was a sound after it that undid her entirely: a small cough, the wet rattling kind, and then silence — the particular silence of someone who has decided that if help is coming, it will have to come on its own, and who sits down to wait for whatever happens next.

Beatrix crossed the workshop. She passed the drying racks hung with proofs like laundry, passed the great press under its dust sheet shaped like a sleeping animal, passed the locked cabinet where the master atlas lay in its half-finished glory, and reached the door with its iron bolts she had installed herself, four of them, each one a vow.

She drew them back. All four. And hated herself even as she did it, even as some caged thing in her chest lifted its head and growled *finally.*

* * *

The cold hit her first. Dawn on the river in late winter was not cold so much as a presence, a gray-white weight that pressed through wool and skin into the teeth. The fog off the Aldwater had frozen into rime on every surface, so that the world looked furred, muffled, drawn in white ink. Her step was glazed. Beyond it, the lane ran empty in both directions, and beyond the lane the river lay like a road to nowhere, ice-locked, holding its breath.

And on the step, sitting in the drift where the wall met the doorframe, was a child.

A girl, by the coat — though the coat gave no real evidence, being a soldier's greatcoat cut down and re-stitched, gray wool gone greenish, sleeves rolled twice at the cuffs. She was perhaps seven years old, perhaps younger; malnutrition made arithmetic unreliable. Her hair was dark, cut short and crookedly, as if done in a hurry with shears meant for cloth. Her face was the color of the fog. And her hands —

Her hands were bad. Beatrix saw that immediately, with the clinical part of her mind that assessed damage the way other people assessed weather. The left glove was gone entirely, and the fingers were swollen, waxy, white at the tips going gray-blue at the nails. Frostbite, second degree at least, maybe third. The right hand was gloved and clenched around an object the child had evidently refused to release even while her body failed around it: half of a leather dispatch case, torn cleanly along the spine, its brass fittings green with age, its strap bitten through.

The child looked up at her. Her eyes were enormous, dark, and utterly calm — not the calm of trust but the calm of someone who had run out of expectations. She did not cry. She did not beg. She looked at Beatrix the way one looks at a door that might open or might not, having already survived both possibilities.

"Where is your mother?" Beatrix asked, and was startled by the sound of her own voice — rough, rusted, a hinge unused for seasons. "Your father? Anyone?"

The child opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She tried again, working her jaw like someone testing a lock, and produced only a thread of breath. Then, slowly, deliberately, she shook her head once — *no* — and Beatrix could not tell whether the gesture meant *I don't know* or *I won't say* or simply *there is no such person anymore.* There are kinds of no that contain whole histories.

"You can't stay on my step."

The child nodded, as if this were reasonable, and began — with terrible dignity — to push herself upright against the wall. Her legs buckled. She caught herself on the doorframe with the damaged hand and made no sound at all, which was worse than screaming. Beatrix watched the small body fight gravity and lose, slide back down into the drift, and settle again into waiting, and something in her chest that had been locked for five years broke its chain all at once.

"Inside," she said. "Now. Before I change my mind."

She got an arm around the child and hauled her over the threshold, kicked the door shut behind her, and threw the bolts home again — all four, faster than she had ever drawn them — and only then, in the sudden candle-warm dimness of the workshop, surrounded by the smell of linseed oil and paper and her own five years of careful silence, did she understand what she had done.

She had let the world past her margin.

* * *

Frostbite must not be thawed fast. This she knew from Aldous, who had marched with survey teams in the northern campaigns and had lost two toes to the Vharnic passes and spoken of it only as a lesson in measurement: *the body, like the plate, must be warmed gradually, or it cracks.* So Beatrix sat the child by the stove — the little iron stove in the corner that heated the workshop and was banked low during the day to keep the humidity steady for the inks — and fed the fire splinter by splinter, raising the temperature by degrees, and held the ruined left hand wrapped in cloth soaked in cool water while the girl watched her with those bottomless dark eyes.

The swelling was worst in the index and middle finger. Beatrix worked quietly, professionally, saying nothing, because saying things was not in her nature anymore, and the child said nothing either, which Beatrix told herself was convenient, until she understood that it was not convenience. The girl was not silent the way the tired or the shy are silent. She was silent the way a burned house is silent. Something had been taken out of her.

When the fingers had flushed from white to angry red — blood returning, the good sign, the painful sign — Beatrix bound them in linen strips and lard, wrapped the whole hand in a sock with the toe cut off, and went to find food. There was bread, two days old. There was dripping. There was weak tea, because strong tea was for emergencies and she was not yet willing to admit what kind of morning this was. She put all of it in front of the child and watched her eat with a controlled ferocity, small bites swallowed fast, eyes always moving — to the door, to the window, to Beatrix's hands, to the dispatch case, which the girl had placed on the floor beside her chair and anchored with her boot, and which she checked between every mouthful the way a sailor checks the horizon.

Only when the bread was gone did Beatrix notice the second thing pinned beneath the strap of the case. A folded square of paper, grimy, creased into eighths, handled many times.

She took it. The child let her take it — watched her do so with a fixed, deliberate permission, as though handing over the last item in an inventory — and Beatrix unfolded it under the lamp.

The handwriting was a soldier's hand, blocky, hurried, the letters of a man who wrote rarely and badly:

*Mistress Halloran. She is the loose end. They will come for what she carries. Burn the case, forget the face, and you were never seen. I am sorry. — T.C.*

Beatrix read it three times.

The words moved on the page the way words move when the blood leaves your head. *Mistress Halloran* — so whoever T.C. was, he knew her name, knew her cover, knew the fiction of the widow that the town had built around her without her asking. *They will come.* *Burn the case.* *Forget the face.*

And the child, sitting at her stove with her bandaged hand in her lap and her boot still resting on the leather case like a woman resting her foot on a chest of gold, looked up at Beatrix and waited to see whether the instruction would be obeyed.

Five years of discipline rose in Beatrix like floodwater. *Burn the case.* It would be easy. The stove was right there. The case was leather and brass and paper and would go up in a minute, and the child could be fed and warmed and given to the Sisters at the almshouse across the bridge with a story about foundlings, and the margin would close over all of it, clean, unbled, safe. Safe. Five years she had bought safety at the price of everything else — at the price of her own name on her own work, at the price of speaking above a whisper, at the price of ever again being the person who ran laughing down the towpath with her sleeves rolled up. She had paid. She had paid punctually. She had thought the account settled.

But the account, apparently, had reopened. Because Aldous had died for something, and she had spent five years telling herself she knew what, and here was a scrap of a soldier's handwriting suggesting she had known almost none of it — and the proof of the rest was sitting under a seven-year-old's boot, and the men who wanted it burned knew her name.

"Who gave you this?" she asked. "T.C. Who is T.C.?"

The child's mouth worked. Her throat moved. A tear slid down the wax-pale cheek without any change of expression at all, the way ice melts off stone, and no sound followed it. She pointed — with her good hand, clumsily, the bandaged one held stiff against her chest — first at the dispatch case, then at her own eyes, then out toward the river.

The case. Her eyes. The river.

Then she pointed at the case again, and this time touched her own forehead, and folded her small fists together in a shape that Beatrix, after a moment, recognized: a book closing. A record being kept.

*I carried it. I saw it. From the river. I remember.*

Beatrix crouched down until her face was level with the child's, close enough to see that the dark irises were flecked with amber, close enough to see the chapped split at the corner of the small mouth and the exhaustion underneath the calm like a crack running under paint. Her conscience, that caged animal, was pressing against the bars so hard it hurt.

"I'm going to ask you your name," Beatrix said slowly. "You don't have to say it. You can shake your head. But if you ever want to say it — to me, only me, in this room where no one else will ever hear — then this is the room, and I am the person, and today is as good a day as any."

The child studied her for a long time. Somewhere outside, far off, the ice on the Aldwater groaned — a long, deep, tectonic complaint, the sound of a river turning over in its sleep, and both of them glanced toward the window at it, and something passed between them that neither would have been able to explain: the knowledge that winter does not hold forever, and that everything frozen eventually moves.

The girl shook her head. *No name.* Then, deliberately, she picked up the stub of charcoal Beatrix kept on the hearth ledge for marking stove temperatures, and took the slate from the nail beside the door — the slate on which Beatrix recorded barometric readings — and erased a week of careful numbers with her sleeve.

On the blank slate she drew, in wobbly, determined strokes, a boat. An overturned boat, hull up, with lines radiating from it that might have been water or might have been hair floating. Beside the boat, a stick figure lying very still.

Then she stopped. Looked at the drawing. Looked at Beatrix. And scrubbed it out with her palm, all of it, furiously, until the slate was black and clean, and handed the charcoal back, and folded her hands, and was once more the calmest object in the room.

Beatrix stood there with the charcoal in her fingers and her heart going like a hammer in a stamp-mill, and understood that she had just watched a child decide, with the same deliberateness she herself applied to inking coastlines, exactly how much truth could safely be shown — and that the decision had been made to protect *her*, Beatrix, the stranger, the woman with the bolted door.

"They'll come for you," Beatrix said quietly. It was not a question and not quite a promise. It was the margin breaking open. "Whoever they are — the ones the note speaks of — they'll come here. For what you carry. For you." She crouched again. "So you need to understand what you've walked into, little one. This is a house where a man already died for what he wouldn't stop saying. Do you understand me?"

The child looked at her with those amber-flecked eyes, and nodded once — slow, grave, unsurprised — and Beatrix felt the last of her resistance go out of her like heat from an opened kiln.

"Well, then," she said, in the tone Aldous had used when a commission arrived that was impossible, unethical, and payable — the tone of a professional accepting a job she should refuse. "We'll need names. Not yours — we'll make you one. Petra. Stone. Something that doesn't break." The child blinked at the word, tasting it. "Petra. Yes. And rules — there will be rules, more rules than you've ever heard in your life, because staying alive in this house is a craft like any other, and I am very good at my craft."

She gathered the blankets from the cot behind the partition wall — the cot she had never once needed in five years and had never once let herself remove — and wrapped the child in them, and banked the stove higher than the inks could safely bear, and told herself she would worry about humidity later.

Then, at last, she looked down at the dispatch case.

Half a case, torn along the spine, strap bitten through, brass green with age. Military issue — she knew the pattern, Aldous had carried ten thousand of them in his survey years, and this one was old, older than the war's end, older perhaps than the child. The torn edge was not worn. It had been ripped recently, violently, by someone in a hurry, and the bite mark on the strap —

Beatrix went very still.

The bite mark was small. Small teeth. A child's teeth, clamped down on leather hard enough to punch through, and she looked at the girl wrapped in blankets by the stove — at the set of that stubborn jaw — and understood that somewhere between the river and this door, someone had tried to take this case from her, and the child had held on with her teeth.

Whatever was inside, the girl had chosen it over her own hands. Over her own skin.

Beatrix knelt, and unbuckled the strap with fingers that were not entirely steady, and opened the flap.

* * *

The light of the stove fell across the contents, and Beatrix Halloran forgot how to breathe.

Paper. Orders — regimental route orders, the printed-and-handwritten kind she had spent her whole life reproducing, the kind whose authority lived in their precise, boring exactness. This one was torn in half. But the surviving half bore a heading she knew better than scripture: *Ninth Regiment of Foot — Route Order — Confidential.* And across its face, in a hand she would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the river, in death —

— in Aldous's hand, tight and furious, crowding the printed columns with annotations, arrows, corrections, a whole private argument conducted in ink against the official line —

— was written the route the Ninth had actually taken, marching north-northeast, off every map, into country where the official record swore no soldier of the Ninth had ever been ordered to go.

And beneath it, underlined twice, in her dead mentor's writing, one sentence:

*They were sent where no order says they went. Find who signed it. — A.H.*

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, closer. Beatrix did not look up. She was staring at the handwriting of a dead man, in a room that had just become, without her consent and with her entire heart, a battlefield.
