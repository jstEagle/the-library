## 3. What the Case Contains

The child slept like something drowned.

Beatrix had laid her on the cot behind the paper press, wrapped in the grey army blanket she kept for the coldest nights of plate-polishing, and still the girl's fingers had stayed curled around that half of a dispatch case as though someone might prise it from her even in sleep. Beatrix had tried once — gently, gently — to slide it free, and the child's whole body had convulsed, a small animal sound rising in her throat, her eyes flying open though they saw nothing, fixed on some middle distance where perhaps soldiers were still walking.

So Beatrix had let her keep it. She had tucked the blanket instead, and sat down at her own workbench with a lamp turned low, and looked at the thing from across the room for the better part of an hour before she dared approach it again.

It was half a case. That was the first fact, and it was not nothing. Dispatch cases came in pairs — a leather shell and its stiffened interior frame, buckled face to face so that papers rode between them like meat in a sandwich. This was the front half only: the flap, the buckle-plates, the brass corner-caps gone green at the seams. The back half, with its hinges and lock-plate, was gone entirely, torn away along a line that was not torn but *cut* — she saw it when she turned the leather under the lamp, the blade-work clean and deliberate, done by someone who had wanted what was inside and had not wanted to take the whole weight of the case away with him.

Or by someone who had wanted the case to be found light enough to float.

The river had given it up, then. That was the second fact. The child had come out of the dark along the tow-path with frost in her hair and ice-melt soaking her boots, and the leather in her arms was swollen with water, stiff as board in places, soft as skin in others. Someone upstream — or upriver, which was worse, which was *weeks* ago — had put this into the ice and let the river do the carrying. The river did not carry things to people at random. The river carried things to the places rivers knew, and every surveyor on this stretch of water, living and dead, had taught the river where the workshop stood.

Aldous would have said it differently. Aldous would have said, *the water remembers where we live, girl; we spent thirty years telling it.*

She missed him most in the mornings, and it was morning now, the grey coming up through the frosted glass of the south windows, laying itself across the benches and the hanging rolls of paper and the great flat table where the atlas lay open to its last unfinished sheet. She had been working when the knock came — no, not a knock. A scratching. Like a bird against the door. She had nearly not opened it. Five years of rules pressed down on her hand at the latch: *open to no one before full daylight, open to no one you cannot see the hands of, open to no one at all when the river is frozen because frozen rivers mean soldiers.* And then she had opened it anyway, and there had been the child, and there had been the note pinned inside the case-flap with a rusted pin, and she had read it standing in the doorway with the cold pouring past her ankles:

*forget the face*

Three words. No signature. The handwriting was cramped and hurried and it leaned hard to the right, and Beatrix had stared at it for a long moment in the doorway because there was something in the formation of the letters — the way the *f* crossed low, the way the *t* was struck twice like a man insisting — that pulled at a memory she could not surface, the way a shape under ice pulls at a skater.

She had the note now, flattened under the corner of her inkstone, and she looked at it again while the child slept, and the memory would not come, and the not-coming of it frightened her more than the note itself.

*Forget the face.* Whose face? The child's? As in: do not look too long at her, do not learn her, do not make yourself responsible? Or — and here the thought arrived cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts always did — as in: *she has seen a face, and if you value either of you, you will never ask her whose.*

Beatrix unbuckled the case.

The straps fought her; the leather had dried unevenly through the night and the tongue of the near buckle had swelled into its hole. She worked it loose with a bone folder, taking her time, making herself slow when everything in her chest wanted speed. Discipline. That was the whole of what Aldous had left her, in the end — not the workshop, not the plates, not even the atlas, but the discipline of slowness. *A mapmaker who hurries,* he used to say, *is only a faster kind of liar.*

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had kept the water out better than any of them could have hoped, were papers.

Three of them. She counted them twice, touching each one as if it might go off. The topmost was a supply requisition, routine, regimental stamp blurred beyond reading. The bottom was a letter, private hand, sealed with wax that had cracked along the fold — she did not break it further; she set it aside unopened, and her heart was hammering at the mere weight of it, at the presumption folded inside it, whatever names it held.

The middle paper was a route order.

She knew the format the way she knew her own hands. Route orders for marching regiments came off the Surveyor-General's presses in the capital in runs of hundreds, all of them built on the base sheets her own master had engraved — she had inked the border ornaments of that very template, aged nineteen, her first paid work, the year the Ninth Regiment was still a name in the gazette and not yet a silence. This one bore the Ninth's number. It bore a date in early autumn five years past. It bore the columns of stages and halts and rations-drawn that every quartermaster filled in with his neat clerk's fist.

And it bore annotations in Aldous Halloran's hand.

Not his fair hand — his working hand, the small fierce script he used on field proofs, the letters crowded together like men huddled against weather. Beatrix had spent five years transcribing that hand, deciphering it, learning its tempers, and she would have known it blindfolded, known it underwater, known it in a dream. It was there in the margin beside the third stage of the march: *no such road.* There again beside the fifth: *bridge out since '09 — order must be wrong OR route falsified.* And then, running vertically up the left edge of the sheet in strokes so hard the pen had near-cut the paper:

*THEY WENT NORTH OF THE FORD. I HAVE THE SURVEY. THIS SHEET IS A LIE AND THE MAN WHO SIGNED IT KNOWS WHY.*

Below that, smaller, almost gentle, as if he had caught himself shouting:

*B. — if this reaches you, finish it.*

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The frost on the windows made a faint ticking as the sun found it. Somewhere behind her the child breathed, small and even, and Beatrix became aware that she had been holding her own breath long enough for her vision to spot at the edges.

She put both hands flat on the bench and breathed until the spots went away.

Five years. Five years she had told herself the story the coroner's man had handed down like a receipt: Master cartographer Aldous Halloran, sixty-one years of age, slipped on the wet stones of the river stair in the month of the thaw, struck his head, drowned in eighteen inches of water. A tragedy. An accident. The town had believed it because the town wanted to believe things were simple, and Beatrix had believed it because believing otherwise meant believing that someone in this world had wanted Aldous dead, and that the workshop's careful silences had not protected him after all, and that she had buried a murder under a headstone and called it grief.

Now the paper lay in front of her saying *finish it*, and the word *accident* curdled in her stomach like bad milk.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, plain as a scale bar. The official record — she had copied it herself, years ago, for the atlas's regimental appendix — recorded that the Ninth Regiment, having marched out on this date, proceeded by the southern road to the garrison at Veldmaren, arriving understrength but accounted for, losses attributed to fever. The southern road. The bridge that had been out since '09. A route that could not be walked, annotated in her master's hand with the certainty of a man who had surveyed every stone of it.

But the route order in her hands — this copy, this one surviving copy — showed the northern stages. North of the ford. Into the high country where the atlas she had spent five years finishing showed, on Aldous's final plates, nothing but blank terrain and a dotted conjecture-line marked *unverified.* She had always assumed the blanks were gaps in his surveying, gaps the winter and then his death had closed over forever. Now she understood they might be gaps he had been forbidden to fill. Gaps other people needed empty.

The Ninth had gone north. The record said south. Somewhere between those two sentences lay whatever had happened to the two hundred men the gazette had quietly dropped from the roll between muster and arrival — the losses attributed, in three bland words, to fever.

Fever does not require a falsified route order. Fever does not require a man who surveys roads to write *THIS SHEET IS A LIE* in pen-strokes that tear paper. Fever does not need anyone to forget its face.

Beatrix looked at the sleeping child, and the thought arrived whole, without mercy: *the fever has a face, and it is seven years old, and someone cut this case in half and gave it to the river rather than let it reach a shore.*

* * *

She did not wake her. That was the discipline, again — the slowness. She spent the morning doing ordinary things with extraordinary care: banking the stove, grinding ink, sweeping the step, laying out bread and dripping and the last of the honey for when the child woke. Her hands performed the workshop's liturgy while her mind ran north of the ford again and again, pacing out the stages, cross-checking the annotated distances against the base sheets in the plan-chest, and finding — this was the part that made her sit down abruptly on the stool, mid-motion, flour on her fingers from the bread — finding that the numbers *fit*. Every stage distance Aldous had scrawled in the margin matched, within a furlong, the measurements of the northern tracks she had spent five years assuming were conjecture. He had surveyed the true road. He had carried it in his head and in his field books, and the route order proved he had walked its paper double, and somewhere between the two of them the truth had been cut in half and set adrift.

By afternoon the child woke, and Beatrix learned the third fact of the day, which was that you cannot feed a mute child honey-bread and ask her questions in words and expect anything back but those enormous grey eyes going flat and shut like windows painted over.

So she stopped asking in words.

She cleared a space on the low table by the stove. She took a sheet of her cheapest stock — good paper, actually, better than the child deserved, but Beatrix's hands refused to give a child anything but the best stock in the house — and she sharpened a piece of charcoal from the drawing tin, and she knelt down on the flags opposite her, knee to knee, at the level of a seven-year-old's eyes.

"My name is Beatrix," she said. "This is my workshop. Nobody comes here. Nobody looks in here." She drew as she spoke, because the child watched the charcoal the way starving people watch bread. A rectangle for the workshop. A wavy line for the river. "You came from along the river."

She drew the tow-path. Then she stopped, and held out the charcoal, handle-first, the way you offer anything to a creature you are trying not to frighten.

For a long time the child did not move. The fire ticked. Outside, far down the frozen river, the ice boomed once — that deep settling groan it made in the cold of late afternoon, the sound Beatrix had lived with for five winters and never once heard as anything but the sound of being alone. The child flinched at it. Her eyes went to the window, to the white glare of the river, and something moved behind them, quick and terrible, like fish turning under ice.

Then she took the charcoal.

She drew the way Beatrix had seen exhausted surveyors draw: fast, certain, without lifting the stick more than she had to. First the river, wider than Beatrix's, with a ford marked by three short strokes — *the ford*, drawn with a child's emphasis, drawn twice over, pressed hard. Then a track leaving the ford and climbing — zigzag switchbacks, rendered with the impatient fluency of someone who had walked them, had walked them *at night*, had stumbled on them. Then a valley, cupped between two hills, and in the valley a cluster of little marks — houses, walls — and beside them a larger rectangle with a line of small stick figures marching into it.

Soldiers. Going into a walled enclosure in a valley north of the ford.

The child's hand stopped. Hovered. Then, below the rectangle, she drew a row of figures lying flat on the ground, small and straight and side by side, more of them than Beatrix could count at a glance — twenty, thirty, drawn in the swift unhesitating rhythm of a hand that had drawn this before, many times, in the dark, in the only language left to it.

Rows of men on the ground. In a valley that appeared on Aldous's finished plates as blank terrain.

Beatrix did not move. She was afraid that if she moved — reached out, spoke, breathed too loudly — the hand would stop, the windows would paint themselves over, and the child would carry it all back down into wherever she kept it. So she knelt, knee to knee, and watched a seven-year-old map a massacre in eleven strokes of charcoal, and understood that she was looking at the only surviving copy of the northern route that mattered: not the one in Aldous's margins, not the one in the torn order, but the one behind those flat grey eyes, drawn from memory because the memory had nowhere else to live.

The child added one thing more. At the top of her page, above the valley, she drew a single figure on a horse — bigger than the others, drawn last, drawn slowly, with a heavy black scribble where its face should be.

Then she put down the charcoal, and looked at Beatrix, and pointed — deliberately, gravely, the way a magistrate points — at the route order lying on the bench, and then at her own drawing, and then back again. *These go together.* She had understood exactly what the paper was. Whatever else had been taken from her, they had not taken that.

"North of the ford," Beatrix said quietly. Not a question. The child nodded once, a small precise dip of the chin, old as any officer's.

* * *

That evening Beatrix unlocked the plan-chest's lowest drawer — the false-bottomed one that had held nothing but Aldous's unused gravers for five years, because she could not bring herself to decide what belonged beneath it — and she laid the route order flat in the dark space beside his oldest field books, and she sat back on her heels and felt the shape of her life change around her like a house shifting on its foundations.

For five years the atlas had been a memorial. She had told herself that every morning at the lamp: that she inked coastlines and hill-shading and border fleurons in a dead man's name because love required a vessel and this was the one he'd left her. A safe work. A silent work. A beautiful lie of attribution, and nothing more.

Now it lay open on the great table under the lamp, and she looked at it and saw what it had become. Two hundred pages of authoritative ground. Every road, every ford, every valley — including, on the northern sheets, those careful blanks. A document that would pass any inspection, bear any seal, sit on any ministerial shelf and say *this is the land as it is.* And threaded through it, waiting only for a steady hand and a hidden plate, the true route of the Ninth: north of the ford, to the walled enclosure in the valley, to the rows of men lying flat in the snow.

An atlas in the wrong hands is a memorial. In the right hands, it is a weapon — the only kind that cuts without a blade, that cannot be burned out of ten thousand copies once the presses run, that tells its truth to whoever opens it for a hundred years.

Someone upstream had cut a case in half and trusted the river. Someone had written *forget the face* and sent a child through the frost to her door. The net was already closing; she could feel it in the way the ice boomed at dusk, in the way the town below had gone quiet, in the note under her inkstone leaning its cramped letters toward her like a warning she still couldn't place.

Beatrix banked the fire, checked the bolt on the door, and took out the finest graver in the drawer.

If they were coming, then the copper would be ready for them.
