## 4. The Colonel's Cartographer

The river town announced itself to Elias Vane the way all dying places did: by smell before sight. Tanneries and coal smoke and beneath it the green rot of a river locked under ice, holding its dead until spring. He stood at the crest of the coach road with his one valise in hand and let the wind off the water cut through his civilian coat — good wool, deliberately unremarkable, the coat of a man with money enough and nothing to prove — and told himself, as he had told himself for six hundred miles, that this was simply another errand.

Locate the case. Recover or destroy its contents. Resolve the loose end.

Colonel Merrick had used the word *resolve* with the mildness of a man discussing a leaky roof. *The loose end attached to it,* he had said, turning his signet ring, not looking up from his papers. *You'll know it when you see it.* Elias had known even then that the loose end was a child; the agent who'd preceded him had been explicit in his written briefing, which Merrick had burned after Elias read it. A girl of about seven, last seen on the ice above Kettering Ford. The dispatch case she'd carried was half-gone — torn, dragged, scarred by something that had happened out on the frozen river in the last week of winter — and whatever it held was worth enough to send a disgraced lieutenant three weeks' travel north to a town whose name appeared on no map of consequence.

Which was, of course, why it appeared on this one. Aldous Halloran had drawn this town twice in his career: once in the great coastal survey that made his name, and once more, small and careful, in the margins of an atlas that had never been published. Elias had spent four days in the capital's archive reading everything the army possessed on Master Cartographer Aldous Halloran, which amounted to two commendations, one invoice for copper plates, and a coroner's note. *Accidental death by misadventure. Slipped on the ice of his own courtyard. Closed.*

Two days after refusing, according to a marginal annotation someone had failed to burn, to certify a route correction.

Elias adjusted his collar and walked down into the town.

* * *

He gave himself three days of being nobody.

This was the craft of it, the part he had always been good at, back when being good at things still meant something. A man arriving with questions is a man noticed; a man arriving to buy is a man welcomed. So Elias Vane became Mr. Elias Vane, dealer in prints and geographical curiosities out of the southern markets, and he took a room at the Ferryman's Rest where the landlord talked too much and remembered too little, and he spent those first days doing what any legitimate buyer would do: drinking bad coffee, examining bad prints, and asking, with the apologetic air of a man embarrassed by his own enthusiasm, whether anyone in town still practiced the old engraving arts.

Everyone pointed him the same direction, eventually. Up the lane past the chandler's, where the cobbles gave way to packed earth, to a long low building of grey stone with a slate roof furred with frost, its single chimney breathing a thin thread of smoke into the white sky. A brass plate by the door, gone soft-edged with weather: **HALLORAN. CARTOGRAPHER & ENGRAVER.**

No new name. Five years since the master died, and the sign still bore only his.

"Shop's open," said a voice from within, when he knocked — a woman's voice, low and unhurried, with the particular flatness of someone speaking to be done with speaking. "Mind the ink on the floor."

* * *

The interior stopped him in the doorway, though he was careful not to show it.

He had grown up around maps — every officer had — but he had never stood inside the place where they were made. The workshop smelled of beeswax and lampblack and the cold mineral bite of acid, and it was dim in the way workshops are dim, light admitted grudgingly through high windows glazed with old glass, falling in long slanted blades across workbenches crowded with the instruments of precision: burins laid out like surgical steel, burnishers, scrapers, a rolling press along the far wall with its great star-wheel of ironwood. Copper plates leaned in racks against the stone, each one wrapped in paper against tarnish, each one, presumably, a country or a coastline waiting to exist.

And at the drafting table in the center of all that ordered gloom sat a woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands black to the second knuckle, bent over a plate so intently that she finished her stroke before she looked up.

"Ink," she said again, nodding at the floorboards, where dark stains bloomed like islands on an uncharted sea. "It doesn't come out. It never comes out."

"I'll keep to the dry ground." He smiled and offered his card, and watched her take it without wiping her hands, so that the corner of good stock came away smudged. She didn't appear to notice, or didn't care. That was his first observation about Beatrix Halloran — if it was Halloran; the resemblance to the master's portrait in the archive file was there in the jaw and the set of the brow, but five years was five years — and it was this: she handled the world the way she apparently handled her plates. Deliberately. Without wasted motion. As though everything that came near her would be measured first and permitted second.

"Elias Vane," he said. "Prints and geographical curiosities. I'm told you're the last real hand left on this stretch of the river."

"Told by whom?"

"The chandler. Two men at the Ferryman's. A boy selling chestnuts who seemed to think you were a witch." Another smile, easy, self-deprecating. She received it the way a wall receives weather.

"What do you want to buy, Mr. Vane?"

"Coastal surveys, chiefly. Old ones especially. There's a market in the south for the pre-war charts — the accuracy of them, the craft. Everything drawn since has been—" he gestured vaguely, letting the sentence die, and watched to see whether she would finish it for him.

She didn't. She set his card down beside a jar of brushes and waited.

So he tried the other door, the one he'd rehearsed on the road. "I understand the late master kept unpublished material. Private commissions. I'd pay well for access — legitimately, of course, with the family's blessing, if there is a family."

"There's me." Her eyes came up fully then, and he saw they were grey, the grey of the river ice outside, and that behind the wariness in them was something older and more tired than suspicion alone could account for. "There's no unpublished material for sale. There's the atlas, and it's spoken for, and there's repair work, and I'm behind on both. If you want charts, I have coastal surveys of the northern approaches, twenty years old, accurate to the width of my smallest burin. If you want something else—"

"I want exactly what I say I want."

"No one wants exactly what they say they want." She said it without heat, almost absently, already bending back toward her plate, and dismissed him with the tilt of her head toward the racks. "Surveys are in the second rack. Prices are marked. Don't touch the copper bare-handed — the oil in skin ruins the bite."

* * *

He bought two charts he did not want, because a man who leaves empty-handed after asking about unpublished material is a man remembered, and paid in coin counted out slowly, and used the transaction as license to stand in the workshop another quarter hour while she wrapped the prints in brown paper with quick, exact folds.

He used the time the way he'd been trained to use time.

The workshop was a fortress pretending to be a shop, and now that he looked properly, he could see the fortification everywhere. The high windows were glazed with glass thick enough to stop a stone, and the frames were newer than the building. The door was oak, iron-banded, and the bolt that had been drawn back to admit him was the size of a man's forearm. The floor stains of ink were old and layered, years deep, but there were scuff marks in the dust along the base of the back wall that suggested heavy furniture moved often and recently. And there — half-hidden behind the rolling press, so casual it could only be deliberate — a second table, smaller, cleared utterly bare, its surface scrubbed pale, holding nothing at all in a room where every other surface held everything.

People who hid things, he knew, also hid the hiding of them.

"You knew the master?" he asked, conversational, examining a framed proof sheet on the wall — a beautiful thing, a river delta rendered in lines so fine they seemed breathed onto the paper rather than cut.

"My father." Not looking up. The burin whispered through wax.

"Then the loss was—"

"The atlas will be published in spring. Under his name. If you're interested in that, write to me then." The whispering stopped. She looked at him across the lamplight with the patience of a woman who had ended a thousand conversations this way. "Was there anything else, Mr. Vane?"

There was a question he wanted to ask — *why does a cartographer publish posthumously under a dead man's name, and why does the phrase 'under his name' sit in your mouth like a stone?* — and he did not ask it, because he was good at this, because detachment was the whole of his remaining profession, because the Colonel had chosen him precisely because Elias Vane had once proven, in a court-martial room that smelled of wet wool, that he could follow orders past the point where following orders cost him everything else.

"Only directions to a decent dinner," he said, "and my thanks for your time."

"Kitchen at the Ferryman's until eight. Mind the fish." A pause, and then, as he reached the door, so quietly he nearly missed it: "You walk like a soldier, Mr. Vane."

He turned. She had returned to her plate; the lamplight caught only the ink-black curve of her hands moving in their small exact orbit.

"Excuse me?"

"Civilians look where they're going. You look at exits." The burin whispered on. "Good day."

* * *

That night he wrote the report he was supposed to write and did not send it.

*Arrived 14th. Subject premises identified and entered under commercial pretext. One adult female resident, presumed daughter, sole occupant. No sign of the case. No sign of the child. Will continue surveillance.*

All true. Every word of it true, and arranged with the care of a man laying a table — and it sat in his breast pocket all evening while he ate the Ferryman's indifferent fish and listened to the landlord explain, unprompted, that the river ice had been groaning for three nights now, that the thaw was early this year, that the boats would come up from the garrison within the month and soldiers with them, and wasn't it strange, the landlord said, leaning in with the confiding air of a man selling information he didn't know he had, wasn't it strange how the army had come asking after lost property twice already this winter?

"Twice," Elias said.

"Before you, sir. A captain, back in the autumn — very polite, wanted to know if anyone had pulled anything odd out of the ice. And then a sergeant just last week, going house to house." The landlord shrugged. "River gives things back, see. Every spring. Drowned dogs. Fence posts. Once, God rest him, a tax collector. Folk hereabouts figure the ice keeps a ledger, and thaw is when it settles accounts."

Elias paid for his dinner and climbed to his cold room and sat by the window a long while, watching moonlight lie along the frozen river like a road drawn on the world.

A ledger. Yes. He knew something about ledgers. His own ran back seven years, to a bridge in a border province and an order he should have refused and hadn't, and a lieutenant's commission thereafter traded away piece by piece until the only thing he was fit for was this: walking into warm rooms full of light and instruments, wearing a civilian's coat over a soldier's spine, hunting a seven-year-old girl for a colonel who turned his signet ring when he lied.

*Resolve the loose end.* He heard it now with different ears. He had heard worse phrasing attached to worse things and had saluted anyway; that was the record; that was the ledger; that was why he was here, in exile dressed as employment, trusted with dirty work precisely because he had never once dropped it.

Through the window, upstream, the ice boomed — a long tectonic groan that shivered the frost ferns on the glass, the sound of a season changing its mind. The thaw was early. The boats would come. Whatever window existed between *no sign of the child* and the arrival of men who did not write reports, it was closing at the speed of warm weather.

He took out the unsent report, read it once, and put it away unburned — telling himself that was caution, that evidence destroyed was evidence missed, that a professional kept his options open. He was aware, distantly, in the part of himself that had once been honest, that this was the first lie he had ever told himself on the Colonel's behalf, and that it would not be the last.

* * *

He went back to the workshop on the third day, because a legitimate buyer would, and because watching the front of the building from the chandler's doorway for two hours had produced exactly one fact: no child had entered or left. Which meant either she was not there — and the search would move on, and the loose end would stay loose somewhere colder and further — or she was there, and had not been let out since dawn.

He knocked. "Shop's open. Mind the ink."

But today she was not at the central table. Today she stood at the back of the workshop with her body angled, quite deliberately, between him and the small bare table behind the rolling press — the table that had been scrubbed clean, that was scoured of dust, that now, unmistakably, bore the faint impression of small fingers in a film of lampblack along its edge.

Small fingers. Recent.

And beneath the smell of beeswax and acid, faint but present, the ghost of something a soldier knows and a print-seller shouldn't: porridge. Cheap oats, cooked that morning, in a workshop occupied by one woman who moved like a sentry.

"You've brought the charts back?" she asked. Her voice was level. Her weight had shifted onto her back foot.

"One of them was misfolded," he said smoothly, and produced the chart, and watched her take it, and watched her eyes — grey, tireless, missing nothing — watch him right back, two professionals across a drafting table, each measuring the depth of the other's water.

"Mr. Vane," she said, folding the chart without checking it. "Why does a buyer from the southern markets ask three times about unpublished material, count the exits of my shop, and arrive the same week the army sends sergeants door to door?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls the frost creaked. Elias Vane, who had lied to courts and colonels and the woman who had once agreed to marry him, opened his mouth to produce the answer that kept him safe —

— and found, to his considerable professional alarm, that what he said instead was: "Because I'm not what I claim to be, and neither, Mrs. Halloran, is what's behind your press."

The burin was in her hand before he finished speaking, small and bright and steady, and she did not look frightened at all. She looked like a woman who had been waiting five years for the knock to finally come.

"Get out," she said softly, "before I decide the floor can hold more ink."
