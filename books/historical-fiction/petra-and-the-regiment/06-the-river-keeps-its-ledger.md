## 6. The River Keeps Its Ledger

Elias Vane had been taught, in the first month of his commission, that a river was a document. His instructor — a gaunt major with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like dry gravel — had made the whole cadet class stand on the banks of the Corren in flood and read it aloud: where the current ran fast and brown with loam, where it pooled and went glassy, where debris snagged and told you what lay upstream. A river keeps a ledger, the major said. It writes down everything that enters it. Your work is to learn to read the entries.

Five years later, standing at midnight on the frozen face of that same river with a lantern held low against his knee, Elias thought the old major would have wept at how well he'd learned the lesson.

The ice here was a foot thick, maybe more — it had been a bitter winter even by northern standards — but he walked as though it were paper stretched over a well. He kept to the channel margins where the current slowed beneath the frost and froze harder, and he moved with the flat-footed shuffle of a man who had spent too many winters on frozen ground to trust his ankles to anything else. The town lay behind him, dark except for the taverns and one stubborn window up the hill that he did not let himself look at more than once.

The workshop window. Lamplight burning past midnight in the House of Halloran.

He had noted it the way he noted everything now — automatically, in that small neat hand of his mind, date and hour and observation — and then he had hated himself for noting it, because noting was the first step toward reporting, and reporting was the thing his boots refused to walk him toward tonight. So instead they walked him out onto the river, toward the ledger itself.

Three days ago, half a leather dispatch case had come through the toll bridge gate in a fish cart, and Elias had paid the toll-keeper's boy two coppers to tell him about everything strange the ice gave up that week. The answer had come this morning, wrapped around a smoked herring the boy's mother apparently considered fair trade: yes, sir, there was a thing came down with the break-up water above the mill weir — a strap, a buckle, some bits of black leather all froze together in a cake of ice like a plum in a pudding. Fisherman cracked it open with an axe. Kept the buckle, threw the rest back, or so he says. And before that, sir, before the deep cold set — there'd been a soldier's kitbag found wedged in the willows at Miller's Bend, nothing in it but rot and a shoulder-board.

A shoulder-board. Ninth Regiment, if the boy's description held — silver thread on green felt, worn soft. Elias had turned the description over in his mind all day the way a man turns a stone in his pocket, feeling its weight, its edges, its terrible familiarity.

Because the Ninth had no business in this river at all. The official record — the record Elias himself had helped maintain, in another life, in a clean office that smelled of sealing wax — placed the Ninth four hundred miles south, garrison duty, quiet as churchwork, through the whole season in question. That was the entry in the ledger of the War Office. But the ledger of the river said otherwise. The river said: something military came into my water upstream, upstream of the mill, upstream of Miller's Bend, in the dead of that winter, and I have kept hold of a piece of it ever since, and here it is, delivered to your hand like a bill come due.

He reached Miller's Bend just after one o'clock and stopped.

The willows stood along the near bank in their winter skeletons, and between them the ice shelved up where the current undercut the bank, ridged and refrozen into grey steps. He raised the lantern. The light slid across the ice and caught, faintly, a mark cut into a boulder at the water's edge — a chiseled line, deliberate, precise, half-filled with rime. He knelt. Brushed the frost away with his glove.

A benchmark. Cut level, with a reference number beside it in the tight economical lettering he knew from a hundred plates: the survey hand of Aldous Halloran.

Elias stayed kneeling a long moment, breath smoking through the wool over his mouth, and felt the first true crack open in him — not in the ice, which held, but somewhere under his sternum, thin and high and unmistakable.

He knew that hand. Every officer who had ever studied logistics knew it, or ought to. Aldous Halloran had surveyed more of the empire's rivers than any man alive or dead; his benchmarks were scattered along the waterways like stitches in a hem. And Halloran had died — the file said accidentally, the file always said accidentally — five years ago, in a workshop in the very town whose bridge Elias had stood upon three nights ago hearing the ice fail.

*What are you doing out here?* asked the voice of Colonel Merrick's agent, dry and patient, the voice that lived now in the back of Elias's skull like a tenant who paid no rent. *You were sent to find a case and a loose end. You have found neither, and you are reading rocks at midnight.*

*I am reading the ledger,* Elias answered it silently, rising, dusting his knees. *You should try it sometime. It doesn't lie.*

He walked on upstream, and the marks kept coming.

* * *

They came every quarter-mile, it seemed, once he learned to look: benchmarks on bridge abutments, on mooring posts, on the raw faces of cuttings where the road hugged the river. Halloran had walked this water with chain and level and notebook, probably in the autumn of the year before his death, and he had done it with the thoroughness of a man who intended his measurements to outlive argument. Elias began to understand that he wasn't walking a river at all. He was walking a man's last field-work. A testament, cut in stone, by someone who must have known — mustn't he? — that the truth he was measuring might someday be the only version left standing.

At the third benchmark past Miller's Bend, Elias stopped again, because the mark was different.

Not a benchmark. A symbol: a short vertical stroke crossed by two horizontals, cut shallow, almost furtive, tucked into the shadow side of the stone where only someone looking deliberately would find it. He knew that too. Every staff officer of a certain vintage knew it. It was the old regimental route-mark of the Ninth — a wayfinding sign cut at fords and halts, the kind of thing quartermasters scratched when orders were verbal and records were meant to be deniable.

The Ninth had marched along this river. Not four hundred miles south. Here. In the dead of winter. Upstream, past the mill weir, past Miller's Bend, following the frozen road into the high country beyond the gorge — and someone had marked their passage in the language of maps, quietly, permanently, on the stones.

And then the river had received them. Or received enough of them. A kitbag in the willows. Leather frozen into the ice.

Elias stood in the blue dark with his lantern guttering and let the arithmetic finish itself, though every part of him wanted to interrupt it. If the Ninth came up this river, then the route order that Beatrix Halloran was hiding — the torn page he had glimpsed, God forgive him, through the gap of a false drawer while pretending to admire her border engravings — was genuine. If the route order was genuine, then the official record was false. And if the record was false, it had been made false deliberately, by someone with authority to make it so, and there were perhaps six men in the empire with that authority, and one of them was the charming, patient man who paid Elias's expenses and signed his instructions in violet ink.

*Colonel Merrick,* said the tenant in his skull, pleasantly, *wants a report by Thursday.*

*Thursday,* Elias agreed, and blew out the lantern, and stood in the starlight listening to the river talk in its sleep beneath his feet — that long groan, that rifle-crack, repeated now and again as the thaw worked its slow fingers into the joints of the winter. Three weeks, perhaps four, and the ice would go altogether. Supply boats would come up from the capital. Soldiers would follow the boats. Whatever the House of Halloran contained — a grieving mapmaker, a mute child, a hidden drawer, a torn order annotated in a dead man's hand — would be found, or burned, or both.

He had until the ice went. He had, in fact, already used up two days of it walking riverbanks instead of writing reports.

That was the thing he could not explain, even to himself, even in the privacy of his own skull where the agent's voice could not follow: the image of her hands. Ink-stained to the second knuckle, steady as instruments, moving over the copper plate with a care he had only seen before in surgeons and priests. She was hiding something — of course she was hiding something; the false drawer alone proved that, and the child proved more — but she hid it the way a mason hides a keystone: not to deceive, but to keep the arch standing. There was craft in her concealment. There was even, he suspected, conscience.

And there was the child. Seven years old, perhaps, frostbitten, silent, carrying half a dispatch case like a relic. Elias had seen enough of war's leavings to know what a child like that usually carried inside her instead of words. The men who had ordered the case retrieved — the men who wanted the loose end tied off — they knew what the child had seen. That was why they wanted her. Not for testimony. For erasure.

*Your duty,* said the tenant, gently now, reasonably, the way temptation always spoke when it had given up shouting. *Your duty is a case and a loose end. Bring them in and your name is restored. Refuse, and you are a disgraced officer skulking on a frozen river at two in the morning, talking to rocks.*

"My duty," Elias said aloud, and heard his own voice crack with the cold, "is to the record."

The river groaned beneath him, long and low, like a creature turning over in its sleep, and he took that for whatever answer the night cared to give.

* * *

He wrote the report on Wednesday, in his rented room above the cooperage, by the light of a candle he allowed himself to burn down nearly to the socket. It took him four drafts, and the difference between them was the whole distance between the man he had been a week ago and the man the river had made of him.

The first draft told the truth: case recovered from the ice upstream, traces of military origin consistent with the Ninth Regiment, evidence of unauthorized route markings along the north bank, probable connection to the House of Halloran, investigation continuing.

He burned it.

The second draft told less: no trace of the case yet; the ice has yielded only debris of civilian character; inquiries among ferrymen and fishermen ongoing. Honest, defensible, useless to Merrick, and it bought nothing — a blank report would simply bring other eyes, sharper ones, down from the capital.

He burned that too.

The third draft was the work of art, and he despised himself even as he admired it. It confirmed the case's existence and its likely recovery point — accurate, verifiable, the kind of detail that made a handler nod — and then it misdirected: the recovered material suggested the case had gone into the water at the mill weir during the autumn floods, months before the deep freeze; the trail downstream of the weir was broken by the spring market traffic; several items matching the description had passed through the toll bridge in fish carts bound for the coast towns. Recommendation: extend inquiry downstream. Time required: a fortnight at minimum, given the thaw and the state of the coast roads.

It was a lie built entirely out of truths, each plank sound, the whole structure bearing a man away from where he needed to be followed. His instructor at the academy — not the major with the gravel voice, but the other one, the one who taught deception — would have graded it excellent. *The best lie,* that instructor had said, *is a fence made of true posts.* Elias sealed it, addressed it, and set it on the table by the door for the morning carrier, and sat for a long time afterward with his head in his ink-stained hands — her color, he noticed, her stain, as if the workshop had gotten into him through the skin.

His first treason, committed with pen and sealing wax and a steady hand. It had taken less effort than signing for his rations. That was the part that frightened him: how easy the crossing was, once you decided the far shore was worth reaching.

On Thursday morning he did not take the coast road. He walked up through the sleeping town, past the shuttered chandlery, past the church whose bell had not rung properly since the frost cracked it, up the hill to the street where the lamplight burned late. The House of Halloran stood black-fronted against the paling sky, smoke rising straight from its chimney, one window lit — the workroom, he judged, by the angle of the light and the faint smell of lamp-black that drifted down even in the cold.

He stood across the street in the shadow of the pump housing, a civilian in a good coat, watching the window the way a man watches a lit doorway of a house where someone is dying — wanting to knock, not knowing what he could say that wouldn't be a weapon in either direction. *I have lied for you* was not a sentence one offered to a stranger. *I have read your master's stones* was worse; it was a confession of surveillance.

The window-light shifted. A smaller silhouette joined the taller one — the child, standing beside the woman at the workbench, both of them bent over something he could not see. Two heads close together over a task. The taller one guiding the smaller one's hands.

Something in Elias's chest performed the maneuver that the river performs on ice in March: a groan, a shift, a crack running suddenly from shore to shore.

He turned and walked back down the hill before he could be seen, and behind him the river spoke again — louder now, more frequent, the ledger beginning audibly to balance its accounts — and ahead of him, at the bottom of the street where the carrier waited with the morning mail, a traveling coach stood at the inn door, mud-spattered, its horses steaming, its door bearing a crest he recognized from a hundred violet-inked letters.

The coach was early. The thaw was early. Everything, now, was early — and Elias Vane, traitor of forty hours' standing, quickened his step toward the inn to learn what his master had come north to see for himself.
