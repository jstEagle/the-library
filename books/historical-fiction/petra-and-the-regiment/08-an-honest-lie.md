## 8. An Honest Lie

The room above the Ferryman's Rest smelled of tallow and old beer, and of a man who believed that discomfort was a form of discipline. Lieutenant Elias Vane had been sitting in it for two hours, and in those two hours he had learned the geography of his own treachery the way he had once learned coastlines: by patient sounding, one lie at a time.

Across the table sat Major Corven — though *major* was a costume, as *Vane* was, and they both knew it. Corven was Merrick's hand. Not the fist; the fingers that counted things. He was a narrow man with a clerk's posture and a fencer's eyes, and he drank nothing, which told Elias more than any question had.

"Again," Corven said. "From the ice upstream."

"From the ice upstream." Elias kept his voice level, bored even. A man performing boredom had to be careful not to perform it too well; genuine boredom had edges of irritation. He let a little irritation show. "A carter found it snagged against the weir pilings on the north bank. Half a dispatch case, regimental leather, Ninth's stamp. He sold it to me because I asked after river salvage when I came through, and because carters sell anything."

"And the contents?"

"Wet through. Paper pulp, mostly. A scrap of route notation, illegible." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something he would examine later, alone, in the dark. "I kept it for the stamp. Thought it might interest collectors."

Corven did not write this down. That was the worst of it. Men who wrote things down were doing their work honestly; men who didn't were doing it from memory, and memory could be checked against other men's memories later, in rooms like this, with worse furniture.

"The carter," Corven said. "Name?"

"I didn't ask. I paid him and he went on toward Millbrook."

"You paid him how much?"

"Two crowns. He'd have taken one."

Something moved at the corner of Corven's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would be stored. "You have an honest eye for what a thing is worth, Lieutenant."

*Vane,* Elias almost said. *I'm Vane now.* But correcting the name would be a kind of honesty too, and honesty had become a currency he could no longer afford to spend freely. "I was in supply before I was anything else," he said instead. "You learn what things cost."

"Yes." Corven turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table without drinking. "You were in supply. Then you were in the Ninth's rear echelon, briefly, before the Ninth ceased — administratively speaking — to exist. Then you were cashiered. Falsified manifests, wasn't it? Or so the record says."

The word *says* hung in the air between them like smoke. Elias met it without flinching, because flinching was the one luxury left to him that he absolutely could not buy. "The record says many things."

"It does." Corven leaned back. Outside the window, the frozen river lay white and silent under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and somewhere beneath all that ice the water was moving, patient, keeping its accounts. "Here is where we are, Lieutenant Vane, or whatever your name is. The Colonel wants three things. The case. Whatever paper it carried. And the loose end attached to it — a child, we believe, seven or eight years old, last seen on the ice near Harrow's Bend six weeks ago. You will understand that the child is not wanted for harm. She is wanted for *completeness*. An archive must be whole. Incomplete archives cause questions, and questions cause inquiries, and inquiries are vulgar."

"I understand completeness," Elias said.

"I believe you do. That is why you're here." Corven stood, buttoned his coat with small precise motions. "You'll remain in town. You'll continue your — salvage interests. If the case surfaces, or anything bearing the Ninth's annotations, or if you learn where the child has gone, you will send word within the day. Not two days. Within the day." He paused at the door. "And Vane. If I learn that you found something and sat on it — if I learn it secondhand, or thirdhand, or from the shape of your silence — then the falsified manifests stop being a story about a disgraced supply officer and become a lever. Do you take my meaning?"

"Perfectly."

"Good. Honesty, between us, from here on." Corven smiled the stored smile at last, and took it with him out the door, and his boots went down the stairs evenly, unhurried, a man with no reason in the world to run.

Elias sat alone in the tallow smell for a long time.

He thought: *I have already lied four times today.* The carter existed only as a shape he had invented on the walk over — gray coat, red nose, breath steaming. The scrap of route notation had never been wet; he had never seen it at all. Two crowns had never changed hands. And underneath all of them, wide and load-bearing as a bridge pier, lay the fifth lie, the one he had told with his whole body since the moment he'd stood outside a frost-feathered window holding Aldous Halloran's notebook up to the glass: that he had come to this town to close an account, when some part of him — a part he had not consulted, had not authorized, had found mutinous in his chest like a second heartbeat — had already decided how this account would close.

He thought about the first lie, the smallest one, the carter. How easy it had been. How the words had come out of him already shaped, smooth as stones worn in a current, requiring no effort at all. That was what frightened him. Not that he had lied, but that treason, once begun, felt less like breaking a thing than like setting down a weight he had forgotten he carried.

He had spent eleven years being the man who reported accurately. Supply officers lived or died by accuracy; a miscounted ration column starved a company, a mislabeled crate killed a gun crew waiting for powder. Accuracy had been his religion, and when the Ninth's manifests came through his hands that autumn — the ones the record said were falsified, and they were, though not by him, not in the direction the record claimed — accuracy had stopped being a religion and started being evidence. He had been cashiered for noticing. That was the whole crime. He had counted wagons going out and wagons coming back, and the difference had a name, and the name had been expunged, and when he wrote the difference down he had discovered what happened to arithmetic the army disliked.

Now he was counting again. Wagons out, wagons back. Except the cargo was a woman with copper under her fingernails, and a mute child, and a plate hidden somewhere in that workshop with a dead man's truth engraved into its skin.

He put on his coat and went down into the cold.

* * *

The river was talking.

It had begun three days ago — a sound below hearing, felt in the boot-soles before the ear caught it, a long subterranean groan like a giant turning in its sleep. By evening it had words: cracks propagating outward from the shore with reports like distant musketry, the ice shrinking from its moorings as the water beneath warmed and lifted. Every night the voice grew bolder. Every night Elias walked the white plain of it anyway, because it was the only place in town where a man could think without walls listening, and because Aldous Halloran's survey marks were there.

He found the first one where it always was, a foot of weathered oak driven into the north bank, crowned with the master's chiseled cipher: a triangle over a plumb line. Halloran had surveyed this reach twenty years ago, before Elias was old enough to hold a chain, and the marks still stood true — which meant the river had held true, which meant the map in the atlas was true, which meant everything else — the official routes, the garrison records, the comfortable paper fiction of where the Ninth had marched — was false. Truth was durable. Lies needed maintenance. He had never understood that so clearly as he did standing on a frozen river reading a dead man's carpentry.

There were fresh tracks near the mark. Small ones. Booted, but small — a child's boots, patched, the left one resoled with leather that didn't match. They came down from the town side, wandered along the bank past the mark, and went back. Petra. The girl walked the river the way he did. He wondered if she listened to it too, if she heard in the groaning of the ice whatever it was that had sealed her voice shut. He wondered what the river sounded like to someone who had crossed it in winter carrying half of a dead soldier's case and a note saying *forget the face.*

He turned back toward town while the light was still gray-gold, and made himself say the sentence he had been avoiding for two days:

*I have to warn her.*

Not because warning her was safe. It was possibly the least safe act available to him, ranked just below standing in front of a firing squad and announcing his opinions. Warning her meant crossing lines that could not be uncrossed; it meant that when Corven's memory came calling, checking itself against other memories, there would be no innocent version of Elias Vane left to retreat into. It meant — and here his mind shied like a horse at a fence — it meant that Beatrix Halloran would have to decide whether to trust a man sent to destroy her, on the strength of nothing but the fact that he had failed to do it.

But the alternative was arithmetic he could no longer live inside. Wagons out, wagons back. Corven wanted the case, the paper, and the child, for *completeness*, and everyone in that room upstairs knew what completeness meant when the archive was a massacre: no witnesses, no loose ends, no faces to remember. He had watched the machinery work once already from the safe side of a ledger. He was not going to watch it work again from the inside.

He climbed the bank. The workshop stood at the end of Cartwright Lane with its chimney smoking, its windows gold-lit, looking exactly like what it pretended to be — a mapmaker's shop, quiet, harmless, full of careful silences. Behind one of those windows, a woman was engraving treason into copper by candlelight, and a child was grinding ink for her, and neither of them knew that the man who had brought Aldous's notebook to their door had been questioned that morning by the man whose order had killed the Ninth.

They deserved better than ignorance. Ignorance was what people like Merrick fed on.

He knocked.

* * *

She opened the door on the chain, as she always did — six inches of lamplight and suspicion, her face half in shadow. He had learned her thresholds. Six inches meant *state your business*. A foot meant *come to the threshold*. The chain off meant he had become, temporarily, a person. Tonight he intended to spend whatever credit remained and go into debt.

"You've been walking the river," she said.

"I walk the river."

"At dusk. Past the survey marks. Every night for a week." Her eyes were the color of the ice, and just as good at showing him his own reflection. "Men who walk rivers at dusk in winter are either surveyors, smugglers, or mourners. You're none of those, or you're all of them, and either way you should know that the major from the capital took a room at the Ferryman's Rest this morning, and this afternoon he called on the harbor office asking after river salvage."

So she already knew. Of course she knew; she had survived five years in this town by knowing. Something in his chest unclenched and clenched again in the same breath.

"He called on me," Elias said. "This morning. Two hours."

The chain did not move. Neither did she. Behind her, deeper in the shop, a candle guttered and righted, and he heard — very faintly — the small scuff of a child's feet going still.

"And?" she said.

"And I told him the case was found by a carter who doesn't exist, bought for a price I invented, its contents ruined beyond reading." He heard his own voice drop, steadied itself. "I told him the paper in it was pulp. I told him four lies before noon, Mrs. Halloran, and I expect to tell a great many more, and the reason I'm standing on your step telling you this is that you deserve to hear it from me rather than discover it in the way these things are usually discovered — which is afterward, and fatally."

Silence. The river spoke distantly from beyond the rooftops, one long cracking report, like a door closing somewhere far away.

"Why?" she said at last. Just the word. No warmth in it, no invitation — an engineer testing a load-bearing wall.

"Because I was in the Ninth's supply train," he said. "Before it marched. Before wherever it marched. I counted the wagons that went out and I counted the wagons that came back and I wrote the difference down, and for that they took my commission, my name, and eleven years of my life, and gave me a new name and a set of orders and sent me to find a child." His jaw tightened. "I am the loose end, Mrs. Halloran. Or I was supposed to be. Somewhere between the Ferryman's Rest and your door I appear to have tied myself to the other end of the rope."

"That's a confession," she said quietly. "Do you know what I could do with it?"

"I know exactly what you could do with it. It's why I'm giving it to you. A man can't be blackmailed with a thing he's handed over freely." He held her gaze through the six inches of lamplight. "You don't have to trust me. Trust is for people with clean hands. All I'm asking is that you know what's coming, so that when it comes, it finds you awake."

For a long moment she simply looked at him, and he understood that behind her eyes a ledger was turning pages — weighing his lies against his presence, his uniform against his voice, the notebook he'd carried against the orders he hadn't yet obeyed. He let her weigh. It was, he thought, the most honest transaction he had participated in for years.

Then the chain slid free, link by link, bright metal sound in the cold.

She stepped back, and the door opened fully, and warm air and the smell of ink and copper came out to meet him like a country he'd been exiled from.

"There's a rule in this house," Beatrix Halloran said. "We keep silences that keep people safe. So understand me, Lieutenant — whoever you are tonight, whatever you were this morning — the moment you cross this threshold, you are inside the silence. What you know, we carry together. What comes for us, comes for you. There is no standing in the doorway."

"No," Elias said, stepping across. "There isn't. I checked."

Behind him the door closed and the bolt went home, and outside, far down the valley, the river answered with a sound like the whole winter letting go of something at once — and in the back room, unnoticed by either of them until the lamp was raised, Petra stood in the shadow of the press with her chalk already in her hand, drawing on the slate, fast and urgent, a picture she had been keeping to herself for days: a row of small figures on a frozen road, and behind them, drawn larger than all the rest, a man in a fine coat with a face she remembered perfectly.

The face of Colonel Merrick.
