## 9. Two Kinds of Maps

### Beatrix

The buyer came back on the third day, and Beatrix had known he would.

Men who bought maps the way Lieutenant—no, *Mister* Vane, as his card insisted—bought maps did not return on the third day. They sent clerks. They haggled by letter through intermediaries who smelled of tallow and government ink. A man came back himself only when what he wanted could not be purchased.

She set him at the long drafting table rather than the parlor, where she received honest customers. The drafting table was her territory: its surface scarred with compass points and blade-cuts, its drawers full of instruments arranged in an order only she understood, its lamp angled so that whoever sat across from it sat in shadow while she worked in light. Aldous had taught her that much about receiving strangers. *Let them watch your hands, Trixie. Hands tell fewer lies than faces, but they tell them slower.*

"Another look at the coastal sheets," Vane said. He had removed his gloves this time, she noticed. His hands were long-knuckled, a soldier's hands, but there was a faded stain of something dark in the crease of his right thumb that no amount of scrubbing quite lifts—the mark of a man who had once held a pen for more than signing his name.

"Coastal sheets are finished work," Beatrix said, unrolling one anyway. "You said yesterday you were interested in commissioning."

"I am." He leaned over the coastline of the southern estuary without touching it, the way a man leans toward a fire. "A survey of the river towns. Commercial traffic, depths, landing rights. My employers have interests here."

"Your employers," Beatrix repeated. "You still haven't named them."

"A shipping concern." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced at dinner tables in garrison towns where a smile was often the only weapon permitted. "You'd find them dull, Miss Halloran. All ledgers and tonnage."

"I find most men dull, Mister Vane. It's their lies I find interesting." She weighted the corner of the sheet with an inkstone and met his eyes across the lamplight. "A commercial survey of this river is a strange commission in February. The ice won't clear for six weeks. Nothing floats but rumors until then."

"Then you'll have time to do it properly."

"Or I'll have time to wonder why a shipping concern sends a man whose boots are army-issue resoled twice, to buy charts of a river his employers supposedly already work." She said it evenly, almost gently, the way one remarks on weather. "I'm a mapmaker, Mister Vane. Reading terrain is my trade. Boots are terrain."

For a moment—she counted it, because counting was also her trade—he did not breathe. Then he laughed, low and genuine, and something in her chest turned over treacherously at the sound.

"You're right," he said. "They're army boots. I was in the army. Badly. Now I sell surveys to men who never leave their clubs, and they pay me enough to forget I ever slept in mud." He spread his hands, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "Is that confession enough to buy an hour of your time?"

It was a lie. She was nearly certain it was a lie, and it was beautifully made—a lie with mud on its boots and shame in its mouth, constructed to be caught, so that catching it would feel like winning and she would stop looking further down. Aldous would have called it a decoy contour: the false hill drawn on the map to catch the plagiarist's eye.

"It's a start," she said, and gave him the pen, and began to teach him the river.

That was the danger of him, she thought, watching his hand move across her practice sheet—sure, economical, trained. Not that he might be lying. That he lied the way she did: carefully, structurally, with true things as load-bearing walls. Two kinds of maps, she thought. And both of us drawn by the same hand.

### Elias

She was better at this than he was, and Elias Vane had not expected to think that about anyone again.

He had spent two days establishing himself in town before returning to the workshop—two days of drinking in the Ferryman's Rest and losing small sums at cards and letting the ferryman's boy see him pay with notes from a capital bank, so that when the questions came later, the town's memory of him would be exactly the memory he had built. Merrick's tradecraft, drilled into him over four bitter years: *Never enter a room you haven't already furnished.* He had furnished this room well. He had not accounted for the woman furnishing it faster.

"Depths first," she was saying now, tapping the practice sheet. "Soundings taken in summer lie to you in winter. The river moves its own silt; a channel marked eight feet in July may be four by March. If your employers want truth, they wait for the thaw and pay for new soundings. If they want speed—" she looked up, mild as milk, "—they're not really interested in depth at all."

"And if they're interested in something else?"

"Then they've come to the wrong shop." She took the pen back from him, and her fingers brushed his—ink-stained to the second knuckle, those fingers, stained the way a woman's hands are stained when the work is daily and unapologetic—and drew a line along the estuary with a single stroke he could not have improved. "This is the channel as Master Halloran surveyed it five years ago. Note the annotation."

He noted it. He had been noting it since yesterday, since the moment he had seen it and felt the floor of his professional detachment go soft as riverbank under him. In the margin beside the channel marker, in a cramped, precise hand he knew from a hundred archived documents, someone had written: *Sounding refused by military escort — see field book 41.*

Field book forty-one did not exist. Elias knew that the way he knew his own sins; he had spent three weeks last autumn in the Survey Office archive looking for it, on Merrick's orders, and had found the ledger gap where it should have been—a gap too clean, cut out with a razor, the binding re-sewn by someone careful. Someone like the dead man whose name was still on the door downstairs.

"An odd note for a commercial chart," he said.

"Master Halloran believed maps should carry their doubts with them." Her voice did not change at all, and that was how he knew the sentence mattered. "A chart that pretends certainty gets ships killed. He annotated everything. It made him unpopular with buyers who wanted pretty."

"And with the military escort?"

Something moved behind her eyes then—not fear, exactly. Recognition. As if he had stepped onto ground she had already mapped and mined.

"The escort," she said, "was following orders. Orders are rarely annotated." She rolled the sheet closed with a crisp snap and stood, and the interview was over; he felt it end the way one feels a door close in another room. "I'll draft terms for the survey. You can collect them tomorrow. Good day, Mister Vane."

He rose, bowed, said the correct things. At the foot of the stairs he paused, and—because he was good at his trade, because Merrick had taught him that the last look backward was the cheapest intelligence a man ever gathered—he glanced along the workshop gallery as if admiring the framed charts.

And saw her, at the top of the stairs, watching him with an expression of perfect courteous blankness that told him everything: she had watched him look. She had let him look. And somewhere in that gallery, among the framed coastlines and the pride of the late master's career, was the thing she did not want found, and she had just measured exactly how obvious he thought it was.

Two craftsmen, he thought, descending into the cold. Same guild.

God help whichever of us blinks first.

### Beatrix

Petra knew the man was wrong before Beatrix did.

The child had a way of knowing—she went silent in a different key when danger passed the window, a silence with weight to it, and Beatrix had learned to read it the way sailors read a barometer. On the morning after Vane's second visit, Petra sat at the workbench grinding ink with slow, deliberate strokes of the muller, and her eyes kept going to the false drawer.

Beatrix had built the drawer herself, two years ago, after the first stranger came asking whether Master Halloran had left papers. It was a masterpiece of nothing: a shallow drawer at the end of the bank of instrument drawers, fitted with a false back, containing exactly what a searcher would expect to find and nothing else—old invoices, a bundle of letters from a wine merchant, a half-finished chart of a lake in the northern provinces, deliberately boring. The real plate, the copper plate on which she had begun cutting the Ninth's true route beneath the decorative border of the atlas's final sheet, lived elsewhere entirely, wrapped in oilcloth inside the cold chest where she kept her sizing.

But the drawer existed, and its existence was a confession to anyone who knew how to read furniture.

"He looked at it," Petra signed. The child's hands were quick, expressive, eloquent in a language Beatrix was only beginning to speak. *Yesterday. When you went for the tea.*

"Which drawer?" Beatrix asked aloud, though she knew. Speaking to Petra had become habit already; the child understood every word and answered with her hands, and between the two of them they had built a private country with two languages and no borders.

Petra pointed. Her finger did not waver.

Beatrix stood very still in the middle of her workshop, in the smell of linseed and cold copper, and made herself think like the enemy instead of like a grieving woman. If a searcher comes, what does he search? Papers. Correspondence. The dead man's effects. And what does a careful searcher know? That the valuable thing is never in the obvious place—but that the *second*-obvious place, the decoy, is often guarded by the person hiding the real thing, because people cannot help watching the thing that matters even when it is false.

She had watched the drawer. Yesterday, twice. She remembered doing it—the flick of her eyes when he stood near the bench, the half-second of attention she had given it while pouring tea. She had betrayed it herself, the way a gambler betrays his hand by protecting it.

"Trixy," she murmured to herself, using Aldous's name for her like a slap. *Stupid. Careless. Five years of discipline undone by one pair of grey eyes.*

Because that was the other thing, the thing she refused to examine while the kettle heated and Petra watched her with those grave, patient eyes: she had looked at him too. Not only tactically. She had looked at his hands and thought them beautiful; she had heard him laugh and wanted, with a hunger that shamed her, to hear it again. Five years she had kept her heart in a locked drawer of its own, and this man—one visit, two visits—had put his hand flat against the wood of it.

She decided, stirring the tea, that if he came back she would show him the false drawer herself. Openly. Casually. Let him find the wine merchant's letters and the boring lake, let him conclude he had won, let him carry his victory back to whatever employer sent boots like his. A map that shows the searcher what he expects will lead him anywhere you wish.

It was a good plan. It was Aldous's kind of plan.

It lasted until the knock came at the door that same afternoon, and she opened it, and Vane stood on the step holding out her dropped thimble—which he could only have picked up from the floor beside the drafting table, which meant he had lingered after she dismissed him, alone in her workshop, for however long he pleased.

"Yours, I think," he said. "From yesterday. I only just noticed it in my coat."

She took the thimble. Their fingers touched. Neither of them smiled, and neither of them looked away, and upstairs Petra went very quiet in the dangerous key.

"Will you come in, Mister Vane?" Beatrix heard herself say. "There's a matter of the survey terms we should discuss. Privately."

### Elias

She showed him the drawer.

That was the first sign that everything he had assumed was wrong. He had planned to engineer a moment near the bench—drop a pen, perhaps, ask to wash ink from his fingers—and instead she walked him straight to it, mid-conversation about paper weights, and pulled it open as though showing him a sample of stock.

"Old correspondence," she said. "If you're considering commissioning the atlas reprint, you'll want to know the state of the master's records. Most of it is mundane. Some of it is charming. This, for instance—" she held up a letter, and he read enough to see it was a complaint about delayed wine, written in a fussy merchant's hand, "—goes on for four pages about corks."

Elias examined the drawer the way she clearly intended him to: thoroughly, respectfully, and with growing admiration. The false back was flawless. He found it only because finding such things was his profession—he pressed the left interior wall and felt the give that shouldn't be there—and having found it, he understood the message underneath the message.

*You know I know,* the drawer said. *And I know you know. So let us stop pretending we are talking about paper weights.*

He looked up. She was watching him with the calm of a woman holding a lit fuse and a steady hand, and behind her, on the stair, the child stood with one hand on the rail—silent, watchful, with a face that stopped the breath in his throat, because he had seen that face before. Not her face specifically. That *expression*. On the survivors of places whose names were struck from dispatches. On the girl at Kesselmark, years ago, who had stopped speaking after the winter the village burned, and whom the army had recorded as *relocated*.

*The loose end,* Merrick's letter had said. *Attached to the case.*

"Miss Halloran," Elias said quietly, "who is hunting you?"

The question fell into the room like a stone into a well. He heard it land far below—heard, in the silence that followed, the whole architecture of her caution echo around it. She did not answer. She closed the drawer with a click as precise as a lock engaging.

"That depends," she said, "on who sent you."

And there it was: the point at which two maps of the same territory either agreed or went to war. He could lie again—another decoy contour, another muddy confession—and she would let him, and they would circle each other for weeks while Merrick's real agents closed in from the road, and it would end with soldiers at this door and a child in their hands.

Or.

"I was sent to find a dispatch case," Elias said, "that came off the river ice upstream of this town. And to find what was attached to it." He did not look at the child. Looking would be a confirmation, and confirmations got people killed. "My report is due in nine days. What I write in it has not yet been decided."

Her eyes narrowed—not with triumph, not with trust, but with the sharp attention of a cartographer encountering an unmapped ridge. Something new. Something that changed the shape of everything around it.

"You'll forgive me," she said, "if I don't build my defenses on a man's unfinished sentence."

"No," Elias said. "I'd think less of you if you did." He took his hat from the peg. At the door he paused, and heard himself say the thing he had climbed all these stairs meaning not to say:

"There's a man coming. Older than me, better funded, patient as frost. When he arrives, unfinished sentences will be a luxury neither of us can afford. Decide what you believe about me before then." He settled the hat brim low. "I'll know your decision by whether there's tea."

The door closed behind him on the cold bright afternoon, and Elias Vane walked down through the snow toward the Ferryman's Rest with his heart hammering like a signal drum, understanding that he had just handed a woman he had known three days the power to end him—and that some traitorous part of him had done it gladly.

Behind him, in the warm workshop smelling of ink and copper, Beatrix Halloran stood a long time with her hand flat on the false drawer, feeling through the wood the shape of the empty space where trust would go—if she chose to fill it.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Petra was watching the road through the attic window, where a hired carriage with capital plates had just come around the bend by the mill, moving slowly, unhurried, the way men move when they believe the net is already cast.

The frost was coming early this year. And it was wearing a gentleman's coat.
