## 10. Petra Speaks

The carriage with the capital plates did not stop at the workshop that day. It rolled past, unhurried as a man strolling his own grounds, and took the turning toward the Ferryman's Rest, where gentlemen of means lodged when they wished to be seen lodging well. Beatrix watched it from the doorway until the mill swallowed it, and then she watched the road a while longer, the way one watches a wound to see whether it bleeds.

She did not tell Petra what the carriage might mean. Instead she said, "Supper," and made barley soup with the last of the salt pork, and let the child's silence fill the kitchen like a second occupant.

It had been nine days since Elias Vane had stood in her workshop pretending to be a buyer of maps, and four days since he had come back after midnight and told her, in a voice stripped of everything official, *they will search before the thaw — you must finish before they do*. Four days since she had caught him looking at the false drawer and understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than any soldier could, that he was a liar of exactly her own make.

Since then there had been a new arrangement between them, never spoken aloud. He came at dusk by the mill path. He brought news wrapped in complaints about the weather. He had not once touched the false drawer again, and she had not once thanked him for leaving it alone, and this mutual restraint was, she suspected, the most honest conversation either of them had ever had.

But tonight he did not come. Tonight the frost came instead, early and hard, sealing the puddles by the door in white plates like small maps of nowhere, and Beatrix banked the stove and carried the candle up to the attic where Petra slept.

* * *

The attic room had been a storeroom when Aldous Halloran was alive — full of rolled proofs and spare stretchers, smelling of size and dust. Beatrix had cleared it in the first week, without letting herself think about why she was doing it so quickly, so completely. A bed. A blanket. A stool by the window where the child sat for hours some days, watching the river with an attention no seven-year-old should possess.

The child had been with her three weeks now. Three weeks, and Beatrix still woke sometimes at dawn with her heart hammering, certain that men were coming up the lane for the girl, and went down to check the bolt on the door twice before she could breathe properly.

Petra was sitting on the stool when Beatrix came up, knees drawn to her chest, watching the ice on the river catch the moonlight. She did not turn around. In the candlelight her shadow was very small against the sloped wall, and beside the bed lay the half dispatch case, its broken strap coiled like a dead snake. The child would not let it out of arm's reach. Beatrix had stopped trying to understand this and simply accepted it, the way one accepts a scar.

"I've brought you warm milk," Beatrix said. "There's honey in it."

Petra took the cup with both hands, the way she did everything — carefully, economically, a child who had learned somewhere that waste was dangerous. Her fingers were healed now, mostly; the frostbite had taken only a sliver of skin from two fingertips, and Dr. Lassiter had pronounced them sound enough to hold a pencil, which was the only verdict Beatrix had cared about.

Because the drawings had become their language. Every night, or nearly every night, Beatrix sat with paper and charcoal while Petra drew: the river upstream, the willows, a bridge with a broken parapet, a line of small figures marching in a direction the official maps said no regiment had ever marched. And every night Beatrix asked nothing, because asking was a door slammed in a frightened face, and showed nothing of what the drawings did to her — how each morning she went down to the engraving bench with her hands steady and something behind her sternum burning like a coal.

Tonight, though, Petra set down the cup unfinished, which she never did, and looked at Beatrix with an expression Beatrix had not seen on her before. Not fear. Something older than fear. Something like a person standing at the edge of cold water, knowing they must go in.

"You asked me my name," Petra said.

Beatrix went very still. The words were rusty, small, sanded down at the edges by disuse — a voice that had been locked in a box and was testing its own hinges. But it was a voice. It was *speech*, in her attic, in the candlelight, after twenty-one days of charcoal and gestures and the terrible eloquence of a child's drawings.

"Yes," Beatrix managed. "I did."

"Petra." The girl said it herself, tasting it, deciding whether it fit. "You called me Petra. That's mine now?"

"If you want it."

"I want it." A pause. The moon moved behind a cloud; the river light dimmed and returned. "I couldn't talk before. If I talked, they'd know I heard things. The lady said—" Petra stopped. Started again, quieter. "The woman who left me at your door wrote *forget the face*. She was the cook. At the house. She said if I ever talked about what I saw, they'd find me and finish it. She said forget the faces and stay small and maybe you'd live."

Beatrix lowered herself onto the edge of the bed, slowly, the way one moves near a wild thing. Her mouth had gone dry. "And now you're talking."

"Now I'm talking," Petra agreed gravely, as though reporting a change in the weather. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "Because you're making the map. The real one. I saw the plate under the drawer. You think I don't see things but I see everything."

* * *

What followed took until well past midnight, and Beatrix would remember it for the rest of her life as the longest night of her life, longer even than the night Aldous died.

Piece by piece, in that rusty little voice, with pauses where the child's courage ran dry and Beatrix waited without breathing, the truth assembled itself in the attic room like a picture emerging from an acid bath.

There had been a village upriver. Petra named it — **Vessel Ford** — and the name went through Beatrix like a blade finding the gap in armor, because she knew it. Everyone knew Vessel Ford as a ferry crossing, a dot on the commercial charts. No military record placed the Ninth anywhere within thirty miles of it. The official route order had them marching south-east toward the coast garrisons, a routine redeployment, signed, countersigned, filed.

"They came in winter," Petra said. "Before the ice was thick. The soldiers in grey coats walked through our village and the officer with the loud voice told everyone to give them the barns. They were hungry. Some of them had no boots." She held up her fingers, demonstrating a number too large for her hands, then abandoned the arithmetic. "Lots."

"The Ninth," Beatrix said softly. "The Ninth Regiment."

"I don't know numbers. They sang a song about being ninth." Petra frowned, searching. "Then the loud-voice man came back with other soldiers, different coats, and the barns burned in the night. With them inside." Her voice did not waver; it flattened, the way water flattens before it freezes over. "I was in the loft above our house. I could see. The ones that ran got to the river and the ice—" She stopped. Looked at her hands. "The ice wasn't thick yet either. The loud-voice man stood on the bank. He didn't shout anymore. He talked quiet, giving orders, and quiet was worse. His voice sounded like..." She searched for it, frowning with the effort of a scholar. "Like someone reading a shopping list. Like killing them was eggs and flour."

Beatrix closed her eyes. When she opened them, the candle flame had doubled and swum, and she realized she was crying, silently, in front of the child, and could not stop it.

"He had a ring," Petra went on, watching her cry with a kind of clinical interest, as if this confirmed something she'd already decided. "A gold ring with a red stone. He wore gloves after, but I saw him take one off to point at the river. The cook said his name was Merrick. Colonel Merrick. She said it once, very low, like a bad word, and then she never said it again."

*Merrick.* The name landed in the room and sat down among them like a third guest. The name Elias Vane would not say fully aloud, the name that traveled under the word *they* whenever he spoke of his orders. The colonel whose agent had questioned Elias in the capital. The colonel whose carriage wore capital plates and had rolled past her door that very afternoon, patient as a spider.

"Why did they leave you alive?" Beatrix asked, and immediately hated the question, but Petra only shrugged with terrible practicality.

"I was small and I hid in the flour bin when the soldiers searched. The cook found me after. She said the loud-voice man wanted no witnesses, but a village girl who'd run away wasn't worth hunting." A pause. "Except I was a witness. So we walked for days. She gave me the case and said a mapmaker would know what to do. She said Master Halloran was an honest man. She said—" Petra's voice caught for the first time, fraying. "She said he'd promised somebody he'd make it right. Somebody who died in the barns. Her brother."

*Aldous.* Five years dead, ruled accidental, drowned or fallen or whatever lie the coroner had been paid to write — and all along, a promise. Correspondence Beatrix had not yet finished searching. A widow's letters. A route order annotated in his cramped, exact hand, hidden in a child's frozen grip and carried down a frozen river to her door.

"And you carried it," Beatrix said. "All that way. And you never spoke. Not to anyone. Not even when you were freezing."

"Talking gets people killed," Petra said simply. "The cook said so." Then, after a moment, with devastating gentleness: "But you cry like it matters. And the man with the sad eyes looks at you like you matter. So I decided it's safe here to talk. Just here. Just to you."

Beatrix reached out and the child came to her — not collapsing, but arriving, deliberately, the way she did everything — and Beatrix held her and felt the small heartbeat going fast and steady against her own, and understood that she had just been given something no one had ever entrusted to her before. Not a secret. A testimony. The only living testimony in the world to what happened at Vessel Ford, delivered into her keeping by a seven-year-old who had weighed her, silently, for three weeks, and judged her worthy.

* * *

Later — much later, the candle burned to a stub, the child finally asleep with the dispatch case pulled against her chest like a doll — Beatrix went down to the workshop and did not light a lamp.

She did not need one. She knew the room the way she knew her own hands. She stood in the dark among the benches and the copper and the smell of acid and oil, and let herself feel all of it at once, the thing she had been holding off for three weeks: grief and fury fused into a single element, hot and clear as molten metal.

They had burned a regiment alive in a barn and driven the survivors onto thin ice, and written it down as a coastal redeployment. They had killed Aldous Halloran for refusing to erase it, and called it an accident. And now the man with the gold ring and the red stone was in town, lodging comfortably, waiting for the thaw with the patience of a man who has ordered deaths before and found them administratively convenient.

Against all of that: one copper plate, hidden beneath a decorative border. One engraver with ink-stained hands. One mute child who was no longer mute. One disgraced lieutenant whose loyalty hung in the balance like ice on a warm eave.

It was not an army. It was barely an argument. But ten thousand printed copies, Elias had said once, carelessly, over a map neither of them was supposed to be discussing — *justice comes sideways, madam, not from courts*. And now Beatrix knew what those copies must carry. Not just the route. Not just the annotations. The name of the village. The season. The method. Enough that no clerk could ever file it away, no colonel could ever explain it, no mother in any garrison town could fail to understand what had been done to her sons.

She lit a single candle, uncovered the false drawer, and lifted out the plate. Under the lamplight the true route gleamed in its half-finished state — the river, the ford, the dotted line running where no official line ran, Vessel Ford marked in her own firm hand. She picked up the graver.

Three weeks ago the atlas had been a memorial. Two weeks ago it had become a weapon. Tonight, with a sleeping child overhead who could stand in any court or street or parliament hall and say *I saw it, I heard his voice, I can name him* — tonight it became something else entirely. It became a witness's corroboration. The plate and the child belonged to each other now: the map proved where, and Petra proved who, and neither was complete without the other, and both of them were hers to protect.

She worked until the sky went grey at the edges. The graver bit copper in long clean strokes, and the sound it made in the empty workshop was very soft and very steady, like a heart that had finally decided what it was beating for.

Near dawn, she heard the mill path creak — one careful footstep, then another, the tread of a man who had learned to walk quietly in uniform and never unlearned it. She did not hide the plate. For the first time, she left it lying open on the bench, the true route shining in the candle stub's last light, and turned to face the door as the latch lifted and Elias Vane came in out of the frost, stamping snow from his boots, stopping dead at the sight of her face.

"What is it?" he said. "What's happened?"

"Upstairs," Beatrix said, and her voice did not shake at all. "The child speaks. And she has named the colonel."
