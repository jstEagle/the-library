## 11. The Thaw Begins

The river spoke first.

Elias Vane heard it before he saw it — a sound like a giant knuckle cracking somewhere beneath the ice, deep and resonant, rolling up through the frozen surface and into the pilings of the workshop district as though the water itself were clearing its throat after a long sleep. He had grown up on rivers that froze; he knew every voice they owned. The first groans of the thaw were the most honest sound in the world, because they could not be ordered to be quiet.

He stood at the window of his rented room above the cooper's shop, three doors down from Halloran's workshop, and watched the dawn come up grey and wet over the ice. It had been solid as a road for four months. Now, in the places where the current ran fastest near the middle channel, the surface had gone the colour of old pewter, darkened by water pressing up beneath it, and along the northern bank a seam of open water had opened like a wound that would not close.

Three weeks. Perhaps two, if the warm rain held. Two or three weeks until the first supply boats came upriver from the garrison towns, and behind the boats, the barges, and behind the barges, whatever soldiers Colonel Merrick thought this backwater required. And somewhere among them, sooner than any of that, Merrick's own men — because Merrick was not a man who waited for ferries when he could send riders overland.

*They are already late,* Elias thought, and then corrected himself, because he knew better. *No. They are already coming.*

He had counted the days against his last report — the one he had written ten days ago and never sent, the one describing the workshop's layout in careful professional language, its single entrance, its cellar, its locked drawers. He had burned that one three nights ago in the cooper's grate, feeding it page by page into the fire while Mrs. Cooper's cat watched him with the flat yellow patience of all cats, and he had felt nothing so much as relief, which frightened him more than discovery would have.

Because there was a newer one now. Half-written. Sitting in the false bottom of his travel case with its seal unbroken and its lies unfinished, and every morning he told himself he would complete it and send it south with the mail rider, and every evening he found another reason why tomorrow would serve.

The ice groaned again. Longer this time. A sound like something enormous turning over in its bed.

*You are running out of tomorrows,* said the river.

***

He walked down to the water before the town was properly awake, boots crunching on the crust of refrozen slush, breath smoking. This had become his habit since the night he had traced the dispatch case's journey down the broken ice to the workshop door — walking the riverbank in the grey hours, reading Aldous Halloran's old survey marks cut into the stone mooring posts, the neat chiselled numerals of a man who had measured this river for thirty years and loved it in the only way cartographers love anything: precisely, completely, and without ever being permitted to say so.

Elias crouched at the edge of the ice shelf and looked down into the new-open water. It moved beneath the remaining sheet like a living thing under a blanket — black, muscular, impatient. A chunk of ice the size of a coffin lid drifted past, turning slowly, and snagged against a piling with a sound like teeth.

Two weeks. Maybe less. The thaw did not negotiate.

And Merrick's men would not wait for the boats. Riders could come overland from the garrison in six days if the roads had cleared, five if they pushed hard. Elias had been pushing hard himself for years; he knew what men pushing hard looked like, and he knew what they carried, and he knew that an order phrased as *locate the case and resolve the loose end* did not mean *bring her to trial*. Loose ends were not tried. Loose ends were cut.

A child of seven. That was the loose end. A frostbitten mute girl who drew maps of places no map should record, whose half of a dispatch case had floated down a frozen river into the one town in the empire where someone might still read what was inside it and understand.

And a woman who inked coastlines in her dead father's name because she could not yet bear to sign her own.

He straightened, knees aching with cold, and made himself look north along the river road, the way the riders would come. Empty. Muddy at the edges where the frost was letting go. A dog trotted along it with purpose and no destination.

Empty today. He gave it six days. Then he turned back toward the workshop district, and that was when he saw the horse.

It stood outside the Salt & Anchor inn at the far end of the quay — a bay gelding, army-shod, lathered despite the cold, tied with the quick careless knot of a man who expected to ride off again without ceremony. Steam rose off its flanks in the morning air. There was mud to the girth strap: road mud, long-distance mud, the kind that came from the southern post roads, not from anywhere within forty miles.

Elias did not break stride. He walked past the inn at the pace of a man going about dull morning business, and let his eyes do the work his feet refused to hurry. Through the inn's low window: one man seated at a table, civilian coat, travelling cloak still on, a cup of something untouched before him. He sat the way soldiers sit when they are waiting rather than drinking — upright, weight even, hands visible. His eyes were on the street, moving door to door, window to window, cataloguing.

Elias kept walking. Past the inn. Past the fishmonger opening her shutters. Past the workshop with its painted sign and its curtained windows, where a light already burned — Beatrix was always at work before dawn, he'd learned that much — and he did not slow, did not look, though everything in him wanted to turn and put his body between that lit window and the whole southern road.

One man. There would be more. One man was a scout; scouts travelled ahead of the net.

*Six days,* he had thought. *I gave myself six.*

The ice groaned behind him, long and low, like a countdown.

***

By mid-morning the town had learned what the horse meant.

It came to Elias in fragments, the way news travels in small places — the cooper's apprentice bursting in to say soldiers were asking questions at the ferry office; the fishmonger saying no, not soldiers, tax assessors, the colonel's own cousin had sent them; the ferryman saying they'd asked who owned the buildings along Cartwright's Row and written the answers in a little book. By noon the fragments had assembled themselves into a shape Elias recognized from the inside: a survey. Not the crude kicking-down-doors kind — Merrick did not employ crude men for delicate work — but the patient, official, suffocating kind. Men with papers. Men with authority to enter premises for *assessment of customs liabilities*, which was a phrase that could open any door in the empire and close it behind you.

They started at the warehouse end of the row and worked toward the workshop.

Elias watched from the cooper's upper window, and what he felt was not fear exactly. It was stranger than fear — it was the sensation of watching a machine he had been part of operate without him, gears he knew intimately turning in a pattern he could no longer pretend served anything but itself. The tall one, Corben — he knew the face from Merrick's household, had drunk wine across a table from him two winters ago — stood in the doorway of the sailmaker's shop with his notebook open, asking questions in a voice pitched to be pleasant. The other two went through the sailmaker's loft like water finding cracks. An hour. Then the chandler's. Forty minutes. They were getting faster, or the buildings were getting simpler.

Then they stopped at the workshop door.

Beatrix opened it herself. Elias saw her framed in the doorway — small, composed, ink to the second knuckle as always, wearing the expression of a woman mildly inconvenienced by officialdom. Behind her the workshop stretched back in lamplit shadow, and somewhere in that shadow, he knew, Petra was supposed to be in the cellar stair alcove with a slate and a biscuit and instructions to be furniture. He had helped drill it. He hated how good at it the child had become.

Corben talked. Beatrix listened. She gestured once — toward the front room, the display atlases, the legitimate commerce — and Corben smiled and stepped past her, and his two men went in after, and the door closed.

Elias's hand found the windowsill and gripped it.

Twenty minutes. He counted them the way a man counts in a surgeon's hallway. Twenty minutes during which he constructed and discarded four plans, none of which survived contact with arithmetic: one man against three, a street too narrow for subtlety, a child in the building. Twenty minutes during which he understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that he was not standing at this window as Merrick's officer observing an operation. He was standing here as a man waiting to learn whether the woman he —

He declined to finish the sentence even in the privacy of his own skull. Some things deserved better than a windowsill and a countdown.

At twenty-three minutes the door opened. Corben emerged, notebook closed, smiling his pleasant smile, and Beatrix stood in the doorway seeing them off with the courtesy of a shopkeeper glad to be done with paperwork. The men moved on down the row, toward the tannery, their backs to her, and only when they had passed out of sight did Beatrix's posture change — a settling, a loosening, barely perceptible, the way a drawn wire goes slack. She looked up and down the street once, carefully, and then withdrew inside.

She had not looked at the cooper's window. She was too disciplined for that. But Elias would have sworn on his commission — on what remained of it — that she knew exactly where he stood.

***

That evening he burned the rest of it.

Not in the cooper's grate — too public, too many comings and goings with strangers in town — but in the old brick kiln behind the ropewalk, a ruin the boys used for smoking eels and the adults pretended not to see. Elias went after full dark with his travel case under his arm and his collar up, and the rain that had come at dusk was turning to sleet, ticking against the kiln bricks like static.

Inside the case: eleven reports. Five months of them. Every one a masterpiece of the craft Merrick had trained him in — accurate enough to pass inspection, incomplete enough to misdirect, each one steering the colonel's attention a degree off true. The workshop is modest. The woman keeps no company. The child, I am satisfied, is a local foundling unrelated to the matter. The case, I believe, broke from the ice further upstream; my enquiries continue.

Enquiries continue. Five months of *enquiries continue*, and every week the truth had grown heavier in his hands until he could no longer lift it and call it duty.

He fed the pages in one at a time. The kiln took them greedily, orange light climbing the old brick, heat pressing at his face. Each report curled, blackened, and came apart into flakes that rose on the updraft and died against the sleet. He read the first lines of each before burning it — some old habit of thoroughness, or perhaps penance — and the phrases went up around him like the liturgy of a faith he was formally renouncing: *the matter remains contained. No evidence of wider knowledge. Subject appears to pose no security concern.*

Subject. He had written Beatrix Halloran as *the subject* for five months. He had written Petra as *the loose end*. The words had come easily once, the way all of Merrick's vocabulary came easily, clean and administrative, designed so that a man never had to look directly at what he was describing. Standing at the kiln with the sleet coming through the broken roof and the last report curling in his fingers, Elias finally understood that the vocabulary had never been designed for Merrick's comfort.

It had been designed for his.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole elegant machinery of it — you gave men soft words so their hands could stay steady, and steady hands did steady work, and the work was done by morning and everyone slept well. He had slept beautifully for eleven years. He was not certain he had truly slept once since the night a mute child's drawings had shown him a village burning, rendered in a seven-year-old's careful crayon, with little stick figures falling down beside the little stick soldiers.

The last page caught. He watched it go. Ash on the wind, ash on the sleet, ash settling on his sleeves like dirty snow. Somewhere out on the river the ice boomed — a report like distant artillery, the sheet giving way somewhere in the middle channel — and the sound rolled through the ground and up through the soles of his boots.

When the case was empty except for the false bottom, he closed it and stood a moment longer in the kiln's dying light. Eleven reports gone. What remained of his correspondence with Colonel Merrick was now a lie of omission growing larger every day, and lies of omission were the kind that got men hanged slowly, in courtrooms, with their families watching. Treason by paper was survivable. Treason by silence — treason by *feeling* — that was the kind that ruined you thoroughly and called it justice.

He found he did not care. That was the remarkable thing. Eleven years of career, family name, pension, survival, and the scales had tipped in a single season, tipped so quietly he hadn't even heard them move, and now here he stood warming his hands over the ashes of his own obedience and feeling — free.

The word alarmed him more than any of the rest of it.

***

He went to the workshop because he could not not go.

He had a pretext prepared — he always had pretexts prepared, that was the whole disease of him — a question about the atlas commission, a delivery, a correction to the coastal survey he'd supposedly commissioned. He knocked the pattern they'd agreed on: two, pause, one. Footsteps. The bar lifting.

Beatrix opened the door and read his face in the space of a breath, because she had learned to read him the way he'd once learned to read terrain — quickly, completely, and with consequences.

"They're in the town," he said quietly, stepping inside as she shut the door behind him and dropped the bar. "Three of them, maybe a fourth not yet arrived. Customs pretext. They searched the row today. They'll come back to yours — they were being polite today. Politeness is the first pass." He stripped off his wet gloves. "The second pass isn't polite."

She stood very still, lamplight catching the ink stains on her fingers, and he watched her take it in — not the facts, he suspected; those she had probably guessed by noon — but the fact of *him*, standing in her workshop at night with river damp on his coat, telling her the shape of the trap.

"How long?" she said.

"Until the river opens? Two weeks, if we're lucky."

"Not the river." Her eyes didn't waver. "How long until they come back?"

He made himself give it to her straight, because she had earned straightness and because lying to her had become a thing his body now refused to do, like swallowing glass. "Days. Three. Four if Corben is cautious, and he is, but caution only buys politeness twice." He hesitated, and then laid the last card down, hilt-first, because there was no other way to hold it anymore. "There's something else. My reports to the colonel — I've stopped sending them. I've burned them. All of them. As of tonight, as far as anyone knows, this town has gone quiet, and a town going quiet is the one thing men like Merrick cannot abide. When the silence is noticed — and it will be noticed — they won't send a customs man next time."

The lamp flame leaned in a draught from under the door. From the back of the workshop came a faint sound — the scratch of a pencil, quickly stilled. Petra, awake in the shadows, listening the way children listen when they have learned that listening keeps them alive.

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she was deciding, she decided it behind those grey eyes and did not show him the working.

"Then we have three days," she said at last, "and a plate hidden under a decorative border, and a child who cannot be moved safely, and a press that isn't mine, and a printer who may or may not be brave enough." She said it evenly, the way Aldous Halloran must once have read out impossible coordinates — not complaining, simply fixing their position. Then, quieter: "Why did you burn them?"

"Because I read them," Elias said. "All the way through, one last time. And I couldn't find a single line in five months of writing that I'd want read aloud in a courtroom, or at a grave, or to her." He nodded toward the shadowed back of the workshop. "That's the whole of it. There wasn't a true sentence left worth keeping."

Something moved in her face — not softening exactly; more like a door unlocking somewhere behind her eyes and not quite opening. She turned away first, toward the workbench, toward the great sheets of the master atlas sleeping under their weights, and when she spoke again her voice had the briskness of a woman putting grief in its pocket and picking up her tools.

"Three days," she said. "Then we stop engraving and start printing. Help me clear this table — and mind the proofs. If we're going to outrun a thaw, Lieutenant, we begin tonight."

Outside, the river answered her — a long, splitting roar out in the darkness, louder than any night before it, the ice giving way by fathoms. The clock in every scene had become audible at last.

And from the shadows at the back of the workshop, small and clear and utterly unexpected, a child's voice said: *"Then I can help carry."*
