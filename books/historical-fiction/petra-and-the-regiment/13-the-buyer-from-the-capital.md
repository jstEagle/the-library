## 13. The Buyer from the Capital

He came on a Tuesday, when the river still held its ice and the town still believed itself safe.

Elias saw the carriage first — not from the workshop window but from the chandler's steps across the lane, where he had taken to standing each morning with a clay pipe he never lit, watching the road that came down from the capital road. It was a habit Merrick's training had given him and Beatrix's presence had repurposed: the officer's vigilance turned house-guard. He watched the black lacquered carriage take the corner too wide for its own dignity, horses steaming in the cold, and felt the nausea arrive before his mind finished the arithmetic.

Two outriders. Crested door. A man who traveled with outriders in winter was either fleeing something or hunting it.

*Not yet,* Elias told himself. *It could be anyone. A wool factor. A magistrate.*

But he had spent eleven years learning to read men the way Beatrix read coastlines — by their bearing against known features — and the man who stepped down onto the frozen mud of Halloran Lane wore the particular patience of a buyer who did not intend to buy anything. Grey greatcoat, uncreased. Gloves of kid leather, thin enough for a gentleman who expected his hands to stay warm indoors. He stood a moment after dismounting, unhurried, letting his breath hang in the air while his eyes made one slow circuit of the street: the workshop's shuttered upper windows, the chimney breathing its thin ribbon of smoke, the sign with Aldous Halloran's name flaking above the door.

Then the man smiled, as if at a private joke, and went inside.

Elias dropped the pipe into the snow and crossed the lane at a civilian's pace, which was the hardest pace he knew.

* * *

The bell above the workshop door gave its small tin note, and the warmth inside folded around him — lamp oil, linseed, the iron-and-vinegar smell of the etching bath, and under it all the ghost of pipe smoke that five years had failed to evict from the rafters. Beatrix stood at the long drafting table with a burnisher in her hand, and Petra sat in the corner behind the paper press, ostensibly sorting offcuts, actually watching the door the way she always watched doors: with her whole body, still as a held breath.

"Sir," Beatrix said. Her voice did what it always did with strangers — went flat and courteous, a shutters-drawn voice. "If you're wanting the atlas, I'm afraid the master copy is spoken for."

"I'm not wanting to buy it." The man removed his hat, revealing silver hair combed back with soldierly precision, and a face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, to be comfortable instead. "I'm wanting you to make me one."

Elias closed the door behind him and arranged himself by the coat pegs, a hired man's position, a body that belonged near doors. The stranger glanced at him once — the glance of a man inventorying furniture — and dismissed him. That dismissal was the most dangerous thing that had happened all morning.

"Colonel Augustus Merrick," the man said, extending a hand Beatrix did not immediately take. "Retired. Though the title outlives the service, as titles do. You'll be Mistress Halloran?"

"Beatrix Halloran. Master Halloran was my—" a pause so slight only Elias caught its engineering, "—my uncle."

"Aldous Halloran." Merrick said the name the way a man says the name of a wine he remembers fondly from a better year. "I had the pleasure of his acquaintance, some years ago. A craftsman of the old school. There are perhaps four men in the empire who can cut a coastline like Aldous Halloran could, and I have bought maps from three of them."

"He'd have been flattered," Beatrix said. She had taken his hand at last, briefly, and returned to her burnisher. "What map is it you want copied?"

"The atlas." Merrick drifted along the wall of framed proofs, hands clasped behind him, reading the margins the way other men read scripture. "The master work. Every plate, every sheet, bound in the full calf with the gilt tooling. My library has a bay window that wants filling, and there is no ornament so honest as a good map." He stopped before a proof of the northern coast and leaned close. "Look at that hatching. Look at the confidence of it. He never hesitated, did he? Every line laid down as if the coast itself had asked his permission first."

"He didn't much ask permission," Beatrix said. "Of coasts or anyone."

"No," Merrick said softly. "No, I don't imagine he did."

Elias kept his eyes on the middle distance and his hands loose at his sides and thought about anything except the room he was standing in. He thought about the route order locked beneath the false drawer six feet from where the man who had written it now warmed his gloved fingers at Beatrix's stove. He thought about the word *erasure*, which Merrick's agent Corvin had used in their first meeting, meaning Petra, meaning witnesses, meaning the tidy arithmetic by which nine hundred men of the Ninth Regiment had been marched out of the official record and into the ground at a village whose name Elias now knew because a seven-year-old child had broken her silence to say it aloud in the dark.

And this man praised the hatching of the coastlines.

"You knew my uncle's work well, then," Beatrix said. Her burnisher moved in slow circles over a copper plate, and her voice moved with it, level and grinding.

"Well enough to regret our last conversation." Merrick turned from the proofs. His smile was a masterpiece of mildness. "We disagreed about a route. A matter of some tedium — regimental logistics, nothing that would interest a lady of the drawing board. He insisted on accuracy; the army insisted on economy. These collisions happen. I was sorry to hear of his accident."

"It wasn't an accident," Beatrix said.

The words fell into the room like a dropped weight. Elias did not move. Beside the press, Petra's sorting hands had gone still.

Merrick's expression performed surprise beautifully — brows lifting, head tilting, the whole theatre of a man hearing sad news for the second time. "No? What was it, then?"

"Carelessness," Beatrix said. "His own. He worked late. The river path is treacherous in thaw." Her eyes stayed on the plate. "That's what the coroner wrote. But my uncle was not careless. He had walked that path twenty years. So either the coroner lied, or someone knows something they've never said." She set down the burnisher and looked up, and for the first time since the bell rang she gave Merrick her whole gaze, and Elias saw the man register — a flicker, no more — that the niece was more than furniture. "Which is it, Colonel? You knew him. Was he careless?"

For three seconds the workshop was silent enough that Elias heard the stove tick.

"What a loyal creature you are," Merrick said at last, warmly. "Five years, and you still interrogate strangers on his behalf." He spread his hands. "I couldn't say. Men are careless in ways their families can't credit. Grief wants murderers; usually it gets ice." He let that settle, then turned briskly, the buyer again. "Now. The commission. How long for a full copy of the atlas?"

"Months," Beatrix said. "Every plate re-inked and pulled fresh, the binding done proper. If your library can wait until autumn—"

"My library," Merrick said, "can wait until spring."

*Spring.* The word went through Elias like cold water through cloth. Spring meant thaw. Thaw meant boats, supply columns, garrison traffic — meant that whatever Merrick intended, he intended to see it through while the river was open and his authority flowed freely downstream. And it meant the colonel expected to still be coming here, week upon week, into this room, past this door.

"Spring," Beatrix repeated. Noncommittal. A cartographer's word — accurate, promising nothing.

"Perhaps I might inspect the premises," Merrick said pleasantly. "A commission of this size — a man likes to know his money sleeps among honest tools."

* * *

There was nothing to hide. That was the problem. Elias had spent two nights with Beatrix going over the workshop the way sappers go over a bridge, and they had concluded, with the grim satisfaction of conspirators, that the room confessed nothing: the true plates slept in the coal cellar wrapped in oilcloth, the route order lay in the false drawer beneath the drafting table's main tray, and the decorative border that hid the engraved truth ran innocently along the top margin of a plate any inspector would admire without a second glance.

But Merrick was not any inspector. Merrick had commissioned the massacre and then commissioned its forgetting; he read rooms the way Beatrix read charts.

So Elias did the only thing his craft offered. While Merrick toured the drying racks and the stone sinks, Elias positioned himself — naturally, lazily, a servant minding his master's interests — between the colonel and the drafting table. Not blocking. Never blocking. Simply present, the way furniture is present, forcing any approach to curve around him, buying seconds, and hating himself for how little seconds were worth.

"You'll have been with the family long?" Merrick asked him suddenly, without turning.

"Two years, sir. Assistant and general hands."

"Mmm." Merrick lifted a proof from the rack, held it to the light, found it flawless, and put it back with a small sound of satisfaction. "Halloran trained his people well. Or perhaps," and here he smiled at Beatrix across the room, "the school continues itself."

"She's the best hand I've seen," Elias said. He heard his own voice come out steady and was distantly amazed. "Better than the master, some say. She sees water where he saw land."

"Does she." Something moved behind Merrick's pleasantness — interest sharpening into appetite, appetite into calculation, all of it sheathed so fast Elias might have imagined it. "Then I look forward to seeing what she does with my edition."

*Your edition.* The nausea rose clean and cold in Elias's throat, and he mastered it the way he had mastered worse, in worse rooms, and understood even as he swallowed it down that mastery was no longer enough. There was a version of this scene in which he was still the man Merrick had sent — Corvin's instrument, the loose end's designated eraser — and in that version he would be noting the cellar door, the habits, the child's schedule, composing tonight's report in his head. He searched himself for that man and found the report already burned, weeks ago, page by page in the stove while Beatrix slept upstairs; found instead something harder and quieter taking its place, a thing with no name he respected, though he supposed the army had one for it.

Treason was the army's word. He had begun to think of it as alignment.

Petra chose that moment to move. Whether from nerves or from the child's unfailing instinct for the exits, she slipped from behind the press toward the stair to the loft — and Merrick's head turned, smooth as a compass needle, and followed her the whole way.

He looked at her for perhaps four seconds. Elias counted them. In those four seconds the colonel's face did nothing at all, which was itself an event: every other glance he had given the room had performed appreciation, nostalgia, the harmless vanity of a collector. This glance performed nothing. It weighed. It measured a small frost-scarred girl against some private ledger and found the entry familiar.

Then Petra reached the stairs and was gone, and Merrick turned back to Beatrix with his warmth restored, complete, seamless, and asked whether the binding could be done in oxblood rather than green.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Oxblood costs the same."

"Then oxblood." He clapped his gloved hands together softly, a man concluding a satisfactory purchase of ribbons. "I'll leave a deposit. Half now, half on delivery. And I shall return weekly to observe progress — a patron's privilege, I hope? I find I miss the smell of workshops. They smell of certainty." At the door he paused, hat in hand, and delivered his farewell directly to Beatrix with something that in a better man would have been respect. "Your uncle once told me that a map is a promise the land keeps to the traveler. I've thought of that sentence for five years. Do finish the atlas beautifully, Mistress Halloran. Some things deserve to be finished properly — don't you find?"

The bell rang. The carriage took him away.

* * *

Beatrix stood unmoving in the middle of the floor for a long moment after the sound of wheels faded. Then she walked to the window, eased the shutter a finger's width, and watched the lane until the carriage cleared the corner.

"Weekly," she said.

"Weekly," Elias agreed.

"He knows." She turned. Her face in the lamplight was the face he had first mistaken for cold and now knew for granite — load-bearing, weathered, holding. "About the case, about the plate, about her. He doesn't know where any of it is, but he knows it exists, and he's going to stand in this room every week until he finds it. That inspection wasn't an inspection. It was him telling us he's patient."

"He told you something else." Elias crossed to her. "He told you he regrets his last conversation with Aldous. He came here to see whether you know what that conversation was. And you handed him a question instead of an answer — *was he careless?* — which means he leaves knowing you suspect. He'll weigh that. A careful man would have played the grieving niece."

"A careful woman would have." Beatrix's jaw tightened. "You should have seen his face when he looked at her, Elias. Four seconds. I've watched men look at ledgers with less attention."

"I saw it."

"And you stood by the door and called me the best hand you've ever seen."

"It happens to be true."

Something cracked in her then — not the granite, but the mortar between stones. She laughed, one short exhale, half horror, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. "He praised the hatching. He stood there praising the hatching of the coast of the country where he buried a regiment, and he quoted Aldous to me, and he wants it in oxblood." The laugh died. "I'm going to engrave his name into the evidence. He's commissioning the instrument of his own hanging, and paying half up front."

"That," Elias said, "is the finest thing I have ever heard a cartographer say."

She looked at him then, fully, the way she had looked at Merrick — but with the opposite current running under it. Outside, the light was failing early, blue dusk coming up the river valley, and in the loft above them a floorboard creaked: Petra, keeping her own watch, or simply being a child alone in a high room. Beatrix's hand found Elias's sleeve — not his hand, never quite his hand, but the sleeve, which between them had come to mean the same territory.

"When the ice goes," she said quietly, "he'll stop pretending to be a buyer. Whatever he plans, it happens with the thaw. We have until then to pull the sheets, get them out, and disappear. Weekly visits, Elias. He'll be sitting at that table while we print treason in his own commissioned copy."

"Then we'd best make his copy beautiful," Elias said. "He deserves craftsmanship."

She almost smiled. Below them both, in the coal-dark cellar, thirty feet of engraved copper waited with nine hundred names hidden in its border, and upstairs a child waited who had heard a colonel's voice before and would know it again.

From the loft came a small, deliberate sound — three knocks on the floorboards. Their signal: *someone at the lane.*

Elias went to the window. Far down the frozen road, in the last grey light, a lone rider had stopped where the carriage had paused that morning, and sat looking at the workshop, not moving, not leaving, patient as the ice itself.

"He's not done watching," Beatrix said behind him.

"No," Elias said. "But neither are we."
