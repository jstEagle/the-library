## 14. Hidden Plate, Open Hand

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Beatrix decided she could no longer carry it alone.

She had been telling herself for three days that she was waiting for certainty — that she would confess to Elias Vane the moment she was sure of him, and not one hour before. But certainty did not come the way certainty used to come, in the clean geometry of a coastline or the fixed point of a surveyor's mark. Certainty had become a moving thing, a thing that breathed and watched her across the drafting table with grey eyes that gave away less than any map she had ever read.

He was at the far bench now, ostensibly copying Aldous's marginal notes into a buyer's ledger. He had been ostensibly doing things in her workshop for eleven days. Ostensibly examining her father's — her *master's* — tools with a collector's reverence. Ostensibly admiring the atlas plates while his gaze slid, always, toward the cabinet on the north wall, toward the false drawer behind which she kept the letters she had not yet finished reading.

He thought she hadn't seen him find the drawer. She had seen everything: the way his knuckles had brushed the panel twice before he understood there was a hollow behind it, the half-second stillness in his shoulders when the wood answered him differently than solid oak should answer, the careful way he had stepped back as though the drawer were a sleeping animal he didn't wish to wake.

Two liars, she thought, working in the same small room, each convinced they were better at it than the other.

Petra was asleep in the loft, curled around the half dispatch case like a child curled around a doll, because to her it was not leather and brass but the last thing that had crossed the ice with her. Beatrix had checked on her an hour ago, had drawn the blanket up over the frost-scarred fingers, and had stood there in the dark listening to the small breaths and thinking: *I cannot do this alone anymore. Whatever he is, I cannot do this alone.*

So she trimmed the candle, set another beside it so the light would last the hour, and called down from the foot of the ladder in a voice she kept low enough not to wake the child.

"Mr. Vane. Bring your ledger."

* * *

He came. He always came when she summoned; that was one of the things about him that had begun to frighten her, how reliably he arranged himself where she needed him, how a man so obviously trained to move through rooms like smoke would plant himself in hers day after day as if he belonged among the ink pots and the drying racks. He sat across the table from her, laid the ledger down, folded his hands, and waited. Soldiers waited well. She had learned that much about him without learning anything else.

"Before you open that," she said, "I want you to look at something."

"I've been looking at something for eleven days, Mrs. Halloran."

"Not this." She rose, crossed to the north wall, and opened the cabinet — not the false drawer, not first. First she took down the copper plate she had been engraving by candlelight for nine nights, wrapped in oilcloth against tarnish, and carried it back to the table between them like a woman carrying a child across a flooded street.

She unwrapped it under the candlelight.

Elias Vane went very still.

It was the atlas's border plate — the decorative frame of vines and river gods and compass roses that would surround every page of the master atlas, the work of months, beautiful and worthless except as ornament. Except that beneath the vines, cut fine and shallow where only a printer's eye would ever catch it, ran a second line entirely: a route. Dates in Aldous's cipher. Place names disguised as flourishes. The true march of the Ninth Regiment, out of the official record and out of every honest map in the empire, engraved in copper where no censor would think to look because censors looked at maps, never at their margins.

"You're a buyer," Beatrix said. "You came here to buy an atlas. So buy it. Tell me what you see."

He did not touch the plate. His hands stayed folded, but she saw the tendons shift, saw the discipline holding them flat the way a dam holds a river.

"A border," he said quietly. "Very fine work."

"Liar."

"Yes." The word came out of him without hesitation, without flinch, almost with relief. "Border, and a route. The Ninth's route. Marched north along the frozen tributary to a village the army lists as unsurveyed." His eyes came up to hers. "Vell's Ford."

The name landed in the room like a dropped weight. Beatrix made herself breathe through it. "Say it again."

"Vell's Ford. Where the Ninth was sent in the winter of the year your master died." He unfolded his hands at last, and she saw they were not steady. "Where the record says they were never sent at all."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The candles guttered in a draft from under the door; somewhere in the walls the old workshop ticked and settled, the sounds it had made every night of her five years of silence, sounds she had once found comforting and now heard like footsteps.

"How long have you known what that case was?" she asked.

"Since before I knocked on your door." He said it plainly, and she gave him credit for that. "I was sent to find it. And to find the loose end attached to it." His gaze flicked upward, toward the loft, toward the small breathing weight of the sleeping child, and came back. "That's what the order said. *The loose end.* Not a child. A loose end."

"And you're going to report it. Report us both." It was not quite a question. Her voice held, because she had spent five years training her voice to hold, but underneath it something was tearing loose, some plank she had nailed down the night she'd taken Petra in off the ice.

"No," Elias said.

The word hung there. Beatrix realized her hands were flat on the table on either side of the copper plate, guarding it, and that she had done it without deciding to.

"No," he said again, more softly. "I haven't reported since the third day. My reports since then have been fiction. Careful ones — I'm told I write convincing ones — but fiction. The case is lost on the river, I wrote. The child was never at the workshop. The cartographer keeps her accounts and sees no one." A breath moved through him that might have been a laugh if it had been allowed to be anything. "Eleven days of treason, Mrs. Halloran, written in my best hand."

"Why?"

"That is not a question I can answer to your satisfaction, because I can't fully answer it to mine. I walked the river for two nights reading your master's survey marks, and somewhere out on that ice I stopped being able to tell which orders were mine to follow." He turned his palms up on the table — an officer's gesture of surrender, of empty hands — and she saw the ink stain on the side of his right forefinger, the stain he'd earned grinding pigment for her because she'd asked and he'd stayed. "But I know what I'm asking now."

"Then ask."

"I'm asking you to trust me. Knowing you have no reason to. Knowing that everything about how I arrived here was a lie, and that the man who sent me will send others who won't lie." He leaned forward slightly, and the candlelight caught the tired lines of his face, the disgrace he wore like a second uniform. "Merrick is coming. Not his agents — Merrick himself. He'll arrive under cover of commissioning a copy of the atlas. He'll be charming. He'll praise your master's craft to your face while he decides how you die. I know because I know how he works, and because—" he hesitated, the first crack in all that discipline, "—because I received the travel schedule this morning, and I've been trying all day to decide whether giving it to you makes me a traitor or finally an honest man."

Beatrix looked at him for a long time. The candle flame stood straight between them, and beyond it his face was half-lit and half-dark, and she thought: *this is the moment. Everything after this moment is a different life.*

Trust offered hilt-first, she thought, like a blade turned so the handle faces you and the edge faces the man offering it. She had spent five years learning that the safe hand was the closed one. Now a man was opening his across her table and asking her to take hold.

She reached under the table, into the drawer that was not false at all, and brought out the letters.

Aldous's correspondence — the widow's letters, the route order with its annotations in his fierce small hand, the refusal he had written two days before his death was ruled accidental by men who had never once in their lives had an accident. She stacked them beside the copper plate, beside the atlas proofs, until the whole truth of the workshop lay open on the table between them: the route, the proof, the witness asleep above their heads, and the dead man whose name had kept it all alive.

"There," she said. "Everything. The plate. The route. The girl. Her name is Petra; she watched soldiers die at Vell's Ford and has not spoken a word since, except to me, once, at night, when she named that village and described a voice. She is seven years old and she is the only living witness to the massacre your colonel ordered, and I will burn this workshop down around myself before I let anyone make her a loose end." Her voice did shake now, and she let it. "Your turn, Lieutenant. All of it. What you are, whom you serve, why you're really here — and then choose. Because I'm done guessing at men across a drafting table. Choose, and live inside your choice."

He chose.

He told her about the regiment he had served and the order he had refused and the career it had cost him — not destroyed, which would have been cleaner, but *disgraced*, the slow poison, the postings nobody wanted, until Colonel Merrick had found him drowning and offered him rope with a hook in it. He told her what the dispatch case had been: the property of a lieutenant of the Ninth who had carried the true route order out of Vell's Ford and died on the ice upstream with it strapped to him, and how the river, thawing in its own season, had delivered it to a child instead of to the men who wanted it buried. He told her about the reports he had burned in his lodgings two nights ago, page by page, feeding his own words to the fire.

And then he drew a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the table beside Aldous's letters, and did not take his hand off it until she covered it with hers.

"Merrick's itinerary. Arrival by packet boat Thursday, under the name Corvus. He'll stay at the Ferryman's Rest because he likes a room overlooking water he can control. He'll come here Friday morning, before market, when the street is empty." Elias's voice was level, but his eyes were not; they had gone bright and hard, the brightness of a man who had stepped off a cliff and discovered mid-fall that he preferred the air. "Friday morning, Beatrix. That's how long we have."

"We." She said the word carefully, testing its weight.

"If you'll allow it."

She should ask more questions. She knew she should — about what he intended, about what a disgraced officer could actually stand between a colonel and a child, about whether eleven days of quiet kindness could possibly outweigh the machinery of a man who had ordered a regiment marched into a ravine and erased. She should ask whether she was handing the last witness of Vell's Ford to the one person in the world who could destroy them all with a signature.

Instead she looked at his hands on the table — the ink-stained hand, the scarred one, both of them open — and she remembered Master Aldous saying once, over a chart that refused to reconcile, *the truth doesn't need to be defended, Bea, it needs to be printed.* And she thought: this man burned his reports. This man sat in the room with the man who owned his ruin and chose a cartographer and a mute child instead.

"Thursday," she said. "Then we have tonight and tomorrow to finish the plate. If Merrick wants to commission a copy of the atlas, he'll inspect the press, the plates, the proofs — everything. Which means everything must be ready to show him, and nothing must be ready to tell him." She pulled the oilcloth back over the copper plate, and her hands had gone steady again, the way they always did when the work began. "Can you get word to the printer's widow without Merrick's people seeing?"

"I can get word anywhere without Merrick's people seeing. It's the one skill they left me." He rose, gathered the ledger, and paused at the door — and for a moment the officer fell away entirely and he was just a tired man standing in lamplight, looking at a woman who had handed him everything she had and asked nothing but honesty in return. "Why did you show me the plate? Before you knew. You had no reason."

"I had five years of reasons," Beatrix said. "They were getting heavy."

He nodded slowly, as if she had given him something more valuable than the schedule in his coat, and went out into the dark.

Above her, in the loft, Petra stirred in her sleep and murmured something too soft to be a word. Beatrix banked the candles, climbed the ladder, and lay down beside the child, listening to the river ice groan and shift in the distance — the sound of winter loosening its grip, the sound of everything coming.

Three days, she thought, holding the small warm weight of Petra's shoulder through the blanket. Three days until the charming man came to admire her master's craft.

Outside, the ice cracked again, long and deep, like something enormous beginning to move.
