## 15. Proofs and Pursuit

The first proof sheet came off the press at half past two in the morning, damp as a newborn thing, and Beatrix held it up to the candle with hands that would not entirely stop shaking.

The paper was cheap — the printer's widow, Mrs. Quill, had insisted on it, saying fine stock drew the eye of customs men and cheap stock drew only flies — but the image on it was anything but cheap. There, beneath the decorative border of scrollwork and river-horses that Aldous had engraved twenty years ago and Beatrix had recut in secret over eleven sleepless nights, ran a second line. A hidden line. The true route of the Ninth Regiment, marching east out of Kesselmark toward a village whose name appeared in no official ledger, rendered in Aldous's own annotation hand: *ordered by Col. E. Merrick, by authority of —* and then the torn edge where the dispatch case's document had been ripped, and beyond the rip, in Petra's careful childish letters transcribed into copper, the word *Vhale*.

The proof was beautiful. That was the terrible part of it. Truth, set in copper and ink, looked like any other map — rivers blue-brown, roads stitched in dotted confidence, the little regimental standard printed marching along a road that the War Office swore had never existed.

"It's crooked," said Mrs. Quill from the doorway of the cellar, arms folded against the cold. She was a broad woman gone soft at the middle, with her late husband's ink permanently ground into the creases of her knuckles, and she had not asked a single question in three weeks that Beatrix had not chosen to answer. "The border sits two degrees proud of the plate on the west margin. You'll want to pack it out before you run three hundred."

Beatrix looked closer. The woman was right. The border sat proud by the width of a hair.

"Eleven nights," Beatrix murmured, "and I pack it out with a shaving of cork."

"Everything worth doing comes down to shavings of cork." Mrs. Quill took the proof from her, held it at arm's length, squinted. For a long moment she said nothing, and Beatrix watched her read the hidden line — really read it — and understood that whatever this woman had guessed about the nature of the work, she now knew. "My husband printed tax ledgers for the garrison for thirty years," Mrs. Quill said finally. "Numbers don't lie, but men who count them do. He used to say a press is just an honest mouth that never gets tired. Run your three hundred through my mouth, Miss Halloran. It won't tire."

* * *

They pulled proofs until dawn. By the time grey light came through the cellar grate, six sheets hung drying on lines strung between the presses, and Beatrix stood among them like a woman standing in a strange orchard, fruit she had grown in secret now hanging naked in other people's air.

She should have felt triumphant. She felt exposed. Every sheet was a confession; every hour they dried was an hour someone might walk through that door above and find the whole testimony hanging like laundry.

Elias arrived at four, coming down the outside stair so as not to pass the front of the shop, his collar up, snow in his hair. He had taken to entering rooms the way he'd once left them — backwards, watching the street.

"Merrick's men found the house in Candle Lane," he said, without preamble. "The widow Ostrava's lodgings. Empty since autumn, but they turned it inside out anyway. Floorboards, mattress ticking, the chimney flue."

Beatrix went still. "The letters."

"They didn't find them."

"They're not there to find. They're here." She touched nothing as she said it; her eyes went instead to the false drawer beneath the drafting table, and Elias followed her glance and shook his head once, sharply.

"Not here either. Not anymore."

"What do you mean —"

"I mean that three days ago I moved them. The correspondence, the route order, everything that can hang us — it's sewn into the lining of a surveyor's satchel under the floor of the boat shed on Tanner's Reach, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted to sink if anyone but me opens the hatch." He said it flatly, the way a man reports the position of enemy guns. "I should have told you sooner. If I'd been taken — if they'd searched here —"

"You were protecting the work."

"I was protecting you from knowing too much to survive a search." His jaw tightened. "It's what Merrick does, you understand. Not cruelty first — arithmetic first. He removes what a person knows, and then the person is safe, because the person is nothing. I've spent two years being nothing on purpose. It stops working the moment you start loving things in the open."

The word landed between them and neither pretended it hadn't. Beatrix looked at the drying proofs, six pale witnesses swaying slightly in the cellar draft.

"Then we stop keeping things in the open," she said. "Starting with Petra."

* * *

Petra was awake when Beatrix came up — she was always awake at dawn, a habit Beatrix suspected came from wherever the child had walked from, some instinct that said morning was when the bad things checked the doors. She sat cross-legged on the workbench in the lamplight with a stub of graphite, drawing on the backs of Beatrix's spoiled proofs: the workshop cat that did not exist, drawn anyway; the river drawn as a fat sleeping animal; the press drawn as a church.

"We're going on a visit," Beatrix said, kneeling to be at her level. She kept her voice light, cartographer's-light, the tone one used for labeling coastlines. "To Mrs. Quill's house. Just for a few days. Until the ice is fully gone and the boats come."

Petra looked at her. The child's silences had changed quality over the winter — no longer the silence of damage but the silence of judgment, a small person weighing each adult statement against some inner ledger of promises kept and broken. Beatrix had learned to wait out that weighing.

"You're staying here," Petra signed. Her hands were quick now; five months of the workshop's language of fingers and chalk and margins.

"For now. Someone has to finish the atlas."

"Men came to the window last night," Petra signed. "Two. They counted the windows."

Ice went through Beatrix's spine. "You saw them?"

"They thought I was asleep. I wasn't." The child's chin lifted. "I'm never asleep when they think I am. That's how I stayed alive."

Beatrix gathered her in then, graphite-smudged fingers and all, and felt the small rigid body slowly decide to soften. Over the girl's head she could see the proofs hanging below-stairs through the open cellar door, and above them the atlas itself on its table, and around all of it the workshop that had been a fortress for five years and was now, by every sign, a net closing.

"There are soldiers who want to take you away," she said quietly. "Not because you did anything wrong. Because you remember something they wish nobody remembered. So we do what mapmakers do when the territory gets dangerous. We move the treasure."

"Am I the treasure?"

"You are the whole country, Petra. Everything else is coastline."

* * *

Packing the child took ten minutes, which broke Beatrix's heart more efficiently than any siege could have. Five months in the workshop, and everything Petra owned fit into one satchel: three dresses, the half dispatch case she refused to surrender even to sleep, a tin of graphite stubs, and the drawings — dozens of them, the whole winter's testimony in pictures — rolled and tied with string.

At the door the child stopped. Turned. Looked back at the great room — at the drafting table with its worn hollows where Aldous's elbows had rested, at the plate cabinet, at the candle guttering in its dish — the way pilgrims look back at shrines.

Then she reached up and took Beatrix's hand.

And did not let go.

They got as far as the second step of the outside stair. Beatrix knelt again, prying gently at the small grip finger by finger, and Petra simply re-gripped, both hands now, her face set in the expression Beatrix had come to know as the one that outlasted frostbite and hunger and soldiers.

"Mrs. Quill has a dog," Beatrix tried. "A monstrous dog. He eats children who hold hands too long."

Petra did not smile. She signed with one hand while keeping the other locked in Beatrix's: *"If I go, they'll hurt you while I'm not looking."*

"No one will hurt me."

"You said Master Aldous was safe too."

The words went in under the ribs. Beatrix closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the child was watching her with the terrible patience of the bereaved.

"Listen to me," Beatrix said. "There are maps I make that are lies — beautiful ones, official ones, the kind that keep everyone comfortable. And there is one map that is true, and I cannot engrave it and guard you at the same time, because the guarding takes both hands and the engraving takes both hands, and God did not give me four." She turned Petra's small hands over, palms up, ink-stained at the edges now, apprentice-stained. "These hands are going to learn lettering someday. But first they have to survive the spring. Do you understand what I'm asking?"

Petra looked at their joined hands for a long time. Then she let go — deliberately, finger by finger, the way one strikes a camp — and signed: *"Three days. Then I come back and check the windows myself."*

"Agreed. Three days."

The child went down the stairs without looking back this time, satchel bouncing, and Mrs. Quill met her at the bottom with the monstrous dog already nosing at the satchel strings, and Beatrix stood on the landing in the grey cold with her right hand still curved around the ghost of a smaller one, feeling the shape of the absence all the way up her arm.

* * *

By noon the net had drawn tighter than Elias had predicted.

He saw it from the window of the chandler's shop across the lane, where he'd established himself with a fictitious order for lamp oil and a view of the workshop door. Two men in civilian wool — wrong shoulders, wrong boots, army boots under merchant coats, a disguise so lazy it insulted him — had taken positions at either end of Cartwright's Row. A third had gone into the harbormaster's office carrying a document folder with government binding. Within the hour, every manifest on the river would carry a description: *woman, dark-haired, mid-thirties; child, seven, mute; deserter officer, surname Vane.*

His own name. His own crime, printed on a hunting license.

He paid for oil he didn't need and walked back the long way, along the frozen towpath, doing the arithmetic he hated. The proofs were dry by tomorrow night. The full run needed two nights in the cellar after that. Merrick's men had found the empty lodgings yesterday; today they'd sealed the row; tomorrow they would knock on the workshop door with a warrant and courtesy. He had perhaps sixty hours, and the woman upstairs intended to spend forty of them engraving corrections onto a copper plate by candlelight because a border sat two degrees proud.

He climbed the outside stair and found her exactly where he'd known she'd be, sleeve pinned back, graver in hand, bent over the plate with a magnifier screwed into her eye socket like a surgeon.

"The border can be crooked," he said from the doorway.

"It cannot."

"People die of crooked borders every day, Beatrix. Usually the borders themselves."

"Pack it out with cork or lose the margin, but the line stays true." She didn't look up. The graver whispered along the copper, lifting a thread of metal finer than hair. "Aldous used to say a mapmaker's shame outlives her by a century. Somewhere a hundred years from now, someone unrolls this and checks it against the ground, and either I lied beautifully or I told the truth imperfectly. I've made my choice."

He stood in the doorway a moment longer, watching her work, and understood that he was looking at the reason he had burned his own reports, betrayed his colonel, and was presently a wanted man standing in a freezing attic. Not the atlas. The hands making it.

"They've named me on the manifests," he said. "Vane. Deserter. They'll show my likeness at the toll bridges by morning."

Now she looked up, magnifier still in her eye, so that for a moment her gaze was enormous and distorted and he nearly laughed despite everything.

"Then you can't leave the workshop," she said.

"I can't stay either. If they find me here, they find the proofs."

"Then we finish tonight." She pulled the magnifier free and set down the graver, and her voice had gone very quiet and very steady, the voice she used on the last inch of a coastline. "Tonight we pull the corrected proof, and tomorrow night we start the run, and you — Elias Vane, deserter, liar, cartographer of other people's sins — are going to sit at my table and learn to pack out a border with cork, because I refuse to do the last of this alone."

Outside, down on the frozen river, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep, the first true complaint of the thaw. Both of them heard it. Neither spoke.

But Beatrix picked up the cork and the knife, and cut the first shaving.
