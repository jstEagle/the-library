## 17. Merrick's Offer

The summons came by a boy who did not knock so much as stand in the doorway of the workshop until Beatrix looked up from her drafting table. He was perhaps twelve, dressed too finely for the river town, and he held out a card as though it might bite him: heavy stock, cream-colored, embossed with a crest she did not recognize and a name she had come to dread without yet seeing it written down.

*Colonel A. Merrick. Requests the pleasure of Mrs. Halloran's company. This evening. The Mermaid's Rest, private room.*

No threat in the wording. No threat anywhere on the card. That was the first thing she understood about the man before she ever met him — that his violence arrived dressed as courtesy, folded twice, with an invitation.

She read it three times while the boy waited, shifting his weight, and then she said, "Tell your master I will come."

The boy's eyes flicked up, surprised — perhaps he had been instructed to expect refusal, or pleading, or some third thing he had not been given lines for. He left.

Elias was not there to argue with her. He had gone at dusk to watch the garrison road, where wagons had been moving all afternoon in a rhythm too regular for supply and too irregular for drill, and she had watched him go with a feeling she could only describe as standing at the edge of thawing ice — knowing the sound beneath her feet, knowing what it meant, unable to stop listening for the crack.

So it was alone that she pinned her collar, banked the stove low, and told Petra, in the careful voice they had built together over weeks of drawings and silence, "I have an errand. You know the rules."

Petra knew the rules. Petra pressed herself against Beatrix's legs anyway, small arms tight as a rope knot, and Beatrix knelt and held her and felt the child's heart going fast against her own ribs like a bird trapped under glass.

"I'll come back," she said. "I always come back."

It was not entirely true. It had been true so far. She kissed the top of Petra's head, smelled woodsmoke and ink in her hair — *my* smells now, she thought absurdly, *the workshop's* smells, transferred through proximity like dye through water — and made herself stand up and walk away before either of them could make it harder than it already was.

The Mermaid's Rest sat where the high street met the quay, a coaching inn grown fat on the coming season's trade. The public rooms were loud with boatmen and merchants drunk on the promise of open water, but a servant in livery led her past all of it, up a narrow stair, down a corridor paneled in dark wood, to a door that opened onto warmth and lamplight and a man rising from behind a desk that had clearly been carried in for the occasion.

"Mrs. Halloran." Colonel Ambrose Merrick was not what she had expected, though she could not afterward have said what she *had* expected. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, built like a man who had once been athletic and had since settled comfortably into his own authority. His face was the kind that portraits were made from — strong jaw, level gray eyes, an expression of patient good humor that seemed genuinely worn rather than painted on. "You honor me. I confess I've been looking forward to this conversation for some time. May I offer you wine? The inn's cellar is modest but the claret is honest."

"No, thank you."

"Then sit, at least." He gestured to a chair set across the desk from his own, angled just slightly — she noted it automatically, the cartographer's habit — so that no lamp stood between them. So that each could see the other's face plainly. A courtesy, or a tactic, or both.

She sat.

He studied her for a moment with what appeared to be real interest, the way a collector studies a piece whose provenance has finally been confirmed. "You have her hands," he said. "I never met Aldous Halloran myself — my loss, everyone tells me. But I knew his work before most people knew his name. There is a hand that draws coastlines, and there is a hand that merely copies them. Yours, I am told, is the former. Forgive an old soldier's curiosity. I wanted to see it in person."

"You've seen my work?"

"My dear woman, I've seen everything you've done for five years." He smiled, and the smile was warm, and something cold moved down Beatrix's spine like water finding a crack in stone. "The atlas. The survey charts sold to the harbor trust under the old man's name after his death. The corrections to the tidal tables. You are better than he was, if you'll permit me to say so — he had the greater eye, but you have the steadier nerve. Grief does that, sometimes. Concentrates a person."

"You know a great deal about me."

"I make it my business to know about craftsmen. Good ones are rare, and rarer still are good ones who can keep their mouths shut." He leaned back, folding his hands over his waistcoat, a man settling in for a pleasant evening. "Which brings me, I'm afraid, to business. But first — the child."

Beatrix kept her face still. She had spent five years learning to keep her face still, and if any hour of that discipline had ever been worth its cost it was this one.

"The child," Merrick went on, gently, as though continuing a thought they had shared, "was seen. In your workshop. A girl, seven years old or thereabouts, mute, arrived sometime around the last hard frost carrying half of a military dispatch case. I mention this not to alarm you but because I want us to begin honestly. Everything tonight begins honestly; I insist upon it. You took her in. An act of charity — very much to your credit. The question is simply whether you understand *what* you took in."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then we may speak plainly, and I think you will find plain speech a relief. You've lived in plain sight and total silence for five years, Mrs. Halloran. Silence is exhausting. I've watched it wear people down to nothing." His voice softened, and the terrible thing — the thing she would remember later, lying awake — was that the softness sounded genuine. "The Ninth Regiment marched out of this province eleven years ago, two hundred and forty men, and the official record states they were rerouted to the coastal garrison at Vellmark, where they were subsequently disbanded and dispersed. The official record is a document. Documents are written by clerks. Clerks write what they are given." He spread his hands, an elegant little shrug. "You have found something that suggests the record is incomplete. Perhaps you have even found the rest of it. And you are sitting on it, in a wooden building beside a frozen river, with a child who cannot testify and a deserter officer who thinks I don't know he's here."

The room did not spin. She gave it permission to do nothing of the kind. Somewhere below them the inn roared with ordinary laughter, and the ordinary laughter made the quiet between them feel like the inside of a bell.

"Elias Vane," she said.

"Is a disappointment to his family and a curiosity to me. Let's leave him aside; he's a weathercock, they always are. I want to talk about *you*." Merrick rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the dark river with his hands clasped behind him — a commander reviewing ground, she thought, though there was nothing left to review but ice. "Here is what I came to offer, and I offer it sincerely, because I believe you are intelligent enough to weigh it properly.

"Finish the atlas. Finish it exactly as you have been finishing it — every coastline, every sound, every depth sounding, the finest piece of cartography this province has ever produced. I have read the proof sheets your printer's man let slip, and I tell you frankly it is magnificent. But it will bear *my* name. Colonel A. Merrick, by appointment, presented to the Survey Office and to Parliament's committee on provincial administration. Your name appears nowhere. Aldous Halloran's name appears nowhere. The route order you found — and yes, I know about the route order, I have known about it since before you did, since the day it was carried out of a burning farmhouse by a child who should have died with the rest of them — goes into my fire, along with the plate you've been engraving beneath the decorative border of sheet nine. I admire the hiding place, by the way. Aldous used the same trick in '61. Craftsmen repeat themselves; it's how we're caught.

"In exchange: a house. Not here — here is about to become unhealthy for you — but in the capital, or Vellmark, wherever you please. A stipend that will make this"—he gestured vaguely at her worn cuffs, her ink-stained fingers—"*this* look like what it was, which is a long apprenticeship in poverty. Commission work, if you want it; obscurity, if you don't. And the girl—" he turned back from the window, and his face was still kind, still reasonable, still the face from the portrait, "—the girl comes to me. She will be raised comfortably. Educated. Her silence will be treated by competent physicians. She will want for nothing, and she will remember nothing, because children forget, Mrs. Halloran, when they are given enough new things to look at. That is not cruelty. That is mercy, of a kind. The alternative to my mercy is far less comfortable for everyone involved, including her."

He sat back down. He poured himself a measure of claret, unhurried, and did not offer her one again, because the offer had been made and repeated and the wine was only for men who had finished talking.

"So," he said. "There it is. Comfort, safety, your craft practiced at the highest level, a child alive and fed and eventually whole — weighed against a copper plate and a dead man's grievance. I don't think it's a difficult sum, Mrs. Halloran. I think you've simply never been permitted to do the arithmetic honestly, because grief makes poor bookkeepers of us all."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment.

She found that she was not afraid — or rather, that fear was present, somewhere in the room, the way weather is present behind glass, but it was not *hers*, not in the way it had been hers for five years, hers like a coat she wore indoors. What she felt instead was a strange, clean clarity, the clarity of the moment when ink meets plate and there is no taking the line back.

"You said we would begin honestly," she said.

"I did."

"Then let me be honest." She laid her hands flat on the desk between them, palms down, the way she braced paper before cutting. "You are right that I cannot remember Master Aldous's face clearly anymore. Five years will do that. I remember his hands, and his cough, and the way he said *again* when a line wasn't true. I have built my whole life since his death on not remembering — on finishing his atlas and signing it with his name and telling myself that was devotion, when it was also cowardice, because as long as I hid inside his name I never had to say what I knew or act on it. You are offering to buy the same cowardice back from me at a higher price."

Merrick's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, minutely — a man recalculating, discovering the map did not match the ground.

"A map," she went on, quietly, "is a promise. That is the first thing he ever taught me, before I could hold a graver steady. Anyone can draw a coastline. The promise is that the coastline is *true* — that a ship's master trusts his life to your line and is not betrayed by it. Every map that lies is a murder postponed, he said. He used to say it to apprentices who fudged a sounding to save an afternoon's work." She lifted her eyes to Merrick's, and she let him see everything in hers, because concealment was over; concealment had been the whole of her life and it had bought her nothing but this room and this man. "Two hundred and forty men were marched off the official record and killed, and a village burned around a seven-year-old girl, so that your name would stay clean. And you sit there and offer me comfort to engrave your lie into copper — to make it beautiful, to make it *last*. Copper endures, Colonel. That is why we use it. You are asking me to forge a document that will outlive both of us."

"And I am offering you life in exchange," Merrick said. His voice was still mild. Only his hands had gone quiet on the desk, and she understood that the mildness had always been the weapon, that the gun was the room itself, the soldiers downstairs, the wagons on the garrison road. "I want you to hear the arithmetic one more time. Refuse me, and tomorrow morning this town fills with men whose instructions I will not be able to soften a second time. Your workshop. Your printer. The widow who keeps your fosterling. Yourself. And the girl — I will have her regardless, Mrs. Halloran. That is not a threat; it is a fact of the situation, the way the thaw is a fact. You cannot engrave fast enough. You cannot hide her well enough. The only variable left in this equation is how many people die around you while you try. Accept, and no one dies. Refuse, and many do. That is the whole of it."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "That is the whole of it."

She stood. Her knees were steady, which surprised her; she would have expected them to shake. She picked up her gloves from the arm of the chair and did not put them on, because her hands were ink-stained and she wanted them seen.

"Master Aldous had a phrase," she said. "For maps that tell the truth. He said the truth needs no patron — it only needs *hands*. Someone willing to hold the line while the world argues with it. He held it, and you had him drowned in the mill race and called it an accident. I have held it for five years in silence, and I am done being silent. So my answer is no. Keep your house and your stipend. Keep your clean record. I will finish the atlas — every line of it, truer than anything your Survey Office has ever stamped its seal on — and it will carry the names of the people who actually made it, and the route order will be engraved where no fire of yours can reach it, and the girl will grow up knowing that the men who killed her village lost."

Merrick looked at her for a long time. When he spoke again, all the warmth had drained out of his voice and left behind something flat and administrative, the tone of a man initialing a requisition.

"You misunderstand your position."

"No," Beatrix said. "I understand it perfectly. I simply decline it."

At the door she paused, her hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"There is one more thing you should know, Colonel, since we began honestly. You asked what I took in, the night the child came to my door. I took in a witness. And witnesses are like plates, sir — you can burn the workshop, but the impression has already been pulled."

She heard him rise behind her, heard the scrape of the chair, and for one suspended moment she thought he would order her stopped — thought of the soldiers below, the dark stair, the short walk to the quay where the ice was groaning out its nightly ledger. But the latch gave under her hand, and the corridor was empty, and no voice called after her, and only when she was three streets away did she understand why he had let her go.

He had not needed to stop her. He had needed only to confirm what was in the workshop, and how much of it, and who would be inside when the match was struck.

She ran the last stretch through the freezing dark, skirts gathered in both fists, breath tearing, and saw it from the corner of Quay Lane — a single figure crouched in the lee of the chandlery across from her door, watching the workshop's windows with the stillness of a man counting lights.

Not Elias. Too broad, wrong coat.

And behind the workshop's frost-feathered glass, one candle still burned at Petra's bedside, small and gold and utterly exposed, like a signal lit for anyone on the river who had been paid to read it.
