## 18. Fire on the Water

### Beatrix

She smelled it before she saw it — a sweetness under the cold, like tallow left too near a flame — and her hands went still over the proof sheets before her mind caught up to what her nose already knew.

Smoke.

"Petra." The name came out flat and low, the way she had trained herself to say it in these weeks of hiding: not a cry, never a cry, but a bell rung once inside the house. "Petra, wake up. Boots. Now."

The girl was already moving. Seven years old and she knew the sound of trouble in a building better than any grown woman Beatrix had ever met; she came down the attic ladder in her shift, dragging her coat, her eyes wide and terribly calm in the candlelight. She did not ask questions. That, too, was something the world had taught her that no child should have to learn.

Beatrix crossed to the front window and pulled back the shutter a finger's width.

They were coming up Lantern Row from the river side, three men in plain coats, walking without hurry, the way men walk when they own the night and know it. One carried a dark lantern. Another carried something else — a squat clay vessel with a rag at its neck, and even at this distance, even by that thin moonlight, Beatrix knew what it was. Aldous had kept one in the workshop for melting pitch, years ago. She had held it in her own hands a hundred times.

*They're going to burn it,* she thought, and the thought arrived strangely, almost gently, the way a surgeon's knife arrives. *Five years of my life. His whole life. They're going to burn it down around a child.*

No. Not around the child. Around the plate.

The hidden copper plate lay in its slot beneath the false drawer of the drafting table — eleven pounds of engraved truth, the Ninth's true route cut line by line into metal by candlelight over six weeks of stolen nights. It could not be replaced. Her hands could be replaced by no one; Petra's testimony lived only in Petra; but the plate was the thing itself, the argument made permanent, the truth you could hold. If Merrick burned the workshop, he would burn the atlas's heart along with its body.

And he would burn them with it, if they were found inside.

"Elias," she whispered, though Elias was not there — Elias was across town in the printer's cellar watching the first three hundred copies come off the press, and God only knew when he'd return, or whether he'd return to ash. She said his name anyway, the way one says a prayer one doesn't believe in, and then stopped saying prayers and started working.

"The coal chute," she said to Petra, kneeling so their faces were level. "You remember. We practiced."

Petra nodded. Once.

"We take nothing but the plate. Nothing. Do you understand? Not the drawings. Not your mother's—" Beatrix caught herself; Petra had no mother's anything here, only the dispatch case half, which the girl slept with under her pillow like a talisman, and which Beatrix saw now, in the corner of her eye, being buckled onto the small body without being told. Good. Let her keep that. Some things were worth more than rules.

The smell of smoke thickened. Under the door seam, along the floorboards, a thread of gray crept like a living thing.

"They've lit the back stairs," Beatrix breathed. Of course they had. Seal the exits, then feed the fire — the same thoroughness that had marched a regiment into a valley and written *never there* in the official record. These were careful men. Careful men were the most dangerous kind, because they left nothing behind, not evidence, not witnesses, not even regret.

She took up the poker and opened the false drawer the way Aldous had taught her to open all secrets — smoothly, without sound, as if the wood had always meant to move that way. The plate lay beneath, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. Eleven pounds. She could carry it. She had carried heavier griefs for longer distances.

From the back of the house came a soft whump, the sound of flame finding dry pine, and heat pressed against the kitchen door like a palm.

"Chute," she said again, and took Petra's hand, and ran.

* * *

### Elias

He smelled the fire from four streets away and began to run.

It was a mistake and he did it anyway. Four streets of running through a sleeping town in an officer's stride he hadn't unlearned in eight years of trying — any sentry, any drunk constable would mark him — but the smoke stood up over the rooftops on Lantern Row black and wrong, black the way coal-oil burns rather than the way accidents burn, and every disciplined thought in him drowned under one word: *Petra*, and under that, quieter and worse, one name he had not yet said aloud to anyone including himself: *Beatrix*.

He had spent the night in the cellar with ink to his elbows and triumph in his mouth. Three hundred copies. Three hundred copies of the atlas rolling off the press while a sergeant's boots crossed the floorboards overhead, and Elias had talked his way past the man with a bill of lading and a lie about government freight, and for one hour he had believed the worst was over. He had actually believed it. He had walked out into the frozen street thinking, *it's done, it's loose in the world, no one can unprint it* —

—and the whole time, Merrick's men had been walking up Lantern Row with a fire pot in their hands.

Because of course. Of course. Burning paper was cheap; burning a workshop was cheaper than a trial; and a fire explained everything — the missing case, the missing widow's letters, the missing cartographer and her bastard officer. Accidents were how the army wrote its corrections. He had served under that grammar for eight years. He should have seen it coming in his own handwriting.

He came around the corner by the chandler's and stopped dead, because the workshop was already alight — the back of it, the roofline above the old survey room glowing from within like a lantern with a soul trapped in it — and two figures were crouched at the coal chute door on the alley side, and one of them was small, and the other was hauling at something heavy, and the relief that went through him nearly put him on his knees in the snow.

Then he saw the fourth man.

The watcher stood at the mouth of the alley, out of the light, arms folded, waiting for the building to finish its work. A professional. And between the professional and the chute door lay thirty feet of open snow with nowhere to hide.

Elias did the arithmetic the way he'd once done it at fords and bridges: distance, time, angles, the weight of the man, the length of his reach. He had no weapon. He had a strong back, eight years of soldiering, and a fury he had been carrying since the day he'd read a route order and understood what he was being asked to clean up after.

He picked up the iron bar lying beside the chandler's gate and walked into the open.

* * *

### Beatrix

The chute was built for coal, not people, and certainly not for copper plates wrapped in oilcloth. It was barely wider than her shoulders, pitched steeply down into the cellar where the bin sat, and it stank of a decade of black dust. Behind her, the kitchen door had begun to breathe smoke through its seams in slow pulses, like some vast animal sleeping against the house.

"In front of me," she told Petra. "Slide. Don't stand. Go."

The girl went down into the dark without a sound, and Beatrix fed the plate in after her, guiding it by touch as it scraped and groaned along the iron, and then the heat on her back turned from a palm to a hand to a grip, and she went in headfirst after the truth of the Ninth Regiment, down the coal chute of her own home, into the dark.

The cellar received her badly — she landed half in the coal bin, elbow cracking against iron, breath knocked loose — but Petra's small hands found her in the blackness, steadying, urgent, and the plate came sliding down last with a clang that sounded, in that enclosed dark, like a church bell.

Above them the floor began to talk in fire's voice — creaks and ticks and long settling sighs, the house announcing its own death in the language of timber.

"Cellar door," Beatrix gasped. "The yard door — the bolt—"

"I know." Petra's voice, in the dark, steady as a surveyor's. "You showed me. First the top, then the bottom."

Of course she knew. Beatrix had drilled her in every exit of the workshop the way other women taught catechism, and the child had memorized it all with that terrible competence the orphaned carry like a second coat. Petra got the bolts — top, then bottom — and hauled the door open onto the frozen yard, and the cold came in like mercy itself.

Which was when the roof beam let go.

It happened somewhere above the survey room — a crack like a ship breaking, then a rush, then a roar as five years of Aldous Halloran's life came down into flame. Beatrix stood in the yard with the plate in her arms and watched the windows blow outward one after another, each one breathing out a fistful of sparks that climbed into the winter sky and died there, and she understood that she was watching her own past become weather.

The drafting table. The coastlines she'd inked alone at midnight for five winters. The chair with the worn left armrest where Aldous had leaned his elbow while he argued with the horizon. The drawer where his spectacles still sat folded, because she had never been able to move them. All of it going up in orange light, and the strange thing — the thing she would remember afterward with shame and clarity both — was how little of it hurt compared to what might have gone up with it. The plate was safe against her chest. The child was holding her hand. Everything else was only wood and paper, and wood and paper could be forgiven their burning.

Then a shape came out of the alley gate, staggering, and it was Elias, and he was bleeding.

* * *

### Elias

The watcher had been quick. Quicker than Elias, anyway — the iron bar caught the man's shoulder and spun him rather than felling him, and then they were grappling in the snow and the man's knife was out, and Elias took it along the outside of his forearm doing the one thing drill sergeants spend years teaching you not to do, which is to close with a blade instead of away from it.

But closing was the point. Inside the arc of the knife there was no knife, only a throat and a collar and a knee, and Elias put the man down hard and put his own weight on top and held him there face-down in the snow until the fight went out of him, which took longer than it should have, because professionals don't quit.

"Run," Elias told him, when he finally let him up. "Tell Merrick the plate is already in the city. Tell him it's in ten thousand hands. Tell him whatever you like — it won't matter by morning."

The man looked at him — a long look, the look of a tradesman assessing another tradesman's work — and then he gathered his dignity off the snow and limped away toward the river, and Elias discovered that his left sleeve was soaked through and sticking to his arm, and that the cold had been keeping the pain off the books and had just presented the account.

He went through the yard gate anyway. Because the chute door was open and the cellar door was open and neither figure he'd seen at the top of the alley was either of the two he loved, and a man can bleed later.

Beatrix met him at the yard door with the plate hugged to her chest like a child of her own and Petra's hand locked in hers, and for a moment none of them said anything at all. The workshop roared behind them. Snow fell into it through the broken roof and hissed to steam, and the steam rose white against the black smoke, and it was, Elias thought distantly, the most honest thing the building had ever done — telling the truth about its own dying.

"Your arm," Beatrix said.

"It's nothing."

"It's bleeding through your coat."

"It's *nothing*," he said again, more roughly than he meant, because the alternative was to look at the fire, and he could not yet look at the fire. "The presses are running, Trix. Three hundred copies are in the world. Whatever he burns tonight, he's burning a house. Not the truth."

She turned then, finally, and looked at what was left of her life. The roof over the survey room sagged, glowed, and folded inward with a sound like a great book closing, and the sparks went up in a column, and down the river the ice groaned — a long, deep complaint, the thaw beginning early this year, as if even the water knew the season had turned.

"Aldous bought that table the year I was born," she said. Her voice didn't break. It did something worse; it went very quiet and very level, the voice of a woman reading coordinates off a chart. "I learned letters on it. I learned to hold a pen on it. And they burned it to destroy a route order, as if a route order were ever the point." She shifted the plate in her arms. "The point was always the people who walked it."

Petra tugged her hand. The girl was looking up at the burning house with an expression Elias recognized from mirrors — the look of someone watching a prison burn and grieving it anyway, because a prison is also a shelter, and the heart does not distinguish.

"We can't stay in the yard," he said. "When the watch comes, we're three survivors with no story. Come on. The printer's cellar has room, ink, and a door I know how to guard."

Beatrix looked at him then, and in the firelight her face was stripped of the last of the careful silences he'd fallen for — and fallen for precisely because of what lay underneath them.

"The plate comes with us," she said. "And tomorrow it goes to the printer. We pull proofs from the master plate before dawn. If Merrick wants to burn the truth out of the world, he'll have to burn a printing house next, and then another, and then another — because tonight it stops living in copper and starts living in paper, and paper travels."

Down the river the ice boomed again, closer, a report like distant artillery. Somewhere across town, faintly, carried on the cold air, Elias could have sworn he heard the rhythm of the press — the steady iron heartbeat of three hundred copies becoming an argument no colonel could answer.

He took the plate from her arms because she'd been carrying it too long, and it was heavier than it looked, and lighter than it needed to be, and together the three of them walked out of the yard of the only home Beatrix Halloran had known for five years, leaving it to the flames, and did not look back — except once, all three of them, at exactly the same moment, as if the house had called their names.

Behind them the roof fell in entirely, and the fire stood up over Lantern Row like a standard raised over a lost field, and the river ice broke a little further, and a little further, getting ready to carry the spring — and whatever the spring brought — down to the sea.
