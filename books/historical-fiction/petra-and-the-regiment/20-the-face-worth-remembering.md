## 20. The Face Worth Remembering

Petra had a word for the fire before she had words for anything else.

She did not know its name in the way grown-ups knew names, tidy and spelled out and kept in boxes. She knew it the way you knew a dog that had bitten you: by its smell first, then its sound, then the color it made of everything. The fire smelled like the end of bread and the beginning of something else. It sounded like a crowd that never stopped cheering. It turned the whole sky over the river the color of the inside of a shell.

She watched it from the window of Mrs. Ansel's house above the print shop, with her nose pressed to the cold glass and Mrs. Ansel's hand heavy and warm on top of her head, and she did not cry, because crying was for things that were gone forever, and Petra had already decided that nothing in that burning building was gone forever if she did not let it be.

That was the trick she had learned when she was very small, smaller than now, back in the village whose name lived behind her teeth like a stone. You could not keep a thing safe by hiding it from yourself. Hiding was what the soldiers wanted. *Forget the face*, the note had said, the note pinned to her coat the morning Beatrix found her on the step, and Petra had spent one whole day trying, sitting on the workshop floor with her eyes squeezed shut, pushing the faces down like pushing corks under bathwater. They popped up every time. Faces always pop up. That is what faces are for.

So instead she had done the other thing. The harder thing. The thing nobody asked a child of seven to do.

She had learned them all by heart.

* * *

The fire started, she learned later, on the side where the coal chute was, which meant somebody had wanted it to look like an accident with the stove, which meant the men who set it thought other people were as stupid as they hoped everyone was. Petra knew about fires that pretended. In the village, the fire that took the granary had pretended too. It had started neat and small and sensible, right where a lamp might have fallen, and everyone had nodded sadly and said *these things happen*, and only later, much later, standing in the ash with her mouth full of the taste of it, had Petra understood that lamps do not fall in straight lines and fires that are accidents do not begin so conveniently near the thing someone wished would burn.

She had told no one this. Telling was dangerous. But knowing was hers, and nobody could take the knowing away, not even men with colonel voices.

From Mrs. Ansel's window the workshop burned like a lantern held up against the dark, and inside the lantern-shape Petra could find every room without trying. There was the drafting table where Beatrix bent for hours until her shoulders made a question mark. There was the false drawer, the one that wasn't really a drawer, where the copper plate slept wrapped in oilcloth like a secret wearing a blanket. There was the corner where Petra ground ink, black dust on her fingers, black smudge on her chin that Beatrix wiped with a thumb and a small smile that said *you are allowed to be here*. There was the shelf of Aldous Halloran's old tools, handles worn smooth by a dead man's hands, which Petra had been told were precious and which she understood, in her own private accounting, were not half so precious as the hands that still moved.

The roof fell while she watched.

It went slowly, the way big things go, almost politely, folding inward like a giant kneeling down to sleep. Sparks stood up off it in a column, hundreds of them, thousands, rising into the winter dark, and Petra thought: *those are all the marks she ever drew. Every coastline. Every road. Every little careful line of Master Aldous's hand.* The sparks rose and rose and then came apart, and the snow caught some of them, and the river caught the rest, and the river carried them off downstream glowing, little orange boats going somewhere warm.

Mrs. Ansel said something soft about God keeping them. Petra didn't say anything. God had not been mentioned in the village either, not afterward, and Petra had decided that grown-ups reached for God the way they reached for a blanket when they were scared, and that was allowed, but she herself was busy. She was memorizing the fire. Somebody ought to remember exactly how it looked when men burned down a house because of a map. If she remembered it exactly, then someday, in front of whoever needed to hear it, she could say it out loud, and saying it out loud was the only kind of justice Petra had ever seen actually work.

Then came the knock at Mrs. Ansel's door, three knocks and a pause and two more, which was Beatrix's knock, which Beatrix had taught her the first week — *if ever you must come to me in the dark, knock so* — and Petra was down the stairs before Mrs. Ansel finished gasping.

They stood in the doorway steaming like horses. Beatrix's hair was half-down and there was soot along one cheekbone like a smudged pencil line, and Elias leaned against the doorframe holding his arm close to his body the way you hold a bird you've caught and don't want to hurt worse, and his coat was scorched at the hem and he was gray-faced and smiling, actually smiling, which Petra had learned meant he was in pain or being brave or both.

Beatrix held up the plate.

Wrapped in oilcloth, streaked with coal dust, clutched against her chest with both arms, and even before she unwrapped it Petra knew it was whole, knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, because Beatrix would have gone back into the fire for it and Petra had seen her go in. The copper caught the lamplight and threw a pale coin of it onto the ceiling, and the fine engraved lines shimmered, roads and rivers and one route that official maps said no regiment ever marched, and Petra put her sooty fingers on it, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

"It came through," Beatrix said. Her voice cracked in the middle like ice underfoot. "We came through."

Petra looked at Elias's arm. Elias looked at his arm too, as though noticing it for the first time. "It's mostly attached," he said.

Petra got the clean rags. She had been practicing bandaging on Mrs. Ansel's cat, who disapproved, so she was quick now, and while she wound the linen around the long shallow burn along his forearm she studied his face the way she studied everything, because faces were her work now, her trade, her inheritance. He let her look. That was new. Grown-ups usually didn't let children look at them properly. But Elias had stopped flinching from being seen sometime in the last weeks — Petra had watched it happen, the way you watch frost leave a window — and now he sat still under her eyes and even turned his hand so she could reach the underside of the wrist, which was trust, plain and simple, trust handed over like something breakable.

"You saved it," she said.

Her voice surprised her sometimes. Since the night she'd spoken again — the night she'd said the village's name out loud into Beatrix's shoulder, both of them crying, the stone finally out from behind her teeth — the words came easier, but each one still felt slightly borrowed, slightly miraculous, like coins found in an old coat.

Elias shook his head. "I held a door. Your Beatrix went through a burning building." He glanced sideways at Beatrix, who was pretending to inspect the oilcloth with great attention. "Your Beatrix," he repeated, and something passed between him and Beatrix that Petra didn't have a word for yet, but she filed it away with all the others, in the growing room inside her where she kept things that mattered.

* * *

They stayed three days at Mrs. Ansel's, sleeping on the floor of the back room among bolts of paper, and Petra lay awake listening to the sounds of the house the way other children listened to lullabies.

She heard Beatrix and Elias talking low in the next room, their voices a river sound, going on and on. She heard Mrs. Ansel's press thump below, slow and patient, the heartbeat of the building, printing and printing. She heard the town outside: boots, more boots than before, soldiers' boots, and once, past midnight, running feet and a shout cut short, and Elias's chair scrape softly as he sat up listening, and then, after a long time, lying back down.

And in the mornings Petra did her work.

Her work was remembering. She sat by the window with Mrs. Ansel's scrap paper and a nub of charcoal and she drew the faces, all of them, the ones from the note and the ones from before the note, and she had made a rule for herself that she explained to no one because no one needed explaining to: one face every morning, drawn as exact as she could make it, and then folded small and hidden inside the lining of her coat, where the dispatch case half had been sewn, where important things lived.

There was Sergeant Bray, who used to let her sit on his knee and wore a beard with grey in it like frost on a fence rail. There was Corporal Ives, who whistled badly. There was the tall lieutenant whose face she had almost lost, and drawing him was like carrying water a long way in her cupped hands, and some days she spilled part of him — the shape of his nose went soft, or she couldn't fix whether his eyebrows met — and those days she cried a little, quietly, and then drew him again anyway, because forgetting a piece was not the same as losing the whole, and the whole was worth spilling for.

The soldiers' faces she drew too. Not all of them — she hadn't known their names — but the ones she'd seen closest. The one who had knelt down to her level in the village street and spoken gently, which was somehow worse than shouting, and whose voice she could still produce inside her head whenever she wished, which was never, except when she made herself practice it, because Beatrix had said, very carefully, sitting beside her with the atlas proofs spread out between them: *One day, Petra, people will ask you what happened, and you will need to tell them truly, and telling truly means knowing truly. You don't owe anyone the memory. But it's yours. Whatever else they took, it's yours.*

Yours. Nobody had ever told Petra that a terrible thing could be owned. She had thought terrible things owned *you* — that was how it had felt, the village living inside her chest, walking her around at night, making her mute. But Beatrix turned it over like a plate on a table and showed her the other side: *it's yours,* meaning Petra held it, not it held Petra. Meaning she could take it out and look at it when she chose and put it away when she chose. Meaning the colonel's voice in her head answered to her now, not the other way round.

So on the fourth morning, she drew Beatrix.

This was the hardest one, and she had saved it for last on purpose, though she couldn't have said why until the charcoal was moving. Beatrix's face was difficult because it changed. In the early weeks it had been closed like a drawer — the polite face, the workshop face, the face that said *I am not here and neither are you*. But faces that shut hard also open hard, Petra had discovered. When Beatrix forgot herself over the engraving, her whole face unlocked at once: the brow lifting, the mouth going soft and slightly crooked with concentration, the eyes gone bright and far away like someone reading a letter from a beloved person a long distance off. And lately there was a third face, a new one, the one that arrived when Elias was in the room, and Petra had watched that one being born and considered it the best thing she had ever witnessed, better than the ice breaking, better than the first proof coming off the press with the true route printed on it in fresh black ink.

She drew the third face. The open one. She gave Beatrix ink-smudged fingers and the crooked mouth and the eyes that had decided something, and when she was done she looked at it a long time and then folded it small and put it in her coat lining with the others, and her chest felt strange and light, like a room after a window has been opened.

Because here was the thing Petra understood now, seven years old and older than most soldiers: the note had told her to forget the face, any face, every face, because faces were evidence and witnesses were loose ends and loose ends got tied off. The village had been erased from the maps. The Ninth had been erased from the record. The men who did it believed erasing worked, believed the world stayed blank wherever they scrubbed it.

But Petra was a map now too.

She was the kind no army could burn. Every line of her had been traced by what she'd seen, and she had chosen — sitting in the ashes of her first life, choosing again every morning since — to keep the survey honest. Sergeant Bray's frost-grey beard. Corporal Ives's bad whistle. The gentle kneeling soldier and the colonel's voice, smooth as poured wax. And Beatrix's face, ink-stained, firelit, unhidden. All of it engraved deeper than copper, in a material that did not melt.

* * *

On the fifth morning, Mrs. Ansel called her down early, and the print shop smelled of ink and candle smoke, and the whole floor was covered — *covered* — with atlases.

Hundreds of them, stacked in flat towers like the town had been built indoors overnight. Fresh from the press, the ink still sharp enough to sting the nose, and on the cover, in gold-look lettering that was really just brass powder and patience: the atlas, Aldous Halloran's name and beneath it now another name in smaller letters that made Petra grin until her cheeks hurt, because she knew what signing your own name meant; she was learning letters herself, laboriously, one at a time, and she knew a signature was a person saying *I was here, this is mine, count me*.

Elias stood by the door in a coat that wasn't his, talking fast and low with a man Petra didn't know, and whatever they said, it ended with the man nodding twice and leaving into the blue morning, and Elias turning to Beatrix with a look that Petra read easily, having studied him for weeks: the look of a man who has just done something that cannot be undone, and is glad.

"They're going out today?" Beatrix asked. Her hand rested flat on the tallest stack, the way you rest a hand on a horse you love.

"Every road south. Every garrison town. By week's end—" Elias's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, "—there will be ten thousand copies of the truth sitting on desks belonging to men who would rather have died than read it."

"Ten thousand," Petra said, tasting the number.

"And one more witness," Beatrix added, and looked down at her.

Petra stood up straight. She knew what was being asked without it being asked. Someday soon — maybe soon — there would be a room full of grown-up people with serious faces, and they would want the village named, and the route described, and the colonel's voice identified, and she would have to stand in front of them and open the coat lining of herself and unfold every face, one by one, in order, and tell it all truly.

She wasn't afraid. Or rather — she was afraid, properly, the way you're afraid of deep water, but she had learned that fear and doing-the-thing could occupy the same body at the same time, and neither one cancelled the other, and you simply walked forward carrying both.

"I'm ready," Petra said.

Outside, far off toward the river, there came a sound she knew from last spring and every spring before the village: the long groan and crack of the ice breaking up, the river shrugging off winter all at once, loud as artillery and clean as a bell. Everyone in the room went quiet and listened. Mrs. Ansel crossed herself. Elias went to the window.

And down by the water, Petra saw, a knot of men had gathered at the edge of the broken ice, and they were not looking at the river. They were looking at something in it.
