## 22. The Atlas Under New Hands

The new workshop smelled of pine resin and river water, and Beatrix Halloran woke before dawn on the first morning of spring to the sound of the ice breaking.

It was not the groaning, threatening sound of the old river at Threnlow, the sound that had haunted the last weeks of the old workshop — the sound of something vast and unwilling being torn apart. This was cleaner. The river below the new town of Marrow Bend ran wider, slower, and when its ice went, it went all at once, a long crack like a struck bell followed by a rushing that did not stop. She lay in the narrow bed above the shop with her eyes open and listened to winter end, and for the first time in five years she did not think of what the thaw would bring. Boats. Soldiers. Men with questions. She thought only: *the river is open. Anything can leave now. Anything can arrive.*

She rose and dressed in the dark, out of habit, and came down the stairs with a candle she did not need, because the windows had gone grey-blue with early light and the river was running bright beneath them.

The workshop was not yet a workshop. It was a room with a north-facing window, a hearth that drew well, and a floor Elias had scrubbed on his knees for two days until the boards showed their pale grain. The press stood in the corner under sailcloth, its iron throat still scarred from the fire at Threnlow — the heat had warped one leg and blistered the screw housing, and Elias had spent the winter filing it true, an inch at a time, the way a man mends something he nearly let die. The drafting table sat by the window where the light was honest. And on the table, under a clean linen cloth, lay the atlas.

Not the master atlas. That one had burned with the roof, with the false drawer, with the decorative border that had hidden the true route of the Ninth Regiment for five years. What lay under the cloth was its child: the corrected plate, pulled from the coal chute with scorched gloves while the ceiling came down, and the two hundred and forty-one printed copies that had gone out on the spring boats before the ice closed behind them, into garrison towns and newspaper offices and the hands of Parliament men who owed Merrick nothing and were delighted to be handed a knife.

And now, the final copy. The presentation copy. The one she had promised herself she would not rush.

Beatrix lifted the cloth and looked at it the way she had once looked at blank paper — with the same fear, though it was a different fear now, or perhaps the same one wearing better clothes. The atlas lay open at the last leaf, where the colophon waited, and the colophon was empty. One line of space. She had left it blank through every proof, through every correction, through the long winter of waiting for the inquiry's findings to be read aloud in a hall where Merrick's name was struck from the rolls of the honored. She had told herself she was waiting for the record to be made whole.

She had known, some part of her, that she was waiting for the ice to break.

* * *

Petra came down at half past six, as she always did, barefoot on the stairs until the third step, where she remembered her boots. She was eight now, or nearly — they had settled on a birthday in the autumn, chosen together, which Beatrix considered one of the more honest fictions of her life. Her hair was cut short and uneven because she had cut it herself with the paper shears and refused to apologize for it. She wore the grey wool dress Beatrix had made from a pattern two sizes too large, on the theory that children grow, and she came into the workshop the way she came into every room now: fully, without flinching at corners.

She stopped when she saw the atlas uncovered. She knew what it meant. She had helped grind the ink for the proofs; she had learned to pull a clean sheet without smearing; she had sat under the table at Threnlow with her hands over her ears while soldiers' boots crossed the floor above, and she had come out afterward and asked — in her new voice, the voice she had found in the night at the end of that winter, small and cracked and entirely her own — *did it print?*

"It prints today," Beatrix said. "The last of it. Then it's finished."

Petra came and stood at the table and looked at the open colophon for a long time. She was learning to read — slowly, in the stubborn way of a child who had decided that letters had once been used against her and would now be made to work for her instead. She sounded out the title in a whisper. Then she looked up.

"Both names?" she asked.

"Both names," Beatrix said.

Petra nodded, satisfied, and went to the ink stone and began to grind, because that was what mornings were for. The sound of the mull against the stone filled the room, a small circular industry, and Beatrix watched her and thought about the note that had come pinned to a child's coat on a frozen dawn: *forget the face.* Whoever had written it — some soldier with a conscience, some deserter who could not finish the work — had meant it as a mercy or a threat, and had failed at both. The face had not been forgotten. The face had been remembered so thoroughly that it now hung in three garrison towns in engraved form, and the men who had marched the Ninth into the valley at Kellin's Ford would be answering for it in a courtroom in the capital, or would not, but either way the map told the truth, and the map would outlast them all.

*Forget the face.* She had spent five years forgetting a different face — Aldous's — and the forgetting had been the loneliest discipline of her life. She did not intend to practice it again.

* * *

Elias came in from the river path at seven, stamping the mud off his boots, carrying a crate of type he had traded a month of labor to a Marrow Bend printer to get. He was thinner than he had been at Threnlow, harder in the shoulders, and he still moved like a soldier in a room full of civilians — checking sightlines, counting exits — but the habit was gentler now, more like a man who had learned the price of a locked door and simply preferred to know where the doors were. He had testified in the capital in February. He had stood in the witness box in his one good coat, without his commission, because he no longer had one, and read the route order aloud, and named Colonel Merrick, and answered four hours of questions designed to make him a liar or a madman, and he had done it in the flat, precise voice of a surveyor reading coordinates, and Beatrix had sat in the public gallery and understood that she was watching a man dismantle himself for the record and reassemble as someone else.

He set the crate down. "Ice went at the bend around five," he said. "Whole sheet, clean break. The ferryman says boats from the capital by Thursday."

"Then we finish today."

"Then we finish today." He looked at the colophon, then at her. "Ready?"

"No," Beatrix said. "But I've been ready for five years, and it hasn't helped."

He almost smiled. He was better at it now, smiling — it no longer looked like a maneuver. He went to the press and threw back the sailcloth and began his morning inspection of the screw, and Petra looked up from her ink and said, "Elias. Watch." And she pulled a test sheet from the stack she'd been practicing on and held it out: her own name, in letters she had cut herself, uneven as a fence in wind. *PETRA.* Below it, smaller, laboriously: *HALLORAN.*

Elias took the sheet and studied it with the gravity of a man reviewing a treaty. "Spelled right," he said. "The E is drunk."

"The E is *learning,*" Petra said, and snatched it back, and Beatrix turned to the window so they would not see her face do what it did.

* * *

She inked the plate at midmorning. The corrected route ran beneath the decorative border as it always had — Aldous's trick, the truth hidden in the ornament — but the new plate did not hide it. She had cut the border away entirely, all of it, the flourishes and the false coastlines and the pretty lies that had made the atlas sellable and safe. What remained was the map as it should have been from the beginning: the Ninth's true line of march from Threnlow to Kellin's Ford, every ford and ridge and burned village, the valley where three hundred and six men had been sent by an order that did not exist in the official record, and the annotations in Aldous's hand, copied faithfully, that proved the order had existed, and when, and whose seal had authorized it.

She had cut his annotations herself, letter by letter, working by candlelight in the weeks after the fire, her hands still smelling of smoke. It had been the closest thing to prayer she knew.

Now she pulled the final sheet. Elias worked the press and Petra turned the crank, standing on a stool to reach it, her whole body in the effort, and the screw came down and the impression bit and they lifted the sheet together into the light.

It was clean. Every line true. The river ran where the river ran; the regiment marched where the regiment had marched; the truth sat on the paper as plain as a name.

Beatrix stood over it a long time. Then she took up the pen.

The colophon. She had rehearsed this line for months, in the bath, in the dark, walking the river path. She had drafted it seventeen times and burned every draft, because drafts were a way of lying slowly, and she was done with that. She dipped the pen and wrote, in her best lettering, the hand Aldous had taught her and that was now, unmistakably, her own:

*Surveyed and drawn by Aldous Halloran, Master of the Craft, who died for this truth. Completed and engraved by Beatrix Halloran, his apprentice, who lived to set it down. Marrow Bend, in the year the ice broke clean.*

She signed beneath it. Both names. *A. Halloran. B. Halloran.*

The pen made a small sound on the paper, like a footstep in an empty house that turns out to be someone coming home.

Petra, watching over her elbow, sounded it out. "B. Halloran," she said. "That's you. You're a Halloran."

"I was his apprentice," Beatrix said. "Then I was his heir. It seemed dishonest to stop there."

Petra considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to all questions of justice. "It's not dishonest," she ruled. "It's true. You lived with him. You finished his work. That's a family."

Beatrix set down the pen. She had prepared, over the winter, a number of careful things to say about names and inheritance and the ethics of the craft, and Petra had just said all of them in nine words, and Beatrix found she had nothing to add. She put her arm around the girl's shoulders, and Petra allowed it — allowed it and leaned in, which was the whole of their history in a single gesture: a child who had been taught to forget faces, choosing to be held by one.

* * *

They hung the proof in the window, because Beatrix decided the days of hiding were over, and by noon half of Marrow Bend had found a reason to walk past the new workshop and slow down. The ferryman stopped. The printer whose type Elias had earned stopped and read the colophon through the glass with his lips moving. A woman with a market basket stood a long while at the map of the march, and then at the little inset map of Kellin's Ford, and then she took off her glove and touched the glass, very lightly, where the valley was, and Beatrix understood that she had lost someone there, and that the map had just given her back a piece of the truth she had been denied, and that this — this, and not the inquiry, not the newspapers, not the fall of Merrick — was what the work had been for.

By afternoon a boy had been sent from the inn with a note: a bookseller in the capital wished to correspond regarding an edition. A second note came before dusk, from a surveyor's office, asking whether the workshop took apprentices.

Beatrix read them at the table and did not answer either one, because there was time now. That was the discovery of the spring, the one that kept surprising her: there was time. The ice was out. The boats ran. The work would come to them, or they would go to it, and either way the door could stay unlocked.

Elias mended the press through the afternoon, filing the last burr from the screw, and when he finished he did not cover it with sailcloth again. He oiled it and left it open to the light, iron and wood and scar, standing in the corner like a thing that had earned its place in the room.

* * *

In the evening they ate at the drafting table, which was not proper, which Aldous would have forbidden, which was why Beatrix did it. Bread and river fish and the last of the winter apples, and Petra drawing at the far end — she drew constantly now, and her drawings had changed. They had been black and crowded once, full of soldiers and falling snow. Now they were rivers and boats and a house with a crooked chimney, and once, pinned above her bed where Beatrix had found it and said nothing, three figures on a river path with their backs turned, walking away from a burning building, none of them looking back.

After supper Petra fell asleep in the chair by the hearth, mid-drawing, the pen still in her fist, and Elias carried her up without waking her, and came back down and stood in the middle of the workshop with his coat still on, looking at the press and the plate and the woman at the table, in the way of a man taking inventory of a life he had not expected to be issued.

"The boats come Thursday," he said.

"I know."

"Someone from the capital will come with them. A bookseller, a surveyor, a newspaper man. Someone will want the story. Someone will want *you* to tell the story."

"I know."

"They'll ask about Threnlow. About the fire. About me." He said it evenly, but Beatrix heard the old caution underneath, the deserter's reflex, the man who had spent a career being a name that could be erased. "You could tell it without me. It would be cleaner."

Beatrix looked at him across the table where they had once circled each other with lies, across the table where she had finally laid the hidden plate open between them like a card turned face up. She got up, went to the shelf, and took down the atlas — the finished one, the true one, the one with two names in the colophon — and put it in his hands.

"Read it," she said.

"I've read it."

"Read the colophon again. Slowly. You're a surveyor; you know what an annotation means. Everything in this book is there because it *happened.* I didn't spend five years learning to tell the truth so I could stop one river short of it." She touched the page where his name would need to go — not in the colophon yet; that was a decision for another day, and she was done making decisions for other people's lives — but somewhere. In the record. In the account. "You held the door in a cellar while three hundred copies ran. You testified. You gave them Merrick's schedule when giving them my name would have been easier and safer and what you were ordered to do. That's not a thing I tell without you. That's a thing I tell *because* of you."

Elias looked down at the atlas in his hands for a long moment. When he raised his head, he was not smiling, but his face had done the thing it did instead — the loosening, the laying down of arms.

"Then Thursday," he said, "when the boats come, we'll be at the window."

"Both names," Beatrix said.

Outside, the river ran loud and open in the dark, carrying the last of the ice down toward the sea, and in the workshop above it the press stood uncovered in the lamplight, and the atlas lay finished on the table, and for the first time in as long as any of them could remember, there was nothing in the house that needed hiding.
