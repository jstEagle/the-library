## 1. The Weight of Paper

The scale was a jeweler's scale, borrowed from a watchmaker who no longer asked questions because asking had become expensive in this city. Mirabel Crane set the brass pans level on the windowsill of her rented room, where the light came in clean off the harbor, and began to weigh her country's secrets.

The first packet went into the left pan: four sheets, folded twice, sealed with the foreign ministry's black wax. Two ounces and a half. She had timed its burning in the grate the night before, using a duplicate prepared for exactly that purpose — a wasteful habit, but habits were what kept you alive when luck ran out. Eleven minutes to ash if the fire was hot, twenty if she had to coax it. She wrote *2½ oz / 11 min* in pencil on the inventory card and moved to the second packet.

This was how she worked, and how she had worked for three years now, since the ministry discovered that a woman who looked like a clerk's widow could carry through a checkpoint what a courier in uniform could not. Every document weighed. Every document burned in practice before it was carried in earnest. A dispatch was not paper; it was a small, dense thing with a half-life, dangerous to her from the moment it touched her hands until the moment it passed out of them or into flame.

*Carry little,* her first handler had told her, a gray man named Sorel who was probably dead by now. *Trust less.* He had said it the way a mason says *measure twice*, and she had built her whole profession on the load-bearing truth of it. Everything else — the cover stories, the sewn linings, the memorized ciphers — was ornament. The creed was weight and trust, and she kept both as lean as she could.

Outside, the city made its siege sounds. Distant guns, unhurried, like a man knocking on a door he knows will eventually be opened. The blockade fleet sat beyond the mole in a gray line, patient as arithmetic. Six weeks since a supply ship had run in. Three weeks since bread had been anything but a rumor. In the streets below her window, a fish cart rattled past and nobody chased it, because there were no fish.

Mirabel weighed the second packet. One ounce, ten minutes. Third: three ounces, fourteen minutes, and a cipher appendix she would have to memorize and destroy separately. She worked down the row of them, twelve packets in all, each wrapped in oilcloth against salt water, each labeled only by number, and felt the particular calm that came over her when the work was orderly. This was the gift the war had given her, if it could be called a gift: a mind that went quiet when things got bad. Other people's hands shook. Hers steadied. She had never decided whether that was virtue or damage, and at thirty-one she suspected she never would.

She was halfway through the seventh packet when the knock came.

Two knocks, pause, one. Aubert's pattern — no. Not Aubert. The consul was still in Port Mahon, four hundred miles east along a coast the navy did not control. This was the ministry's pattern, the one they'd assigned her before she left Paris, and the fact that she had to stop and think about which was which told her something about how tired she was.

"Enter."

A boy of perhaps sixteen, in the blue coat of the ministry's messenger service, cheeks wind-burned from the stairs. He held out a single sealed letter and a receipt book. She signed, he vanished, and she stood holding the letter for a moment before weighing it, because she already knew what it was by its thickness and its wax.

Final orders. They always came in this envelope — heavy stock, the good wax, the seal pressed deep enough that forgery would show. She put it on the scale anyway. Four ounces. She didn't know the burn-time yet. She wouldn't burn this one until she'd read it, and she wouldn't read it until she'd earned the right by finishing the inventory.

Discipline was mostly the art of making yourself do things in the correct order when every nerve wanted the opposite order.

She finished the count. Twelve packets, total weight just under two pounds. Total burn-time, if it ever came to that, a little over two hours — too long, and so the real plan, as always, was the harbor. Weighted cloth, black water. Paper sank faster than it burned. She had never yet had to use either method, and she intended to keep it that way, but intention was not a plan and she knew the difference better than most.

Then she sat on the edge of the bed, broke the ministerial seal, and read her final orders for Port Mahon.

They were brief, as final orders are. She was to proceed without delay aboard whatever vessel could be found — the last sailing of the season, the letter said, which meant the last chance before the blockade tightened into winter and nothing moved at all. She was to present herself to Consul Aubert under her established cover. She was to deliver the twelve packets into his custody and obtain his written acknowledgment, witnessed, dated. And then — this paragraph in the minister's own hand rather than a clerk's, which meant it mattered — *you will remain at your post indefinitely. The situation in the Balearics is fluid. Your further instructions will follow.*

*Indefinitely.* Mirabel read the word twice. It was a word ministries used when they meant *until we forget you exist*. Four hundred miles from everything, on an island garrisoned by men who hadn't been paid since spring, holding dispatches whose contents she was not permitted to know, waiting for instructions that might come by way of a fishing boat or might come never.

She should have felt dread. What she felt instead, and what she had learned to recognize as the truest signal she possessed, was a small cold relief. Orders were simpler than uncertainty. A post was a shape you could pour yourself into. For three years she had been poured into shapes — Antwerp, Genoa, Trieste, each one a city where she was trusted by everyone and known by no one — and the pouring hurt less each time, which was either adaptation or erosion, depending on how honest she was willing to be, and honesty was a luxury item these days, like bread.

She copied the orders' essentials into her head — she kept no written record of instructions, ever — and fed the original to the little stove. Four ounces took thirteen minutes. She watched all thirteen, turning the pages with tongs, because watching was part of the discipline. Unwatched paper had a way of leaving corners behind.

* * *

The room she rented belonged to Madame Ferrand, a widow who took in lodgers and asked no questions because her son was at the front and her daughter had gone to Marseille and silence was the only currency that couldn't be requisitioned. Mirabel paid a month ahead, always, and always overpaid slightly, and in exchange Madame Ferrand brought her coffee that was mostly chicory and occasionally said things like *the guns were quieter last night, don't you think* and waited for an answer that never came.

Tonight the coffee came with an extra cup, unasked.

"You're leaving," Madame Ferrand said. Not a question. Widows of sieges developed a nose for departures; they smelled packing straw the way sailors smelled weather.

"In a day or two."

"East?"

"East."

Madame Ferrand nodded, as though east were a place you could point to on a map of grief, and refilled both cups. "My Etienne sailed out of Mahon once. Before the war. He said the harbor there is like a hand." She made a loose fist, thumb crooked inward. "All fingers and shelter. He loved it."

"He may see it again," Mirabel said, which was the kind of sentence people offered each other in sieges, worthless and necessary, like the coins governments printed on tin.

After the widow left, Mirabel sat by the window with the inventory card and did the thing she did every night, the thing that was not on any list of duties and that she would have denied under oath: she thought about Tomas.

Officially, Lieutenant Tomas Crane of the merchant marine had been lost with his ship, the *Cormorant*, eleven months ago, somewhere in the approaches to Cagliari. A survivor's affidavit, a board of inquiry, a letter of condolence written in a secretary's beautiful impersonal hand. *Drowned in the service of the Republic.* She had received the news in Trieste, in another rented room, and had done exactly what she was doing now: finished her inventory, weighed her packets, watched her papers burn on schedule. Grief, like everything else, was a matter of load management. You carried it or you went down, and she had cargo enough.

But eleven months was long enough for the official version to develop cracks, and she had spent enough nights in enough safe houses to hear them. A fisherman's boat had reported wreckage forty miles from where the *Cormorant* went down, and wreckage that included no bodies. The board of inquiry had closed in nine days, which was fast even for wartime. And once — only once, in a dispatch pouch that wasn't hers, a misdirection she'd corrected within the hour but not before reading a line meant for another desk — she had seen the phrase *the Crane matter is not concluded*.

Not concluded. You did not write that about drowned men. You wrote it about men whose deaths were convenient, or whose lives were inconvenient, and either way it meant someone, somewhere, was still thinking about her brother with a pen in hand.

She had reported the misdirected pouch. She had filed the discrepancy about the wreckage in the back of her mind, unburned, unweighed, the one document in her keeping that existed nowhere but in her. It weighed nothing on the jeweler's scale. On the other scale, the one she kept in the dark behind her ribs, it weighed more every month.

*Tomas.* Even the name she rationed. Once a night, after the inventory, before sleep. A name spoken aloud in a besieged city could get a person talked about, and being talked about was how couriers died.

He had taught her the sea. That was the fact she returned to, the fixed star. Their father had been a harbor pilot with no patience for daughters on the water, and Tomas, fifteen and already half in love with rigging, had simply taken her out anyway, a stolen skiff and a stolen afternoon, and shown her how to read wind off the water before it reached her face. *You feel it here first,* he'd said, touching the back of her hand, *then here* — the cheek — *and by the time it hits the sail it's old news. Always be where the wind hasn't arrived yet.* She had been eleven. She had understood him to be talking about sailing for perhaps a decade before she understood he'd been talking about everything.

Now she was the one out ahead of the wind, carrying two pounds of her country's intentions toward an island fortress, and Tomas was old news, dead news, a line in a ledger. Except for the wreckage that held no bodies. Except for *not concluded*.

She blew out the lamp and let the city's dark come in around her. The guns had stopped for the night, or paused; blockades breathed. Somewhere below, Madame Ferrand's shutters clacked closed, and a dog barked itself out, and the harbor lapped at stone the way it had lapped at stone since before anyone's grief.

Tomorrow she would find a captain. The last sailing of the season — some leaky brig with a purser who counted trunks and a master willing to run the gray line of the blockade for triple freight, because courage at sea, unlike courage on land, still had a market price. She would sew what needed sewing into her coat lining. She would memorize what needed memorizing and burn the key. She would stand on a deck in the rain and be nobody, a clerk's widow going east to settle an estate, and the packets would ride in their oilcloth like a heart she'd agreed to carry for strangers.

Carry little. Trust less. She had lived by it so long it had worn smooth as a pebble, and she had never once examined what was underneath.

She slept, and dreamed of a skiff, and a hand on the back of her hand, and wind that hadn't arrived anywhere yet.

* * *

In the morning there was a message waiting downstairs, and it was wrong in three ways before she'd finished reading it.

It came on plain paper, unsigned, which was normal. It named a tavern near the fish market and an hour after dark, which was normal. But it used the old recognition phrase — *the wind is fair for the eastern islands* — which had been retired two months ago in favor of a new one she'd been given in Trieste, and it was signed with a mark instead of a name, and the mark was a small neat anchor drawn with a single unbroken stroke.

Tomas had drawn anchors that way when they were children. One stroke, no lifting the pen. She had teased him about it for years. *You'll waste ink, Tom. Lift the pen.*

No living soul knew that. No living soul except the brother the Republic had buried without a body, eleven months and four hundred miles away, writing to her now across enemy water as if death were a formality he'd neglected to file.

Mirabel stood in the doorway with the paper in her hand while the fish cart rattled past below and the morning went on being ordinary, and for the first time in three years her hands were not steady.

She read it again. Then she did the only thing the creed allowed: she weighed it. Half an ounce. Burn-time, four minutes.

She struck the match, and watched.
