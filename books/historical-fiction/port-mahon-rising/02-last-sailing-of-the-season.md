## 2. Last Sailing of the Season

The brig was called the *Meridian*, which Mirabel took as a small joke against her: a ship named for noon, sailing into winter. She boarded at dusk from a rowing boat that stank of fish oil, and the first thing she noticed was the smell below decks — bilge and tar and old cheese — and the second thing she noticed was the list. Three degrees to port, maybe four. A ship that leaned before it left harbor was a ship confessing something.

"Leaky," said the boatman, cheerfully, in broken French. "But she leaks slow."

Everything leaks slow, Mirabel thought. That is how they get you. You grow accustomed to the water rising an inch an hour, and by the time you notice your knees are wet, the pumps are manned by men who have already given up.

She climbed the side with her single trunk hauled on a rope behind her, one trunk where most passengers made do with three, and stood a moment on the deck to let the wind take her measure. It did. Cold off the northern water, smelling of salt and coal smoke and the particular iron tang of a coast that had been at war too long to remember peace except as rumor. Behind her the city burned its lamps one street at a time, indifferent. Ahead lay open gray water and eleven days to Port Mahon, if the weather held, which the weather would not.

"Papers," said a voice at her elbow.

The purser was a narrow man with a narrow face, the kind of face that had been squeezed by arithmetic for so long it had taken the shape of it. He wore a coat with brass buttons polished to a shine that suggested vanity in nothing else, and he held out his hand the way a toll gate holds out its arm.

Mirabel gave him her passport. It was good work — she had paid for good work, sewn into the lining of her own diligence — issued at Marseille in the name of Madame Lefèvre, widow, born Lyon, traveling to Minorca for reasons of family property. The seal was convincing under lamplight. She knew because she had tested it herself, holding it over a candle until the wax softened and re-formed, learning its flaws the way you learn the flaws of a lock you intend to trust your life to.

The purser read it longer than necessary. Long enough to be rude. His eyes came up once, cataloguing her — hat, gloves, the set of her mouth — and went back down.

"Madame Lefèvre travels light," he said.

"Grief travels light," Mirabel said. "One learns what can be replaced."

It was the correct answer. She had prepared six answers and this was the third-best; she used it anyway, because the best answers are sometimes too good, and a purser who has been bribed before smells perfection like blood in water. He grunted, stamped nothing, handed the passport back, and called for two sailors to swing her trunk aboard.

She watched them carry it down. She counted the steps. Eleven to the hatch, nine below, and then the sound of it settling into the hold, among crates of salt pork and casks of wine that were not all wine, because no ship leaving this coast carried only what its manifest said. The *Meridian* was a smuggler wearing a merchant's coat, and Mirabel had chosen her deliberately. Smugglers asked fewer questions than patriots. Patriots believed things, and believing men were dangerous; a man who believed the Republic was holy would hand her to the authorities as an act of worship. A smuggler would only rob her, and robbery she could survive.

Below decks she found her berth — a shelf with a curtain, in a cabin she shared with five other souls, three of whom were already snoring — and sat with her hands folded while the ship got under way. She felt the anchor come up through the hull like a heartbeat changing rhythm. She felt the deck tilt as the sails filled. And she felt, arriving quietly the way dread always arrived, the understanding that there was no going back now, that the harbor mouth was behind her, that the next land she touched would be the land where her brother had died or had not died, depending on which lie she chose to believe.

*Half an ounce,* she had told herself that morning, striking the match. *Burn-time, four minutes.*

She had watched the letter curl and blacken and go to ash in the saucer, and she had memorized every word of it first — the way you memorize a face across a crowded room knowing you will never be introduced. *The old windmill above the Cala. Come alone. Bring nothing you cannot eat.* And the signature, which was the impossible part, the part that made the fire necessary: Tomas's hand, unmistakable, the capital T with its little flourish like a flag planted on a hill. Dead men wrote neatly. Dead men did not write at all.

She had burned it because a document that could hang you must never be found, and kept it because a document that could hang you must never be forgotten. Both things were true. The creed accommodated both. The creed, Mirabel had learned, could accommodate anything except comfort.

The ship rolled. Somewhere above, canvas cracked and was hauled taut, and the timbers groaned the long complaint of a hull taking its first real water.

* * *

By the second day the storm had found them.

It did not arrive; it accumulated, the way debts accumulate, a gray line on the horizon that became a gray wall that became the whole world. Captain Roque — a Basque who communicated chiefly through his eyebrows — ordered topsails reefed at noon and the hatches battened at two, and by four the *Meridian* was climbing hills of water and sliding down their far sides with a sound like a woman being dragged downstairs.

Mirabel stayed below. This was discipline, not cowardice: on deck she was cargo that could be washed away, and below she was a passenger with papers, and papers required a living owner. She wedged herself into her berth with a rolled cloak braced at her back, and she did what she had trained herself to do in bad hours, which was work.

Her dispatches rode in the trunk below, but their keys rode with her, because a cipher without its key was only paper, and a key without its paper was only memory, and memory could not be confiscated. She had three keys committed: the consular code for Aubert's correspondence, the route code for the relay stations, and the old field cipher — Crane's own, designed by a cousin nobody talked about, a shifting-letter system elegant and slightly mad. Each key lived in her head as a grid of numbers, forty characters square, and she maintained them the way a musician maintains scales, running them silently against invented plaintext while the storm tried to shake them loose.

*Fear sharpens,* her instructor had said, years ago, a dry woman with ink-stained fingers. *Or it shatters. The difference is practice. Fear is a blade either way; make sure you are holding the handle.*

So she ran the grids. Consular code, forward and back. Route code, forward and back. Field cipher, which she saved for last because it was hardest and because working hardest last gave her something to reach for when the ship fell into a trough and her stomach went weightless and some animal part of her brain screamed that the world was ending.

The world did not end. The trough ended. The ship rose, groaning, and the animal brain subsided, and Mirabel ran the field cipher again from the top, and this time her hands were steady.

That was the trick of it, she thought, wedged in the dark with the storm hammering overhead. Not courage. Courage was a word for people watching from shore. It was attention. You pointed your mind at the work like a lamp, and the fear could rage around the beam all it liked, so long as the beam held.

Above her, feet thundered across the deck. A voice shouted something about the mizzen. Something about the pumps. She noted *pumps* the way she noted everything now — filed, weighed, assessed — and decided that if the pumping started in earnest she would need to move the trunk. Water found paper the way gossip found a consul: inevitably, and ruinously.

She slept, or something near sleep, in pieces, and dreamed of a windmill turning slowly on a hill above blue water, and woke with her heart going hard, and could not say why.

* * *

The purser counted her trunk twice.

She learned of it on the fourth day, from the cabin boy, a Majorcan child of perhaps ten who had attached himself to Madame Lefèvre with the shameless pragmatism of the very young. He brought her watered wine and gossip in equal measure. In exchange for a coin and a kindness — she had shown him how to mend a tear in his shirt with a stitch his mother presumably knew but he had forgotten — he told her that Señor Ferran had gone into the hold yesterday evening with a lantern, and again this morning, and had looked at the widow's trunk both times, and had measured it with his arms the second time, like a man hugging a barrel he suspected of being full of something other than clothes.

"Twice," Mirabel repeated pleasantly.

"He counts everything twice," the boy said, with the loyalty of a well-tipped informant. "He counted the chickens twice in Alicante. But he doesn't usually count the passengers' things."

Of course he didn't. Passengers' things were dull. Unless something had made them interesting, and Mirabel spent the afternoon identifying what. She reviewed her own behavior like an auditor reviewing accounts. The boarding: one trunk, unusual but defensible. The passport: sound. Her French: fluent, accented toward Lyon, correct. Her answers aboard: consistent. Widow, property, family. Nothing to explain a second count.

Which meant the interest came from outside her. Someone had talked. A clerk at the booking office, perhaps, paid by someone else to note who bought passage east in the last sailing of the season. Or Ferran himself was more than a purser — a watcher placed aboard precisely for ships bound to a garrison town whose loyalty everyone was suddenly measuring. The war had entered its accounting phase; Mirabel could feel it in the way men looked at manifests now, the way seals were checked twice, the way the word *neutrality* had begun to sound less like a promise and more like an inventory item awaiting a buyer.

Fine. Let him count. The trunk contained clothing, letters from a fictional sister-in-law, deeds to a fictional vineyard, and a false bottom containing nothing at all, because she had emptied it in Marseille and moved the true cargo to where no customs man in Christendom would look: sewn flat against her own body. The forged papers — the second identity, the fallback, Madame Moreau of Barcelona — rode in the lining of her coat, cut into oiled silk panels, stitched by her own hands with a sailmaker's needle borrowed and returned. Against her ribs, left side, over the heart, because sentiment was a form of security: you protected what you loved, and she loved staying alive.

They could weigh the trunk till doomsday. The trunk weighed what it was supposed to weigh.

But knowledge like this wanted acting on, not hoarding, and so that evening she engineered her encounter. She came up on deck during the quiet after supper — the storm had blown itself down to a long ugly swell — and found Ferran checking the lashing on a spare spar, and offered him, of all things, conversation.

"A terrible crossing," she said. "My late husband never sailed. He said the sea was a creditor that never wrote first."

Ferran straightened. Up close he smelled of ink and vinegar. "Most husbands are wise about the sea," he said. "And most wives ignore them."

"I am not brave, señor. Only solvent." She let that sit, then added, lightly, "I noticed your men moving my trunk to the high shelf. Thank you. The vineyard deeds are in it, and my brother-in-law would murder me if the water reached them. Paper drowns faster than people."

Something moved behind his narrow face — disappointment, she thought, or its twin, recalculation. A trunk full of legal documents was a boring trunk. He had hugged it like a barrel and found, by the feel of her words, exactly what a widow ought to be carrying. He bowed the smallest bow a man could bow without lying down.

"The high shelf stays dry," he said.

"I'm sure it does," said Mirabel, and smiled the smile she had practiced in a hundred mirrors, the one that promised a dull voyage, and walked back along the wet deck with her heart going evenly, evenly, evenly, and thought: *he'll count it a third time anyway. Men like him always do. Good. Let him find the deeds.*

* * *

On the sixth night, in the black middle watch, with the ship running clean under a scrap of sail and the stars hard as nailheads, Mirabel destroyed her insurance.

Not the papers — those were load-bearing now, the second identity might save her life at any port. She destroyed a key. The route code, the least valuable of the three, the one that mattered only if she reached the relay stations alive and unsearched. If she were taken tomorrow, the consular cipher and the field cipher were worth fighting for; the route code was worth dying of. A captured courier carrying three codes was a captured network. A captured courier carrying two was a tragedy. The mathematics admitted no argument.

And yet her hands hesitated. That surprised her. She had burned her brother's letter without flinching — almost without flinching — and here she stood over a slip of rice paper no bigger than a prayer card, ink barely legible even by design, feeling like a woman deciding whether to drown a kitten.

Because destroying it meant admitting the possibility. Burning the route code was a vote cast for capture. As long as it existed, whole, in her head and nowhere else, she remained a woman who expected to arrive.

She thought of her instructor's ink-stained fingers. She thought of the ledger-logic of her trade, which she had never quite managed to love and had never once managed to escape: *carry little, trust less, owe nothing.* She thought, unwilled and unwelcome, of Tomas teaching her the shifting-letter game on the floor of their father's study, both of them children, the war still a rumor, the century still fat and golden and apparently endless. He had been eleven. He had beaten her every time, and crowed, and their father had said from behind his desk, without looking up, *Tomas, one day you will meet a mind that does not lose,* and Mirabel had spent her whole life since wondering whether that had been an insult or a prophecy, and to whom.

The route code was forty characters square. She had known it for six years. She ran it now, one final time, top to bottom, forward and reversed, fast, the way you read a page you intend never to open again — and then slower, checking, because memory lies politely when you ask it to hurry — and then once more, cold, with her eyes shut, against invented text, character by character, until the grid stood complete and immovable in the dark behind her eyelids, a fortress with no door from outside.

Then she unfolded the rice paper, touched its corner to the flame of her shaded lantern, and held it until the fire reached her fingers.

Ash into the slop bucket. Ash stirred with a finger. Ash drowned. The smell of burnt paper hung in the cabin a moment, faint, domestic, like a letter from home, and then the sea-air through the shutter seam took it away.

There. She was lighter by half an ounce and heavier by everything else. If they took her now, they would take a widow named Lefèvre with deeds to a vineyard that did not exist and a head full of fire that could not be opened, bled, or begged out. She was, she realized, standing in the swaying dark with her fingertips still warm, exactly what the creed demanded and nothing it promised: self-contained. Deniable. Alone in the precise sense that no one on earth could now prove what she knew.

It should have felt like strength. Mostly it did.

Underneath, small and patient as water in a leaking hull, it felt like the other thing too. Like the moment on a road when you look back and the house is already too far away to call to.

* * *

Land came on the eleventh day, as promised, gray at first and then gold.

She heard it before she saw it — the boy shouting from the bowsprit, the crew answering, the whole ship waking like a dog that knows the sound of its own street — and then she came on deck and there it was: Minorca, rising out of a pewter morning, low and long and limestone-pale, with the great landlocked harbor opening ahead like a held breath. Port Mahon. The deepest natural harbor in the Mediterranean, the prize everyone had fought over and no one had managed to keep, a fist of blue water clenched inside a hand of rock, defended now — if the reports were honest, and reports never were — by a garrison owed eight months of pay and a population owed rather more than that.

Mirabel stood at the rail with the wind pulling her widow's veil sideways and made herself look at it the way she looked at everything: weighing. How many guns on that point. How many hulls at anchor — fewer than there should be, much fewer. Smoke from the town, ordinary smoke, bread-smoke, morning-smoke. A church bell ringing the hour as though the hour were safe.

Somewhere in that town was a consul named Aubert who expected her. Somewhere in that town were sealed orders with her name in the margin, orders whose contents she had not been told, because couriers were told nothing — carry little, trust less. Somewhere in that town, or beyond it in the enemy country she could not see from here, a dead man had written to her in a living hand and asked her to come alone to an old windmill, bringing nothing she could not eat.

The *Meridian* leaned into the turn. The harbor walls drew closer, sunlit, ancient, indifferent, and Mirabel Crane put her gloved hand flat on the rail and felt the ship's trembling come up through her arm and steadied it there, the way you steady anything you intend to keep.

Behind her, unhurried, the purser began counting the passengers.
