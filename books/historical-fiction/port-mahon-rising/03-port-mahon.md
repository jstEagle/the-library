## 3. Port Mahon

The harbor announced itself before the land did.

First there was a smell — salt and stone and something sweeter beneath it, the green breath of fig trees that grew out of cracks in the cliff like they owned the place. Then the sound: bells, two churches arguing about the hour. Then the color, which was the thing no one had warned me about. I had imagined a military port, gray and functional. What opened around our bows instead was a drowned canyon of white limestone, walls rising sheer on three sides, the town stacked up them in tiers like cargo that had been unloaded by a careless god.

The pilot boat came out to meet us flying no colors at all, which told me more than any flag would have. A port with confidence flies its colors. Port Mahon flew nothing because flying anything meant choosing a side, and choosing a side, in the last winter of this century, meant dying for it.

I stood at the rail with my one trunk beside me and did what I always did on arrival: counted.

Two frigates in the outer roads, both careened, hulls open to the air like patients on a table. A powder hulk moored well away from everything else, which at least meant someone in the garrison still remembered why powder hulks existed. The mole was intact. The Martello tower at the harbor mouth was not — its seaward face had slumped into a slope of rubble, and goats grazed on it now, unhurried, unafraid of cannon that would never fire again. Along the quay, soldiers in uniforms that had been blue once and were now the color of old weather moved crates with the particular slowness of men who had stopped believing the work mattered.

Fourteen guns serviceable, I guessed, from what I could see of the batteries. Perhaps ten. Enough to make an attacker polite. Not enough to make him stop.

"Mademoiselle." The purser appeared at my elbow, for the fourth time since dawn, and looked at my trunk for the fifth time. "You'll want a chair carried up. The steps are—"

"I'll carry my own trunk."

He smiled as if I'd made a joke, and I let him think so. Men forgave a woman her strangeness if she let them laugh first. It was a currency I spent carefully and never explained, because explaining devalued it.

The brig bumped against the quay. I stepped across before the lines were fast, because I had learned years ago that the moment your foot touches new ground is the only moment no one is watching you arrive. After that, everyone watches. That is the whole profession, if you're honest about it: arriving without being seen, then being watched forever after by people who trust you completely and know you not at all.

* * *

The consulate occupied a house on the second tier of the town, up a street so steep the buildings seemed to lean on each other for support. Whitewashed walls, green shutters closed against the morning, and a door of black oak with a brass knocker worn smooth by two centuries of hands. Above it, carved into the lintel when this had been someone's home and not an office of a republic that barely noticed it existed: *LA MAR ÉS LA LLEI*. The sea is the law.

The clerk who answered was maybe nineteen and had the eyes of a man of fifty, which happens quickly in garrison towns. He took my card, went inside, came back, and said, in careful French, that Monsieur le Consul would receive me.

Aubert received me in a room that smelled of ink and cold coffee. He was smaller than his reputation — dispatches had described a man of iron presence — and older, with the soft hands of someone who had left physical labor behind so long ago it had become a rumor. His coat was good. His boots were better. I noted both, the way I noted everything, filing details in the ledger I kept behind my eyes: *Consul Aubert, well-shod, tired, watchful.*

"Monsieur Crane," he said, rising. "You are younger than your letters suggested."

"My letters were written by a committee."

That surprised a real smile out of him, and for a moment he looked human. "Sit. You'll take coffee? The coffee here is an insult, but it is a strong insult."

I sat. I took the coffee. We performed the small theater of pleasantries — the crossing, the season, the state of the roads from Barcelona — while he read my credentials, and I watched him read them. This was the actual business. Papers can be forged; reactions cannot, or not easily, or not by a man who believed he was reading genuine orders.

He reached the third page. His thumb paused on the seal. He looked up at me, then back down, then up again, and whatever he found in my face satisfied him, because he nodded once and set the pages down with a care that told me he understood exactly how much they were worth.

"You come at a strange hour, Mademoiselle Crane."

"The hour chose itself. I go where the papers go."

"And the papers say?"

"The papers say what they say. They are sealed. I am their carriage, not their author."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, or respect, or the particular frustration of a man who wanted information he could not politely demand. I drank my bad coffee and gave him nothing, because that was the arrangement: he could not ask, and I could not tell, and between those two impossibilities we built a working relationship in under four minutes.

"There is a room for you at the Fonda del Mig," he said finally. "It is clean. The landlady is discreet, which here means she is expensive. The Committee will want—" He caught himself. "Interested parties will want to meet you. In time. When you've rested."

"When I've rested," I agreed, and put the phrase away in my ledger, where it sat next to his boots and his soft hands. *When you've rested* was what powerful men said when they meant *when I've decided how to use you.*

He walked me to the door himself, which was courtesy or surveillance, and in the doorway he paused.

"A word of advice, freely given, worth exactly what you pay for it." His voice dropped. "In Port Mahon, trust the limestone and the water. Trust very little else. And if a man offers you fish at a price too low, count the bones before you eat."

"Thank you, Consul."

"Don't thank me yet." He almost smiled again. "Thank me in April, if you're still here."

Then the black door closed, and I stood alone on the steep street with my trunk, in a town full of people who would trust me on sight and never know my name was not entirely my own.

* * *

The Fonda del Mig turned out to be six rooms above a wine shop, run by a widow named Serra who was somewhere between sixty and eternity and missed nothing. She showed me my room — narrow bed, narrow window, view of the inner harbor through a gap between roofs — took my coin, and studied me for a long moment with eyes the color of wet slate.

"You'll be staying through the winter," she said. Not a question.

"That's the intention."

"Winters are long here." She set my key on the table between us, deliberately, so I saw her do it. "Long winters make people talk. Talking makes trouble. My house has no trouble. Whatever it is you do—" she gestured vaguely at my traveling clothes, my trunk, my whole careful self "—do it quietly, and we will suit each other."

"Madame, I am the quietest guest you will ever have."

"We'll see." But something in her face eased a degree, some fractional thawing, and she added, on her way out, "Supper is at eight. There is fish. From the count-the-bones school of advice, mine is simpler: eat what the fishermen eat, and you will live as long as they do."

After she left I locked the door, unpacked in the order I always unpacked — documents first, then instruments, then clothes — and stood at the window looking down at the harbor while the light went bronze and then violet.

From up here the decay was easier to see than it had been from the water. The careened frigates were not being repaired; they were being cannibalized, their rigging stripped to keep the smaller vessels alive. On the quay, a squad of militia drilled in the last light, and even from this distance I could read the truth in their movements: they knew the drill words but not the drill. Their officer watched them with his hands clasped behind his back and the expression of a man counting days rather than soldiers.

And threading between all of it, everywhere, unnoticed because they were designed to be unnoticed: the townspeople. Fishermen mending nets with quick fingers and quicker glances. Women carrying water who somehow managed to pass every soldier, every sentry, every stranger, and be seen by none of them. A boy of perhaps twelve who sat on the bollards all afternoon doing nothing, which is the hardest job in intelligence and the one children do best.

Port Mahon was not a garrison town. It was a town that tolerated a garrison, the way a body tolerates a splinter — around it, aware of it, waiting for the chance to push it out. The republic's flag flew over the fort, and the republic's officers dined at the consul's table, and underneath all of it ran the older law, the one carved over Aubert's door. The sea is the law. Everyone in this harbor served it first and every other master second, and everyone knew it, and no one said it.

I had been in such places before. Cadiz, before the fleet burned. Livorno, in the year the markets froze. Towns that lived on trade learn a survival the great capitals never need: they commit to nothing, they welcome everyone, they remember everything. You can be trusted in a town like this by noon of your first day. Trusted with letters, trusted with errands, trusted with your life — the innkeeper will wake you if soldiers come, the fishmonger will warn you off a bad purchase, a total stranger will walk you three streets out of your way to steer you past a patrol.

Trusted by everyone. Known by no one.

There is a loneliness in that which does not look like loneliness from outside. From outside it looks like success. It looks like a woman at a window with wine, respected, safe, connected to the whole warm life of a town. What it actually is, is being a letter. Carried everywhere, valued highly, opened by whoever holds you, and belonging to none of the hands that pass you along.

My brother used to write to me about this. Tomas, who had been dead for fourteen months according to the admiralty's best arithmetic, drowned with his ship somewhere in the cold Atlantic arithmetic of war. *They trust us because we are useful, Mira,* he wrote once, in the summer before the end, when we still exchanged letters like ordinary siblings, like people with a country and a hearth and a future. *Usefulness is not the same as belonging. Remember that. If you ever find yourself confusing the two, come home, and I will remind you what your laugh sounds like.*

I remembered. I could produce the memory on demand, complete, the way I produced ciphers. Tomas at the kitchen table with his terrible handwriting, Tomas teaching me the old game cipher we invented when I was nine because he refused to let me lose gracefully. Tomas laughing.

Dead men should not be this vivid. Grief, done properly, fades into furniture. Mine hadn't. Fourteen months, and he still stood in rooms I entered, still finished sentences I began, still —

I stopped myself. Discipline. I had a discipline for this too, the same as everything: feel it fully, once, at a scheduled time, and then put it away. I allowed myself ninety seconds at the window, wine untouched, watching the lanterns come alive along the mole one by one like a slow constellation being assembled by hand.

Then I sat down at the table, took out the leather folio, and reviewed tomorrow's work by candlelight, because tomorrow I would begin the real errand: mapping who held power in this town, who wanted it, and which of them would sell the harbor and which would merely rent it.

Below my window, on the dark water, a fishing boat came in late. Its single lantern swung as it rowed, and the light it threw against the harbor wall made shapes — regular shapes, repeated. I watched without meaning to, the way one reads without deciding to.

Three flashes. Pause. One flash. Three again.

Not random. Lantern talk, harbor code, the kind every port keeps for fog and night arrivals. I filed it without urgency, one more entry in the ledger: *the fishermen signal; the town speaks in light.*

I did not know then that within a week that same code would carry messages that would crack my life open like a keel on rocks. I did not know that the boy on the bollards had already reported my arrival to three separate masters, or that the widow downstairs priced her discretion in secrets as much as coin, or that the consul's soft hands had signed things that would make my sealed dispatches the most dangerous paper on this island.

I knew only what I always knew: that I had arrived safely, that I was trusted, that I was alone, and that somewhere in the dark town below me, someone was already counting the bones in the fish they intended to serve me.

I blew out the candle and slept the way professionals sleep — lightly, completely, with one ear on the stairs.

At some hour past midnight, I woke to voices in the street below. A knock at a door down the lane. Low words. Then silence, the heavy kind, the kind that has weight in it.

I lay in the dark and listened to the harbor breathe, and thought of my brother, and told myself firmly that dead men stay dead.

It was the last easy lie I would tell for a long time.
