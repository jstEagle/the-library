## 4. A Stranger's Hand

The sea off Minorca at night was the color of a bruise, and Elias Vane had learned long ago not to trust anything that color.

He crouched in the stern of a llaüt no bigger than a coffin, watching the lights of Port Mahon resolve out of the dark — first the fort on the headland, then the scattered lamps along the Moll de Llevant, then the great limestone walls rising pale as bone above the water. The town sat in its harbor like something being kept. He'd been to enough sieges to know what a place looked like when it was waiting for bad news, and Port Mahon had that look the way a sickroom has it: quiet, tidy, hopeful in the way of people who have already counted their spoons.

"Two minutes," said Ferran, the fisherman who rowed him in. Ferran had been paid too much to ask questions, which meant he asked them constantly with his eyes.

Elias didn't answer. He checked the oilskin packet inside his coat one more time — not because he needed to, but because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. Twelve years of running cargo across lines nobody drew on maps, and his nerves had never once behaved like this. It wasn't the landing. Landings were easy; he'd come ashore at night through blockades tighter than this one. It was the packet.

Letters, plural. Sealed, waxed, addressed in three different hands to three different names — but all of them ultimately for one person. And one instruction, delivered by a fat intermediary in Barcelona whose sweat had soaked through his collar: *You will carry these sealed. You will not read them. You will not learn the recipient's true name. You will deliver them into her own hand and no other.*

*Her own hand.* That phrase alone told Elias more than any reading could have. Nobody paid smuggler rates for a package that could be left on a doorstep.

The llaüt scraped shingle. He stepped out into water to his knees, cold enough to make him hiss, and waded up the narrow beach below the old quarantine wall. Ferran was already pushing off, muttering something about saints, and then it was just Elias and the dark and the weight against his ribs.

He gave himself thirty seconds to listen. Wind in the dry grass. The far clang of a buoy. Somewhere above, a sentry coughing on the ramparts — garrison men, badly fed, bored. A town falling apart politely.

Then he climbed the goat path toward the light he'd been told to look for: a single lamp burning in the window of a house on Carrer d'en Serral, second from the corner, behind the chandler's yard. He'd memorized the description in Barcelona until it lived behind his eyes. *She keeps late hours. She will be awake.*

Everyone involved seemed very sure of what Mirabel Crane would do. Elias found that interesting. In his trade, certainty about another person's behavior was usually the first sign you were about to be robbed.

The house, when he reached it, was smaller than the money implied. That told him something else. Whoever she was, she traveled lean — or someone wanted her to look like she did. He'd spent the last hour learning her habits from the shadows across the street, and everything he'd learned fit the same shape. She went out once, to the well, carrying both buckets herself rather than wake the landlady. She walked like a woman counting steps. She came back and stood in the doorway a full ten seconds, scanning the street before she closed the door, and Elias, flattened against a wall twenty feet away, felt professionally seen-through even though she never looked at him.

Discipline, he thought. Real discipline. Not soldier discipline — something older and more private. The kind you build around a wound so nobody can find it.

Now he stood in the alley beside the chandler's yard with the lamp-oil smell thick in his nose, working out how a man delivers letters *into her own hand* without getting shot for his trouble. Knocking was out. She'd have a knife, and she'd use it before she used questions. He settled on the oldest trick in the messenger's book: let the letters introduce themselves.

He took one pebble and tossed it against her shutter. Not hard. Just enough — a sound like a moth with ambitions.

Inside: silence. Then the scrape of something being moved off a table. Then nothing at all, which was worse. She was standing behind the door now, he'd have bet his boat on it, blade low, listening back.

He waited. Counted to forty. Tossed the pebble again.

"Who is there?" Her voice, through the wood. Low, level, unaccented English with a faint French polish underneath — educated, careful, the voice of someone who had decided years ago exactly how much of herself each word would reveal.

"A courier," Elias said, keeping his own voice down and flat. "With letters. I'm told they're only safe if you take them yourself."

Another silence, longer. He could hear her thinking. It sounded like the harbor — deep water under a thin skin of movement.

"Put them under the door."

"I can't." True, and useful. "They're sealed against inspection. My instructions were specific. Your hand, your eyes, or they go back into the sea with me."

That last part was a flourish, but a calculated one. He heard her breath change — the small hitch of a person recalibrating a stranger's value. When she spoke again, the voice hadn't moved a hair, but something beneath it had shifted, the way ballast shifts.

"One moment."

He heard the bar lifted. The door opened four inches — no more — and there she was in the gap: a woman perhaps five-and-twenty, dark hair pinned with severe economy, eyes that did the work of a lantern. She held a knife the way other women held fans, casually and with total sincerity.

"You're not from Barcelona," she said.

"No."

"You're not military."

"No."

"You've been standing in that alley since before I went to the well."

Elias allowed himself half a smile. "Yes."

"Why should I believe the letters are mine?"

"Because," he said, "of how they're addressed." And he held the oilskin packet up into the spill of lamplight from her window, turning it so she could see the outermost letter — the one addressed to a name he didn't know, in a handwriting he wasn't supposed to study and had studied anyway, because a man paid never to read a thing will inevitably memorize its cover.

He watched her face while she read it upside down.

Here is the thing Elias Vane knew about faces: most people lie with their mouths, some lie with their eyes, but almost nobody lies with the muscles around the eyes, because those answer to a paymaster deeper than vanity. He watched hers now. He saw recognition arrive — not surprise, recognition, the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd — and then he saw the discipline clamp down over it like a hatch cover slamming shut. Half a heartbeat of open sea, and then flat calm again.

But he'd seen it. Whatever was in that handwriting, she hadn't expected it. And whatever she'd expected instead, it had been worse, or different, or both.

"Who engaged you?" she asked.

"An intermediary. Fat man, Barcelona, sweats like a keg. I never got a name and I wasn't offered one."

"And the fee?"

"Paid in advance." He shrugged. "Half up front buys my silence about the rest."

"It bought your silence about the contents. You're talking freely enough about everything else."

"Because everything else is worthless," he said. "A fat man, a boat, an address. None of it's worth killing over. What's worth killing over is in here—" he tapped the packet— "and I haven't read it. Which means I know exactly as little as I've been paid to know, and I intend to keep my ignorance intact. Ignorance doesn't hang, señora. Knowledge does."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of one professional appraising another's tools and finding them sound.

"Wait there," she said, and shut the door.

He waited. He was good at waiting; it was most of his profession. Through the shutter slats he saw lamplight move, saw her shadow cross it twice, unhurried. Checking the street again, probably. Deciding whether he was a delivery or a trap. He found he cared which she decided, which was unusual, and he noted that too, filed it away with the rest of the things about tonight that didn't fit.

The door opened fully this time. She stood in the frame with the knife gone and a candle in her hand instead, and the light did her no favors and her no harm — she had the kind of face that made its own arrangements with darkness.

"Give them to me," she said, "and tell me honestly: did anyone see you land?"

"Ferran. A fisherman. Paid too well to talk."

"There's no such amount."

"There isn't," Elias agreed. "Which is why I'll be gone before dawn, and why you should decide quickly what you want to ask me before I go."

"I haven't decided that I want to ask you anything."

"You opened the door twice."

Her eyes came up to his and stayed there, and for a moment neither of them performed anything. It was, absurdly, the most honest exchange he'd had in a year. Then she took the packet from his hands.

He watched her weigh it. Not metaphorically — she actually weighed it in her palm, tilting it, testing, the way he weighed coin, and he understood in that instant that this was a woman who measured things. Paper, risk, loyalty, grief. Everything got put on the scale. He wondered what she'd found herself weighing lately that wouldn't balance.

She broke the outer seal with her thumbnail — a clean, practiced stroke, no hesitation — and unfolded the single sheet. Her eyes went down it once, fast, the way a soldier reads terrain.

And then Elias Vane watched Mirabel Crane's composure crack.

Not dramatically. He doubted anyone else in the world would have caught it. But he was standing close, closer than courtesy, close enough to be either a threat or a witness, and he saw it: the candle trembled in her hand — one ring of light shivering up the wall — and her breath went in and did not come back out, and for two full seconds the woman who counted steps and weighed paper was simply gone, replaced by somebody younger and much more frightened and much more alive.

Her lips shaped one word. No sound. But he read it, because he'd spent twelve years reading lips across dark water, and the word was a name.

*Tomas.*

The name meant nothing to him. The grief in it meant everything.

She refolded the letter with steady hands — hands steadier than her face, which was its own kind of education — and looked up at him, and the composure was back, all of it, seamless as new canvas. Too seamless. He'd seen the tear in it now; he'd always know where the seam ran.

"Thank you for the delivery," she said. "There will be payment arranged through the usual channel."

"The usual channel shorted me on this run already."

"Then name your figure."

"I will," he said. "Tomorrow. After I've slept on it." A gamble, and he knew it, and he took it anyway. "Some fees you set before you understand the job. This one I want to price after."

"After what?"

"After I decide," he said, "whether I'm a courier or something else. Those letters changed the market."

For the first time, something moved through her expression that he couldn't file — anger, maybe, or respect, or the exact point where the two share a border. "You were paid never to read those letters."

"I was." He glanced at the folded page in her hand, at the handwriting that had cracked her open for two seconds and cost him a night's sleep already. "I didn't. But I carried them past a British frigate, a Spanish patrol boat, and the worst crossing of the straits since November, and I watched a woman who fears nothing receive one and stop breathing. So now I know what they're worth, even if I don't know what they say. And a man in my line learns early that the worth of a thing tells you more than the thing itself."

She was quiet a moment. The candle burned between them, patient.

"You're more dangerous than your fee suggests," she said finally.

"Everyone says that. Nobody raises it."

"Go," she said. "Sleep. Come back tomorrow at dusk, if you still want naming figures. The door will be—" she paused, and he caught it, the smallest recalculation— "the door will be answered."

It wasn't an invitation. It was an admission that she'd considered locking it and chosen not to. Elias had been paid, threatened, courted, and shot at in the course of his career, and this was the first time a client's reluctance to bolt a door had felt like winning.

He touched his brow in a sketch of a bow that owed nothing to any country in particular, and turned for the alley.

"Elias Vane," he said over his shoulder, offering the name the way he'd been taught never to offer it — because she'd given him nothing, and fair was fair, and because some part of him already suspected he'd need her to be able to find him later, and that suspicion frightened him considerably more than the frigate had.

He heard her repeat it once, quietly, tasting it. Testing its weight. Of course she was.

He was halfway down the alley when he stopped, because the thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do.

*Fat man in Barcelona. Sweating. Paid in advance.* Intermediaries didn't spend that kind of money on a whim, and they didn't route letters through a smuggler unless every cleaner road was compromised. Someone had gone to real expense and real danger to put that handwriting in front of Mirabel Crane tonight. And whoever it was hadn't done it out of sentiment. Sentiment didn't hire Elias Vane.

Which meant the letters weren't the end of something. They were the opening move of something, and he'd just handed the piece across the board himself.

He stood in the dark smelling of lamp oil and salt, listening to the buoy clang and the town breathe, and understood that he had crossed a line without noticing where it lay. Couriers carried things. Whatever he was now, he was already on the wrong side of that definition — complicit in whatever game had paid him, curious past all professional sense about a woman who weighed letters in her hand and broke for exactly two seconds.

Curiosity, in his trade, was the expensive vice. It always started cheap.

Above the town, the sentry coughed again. Somewhere behind that shuttered window, Mirabel Crane was reading a dead man's handwriting by candlelight, and Elias Vane turned toward the harbor to find a bed he knew he wouldn't sleep in, knowing already — with the flat, cold certainty of a man who has just signed something — that he would be back at dusk.

And that whatever she asked of him then, the fee would not be money.
