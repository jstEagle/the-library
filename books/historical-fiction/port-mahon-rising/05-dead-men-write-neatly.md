## 5. Dead Men Write Neatly

The consulate gave her a room on the third floor with a window that looked out on the inner harbor and a lock that looked like an apology. Mirabel locked it anyway, and then she did what she always did before opening anything meant to be private: she counted.

One candle. One basin of water. Her dispatch case, still sealed with Aubert's wax from this afternoon. And on the bed, wrapped in oilcloth like a fishmonger's parcel, four letters in a hand she had last seen over her father's grave six years ago.

She had not opened them on the quay. She had not opened them in the courtyard while Vane watched her with those patient smuggler's eyes. She had thanked him, paid him half his fee, and told him to come back at ten tomorrow if he wanted the rest — and all the while her hands had been perfectly steady and her heart had been doing something no courier's heart should ever do: hammering like a fist on a door.

Now she was alone, and steadiness could go to the devil for one hour.

She sat on the bed. She unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, because slowness was a discipline and discipline was the only inheritance worth keeping. Four letters. The topmost was addressed to *M. Crane, Courier, Port Mahon*, in ink gone slightly brown with salt air. The address was correct down to the abbreviation — *M.*, never *Miss* or *Mademoiselle*, because Tomas had argued when they were children that titles were just ways of making people smaller than their names.

She knew that handwriting the way she knew her own pulse. The tall ascenders. The way he crossed his t's with a single flat stroke, like a man laying a plank across a gap. Their father had taught them both a copperplate hand; Tomas had spent thirty years quietly ruining his into something faster and more alive.

Dead men did not write neatly. Dead men did not write at all.

Mirabel set the letter face-down on the blanket and stood up and walked to the window, because there was a protocol even for this, and the protocol said: verify the container before trusting the contents. She breathed harbor air until her lungs agreed to behave. A fishing lamp moved along the mole. Somewhere below, the garrison drummer fumbled the evening call, as he had fumbled it every evening since she arrived — a town running out of men who cared about the difference between retreat and reveille.

Then she sat back down and turned the letter over.

The seal was broken. Of course it was broken; Vane had been paid never to read it, but whoever hired him had read it, or wanted her to think someone had, or simply hadn't trusted a stranger with intact wax. She noted it the way she noted everything now — weight, burn-time, deniability — and found, to her irritation, that noting things did nothing at all to slow her hands.

*Mirabel.*

Just that, at the top. No date, which was itself information: he didn't want the letter dated, because dated letters could be placed, and placeable men were dead men. Or living ones.

*I will write this quickly because I do not know how much time they give me, and slowly because I want you to know I am not mad and this is not a trick, and those two requirements fight each other on every line.*

*You were told I drowned. You were told it by people whose business is telling things, and I have learned in two years that drowning is a very convenient death — cheap to declare, expensive to disprove. I am not drowned. I am forty miles from you as the crow flies and considerably farther than that as the truth travels.*

She stopped. She made herself stop, made herself set the page on her knee and look at the candle flame and count to twenty, because hope arriving all at once was not hope, it was a boarding party, and she would not let it take the ship whole.

Two years. Two years of mourning measured out in small economies — leaving his chair where it was for exactly one year and then moving it, wearing black until black became simply her color, teaching herself to stop listening for his step on stairs in three houses in three cities. Grief was work, and she had done the work, and here was a piece of paper telling her the work had been performed against false books.

She picked the letter up again.

*They watch me, so I cannot say where. I can say this much: the windmill above the Albufera road, the one our grandfather used to take us to, the one with the cross carved in the doorframe where we swore the oath about never lying to each other — be there on the night of the fourth, after moonrise. Come alone. Come without papers. Come as yourself, because the woman who arrives under consular cover will be seen coming a mile off, and the people who see her are the same people who arranged for you to believe your brother was at the bottom of the sea.*

*I know how this reads. I know what they taught you: that a letter from a dead man asking you to a lonely place is either a trap or worse than a trap. It may be both. I have considered the possibility that they are using me as bait, and I have decided that the chance you are real outweighs the certainty that they are watching. That is the arithmetic of love, little sister. It has never once balanced, and I have stopped trying to make it balance.*

*Tomas*

Mirabel read it twice more. Then she held the first page close to the candle — not to burn it, only to warm the paper, an old habit of Tomas's own, a joke from childhood: *hold it near the fire, maybe there's invisible ink.* There wasn't. There was only the faint smell of salt and something else, something underneath — resin, perhaps, or the particular staleness of paper kept in a damp place and carried against a body.

The second letter was addressed to no one. It contained a single sheet, unsigned:

*If you are reading this, the first letter reached you intact, and you are therefore either his sister or a very good forgery of one. Consider: who benefits if the consul's new courier rides alone to a ruin on the fourth? Who profits from a Crane out of position, out of cover, out of witnesses? We do not advise. We only count.*

No signature, no cipher, nothing. She turned it over, held it to the light, looked for watermarks. Plain stock, bought anywhere, touched by anyone. It was a message designed to leave no fingerprints, which meant its author believed fingerprints were dangerous, which meant its author was afraid, which meant — her mind supplied, unhelpfully — that everyone in this affair was afraid except possibly the dead man himself.

The third letter stopped her breath.

It was addressed to *T. Crane, wherever the sea keeps him,* and it was in her mother's hand — or rather, in the hand her mother had before the palsy, which meant it was old, which meant it had been written years ago and carried ever since, through whatever country Tomas had actually been living in while the Admiralty filed him under *lost.* It was four lines long. It was about the garden. It said the plum tree had finally fruited and that she wished the boys would stop arguing about politics long enough to taste one.

Mirabel put that one down very carefully, the way you set down something already broken, and did not touch it again for a quarter of an hour.

* * *

The fourth letter was the shortest, and it was the one that changed everything, and afterward she could never quite explain to herself why she saved it for last. Perhaps because the wrapping had felt different — thinner, cheaper paper, the kind sold in fishing villages. Perhaps because some instinct older than her training had known.

*Believe the first letter. Disregard the counter-letter; it is from them, and its purpose is to frighten you into bringing company or staying home, both of which serve them. The mill on the fourth. After moonrise. Burn this immediately.*

*— T.C.*

Her skin went cold and then hot. Because the counter-letter — the anonymous warning, the one advising caution, the one she had already half-credited — was signed in effect by its own paranoia, and paranoia was not the voice of a friend counting costs. It was the voice of a handler managing an asset. *We do not advise. We only count.* Counting was what you did to things you intended to spend.

So the board looked like this: Tomas alive, writing in his own hand, asking her to a place only family knew — the windmill, the carved cross, the oath about lying. Someone else, unnamed, wanting her either absent or escorted. And a smuggler named Vane downstairs in some tavern, holding the other half of his fee, knowing everything and saying nothing, hired by an intermediary he'd never met by people who had read her mail before she did.

She got up and paced the small room, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, the way she'd paced quarantine cabins and customs houses and every other box the world had ever tried to keep her in. Think. Verify. The creed: carry little, trust less. But the creed had never accounted for this — for hope arriving like contraband, thrilling and dangerous and perfectly deniable, the one cargo she had no protocol for weighing.

Verify the hand. She fetched her dispatch case, took out the one document that was genuinely hers and not the Republic's: a letter from Tomas, written the week before his last sailing, tucked inside her copy of their father's atlas where no customs officer in history had ever thought to look. She laid the old letter beside the new one and compared them line by line like a forger hunting flaws.

The ascenders matched. The flat-crossed t's matched. The peculiar rightward drift that worsened toward the bottom of a page — Tomas writing fast, always fast — matched. And there, at the end of the old letter, the same small habit: he dotted his i's not above the stem but slightly left, a defect their schoolmaster had beaten at for a year and surrendered to forever. The new letter did it too. Twice.

Forgeries could copy a hand. Forgeries could copy almost anything. But the windmill — no forger could have known about the windmill unless Tomas himself had talked in captivity, and if he'd talked that much he'd have given them the oath too, and the letter would have asked her to bring the dispatches, or bring Aubert, or come armed. It asked for none of those things. It asked for herself. *Come as yourself.* Either it was truly him, or it was a trap built by someone who understood her well enough to know that the fastest way to move Mirabel Crane was not money or threat but the sentence *your brother is alive.*

Both possibilities ended at the same coordinates. That was the elegant cruelty of it. Whether she believed or doubted, whether she went as sister or as investigator, she would be at the windmill on the night of the fourth, after moonrise — and everyone who counted things would know it.

Unless.

She stopped pacing. She stood very still in the middle of the floor, candlelight making the harbor window into a black mirror, and she thought about the smuggler.

He had watched her receive the letters. He had seen her face do whatever it had done — she genuinely did not know what it had done, and that frightened her more than the ambush possibility did. He knew she had a connection to the sender. He had been hired blind, he claimed, by an intermediary he'd never met, paid in advance, told nothing — which was either the truth or the most economical lie in the Mediterranean. And he had offered, unprompted, to guide her inland if she ever needed roads that didn't exist on maps. He'd said it lightly, the way men offer things they've already decided to do.

The fourth of the month was three days away. The Albufera road ran through olive country where a lone woman on a consular horse would stand out like a signal fire. She could refuse the escort and probably die competently. She could accept it and probably be betrayed — or not. Vane was a variable she couldn't weigh yet, and she despised variables she couldn't weigh.

But there was another use for him, one that required no trust at all. If she rode for the windmill and vanished, someone should know where the trail began — not a friend, necessarily, not an ally, just a witness with mercenary habits and a professional disinclination to let paying clients disappear without collecting. And if Vane was one of *them* — if the intermediary and the counter-letter came from the same desk — then letting him guide her was exactly the mistake they'd designed the trap around.

Or it was the one move they hadn't designed, because they'd assumed she'd either obey her fear or disobey it alone.

She looked at the letters spread on the blanket: the brother, the counter-voice, the mother's plum tree, the warning to burn. Four pieces of paper, and the whole war balanced on which of them was honest.

Mirabel took the counter-letter to the stove and fed it in and watched the anonymous caution curl into ash, because whoever wrote it wanted her passive and passivity was the one instruction she had never once managed to follow. She kept the rest. Then she took out her little notebook — the one cataloguing weights and burn-times, the ledger of her whole creed — and on a fresh page she wrote a single line:

*Windmill. Fourth. Alone is a word other people use on you.*

Below it, after a moment, she wrote: *Ask Vane what he charges for roads.*

Outside, the harbor lights burned steady, and somewhere across forty miles of dark island, a dead man was waiting to find out if his sister still kept oaths.
