## 6. Terms of Carriage

Elias Vane heard her boots on the stairs before he saw her, and he had time to make three decisions in the interval: to keep drinking his coffee as if it were coffee and not the dregs of a pot gone cold an hour ago; to leave the letters where they were, face down on the table between them like cards nobody wanted; and not to reach for the knife.

The last one was the hardest. Instinct is a cheap lodging-house keeper — it wakes you at every sound and charges you for the privilege. But instinct would have him dead or gone by morning, and he'd already decided, somewhere between the second glass of wine and the third, that he wasn't finished with this woman yet.

She came into the room at the back of the Fonda del Mar without knocking, because she'd already established that knocking was for people with something to hide from each other, and she closed the door behind her with a click so quiet it was louder than a slam.

"You followed me," she said.

"I waited," Elias said. "There's a difference. Following costs effort."

"Where did you get these?" She put her hand flat on the letters. She didn't pick them up. He'd noticed that about her already — she touched things the way a surgeon touches a wound, briefly, decisively, and only when necessary.

"A man gave them to me in Algiers."

"What man?"

"That's the interesting part." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. It was a habit he'd picked up from watching gamblers; it made people think you were stalling when actually you were measuring how long they'd let you stall before they started talking. "I never saw his face. He sat behind me in a courtyard with a screen between us, which I'll admit is theatrical, but theatrical men pay best. His voice was ordinary. That's the part that's stayed with me. Not young, not old, not foreign, not local. A voice like a rented room."

"And you took delivery of sealed correspondence from a voice like a rented room."

"He paid in advance. In gold. And he told me two things." Elias held up one finger. "First: deliver these to the Englishwoman at the consulate in Port Mahon, into her own hand, and to no one else. Second: never read them, and if anyone asks, forget his face, because he'd made sure I couldn't remember it anyway." A second finger. "He was right about that. I've tried. There's nothing there. Just the voice, and the smell of the courtyard — orange blossom and tar — and the weight of the purse."

"The Englishwoman," Mirabel said. Her voice didn't change, but something behind it did; he heard it the way you hear a floorboard settle under weight. "Not by name?"

"Not by name. By description. Height, coloring, the way I'd know you. He described your hands."

That landed. He watched it land. Her hands went still on the table — both of them now, flat, fingers spread slightly, as if the wood might pitch and roll.

"My hands."

"Ladylike, he said, but callused at the base of the thumb. From writing, he thought, or from holding a weapon. He said I'd know them when I saw them." Elias shrugged. "It was the strangest part of the whole commission, and the whole commission was strange. I've carried contraband brandy, escaped priests, one very angry falcon, and a box I was told on pain of death never to shake. But I've never been hired to find a woman by her hands."

She sat down. Not gracefully — she lowered herself onto the chair opposite him the way a soldier sits at a table where the maps are bad, all at once, done with standing. Up close, in the gray morning light through the shutters, she looked like she hadn't slept. He knew the look intimately. He wore it most days himself.

"Describe the purse," she said.

"Leather. New stitching. Spanish gold, mostly, some French. Why?"

"Was there anything written inside the flap? Anything scratched, stamped?"

He thought about lying to her and rejected it in the time it takes a coin to fall. Lying to this one would be like lying to a customs officer who has already opened your trunk. "A cipher mark. Two strokes and a circle. I assumed it was the intermediary's tally sign."

"Tally sign," she repeated softly. Something moved across her face — not a smile, nothing so generous. More like weather changing over water. "Two strokes and a circle."

"You recognize it."

"I recognize everything," she said, "except why my brother is alive."

* * *

They let the silence sit for a while after that, because both of them were professionals, and professionals know that silence is where the other party reveals their price. The harbor worked outside the window — hammers from the careening wharf, a bell, gulls conducting their eternal argument. Port Mahon's great drowned canyon of a harbor swallowed sound and gave back echoes, so that even the noise felt half-secret.

Elias refilled her cup without asking. She let him. He counted that as progress, and progress, in his line of work, was measured the way other men measured love.

"Let's be efficient," he said. "You want three things from me. One: the man in Algiers. Two: whether the letters were tampered with in transit. Three: passage to wherever those letters point, which — since you read them and I didn't — I'm guessing is somewhere you don't want to go alone."

"You didn't read them." She said it flatly, testing.

"I told you I was paid not to."

"People lie for less than gold."

"True. But I lie for exactly gold, and the gold required me to carry them unread, because unread letters are worth more than read ones. Read letters can be denied. Unread ones are proof." He leaned back. "Also, and I say this with respect, madam: if I could have read them, I would have. They were sealed with wax and a signet I didn't recognize, and I don't carry the tools for that kind of work. My talents run to knots, distances, and remembering which harbors have informants."

"And what do you charge for talents like that?"

"Now we arrive." He spread his hands. "Passage to the windmill road, guide services included, discretion guaranteed. Fifty pounds."

"No."

"Forty."

"I haven't said yes to any figure. I said no to fifty because you named fifty before knowing the route, which means you're guessing, and I don't buy guesses." She turned her cup the same quarter-turn he'd given his, and he had to admire the theft of the gesture. "How do you know about a windmill?"

"I know the island," Elias said. "There are four ruined mills within a day's walk of this town, and only one of them is on a road a stranger would take, because the other three are on roads goats wouldn't take twice. And I know you asked the landlord yesterday about hire of a mule, inland, and I know the muleteer quoted you double because he thinks English money grows on trees, and I know you paid it, which tells me urgency matters more to you than price." He paused. "So perhaps I should revise. You don't need a guide. You need someone who knows which farmhouses along that road will sell bread to a stranger and which will sell information to the garrison instead."

Her eyes narrowed. "You've been following me."

"I've been observing you. Following costs effort." He smiled, and she did not return it, which he respected. "Look — I'll be honest with you, because honesty is cheaper than the alternative with someone like you. I was going to follow you regardless. The man in Algiers paid for delivery, not for abandonment, and there's a clause in every contract I've ever taken that exists only in my head: finish the job. But you've just told me the letters concern a brother you buried years ago, which means they're either a miracle or a trap, and I've seen enough traps to know they travel better in company. If it's a miracle, I want to witness it. If it's a trap, I want to be paid for walking into it."

"And your price for that?"

"Thirty-five pounds. Half now, half when we see whatever's waiting at the mill — brother, ghost, or ambush."

"Twenty-five."

"Thirty."

"Twenty-five," she repeated, "and the answer to a question."

Elias laughed despite himself. "Everyone wants the question free. The question is always worth more than the money."

"This one isn't. It's worth almost nothing, to almost everyone." She folded her hands — and he found himself looking at them, at the callus at the base of the thumb exactly where the voice in the courtyard had said it would be, and feeling, for the first time since Algiers, the cold draft of a much larger room opening somewhere behind this small one. "Why did you agree to carry letters for a man whose face you never saw?"

"Because of how he talked about you."

She went still again. He was learning to read her stillnesses; they were more eloquent than most people's speeches.

"Not romantically," he said. "Don't worry. He talked about you the way a quartermaster talks about a shipment he can't afford to lose. Careful. Precise. Almost tender, in the way supply officers are tender — he knew your habits, your tolerances, what would break you and what wouldn't. And I thought: here's a man who has studied a stranger for months to get a letter into her hand, and whatever's in that letter, he believes she'll need it. I don't take jobs for causes. Causes are expensive and heavy. But I'll take a job for conviction, even borrowed conviction, even secondhand." He drank his cold coffee and grimaced. "His conviction was real. Whatever else that voice was, it wasn't acting."

Mirabel Crane looked at him for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being appraised by someone who weighed things in ounces — he'd seen her do it with dispatches, with trunks, with the consul's assurances — and being found, provisionally, lighter than he feared.

"Twenty-five pounds," she said. "Half now. We leave tomorrow at first light, ahead of the fish carts. You'll choose the road, the stops, and the lies we tell at them. You'll tell no one what we carry — including, after tomorrow, me. If I give you a packet to hold, you hold it unopened until I ask for it back. Those are my terms."

"Harsh terms."

"They're the only kind I issue."

"And mine," Elias said, raising one finger, "are these. If we're stopped, I do the talking. If we're searched, the letters are yours to hide and yours alone — I won't know where, and I want it that way, because a man who doesn't know can't confess. And if it comes to running—" he hesitated, hearing his own voice change, and hated it a little, "—you don't wait for me. Twenty-five pounds doesn't buy my funeral."

"No," she agreed. "But it might buy your loyalty for a fortnight, and that's more than my government ever managed."

She counted out coins from a purse sewn into her sleeve — of course it was sewn into her sleeve; he was beginning to build a picture of this woman out of such details, and the picture kept getting heavier — and stacked them on the table between them. Twelve pounds, twelve shillings, exact. She'd known the exchange rate to the farthing.

"One more thing," she said, rising. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "The mark on the purse. Two strokes and a circle. When Tomas and I were children — when we were nine and eleven — we invented a cipher. A child's cipher. Strokes meant words, circles meant names. Two strokes and a circle meant—"

She stopped. He watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, not to finish the sentence, and understood that she had just shown him the shape of her trust the way you show someone the edge of a map: enough to prove there's territory, not enough to navigate it.

"It meant come home," she said instead. "That's all you need to know. First light, Mr. Vane. Bring the muleteer's honesty and none of his arithmetic."

The door clicked shut behind her, quiet as a held breath.

Elias looked at the coins. Then at the letters, face-down where she'd left them. Then at the window, where the morning was coming up over the limestone and the harbor was waking into its long pretense of peace.

Come home, he thought. Somebody had paid good gold to send a dead man's handwriting home, and had chosen a smuggler with a memory for voices to do the carrying, and had described a woman by her hands.

He swept the coins into his pocket and went to find a mule.

Tomorrow, first light, the windmill road — and whatever was waiting at the end of it, Elias Vane intended to be paid for every step.
