## 7. The Windmill Road

We left before dawn, while the harbor lamps still burned and the fishermen's mules stood patient in the cold, already saddled with salt and oil as though the day were a cargo they'd agreed to carry without complaint.

Elias Vane had two of them waiting in the lane behind the Casa de la Sal. He'd paid for silence along with the animals — I could tell by the way he counted coins into the stableman's palm without letting the man look up. There was an economy to him that I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting in a stranger's letter: every gesture trimmed of anything that wasn't necessary.

"You ride?" he asked.

"Badly."

"Good. A woman who rides well looks like she has somewhere important to be." He swung up onto his mule with a grace that contradicted everything else about him. "A bad rider looks like a merchant's wife visiting her sister."

"And what am I carrying?"

"Olive oil samples. You're taking orders for a house in Barcelona." He said it flatly, already bored with it. "Don't embellish. Liars who embellish die of their own eloquence."

I mounted, wincing at the saddle, and we went out through the northern gate while the sentry was still yawning over his brazier. The road climbed away from Port Mahon in long switchbacks through terraces of grey stone and silver leaf, and by the time the sun cleared the ridge the town had become a white scatter below us, its harbor a seam of blue-green folded into limestone.

I did not look back. Looking back is a habit for people who expect to return, and I had stopped expecting things some months ago, around the time my brother's name moved from the ledgers of the living to the lists of the lost.

* * *

For the first hour we said almost nothing. That suited me. Silence on a road is not empty; it is a wall two people build together, each adding stones, each checking the other isn't building a door.

It was Elias who broke it first, which surprised me. Mercenaries, in my experience, protect their silences like coin.

"Your brother," he said, eyes on the path ahead, "wrote neatly even as a boy?"

The question came sideways, the way all his questions came. "You never met him."

"I carried his letters. Carrying isn't meeting." He adjusted his hat against the climbing light. "But you know a person's hand after four years of it. The way he crossed his sevens. The way he always underlined the date, twice, like he didn't trust the calendar."

My stomach turned over slowly, the way ballast turns in a hold. "You carried letters from Tomas Crane."

"I carried letters between your house in Cádiz and wherever Tomas happened to be. Lisbon one season, Genoa another. Your father paid in escudos; your mother added a line in every packet asking if he was eating enough." He glanced at me then, briefly. "She wrote a very small hand for such a large question."

The road bent. An olive tree leaned out of its terrace wall, older than any nation I served, its trunk split and hollowed and still stubbornly green.

"When?" I asked.

"Before the war made it complicated. Before your family's name appeared on anyone's list but their own." He let that sit. "I was sixteen. It was the first work I ever did that someone trusted me to do alone."

"And now you carry letters addressed by a dead man."

"Now I carry letters I was paid never to read." He smiled without warmth. "There's a whole profession built on that distinction, señora. Most of us die in it eventually, so I try not to be precious about it."

"Mirabel," I said.

He looked at me properly this time.

"If you knew my family's mail, you know my name. Don't make me ride another league being 'señora' to a man who can describe my mother's handwriting."

"Mirabel," he repeated, testing it. Then, because he was who he was: "You shouldn't have told me that."

"I know."

* * *

We stopped at noon in the shade of a dry-stone shelter where shepherds had chalked dates and initials into the walls going back further than the century. Elias watered the mules from a skin and handed me bread and a hard cheese without ceremony. I noticed he gave himself the smaller piece of both and pretended not to notice myself doing so.

"Why did you take the job?" I asked. "The letters. You said you were paid never to read them — but someone hired you to bring them to *me*, specifically. That's not ordinary carriage."

"No."

"That means whoever paid you knows who I am. Knows what I'm doing in Port Mahon. Which means either they're a friend of mine or they've set a hook in the water and are waiting to see what bites."

He chewed slowly, watching the valley. A hawk hung motionless above the olive terraces, spending nothing, learning everything.

"The intermediary met me in Alicante," he said finally. "Never gave a name. Paid half in advance, half on delivery, and doubled it when I told him the addressee would want to know exactly where the letters came from. He said—" and here Elias paused, and I understood he was deciding how much truth the road could bear, "—he said to tell you: *the wind remembers what the sea was made to forget.*"

The bread went dry in my mouth.

That was Tomas's phrase. Our phrase. From when we were children on the Cádiz coast, from the summer our father took us inland and Tomas, eight years old and furious about it, declared that the windmill on the hill remembered everything the sea tried to wash away — every ship lost, every sailor drowned, every promise the tide broke. We'd used it between ourselves for years as a countersign, a joke, a way of saying *this is really us.*

No one else alive knew it. No one else could know it.

"He's alive," I said. My voice came out level, which cost me something I couldn't itemize yet.

"Or someone wants you to believe it very badly," Elias said. "Which on this island is worth more than money."

"Don't." I set down the bread. "Don't do the thing where you protect me from hope. I've been doing grief professionally for eleven months. I'd like an hour off."

Something shifted in his face — not softness exactly; recalibration. He looked at me the way he must have looked at cargo manifests when the numbers refused to balance.

"All right," he said. "One hour."

* * *

We rode again through the afternoon, and the terraces rose around us like the seats of an empty amphitheater, and somewhere in that landscape the conversation changed character entirely. I couldn't say when. Cover stories don't collapse all at once; they shed layers, the way an onion surrenders, one thin lie at a time.

His name wasn't Elias Vane. I'd guessed that from the first hour aboard the consul's quay — no one born to that name walks with his left shoulder slightly forward, guarding a scar he doesn't explain. But he offered the fact himself somewhere past the third ridge, apropos of nothing.

"Elias Vane was a customs clerk in Bristol who died owing me money," he said. "I inherited the name along with his debts. They cancel each other out."

"And before him?"

"A name you'll get when you've earned it. Everyone gets one name for free." He shrugged. "Yours is Mirabel Crane, and I'd wager that's real, because liars choose prettier ones."

"What makes you think I haven't lied to you?"

"Because when I told you your brother might be bait in a trap, you didn't argue. A liar would have argued. Truth sits differently in a person." He nodded at the road ahead. "Also, you checked the mule's shoes yourself this morning. Spies don't check shoes. People with somewhere to be do."

I laughed. It escaped before I could stop it — a short, undignified sound that startled the mule and, frankly, me. Eleven months since I'd laughed at anything, and this smuggler with a dead man's name had done it in under six hours on a dirt road.

"Don't do that again," I said.

"Laughing?"

"Being right about me. It's unsettling."

"Noted." But there was something in his voice now, a loosening, a rope paying out link by link. "My father was a fisherman," he said, after a while, to the horizon rather than to me. "Off the Breton coast. He carried mail too — letters between families separated by water, before the war closed the channels. He said a letter was the only cargo that got heavier the longer you carried it." A pause. "He was hanged by a privateer crew for refusing to hand over a satchel. Twelve letters. None of them worth a life. All of them worth more than that, to the people waiting."

I said nothing. Some histories you receive standing still.

"So when your family's packets came to me — a boy with a boat and no questions — I understood the trade I was in. And when I delivered your mother's last letter to Tomas in Genoa, the spring before the war, he made me promise something." Elias's jaw tightened. "He said: *if anything happens to me, and anything reaches my sister, see it to her yourself. Not by proxy. Yourself.*"

"He knew he might need you."

"He knew the world he lived in." Elias turned to face me fully, and for once he didn't disguise anything in his expression. "I forgot the promise for years. Promises made in peacetime are easy to forget; they're written in a currency that stops circulating. But when the intermediary described the addressee — a woman traveling alone, careful hands, checks her seals twice — I knew it was you before he said the name. And the promise woke up."

"And now it binds you."

"Now it binds me." He said it without drama, the way a man admits to a debt. "Not to you. To him. There's a difference, and you should keep it, because men bound to promises are reliable and men bound to women are dangerous."

"I wouldn't dream of confusing them."

"See that you don't."

But neither of us quite managed to make it cold. The afternoon light lay across the terraces like spilled honey, and the mules plodded, and something had quietly changed hands between us that neither of us had agreed to sell.

* * *

Near dusk the road crested a final rise and the land opened into a shallow valley, and there it stood: the windmill, or what remained of it. The tower was waist-high in places, shoulder-high in others, its cap gone, its arms long fallen — one lying across the grass like a felled giant's bone. Wild fennel grew through the doorway. The stone was the same honey-colored limestone as everything on this island, the color of old paper, of things that had waited a long time to be read.

Elias reined in beside me and did not move toward it.

"We camp here or we go in now," he said. "Not both. If someone's watching it, they've watched it since morning, and they'll know whether we approach like visitors or like people arriving home."

I studied the ruin. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

"Tomas and I played here once," I heard myself say. "When I was twelve. Our ship put in at Mahón for repairs — my father thought the island neutral enough. Tomas dared me to climb the fallen arm. I got halfway and froze, and he climbed up and sat with me until I stopped shaking, and then we climbed down together and never told anyone." I dismounted. "He wrote neatly even then. Underlined the dates twice."

Elias watched me walk toward the ruin, and I felt his eyes on my back, weighing — ounces and hours, the only currency I'd ever trusted.

"Mirabel." His voice was low. "If this is a trap—"

"Then it's been aimed at me since Cádiz, and walking slower won't turn the blade." I stepped through the fennel, into the mill's shadow. "Stay at the mules. If I scream, ride."

"For the record," he called softly, "I'm not going to ride."

Inside, the air smelled of dust and wild thyme and something faintly metallic. The last of the light fell through the broken crown of the tower and laid a pale column across the floor — and in the center of that column, placed with a precision that made my chest seize, sat a small sealed box, weighted with a stone.

On the stone, written in ink that had not faded, in a hand I would have known blindfolded, with the sevens crossed and the date underlined twice:

*For Mirabel. The wind remembers.*
