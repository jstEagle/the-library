## 8. What the Mill Remembered

The windmill stood on the last shoulder of the ridge before the land fell away toward the sea, and it had been dead longer than I had been alive.

Its tower was a broken tooth against the sky, limestone gone the color of old bone, the cap torn away so that the interior opened to heaven like a skull whose top had been lifted to see what it was thinking. One sail-arm remained, half its length, hanging down the western wall like an amputated arm left in place out of courtesy. The terraces below climbed the hill in gray-green steps, olive trees older than every government that had ever claimed them, and the road we had come by was already invisible behind us — which Elias said was the point, and which I chose to believe because the alternative was turning back.

We had walked since before dawn. My boots were wrong for this country — diplomatic boots, made for marble floors and the short distance from carriage to consulate — and my feet had told me so for two hours with increasing bluntness. I did not say anything about it. Complaining is a currency and I had resolved years ago not to spend where I could not afford it.

Elias Vane stopped at the base of the terrace wall and looked up at the ruin without any expression I could read. He had that talent, or defect: a face like a harbor at dusk, all surface and no confession.

"You're certain of the hour?" he said.

"The letter said noon."

"Letters lie."

"This one was written in my brother's hand," I said. "It may be forged, it may be bait, but it isn't careless."

He considered that. "Careless people die fast. Careful ones die slowly, and worse." He started up the slope. "Come on, then. Noon won't wait for us and neither will whoever's watching us climb."

I let him go first. Not from cowardice. From arithmetic. If there was a rifle on this hillside, it wanted me alive; whatever the letter's purpose, killing me would defeat it. The same logic protected him less, and he knew it, and he climbed anyway without asking me whether I'd decided his life was worth more than mine today. There was something in that I was beginning to find harder and harder to file under *professional*.

The mill door was a slab of driftwood hung on leather hinges gone stiff as cartilage. Elias shouldered it open. Inside, the light came down through the missing cap in a single column, thick with dust motes, and struck the floor where the grinding stones sat under a century of bird droppings and straw. The air smelled of pigeon, sun-baked stone, and something else — tallow. Fresh tallow. Someone had burned a candle here within days.

I held up a hand. He stopped.

On the lower stone, centered in the column of light with a precision that made my skin prickle, lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred cord. Beside it, weighted under a pebble, a folded paper.

No Tomas. No figure waiting in the shadow of the stair, no brother stepping out of the dark with his terrible grin and his apology already rehearsed. Just paper, arranged like an exhibit. Arranged by someone who knew exactly how long I would stand in the doorway before entering, and who wanted me to see it whole before I touched it.

"He's not here," Elias said quietly.

"I can see he's not here." My voice came out level. I was almost proud of that. "Read nothing. Touch nothing until I've looked."

"You're the principal," he said, and leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, deliberately making himself furniture. It was, I understood even then, a kind of gift. A witness who agrees not to witness.

I crossed the floor. The pebble came off the folded paper easily. The paper was one page, unsigned, written in French in a fair commercial hand — a clerk's hand, trained, anonymous.

*Mademoiselle Crane. Your brother is alive. This is the only sentence in this letter that is free.*

Below it, terms. Not demands, exactly — demands are crude, and whoever wrote this was not crude. An invitation. A proposal that the consul's dispatches, currently sealed against my breast in oilskin the way my forged papers were sewn into my coat, be transferred to persons who would then guarantee Tomas Crane's safe delivery, unharmed, to a neutral port, within the month. A time and a place for reply. A warning, phrased with bureaucratic tenderness, about the consequences of involving garrison authorities, consular authorities, or "the gentleman who accompanied you inland."

They knew about Elias. Of course they did. They had probably hired him themselves, or hired the man who hired him, and the chain of intermediaries had been designed — I saw it now with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own autopsy — to deliver exactly one person to this hillside carrying exactly one thing.

*The trap was aimed at me all along.* The thought arrived without shock. Shock is for surprises, and some part of me had known since Port Mahon that a drowned man does not write letters, that grief arriving by courier is merchandise, that hope is the most reliable bait ever compounded. They had not lured me with my brother. They had lured me with my love for him, which is a different commodity, and worth more, and infinitely more reliable.

I set the letter down on the stone. Then I picked up the packet.

The oilcloth was stiff with age and soft with handling. The cord was tied in a particular way — a double hitch with the working end tucked through twice, a habit Tomas had learned from our father and never lost, a knot so characteristic that our mother used to say she could identify his parcels blindfolded. Whoever had wrapped this had either watched Tomas tie knots for a very long time or had been given the packet by Tomas himself, bound and sealed on his own terms.

Inside the oilcloth: a second layer, waxed linen. Inside that: a thick sheaf of papers sewn into a cover of gray board, sealed along three edges with wax stamped by a signet I recognized — not a family crest; we had none; but a small brass stamp shaped like a lighthouse that Tomas had bought in a Lisbon market when he was nineteen and used thereafter for everything from love letters to grocery lists, because Tomas believed solemnity was best carried lightly.

The seal was unbroken.

He had written this himself. Sealed it himself. Given it — to whom? To his keepers, to pass along as proof of life? Or smuggled it out through some channel his captors thought they controlled? Either way, the message underneath the faction's careful invitation was unmistakable: *I am alive, I can write, I can seal, and I am telling you things they do not know I am telling you.*

My hands were not steady. I noticed them the way one notices weather in another country — observed, irrelevant. Relief and fury had braided themselves somewhere behind my sternum into a single cord pulled so tight it hummed. Alive. Alive, and captive, and being spent as coin by men who measured the harbor's neutrality in ounces of paper, exactly as I measured everything, exactly as I had taught myself to do so that I would never be the thing weighed.

I sat down on the lower millstone with the sealed confession in my lap and laughed once, briefly, a sound with no humor in it, and Elias pushed off the doorframe immediately.

"Well?"

"They offered a trade." I kept my voice flat and found I couldn't sustain it. "The consul's dispatches for Tomas. Delivery guaranteed, terms generous, deadline firm. Very reasonable. Very professional." I looked up at him. "They also asked, politely, that I not involve the gentleman accompanying me."

"Flattering," he said. "Did they mention what happens if you refuse?"

"No. Refusal doesn't require description. That's the elegance of it." I turned the gray cover over in my hands. The lighthouse stamp gleamed dully in the column of light. "There's a confession here. Sealed by him. Written by him. And I cannot open it."

"Why not?"

"Because the wax is addressed to circumstances that haven't happened yet." I heard myself say it and realized it was true. "Tomas seals things conditionally. Always has. If this is meant for me, there will be a condition on the outside — a word, a cipher, something that tells me when breaking it serves him rather than them."

I examined the edges. Along the bottom hem of the cover, worked into the sewing thread in tiny cross-stitch, were characters. Numbers. A string of them, twenty-some, meaningless to anyone who didn't know what they keyed to.

And I knew. God help me, I knew. A childhood cipher, built one wet winter between a boy of twelve and a girl of nine, refined over years of intercepted diaries and contraband sweets, abandoned when Tomas went to sea and revived, secretly, shamefully, whenever he wrote to me from ports our parents weren't meant to know about. A cipher that existed nowhere on paper, that lived only in two heads, one of which had been officially underwater for fourteen months.

Which meant the numbers were either a genuine key waiting for the one other living mind that held the lock —

— or they were copied from stolen correspondence by people who had the letters but not the childhood, hoping I would break the seal and thereby authenticate their forgery for them, or reveal myself, or both.

I closed my eyes. The mill turned around me in the dark: pigeons shifting in the capless rafters, wind worrying the loose sail-cloth with a sound like someone patiently untying a knot.

"Mira," Elias said. Not *mademoiselle*. Not *Crane*. He'd begun saying it two days ago on the windmill road, after the third layer of our cover stories had come off, and I had not corrected him, and I was not going to.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. Let me think."

"Take your time. We have until the light goes, and then we don't."

I thought about Tomas at twelve, cross-legged on the nursery floor, explaining to his little sister that a cipher was just a promise with mathematics in it — *you can tell anyone anything, Mira, as long as only the right person knows how to hear it*. I thought about the fact that he had never once, in all those years of secret letters, sent me a key I could not verify. There had always been a check. A word embedded in the plaintext, a nonsense phrase, something only the sender and receiver would recognize as deliberate — *the receipt*, he called it, *always demand a receipt*. Our father's rule for freight, stolen for secrets.

If these numbers were real, the confession inside would contain a receipt. Some detail, trivial and unforgeable, that no interrogator could guess and no forger would think to plant.

If the numbers were false, opening the seal cost me nothing except confirming to whoever watched this hillside — and someone was watching, I would have bet my commission on it, someone had watched us climb — that the sister was sentimental enough to be managed.

But there was a third possibility, the one that sat coldest in my stomach: that the seal was real, the key was real, and the entire apparatus — letter, offer, deadline — was built around the certainty that I would open it. That they wanted me to read my brother's confession. That whatever Tomas had written was itself part of the leverage, a document calibrated to move me precisely as far as they wished and no farther.

Three possibilities. No way to choose among them from inside this tower.

Except one.

"Elias," I said. "When you carried mail. In peacetime. Between us and Tomas."

He'd told me on the road — a boy running letters along the coast for a shilling a packet, before the war made such traffic felony. He had described our house to me with a stranger's accuracy: the blue door, the fig tree leaning over the garden wall, the way our mother paid him always in exact change because she distrusted owing.

"When Tomas gave you a sealed packet for me," I said, "did he ever tell you what to do if it wasn't collected?"

He was quiet for a moment. I watched him decide how much truth the moment could bear.

"Once," he said. "Only once. He gave me a packet and said if you didn't come for it within the season, I was to burn it unread, and he'd know I hadn't read it because—" He stopped.

"Because of the receipt," I said.

"Because there was a word inside he'd ask me about later. Said he'd know by my face." His mouth twisted. "I was eleven. I burned it. I still wonder sometimes what it said."

"So he'd trust a seal to a child and a match." I looked down at the gray cover, at the lighthouse, at the patient stitches of numbers. "Then he trusts this seal to me. Whatever's in it, he wrote it to survive capture — to be found, to be passed along, to reach me by whatever road his jailers allowed. He wouldn't leave a live document in enemy hands unless it was armored."

"Armored against them," Elias said. "Not necessarily against you."

"No," I agreed. "Perhaps not against me. Perhaps especially not against me." I stood. My knees protested; I ignored them. "We're leaving. Now, before the light fails and whoever owns this hillside decides noon was a suggestion."

"And the confession?"

I wrapped it back in waxed linen and oilcloth, retied the double hitch with the working end tucked twice — Tomas's knot, learned at our father's knee, performed now by his sister's hands — and stowed it inside my coat against the dispatches, so that the republic's orders and my brother's testimony lay together over my heart like two loaded pistols.

"It comes with us," I said. "Unopened. Until I can read it somewhere with walls, a lock, and no audience."

"That's going to make our hosts impatient."

"Our hosts," I said, "have been patient for fourteen months. They can manage another night." I took one last look around the mill — the stones, the light, the empty air where my brother should have been — and felt the fury settle into its permanent lodging, cool and specific and very well organized. "They wanted me to come here and be moved. Instead I've come here and taken inventory. Let's see which of us knows better what the goods are worth."

Elias held the driftwood door for me, and outside the afternoon had already begun to lean toward evening, and the olive terraces ran silver-gray down toward a sea I could no longer see but could smell, salt and resin and distance.

We were halfway down the first terrace when he caught my sleeve and pointed.

Down on the windmill road, small in the failing light, a man sat on a mule, motionless, watching the ruin above him with the stillness of someone being paid by the hour. He did not follow us. He did not need to. He simply noted our direction — toward Port Mahon, toward Consul Aubert, toward whatever I did next — and turned his mule with the unhurried confidence of a man whose report would arrive before we did.

They had wanted the dispatches aimed at me. Fine. But they had made one error, the same error every weigher makes eventually:

they had put the scale in my hands.

* * *

We reached the outskirts of Port Mahon after dark, entering separately as agreed, and I was three streets from the consulate, moving through the lantern-lit crush of the fish market's evening teardown, when I understood what the confession's stitched numbers had been trying to tell me all along.

Twenty-two figures. I had counted them in the mill without knowing why. Twenty-two — and the childhood cipher ran in alphabets of twenty-five.

Unless the key wasn't letters at all.

Unless it was weights. Ounces. The system Tomas and I had used for exactly one category of message, the category we had sworn never to use unless the world ended: *numbers disguised as measures*, the freight-code, father's ledger-code, the one cipher whose key changed with every shipment and lived only in the sender's memory.

Twenty-two ounces. The confession wasn't keyed to an alphabet.

It was keyed to a weight — and the last time Tomas and I had used it, the agreed weight had been the mass of a letter he mailed me from Cádiz the week before he sailed, a letter I had carried in my coat pocket across two seas, and destroyed, and remembered to the ounce.

Fourteen ounces of paper and wax.

Which meant the numbers in the stitching were wrong — deliberately wrong, altered by whoever had handled the packet — or they were right, and the rightness proved everything.

And I would not know which until I stood alone in a locked room with a balance scale, my memory, and whatever Consul Aubert had been doing while I was on the hillside learning that the republic I served had priced my brother like freight.

I turned the corner onto the consulate square and stopped.

Every window of the building was lit. At this hour, Aubert's household slept by ten and the chancery kept one candle. Tonight the facade burned with light from cellar to attic, and in the courtyard gate stood two soldiers of the garrison whom I had never seen posted there before, and standing between them, hat in hand, smiling his smooth consular smile at something or someone inside, was the purser from the leaky brig that had carried me to Port Mahon — the man who had counted my trunks twice.

He saw me across the square. He raised one finger, not quite a greeting, not quite an accusation.

And waited.
