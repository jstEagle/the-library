## 10. Salt and Cipher

The confession had defeated Elias for two days.

He'd tried every trick he knew, and he knew a fair number. The pages Tomas Crane had left in the windmill were written in columns of figures and half-letters, neat as a merchant's ledger, and they resisted him the way a locked door resists a shoulder. Elias had carried enough secret correspondence to know the shape of a cipher when he held one — this was no simple substitution, no book code keyed to scripture or almanac. It was something private. Something made.

Which meant it was made for someone.

He sat in the back room of the fisherman's house Mirabel had borrowed, a candle stub between them, the pages spread on a scrubbed table that still smelled of salt and scale. Rain worked at the shutters. Outside, Port Mahon went about its evening — cart wheels on limestone, a dog complaining at the harbor stairs, somewhere a fiddle tuning up for the season's last dances. A town that didn't know what was being weighed above it.

Mirabel hadn't spoken in an hour. She sat across from him with her hands flat on the table, staring at the figures as if she could shame them into sense. She'd been like that since the windmill: controlled, economical, every gesture rationed like hardtack. But he'd watched her composure crack once already — when she'd first seen her dead brother's hand — and he knew what lived under all that discipline now. It wasn't grief anymore. It was arithmetic. Every hour the pages stayed dark was an hour Tomas stayed wherever his captors kept him.

"Read it aloud again," she said. "The part before the figures."

Elias turned back to the first leaf. "It's the same. 'To M.C., if she comes alone and reads carefully. What follows is proof of who I am and where I am held. Forgive the disguise; they search everything, even memory.'" He looked up. "Then numbers. Rows of them."

"'Even memory,'" Mirabel repeated.

"Meaning?"

She didn't answer at first. Her eyes had gone somewhere else — not inward, exactly, but backward. Twenty years backward. He watched her fingers move against the tabletop, small unconscious motions, tracing shapes.

"Tomas couldn't keep secrets from me," she said finally. "Not real ones. When we were children, our father ran dispatches during the last war, and he forbade us to write anything down that mattered. So Tomas invented a way around it." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "A cipher only two people ever learned. We used it for letters between our houses — nonsense to everyone else, including Father. He called it our salt-and-sugar code because we invented it over the kitchen table while preserving plums."

"And you never used it since?"

"You don't use a childhood cipher in adult work. It's too small, too simple. Any professional cryptographer would strip it in an afternoon." She stopped. Her breath changed. "But that's the point, isn't it. He didn't want it secure from professionals. He wanted it readable by exactly one person, and unreadable by everyone else. Including whoever forced him to write this."

Elias felt the thing shift under his hands before he understood it — the same feeling as a tide changing, water moving without permission. "You're saying the figures are salt-and-sugar."

"I'm saying if my brother is alive and thinking clearly, then yes." She pulled the first page toward her. "Give me the candle."

* * *

What followed took three hours, and Elias spent most of them watching her instead of the paper.

The cipher itself, once she began explaining it, was almost embarrassingly simple — a number stood for a letter's position in a word, a word's position in a line, and the lines were counted off in sevens, because seven was how old Tomas had been when he invented it, and he'd never seen reason to change. But there were wrinkles. Reversed pairs for names. A doubled figure for emphasis. And one private mark — a small loop drawn above certain groups — that meant *this is true even if nothing else is*, which apparently ten-year-old Tomas had considered a legally binding clause.

"He was a strange child," Elias said.

"He was precise," Mirabel said, without looking up. "There's a difference."

Letter by letter, the rows of figures gave way to words. Elias copied out the plaintext in his own hand, a second copy, because the first might have to be destroyed at any moment and neither of them said so aloud. The candle burned down twice. The rain stopped and started. And the confession came up out of the salt like a body surfacing.

*They believe I am their instrument. I let them think so. Listen carefully.*

*The faction holding me calls themselves the Friends of Order. They are not friends of anyone. They mean to force the abandonment of Port Mahon — not capture it, abandon it — and use its surrender to buy their own terms with the coalition. The consul knows. Aubert has been corresponding with them since spring. My value to them is that I can authenticate documents. Your value is that you carry the documents themselves.*

*Do not deliver your dispatches. Whatever they contain, whoever ordered them, understand that the moment they leave your hands the town dies by paperwork instead of shot. Burn them if you must. I would rather be hanged for a burned order than live as the man whose signature emptied a harbor.*

*They will offer you an exchange. Me for the dispatches. Take the meeting anyway. I know how to make myself expensive. Insist on Cala Figuera — the north path in, past the old lime kiln. There is a cave below the kiln. If the exchange turns, that is where you run, and I will find you or die trying, which is more than I can say for the republic.*

Elias read it through twice after Mirabel finished transcribing. Then a third time, slower, letting each sentence land.

"Well," he said. "That settles who he is."

"It settles more than that." Her voice was very level, which he was learning meant the opposite of calm. "Aubert. He named Aubert."

"He did."

"The consul I delivered my credentials to. The man who shook my hand and told me the harbor would hold until relieved." She pushed back from the table and stood, and for a moment Elias thought she was going to put her fist through the wall. She didn't. She walked to the window instead, parted the shutter a finger's width, and looked out at the lights of the town she'd been sent to save or bury, depending on whose account you believed.

"You've known men like him," she said. "In your trade."

"I've been men like him," Elias said. "For money, and never quite that far. There's always a floor somewhere. Aubert sold his and kept walking."

"How much do you imagine he was paid?"

"Enough. It's always enough. That's the whole genius of it — treason doesn't require monsters, just men who've done the sums." He gathered the transcription and squared its edges. "The question isn't Aubert's price. The question is yours. You heard what your brother wrote. Burn the dispatches. Those are the sealed orders you crossed a war to protect, and your own brother — the man you came within a mile of meeting tonight — just told you to feed them to a stove."

"I heard it."

"So what's the plan? Because I'll tell you mine, such as it is. We take the north path tomorrow night, we scout the kiln and the cave, and we learn the ground before anyone asks us to fight on it. Then we send word accepting the exchange — through channels they don't control, which means channels I control. And when the day comes, we go in expecting treachery, positioned for treachery, and we walk out with your brother whether the Friends of Order approve or not."

She turned from the window. The candlelight caught the edge of her face, the rest left in shadow, and Elias understood suddenly that he was looking at a woman deciding something, and that the decision was not about tactics.

"Why?" she asked. "Why are you doing this?"

"You paid me to guide you to a windmill. I'm renegotiating upward."

"Don't joke. Not about this." She came back to the table but didn't sit. "You've been paid since the moment you landed. Everything you've done since could still be filed under carriage and delivery. This can't. What I'm asking — what my brother's letter asks — puts you past the point where money explains you. So tell me why, and tell me true, because I need to know what I can spend."

The rain had started again, soft against the shutters. Down in the harbor a ship's bell rang the hour, muffled, counting out time the way it always did, indifferent to what the year intended to do with its remaining days.

Elias thought about lying, and found he'd lost the knack somewhere on the windmill road.

"When I was younger than you were when your father forbade you to write things down," he said, "I carried letters between two houses that weren't supposed to correspond. A family on the coast here — the father exiled, the mother staying put, sons scattered. One son wrote from enemy territory, and the letters went through me because I was cheap and I was quiet and nobody searched a boy selling rope." He watched her face change. "You know this story."

"Tomas mentioned a courier. Once. Years ago. He never gave a name."

"Elias Vane. At your service." He spread his hands. "I carried those letters four years. I never read one. I told myself that was professionalism, and it was, mostly. But there was another reason. As long as I didn't read them, I could pretend I was carrying nothing — just paper, just weight, just freight. The day I read one, whatever was in it became mine too. The risk, the consequence, the meaning. All of it. Mine." He tapped the transcription. "Two days ago I read one. So it's mine now. That's the whole answer, and I know it isn't flattering, but it's true, and you said you needed true."

Mirabel sat down slowly across from him. For a long moment she studied him the way she'd studied the cipher — patiently, systematically, looking for the flaw in the construction.

"There was a promise," she said. "In the peacetime letters. Tomas wrote that you promised to look after the family's correspondence come what may. He was fourteen. He thought it was funny, making a grown smuggler swear an oath over preserved plums."

"He made me swear it over brandy, actually, when he was older, and he wasn't joking. Neither was I." Elias met her eyes. "Come what may, Mirabel. That was the phrase. I've been paid by a dozen masters since, and none of them ever used that phrase, so I assumed the contract had lapsed."

"And now?"

"Now the client's resurfaced, and I find the terms still stand." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Besides, I've read the letter. It's mine. Consequences and all."

Something in her shoulders loosened — a fraction, no more, but Elias had spent a life reading bodies in bad light and he knew release when he saw it. She reached across the table and took the transcription from under his hand, not pulling it away, just resting her fingers on it beside his, so that for a moment they were both holding the same piece of paper.

"Then here's what we spend," she said. "Tomorrow you find me a boat that can reach Cala Figuera and back unseen, and a man who'll crew it who hates Aubert more than he loves money. I'll draft the acceptance of exchange in cipher — salt-and-sugar, since apparently it's the only language in the Mediterranean my family trusts. And Elias—" she paused, and he braced for gratitude, which he'd have known how to deflect, "—when this ends, whatever way it ends, I intend to settle accounts honestly. All of them."

"I don't want settling. I want the story. When it's over, someone has to tell it right, and I've never trusted a woman with a commission to do that."

"Then you'll have to survive to check my work."

"That," Elias said, "is the first sensible strategic objective anyone's proposed all week."

* * *

They worked until the candle died and lit a second one, and then a third.

The plan assembled itself the way cargo gets lashed — awkwardly at first, then tight, then tight enough to trust. Mirabel composed the exchange message in her childhood cipher, and Elias watched her do it, and something about watching a professional spy write love letters in the alphabet of a plum-preserving kitchen undid him slightly, though he said nothing. She encoded Tomas's terms: Cala Figuera, the north path, the lime kiln. Three days hence, at slack water. One courier, unarmed, carrying nothing but the reply.

"They won't accept unarmed," Elias said.

"They'll accept it precisely because it's insulting. Insulting means sincere. Only liars negotiate politely." She sanded the page. "How does it reach them?"

"Through Ferran." He said the name reluctantly. "The harbormaster's clerk. He skims every message that crosses the customs house, and he owes me from a matter in 'ninety-six involving a bale of tobacco and the governor's wife. He'll pass it up the chain to the Friends of Order without knowing he's doing it, and he'll pocket enough to keep silent about having done it."

"You trust him?"

"I trust his greed and his fear, which between them cover most of a man. Yes. Within limits."

By the fourth hour they had the shape of it: the boat, the crewman, the message, the scouting of the kiln path by daylight disguised as lime merchants, and — Elias's own addition, the one Mirabel fought hardest — a signal arrangement for the day itself, lantern codes hung from the headland to tell the boat whether to wait or run.

"You're planning for the possibility I don't come back," she said.

"I'm planning for the possibility nobody comes back, and distinguishing us from the rocks afterward. It's different work." He held her gaze. "Your brother wrote *die trying*. I intend to amend that to *leave trying*. But I don't amend plans by pretending failure isn't on the menu."

She didn't argue further. That was the thing he was coming to respect about her, almost against his will — she argued to test a plan, not to win a point, and when a thing survived her testing she committed to it completely. Somewhere in the last two days the transaction between them had quietly changed currency. He kept catching himself thinking in terms of *we*.

Near dawn he slept two hours in a chair and woke to find her still working, copying the confession a third time, splitting the copies between hiding places — sewn into her coat lining next to the forged papers, rolled into the cork of a wine bottle, and one, at her insistence, in his keeping.

"If they take me," she said simply, "the evidence shouldn't travel with me."

"If they take you, I'm not keeping evidence. I'm finishing the job."

"Both can be true."

Outside, the sky over the harbor had begun to turn the grey of gun metal. A fishing boat was putting out early, its lamp swinging at the masthead, and Elias found himself watching it with an unfamiliar sensation — the sense of having chosen a side in a fight that hadn't officially started yet, knowing the bill would come due regardless of the outcome. He'd spent twenty years keeping both oars in the water. Now one was ashore, planted, and there was no un-planting it.

Ferran would carry the message by noon. The Friends of Order would have it by nightfall. And whatever they answered — yes, no, counteroffer, trap — would arrive inside three days, riding toward them across the same water where the year was running out.

Mirabel blew out the candle as full light came, unnecessary now, and stood at the window with her back to him.

"They'll say yes," she said. "Tomas taught them well enough to know I'll come. That's what makes it a trap."

"Then we'd best be better students than their teachers," Elias said, and went to find Ferran.
