## 11. An Enemy Declared

The notice came on a Tuesday, folded inside a diplomatic pouch that smelled of tar and official indifference.

Mirabel was in the consulate's back office, reconciling Aubert's correspondence against her own ledger of dispatches, when the clerk Ferro brought it up on a salver as if it were a dish at table. He set it down without meeting her eyes. That was the first sign. Clerks who had looked at her directly for three weeks — the polite, assessing looks of men cataloguing a stranger's value — now studied the grain of the desk, the floorboards, their own knuckles.

She waited until his footsteps faded on the limestone stair before she broke the seal.

The paper inside was printed, not written. Printed notices were cheaper than conscience. It bore the double-headed eagle of the Admiralty court and ran to four paragraphs of dense legal French, but she read the substance in the first six lines:

*Whereas one Tomas Crane, formerly an officer of the Republic's naval service, reported lost at sea in the year 1796, is now known to be living and operating within territories hostile to the Republic; and whereas said Crane has been formally designated an agent of enemy powers by decree of the Tribunal of Marine Affairs; be it therefore declared that all intercourse with the said Crane, whether by letter, message, goods, money, or other means, constitutes treason against the Republic, punishable by death without appeal...*

Death without appeal. The words sat on the page with the flat confidence of type that had been set a hundred times before for a hundred other names.

She read the rest anyway. There was a clause about property forfeiture — her family's small house in Rochelle would pass to the state, though there was hardly anything in it worth taking but her mother's mirrors and her father's charts. There was a clause extending liability to *any person knowingly aiding or abetting*, which meant Elias, which meant the fisherman who had rowed them up the coast, which meant, if the tribunal cared to stretch it, anyone who had ever sold Tomas bread.

And there was a final paragraph, unnumbered, in a different hand — inked, not printed, the letters narrow and precise:

*Mme. Crane is requested to attend upon the Consul at her earliest convenience.*

Requested. Not summoned. The distinction was its own message: this was to look like courtesy because it could not afford to look like what it was.

She folded the notice along its original creases and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the desk, feeling the harbor sounds come through the window — the gulls, the block-and-tackle creak of some distant crane, a bell buoy tolling its idiot patience. Three weeks ago these sounds had been the texture of a job. Now they were the sounds of a place where her brother's name was a hanging offense, and hers was printed two paragraphs beneath it in everything but fact.

*All intercourse.* She thought of the ciphered confession hidden in the false bottom of her writing case, twenty pages of her brother's careful hand. She thought of Elias, who had carried those pages across two borders and a war zone for pay he had since, in every way that mattered, refused to treat as payment. She thought of the windmill road, of olive terraces silvering in the wind, of the hour when a stranger's cover story had come apart in her hands like wet paper and something truer had been underneath.

Treason, then. She turned the word over. It weighed less than she expected. Perhaps because she had been carrying it since the windmill, unnamed, and naming a thing only ever costs what you were already paying.

She put on her coat and went down to see the Consul.

* * *

Aubert received her in the map room, which she had learned was where he conducted anything he did not want witnessed. No clerks here, no Ferro with his salver. Just the long table, the charts of the Balearic approaches weighted at the corners with brass objects whose only function was weight, and the Consul himself standing at the window with his back half-turned, watching the harbor as though it owed him an explanation.

"Madame Crane." He gestured her forward without turning fully. "You have seen the morning's pouch."

"I have."

"And you understand what it says."

"It says my brother is alive," Mirabel said, "and that being alive is now a crime."

Aubert smiled — briefly, without warmth, the smile of a man acknowledging a point scored in a game whose rules he had written. "It says rather more than that." He moved to the table and drew from beneath a chart a second paper, this one handwritten, unsigned. He did not offer it to her. He held it, angled so she could read it upside down, which was itself a kind of theater: *I trust you enough to show you, not enough to give you.*

"The Tribunal is not foolish," he said. "They know a man does not survive three years in enemy territory on charm. They know he had help getting out whatever he needed out. And they know — " he let the pause do its work — "that the person best positioned to find Tomas Crane in this harbor shares his name and half his face."

"You are asking me to inform on him."

"I am asking you to do your duty." Aubert set the paper down at last and smoothed it flat with two fingers, a small fussy gesture that contradicted everything else about him. "You took an oath, Madame. So did he. One of you has kept it."

"One of us knows what the oath was actually for."

The Consul's eyes came up sharply. For a moment the mask of the tired administrator slipped and she saw what lived underneath: a man doing arithmetic. Sums of ships, sums of lives, sums of his own skin. She had seen that arithmetic before, in other capitals, in other crises, and she had always told herself the people doing it were exceptions. The rot in one barrel. Now she stood in a room where the whole vintage smelled the same.

"Sit down," Aubert said.

"I prefer to stand."

"Sit *down*." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. She sat. He remained standing, pacing the length of the map table, and when he spoke again his voice had regained its reasonable register, the one he used for trade disputes and quarantine quarrels. "Let me describe your situation plainly, because I think you have been describing it to yourself romantically, and romance is a luxury neither of us can afford.

"You are alone in a city where you know perhaps forty people, and perhaps four of them would cross the street to help you. Your brother is a condemned man. Anyone who aids him joins him. You have no garrison, no fleet, no government behind you that will lift a finger past the end of next month — " he tapped the map, the Balearics, the whole small doomed archipelago of the Republic's Mediterranean presence — "because the government that sent you may not exist by spring. These are facts. I deal in facts. So must you.

"Now." He stopped pacing. "Here is what I am authorized to offer."

*Authorized.* By whom, she wondered. By a tribunal? By whatever faction held Tomas? By himself, in a room with the door shut, wearing authority like a borrowed coat?

"A full pardon for your brother," Aubert said, "conditional on his surrender. A pardon for yourself, conditional on your assistance in obtaining it. Safe passage home for both of you. Restoration of property. In short, Madame Crane: your family, intact, alive, and forgiven — in exchange for one meeting arranged at a time and place of our choosing, at which my people will be present."

"And if he resists?"

"He will not resist. He is your brother. He will come because you ask him."

"That is not an answer to my question."

"It is the only answer that matters." Aubert leaned both hands on the table and regarded her across the spread charts with something that in a kinder man might have been pity. "You believe you love him. Very well. Love him. Bring him in alive, and love him for forty years in Rochelle with a pension and a garden. Refuse, and love him for exactly as long as it takes my soldiers to find him — and they will find him, Madame, because I will turn this harbor inside out, and everything he loves will be evidence against him. Including you."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, Ferro's pen scratched at the day's correspondence, a sound like a small animal burrowing.

Mirabel looked at the map. Port Mahon's great landlocked harbor, deep enough for a fleet, guarded by forts whose guns she had counted on her first week — sixty-one serviceable, by her estimate, out of a hundred and forty mounted. The town huddled around it like a secret kept too long. She thought of the dispatches sewn into her coat lining, the ones she had not yet opened, weighing their ounces against their burn-time every night before sleep. She thought of what they might say, and of what Aubert knew they might say, and of why a man playing both sides of a war would want the sister of a traitor standing in his office holding them.

"You speak of my brother's treason," she said slowly. "Tell me about yours."

Something flickered across the Consul's face — not guilt; she doubted the organ still functioned — but calculation recalibrating. "I beg your pardon?"

"You meet with parties I cannot name. You receive visitors at night by the water gate. You have sent three coded messages this month that never passed through my ledger, which means they never passed through any ledger, which means they do not officially exist." She rose from the chair. Her voice stayed level; she had trained it level through worse rooms than this. "I am the Republic's courier in this port, Monsieur Aubert. Everything that moves through it moves through me, except the things you hide from me. So before we discuss what I owe the Republic, you will tell me what the Republic — or whatever is left of it that signs your papers — intends for this harbor."

For a long moment Aubert simply looked at her. Then he laughed, softly, and shook his head as if admiring a card trick.

"There it is," he said. "The famous Crane composure. Your brother had it too, you know. Right up until the day he stopped believing in what he was composing himself for." He gathered the unsigned paper from the table and slid it into his coat. "You have until the new year. Consider carefully. Loyalty, Madame, is like ballast — useful only while the ship holds together. After that it is just weight that drowns you."

He walked her to the door himself, a courtesy that felt like an escort.

"One more thing," he said, hand on the latch. "Your smuggler. Vane. He burned his safe-conduct papers yesterday — did you know? A man with no papers in a port under martial law is a man with no name and no protection. Whatever arrangement you imagine exists between you, understand that it exists outside the law now. Both of you. Outside everything." He opened the door onto the bright cold street. "Happy Christmas, Madame Crane. If you celebrate such things. I'm told your brother's faction does not."

* * *

She found Elias where she knew he would be — not because he was predictable but because he was the opposite, and the only place a man like that could safely be was somewhere he had told no one he would be. The old powder magazine above the Cala Figuera path, its roof fallen in, its walls thick enough to stop anything smaller than a siege gun. He was sitting on a powder keg older than both of them, cleaning a pistol he probably didn't need to clean, and when she came through the doorway he read her face in the space of a breath.

"They've named him," he said.

"They've named him." She sat down opposite, on a stone that still held the afternoon's thin warmth. "Enemy agent. Treason. Death without appeal. Property forfeit. Aid punishable by the same." She recited it flatly, the way she catalogued dispatches, because the alternative was to hear her own voice shake. "And Aubert offered me a pardon for us both if I deliver Tomas to a meeting of his choosing."

Elias set the pistol down. "What did you tell him?"

"That I'd consider it until the new year."

"Mirabel." Her name in his mouth had changed over these weeks, shed its formality syllable by syllable, until now it sounded less like an address than a hand reaching out. "You don't intend to consider it."

"No. But he believes I do, and belief is a weapon I can't afford to throw away yet." She looked at him across the ruined magazine, at this man who had crossed a sea carrying her brother's handwriting without knowing whose it was, who had burned his own papers rather than sell her, who was sitting in a derelict powder store with no name and no country because of a promise made in peacetime to a family that wasn't his. "Elias. They know about you. Aubert knows about you. There's no version of the next month where you walk out of this port clean."

"I stopped expecting clean some years ago." He picked the pistol up again, then set it down once more, deliberately, giving her his whole attention, which from Elias was rarer and more costly than gold. "Tell me what you're thinking. All of it. Even the parts you think I'll argue with."

So she told him. About the exchange she had begun to build in her head on the walk up from the consulate — herself for Tomas, brokered through the captors' own channels, using the harbor lantern codes she had spent three weeks learning from a fisherman's son who thought she was collecting folklore. About the arithmetic of it: one courier, compromised, expendable, traded for the one life the Republic had already priced at nothing and the one life she could not price at all.

She got as far as *the cove at Cala Figuera, after dark, before the year turns* before Elias stood up.

"No."

"Elias—"

"No." He said it without heat, which somehow made it heavier. "You want to hand yourself to people who hold your brother hostage and call it negotiation. You want to trust the lantern codes of a boy who reports to his uncle, who reports to God knows whom. You want to stand in a dark cove between armed men who have every reason to shoot first and count afterward." He paced to the broken wall and back. "I have watched you weigh things since the day I met you. Dispatches, risks, words. You weigh everything, Mirabel. Weigh this: what happens to Tomas if you're taken? What happens to the dispatches? What happens to—" He stopped himself on the edge of the sentence, jaw tight.

"Say it."

"What happens to the plan if the person holding it is in a cell?" His voice dropped. "You are not a coin to spend. You are the only reason any of this has a shape."

"And Tomas is?" She stood too. The magazine was small; three steps brought them close enough that the argument became something else without either of them naming it. "He is my brother. He wrote to me from hell because he believed I would come. Not send. Come. And you would have me weigh him against my safety as if they were amounts on a scale."

"Yes," Elias said. "Because that is exactly what they are, and pretending otherwise is how good people die stupidly."

The silence between them stretched until the harbor bells marked the quarter hour, far below, indifferent.

"Duty and love," Mirabel said finally, quietly. "My whole life I was told they pointed the same direction. Obedience was love of country; love of country was obedience. Nobody told me what to do on the day they point apart." She looked up at him. "You chose today, in Aubert's office terms. You burned your papers. Don't ask me to be more careful with my soul than you were with yours."

Elias exhaled slowly. When he spoke again, the argument had gone out of his voice and something more dangerous had replaced it. "Then we don't do it your way or my way. We do it a third way. You signal them — fine. You offer the exchange — fine. But we choose the ground, we choose the hour, and we assume betrayal from the first lantern to the last. Because Aubert heard you say you'd consider his offer, and a man like Aubert doesn't wait for answers. He prepares for both."

She hadn't told Aubert about the cove. She was certain she hadn't. But certainty, she had learned, was a currency that devalued daily in Port Mahon, and Elias's face said he had reached the same ledger line.

"New Year's Eve," she said. "Everything converges on New Year's Eve. Aubert's deadline. The faction's patience. The century itself, as if the calendar were conspiring with them."

"Then we have nine days," Elias said, "to make sure that when the century turns, we're the ones still standing on the right side of the water."

Outside the broken magazine, the sun was going down behind the ridge, and the first harbor lanterns were beginning to kindle along the mole — small, patient flames, waiting for someone who knew the code.
