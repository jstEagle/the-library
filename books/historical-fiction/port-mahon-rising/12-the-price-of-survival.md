## 12. The Price of Survival

Elias Vane had three rules for surviving in ports where everyone knew your face and half of them had a reason to hate it.

First: never sleep twice in the same bed. Second: never drink with a man you'd cheated, unless you'd cheated him well enough that he respected the work. Third: when someone from the old life found you, you counted to ten before saying anything, because the first thing out of your mouth was usually the thing that killed you.

He was on count seven when Silas Renner stepped out of the shadow of the customs shed and said his name.

"Vane." The word came across the quay like a thrown knife, flat side first. "Of all the harbors in all the world."

*Count to ten.* Elias finished the count, arranged his face into something between surprise and boredom, and turned around.

Renner hadn't changed much. He was still built like a capstan, still wore his hair cropped close enough that old scars showed through at the temples — one above each ear, souvenirs of a night off Algiers that Elias had been present for and had never once described accurately to anyone. He still dressed like money pretending to be modest: good wool coat, no lace, boots that cost more than a fisherman's boat. Ten years ago they'd run contraband together out of Genoa, splitting cargoes and betrayals down the middle. Then Renner had gone ashore and Elias had stayed on the water, and ashore was where men like Renner ended up when they stopped being smugglers and started being *employed*.

"You look prosperous," Elias said. "Somebody's paying you regular."

"Somebody is." Renner smiled with the bottom half of his face only. He glanced up the street, unhurried, cataloguing the passersby the way Elias himself did — habit worn into instinct. "Walk with me. The harbor air's doing nothing for my lungs."

It wasn't an invitation. It never was, with Renner. Elias fell into step beside him and let the man set the pace, which was slow, which meant he wanted to talk, which meant he wanted something. In Elias's experience, men who wanted to talk were more dangerous than men who wanted to fight, because fighting had rules.

They walked along the mole toward the quarantined hulks, past the fish market where the day's catch lay silvered and dying in shallow boxes. A gull screamed somewhere overhead. Port Mahon went on being beautiful in its limestone way, white walls stacked against the blue water, as if beauty had ever once protected anybody.

"I'll save us the theater," Renner said. "You came in on the *Sable Ann*, six weeks back, carrying letters nobody read. You've been seen with the Englishwoman. The consul's courier." He pronounced it carefully, savoring it. "The one who weighs her papers."

Elias kept his breathing even. "I carry things for people. That's the trade. You know the trade."

"I know you don't do anything without a price," Renner said. "That was always the difference between us, Vane. I could be bought. You could be *rented*." He stopped walking. Two of Aubert's garrison soldiers stood by the customs shed ahead, bored, muskets slung, watching the water instead of the street. Watching, or placed there. Elias decided it didn't matter which. "There's a man in the consulate who pays better than whoever you're working for now. And he has a problem that needs solving, and the solution has your name written on it in a hand you'd recognize."

"Say it plainly."

"The woman." Renner turned to face him fully. Up close there was something almost gentle in his expression, which was how you knew you were in real danger; Renner saved gentleness for the moments he most enjoyed. "She's sitting on dispatches that certain parties want very badly. Parties with reach. She's also, if the gossip holds, sitting on something else — a brother the republic says is dead and says is a traitor, depending on which clerk you ask. Either way, she's a loose thread, and loose threads get cut. The only question worth asking is who does the cutting and what it costs."

"And you're asking me to hold her still while the scissors come out."

"I'm offering you a clean exit." Renner's voice dropped, went almost confiding, which was worse than the threat had been. "Immunity. Full paper, signed under the consul's seal. Everything you've ever carried into this port, everything you've ever carried anywhere — forgiven. No questions waiting for you at any customs house between here and Trieste. You know what that's worth to a man with your history." He paused. "I know what it's worth. I've seen the ledgers, Vane. Your name appears in eleven of them."

The wind came off the harbor and carried the smell of tar and salt and dying fish. Elias looked out at the water, at the moored ships riding their reflections, and made himself think about the ledgers. Eleven of them. Probably accurate. There were ports he couldn't enter and captains who'd put him over the side in chains if they caught him aboard, and every year the list got longer while the list of people who'd take his work got shorter. Immunity wasn't freedom. Immunity was the closest thing to it a man like him would ever be offered, and Renner knew it, and the offer was real because Renner never wasted a lie on a man who could smell one.

"That's a generous price," Elias said.

"It's not a price. It's a gift." Renner smiled again, bottom half only. "The price is hers to pay."

* * *

They walked back along the mole without reaching agreement, because Renner understood that agreements needed time to rot properly. "Three days," he said at the corner by the chandlery. "Come to the consulate, ask for the under-secretary, say you've reconsidered. If you're smart, you'll come tonight." He clapped Elias on the shoulder with a hand like a mooring cleat. "It was good to see you. Genuinely. You were the best partner I ever had, and I've had to become the kind of man who says that honestly."

Then he walked away toward the consulate, and Elias stood in the street and felt the shape of the trap close around him, not snapping shut but settling, patient, like a net sinking through dark water.

Here was the arithmetic he'd lived by for twenty years. Every cargo had a weight, every job had a price, every loyalty ran exactly as far as the coin behind it. He'd told Mirabel as much, the night they'd struck their bargain on the road to the windmill — *I'm a hired hand, not a hero* — and she'd looked at him with those level gray eyes and said, *Good. Heroes die stupidly,* and he'd thought then that he'd met the only person alive who understood the trade as well as he did.

But she wasn't a hired hand anymore. Somewhere between the windmill and the broken magazine, between the confession in Tomas Crane's cipher and the nine days ticking down to New Year's Eve, she had crossed some line he hadn't seen her cross, and he had watched her do it, and he had — this was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed ballast — he had followed her across it without deciding to.

A man with nothing to lose takes the immunity. A man with nothing to lose always takes the immunity, because that's what having nothing to lose means: the scales tip wherever the coin falls. Elias stood in the street and tested himself against the arithmetic and found the scales wouldn't move.

He thought of her hands sorting dispatches by burn-time. He thought of her voice cracking, just once, just barely, when she'd recognized the handwriting on Renner's letters — the dead brother writing neat as a schoolmaster from inside enemy country. He thought of the promise he'd made ten years ago to a woman named Crane in a peacetime port, *I'll see your letters reach him*, a promise he'd kept so long he'd forgotten it was still in his pocket, and how it had turned out to be the only thing he'd ever carried that hadn't been for sale.

And underneath all of it, simpler and worse: he thought of the way she'd looked at him two nights ago, lamplight on her face, when she'd said *we have nine days* and meant *we*, and how he had heard it and answered it and known, even as the words left him, that he'd stopped being a carrier and started being cargo — that whatever happened in this harbor, part of him now went where she went, and no consul's seal could sign that away or buy it back.

Silas Renner had offered him survival. That was the joke of it. Renner thought he was selling the one thing every man wanted, and didn't understand he was offering Elias a choice between surviving and being *someone*, and that for the first time in twenty years the second thing weighed more.

* * *

His lodgings were a rented room above a ropewalk, chosen because the window overlooked two streets and the stairs creaked on the fourth step. He climbed them now, lit the lamp, and pulled his safe-conduct papers from the false seam in his trunk lining — the papers Renner hadn't mentioned, which meant Renner knew about them, which meant they were already worthless except as evidence.

They were good papers. He'd paid a forger in Naples a ruinous sum for them: neutral flags, reciprocal protections, a chain of signatures that would open doors from Barcelona to Corfu. He spread them on the table under the lamp and read them one last time, the way you'd look at the face of someone you were about to stop loving.

This was the whole architecture of his life, laid flat on the wood. Every border crossing, every escape hatch, every *not me, not here, I was never involved*. Twenty years of building exits. And the truth of it, the truth he'd been circling since Renner said *the woman* on the quay, was that every exit pointed away from the same place, and the same person, and that staying meant burning the map.

He thought about the alternative one more time, honestly, because he owed himself that much. Three days. Walk into the consulate, say the words, collect the seal. Mirabel would learn what he'd done within a week — she was the sort who learned everything — and she wouldn't weep about it. She'd recalculate. She'd weigh him at his true ounce-weight and set him aside, and she'd go on alone toward whatever was coming on New Year's Eve, and she'd probably survive it, because she was the most disciplined person he'd ever met. And he would live a long time afterward in ports where nobody knew his name, spending his immunity, and every harbor would look like every other harbor, and he would have his life back, whole and empty as a returned crate.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the papers, to the young man who'd written the first line of those ledgers twenty years ago. It came out steady, which surprised him. Most of the important things he'd ever said had come out steady; the shaking came after.

He folded the safe-conducts together, held them to the lamp flame, and watched them catch.

Paper burns differently depending on what's written on it — he'd learned that from her, from watching her catalog her dispatches by burn-time, ink flashing green where the vitriol bit. These burned slow and thorough, the heavy rag stock curling back on itself like a living thing trying to keep its shape. He held each one until the fire reached his fingertips and dropped it into the chipped basin on the washstand, and when the last corner flared and died he stirred the ash with a pencil stub until there was nothing left but gray.

Twenty years of exits, reduced to a spoonful of dust in a cracked bowl. He tipped it out the window into the wind coming off the harbor, and the wind took it, and that was that.

He felt lighter. He also felt, for the first time in his adult life, genuinely afraid — not the professional fear of a chased man, which was weather, but the other kind, the kind with a face and a name attached. He had something to lose now. That was the whole price of survival, paid in advance: you could keep yourself cheap and safe forever, or you could spend yourself on someone and live the rest of your days knowing exactly how much you had at stake. Renner had understood this once, long ago, and had chosen the other door. Elias watched the ash go and chose his.

Down in the ropewalk, the workers had knocked off for the evening. Out on the water the harbor lights were coming on one by one along the mole and the ramparts, and among them — he'd learned to read them these past weeks, the way you learn any language by needing it — certain lanterns were speaking in short, deliberate phrases to a point far out past the point.

Mirabel's code. Mirabel's negotiation, opening without him.

He pulled on his coat, checked the knife at his belt and the coins in his boot, and took the stairs fast, skipping the fourth step. Three days had just become fewer than that. Whatever answer she was signaling out into the dark, he intended to be standing next to her when it came back.
