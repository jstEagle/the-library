## 13. Night Signals

The lantern code came back to me the way grief does — all at once, in the middle of an ordinary task.

I was mending a hem by the window of the rented room above the chandlery, watching the harbor do what it always did after dark: arrange itself into sentences. Port Mahon's great landlocked bay was a page, and every ship on it wrote. A red light high on a mast meant quarantine. Two whites swinging from the mole meant the pilot was drunk again and everyone should mind their keels. Three short greens from a fishing smack meant herring, come tell the factor before dawn.

The navy had taught me the grammar years ago — not the navy exactly, but a lieutenant named Oakes who had taught it to me at a card table in Gibraltar while Tomas cheated him gently of his winnings. *Light is language, Miss Crane,* Oakes had said, tapping his pipe against the table edge. *Anyone can speak it. The trick is knowing who's listening.*

Tomas had laughed until he choked on his wine.

I put down the needle. I looked at the harbor for a long time.

Three nights earlier, a boy had pressed a folded scrap into my hand outside the fish market — no payment asked, no face I could describe afterward beyond *small, quick, frightened*. The paper held four words in a hand I didn't know: *Cala Figuera. Terms possible.* And beneath that, drawn small and precise, a sequence of lantern figures I recognized as the old signal-house code, the one used by the garrison before the garrison stopped mattering. An address. A channel. Someone wanted to talk to me the way ships talk — in light, across water, deniable to everyone.

They had my brother. They had told me so through the map at the windmill, through the sealed confession that Elias had cracked open with a childhood cipher, through every careful, patient line of it: *alive, useful, held*. And now they were offering terms.

Terms. As if Tomas were cargo. As if he were anything but my brother, alive, which meant the whole world had quietly rearranged itself around that single fact like iron filings around a lodestone.

I knew what they wanted before I finished the note. They wanted the dispatches. They wanted the sealed orders riding in their oilcloth wallet against my ribs, the ones Consul Aubert pretended did not exist and I had not yet found the courage to open. And they wanted them delivered by someone whose hands they could trust — or traded for something better than paper.

For three days I had done nothing. For three days I had carried the note next to the dispatches, two secrets pressed together like flints.

Now I set down the mending and began to count lights.

* * *

Elias found me on the flat roof of the chandlery an hour past midnight. I heard him on the stairs the way I'd learned to hear him — unhurried, deliberate, each footfall placed like a word he'd chosen in advance. He came up carrying two cups of the terrible chicory the landlady sold and stood looking at the array I'd built on the coping stones.

I had three lanterns. The shuttered kind sailors use, borrowed from the chandlery below with money and a lie about signaling a late brig. I'd arranged them in a row, dark for now, and beside them lay my notebook with the code written out in columns, and beneath the notebook, weighted with a pebble so the wind couldn't take it, the fish-market note.

He set the cups down without comment. He read the note. He read the notebook. He looked at the lanterns. Then he sat down slowly on an upturned crate, the way a man sits when sitting is easier than saying what he thinks.

"How long," he said, "have you been planning this without telling me?"

"Since the note." I kept my eyes on the water. "Planning isn't telling."

"No," he agreed. "It's the thing you do instead of telling." He picked up one of the shuttered lanterns, turned it over in his hands, weighed it. "You're going to answer them."

"I'm going to negotiate."

"With the people holding your brother hostage. From a rooftop, in code, alone." He set the lantern down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle something when you'd rather throw it. "Mirabel."

"They offered terms."

"They offered bait. There's a difference, and the difference is usually the width of a noose." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the moon caught the old scar along his jaw, the one he'd never explained and I'd never asked about. "Tell me your plan. All of it. Every step between lighting that lantern and walking away free."

"You know I don't have every step."

"I know you don't have *any* steps. I've watched you work for weeks. You measure everything — weight, burn-time, hours, exits. It's the first thing I ever liked about you." His voice did something complicated on the word *liked*, folded around something larger it was trying not to be. "And now you're about to announce yourself by light to an enemy whose faces you've never seen, over water where any boat can sit between you and them, and your entire contingency plan is — what? Faith?"

"My contingency plan," I said, "is that they need the dispatches more than they need me dead. Dead couriers carry nothing."

"Dead couriers' dispatches get *found*." He said it flatly, without heat, which was worse. "You think they haven't considered that? You think Aubert hasn't considered it? Half this town would sell your route to the consul for the price of a drink, and the other half would sell it to the faction for less. The moment you light those lanterns, you're not a courier anymore. You're a fixed point. Everything converges on fixed points."

"Then everything converges," I said. "That's rather the idea."

He went very still.

I made myself look at him then. That was the discipline of it — the same discipline as weighing dispatches, as memorizing ciphers, as sewing forged papers into coat linings. Look directly at the cost. Never let yourself glance away and call the blur a bargain.

"They have my brother," I said. "Alive. Confirmed twice now. They're holding him because he's worth more to them breathing, as leverage against me, against Aubert, against whoever wins whatever this is. Fine. Leverage cuts both ways. If they want the orders, and the orders are mine to give or keep, then there's a trade to be made. Me for him. Myself for Tomas. It's arithmetic, Elias. I've been doing it since Gibraltar."

"It's not arithmetic." He was on his feet now, and his voice had dropped into the register he used in bad weather, low and level, the voice that never rose because rising was a luxury. "Arithmetic balances. This doesn't. You give yourself to them, and then what? They have the sister *and* the brother *and* the dispatches. One transaction, total return. Why would they ever honor the exchange?"

"Because they'll want more trades later. Reputation is currency too — even among smugglers and factions. Especially among—"

"Don't." He said it quietly, and the quiet had a blade in it. "Don't explain my own trade to me. I spent eleven years learning exactly how much reputation buys and exactly how fast it burns. I once watched a captain in Trieste honor an exchange to the letter — man for man, coin counted twice — and then hang the freed man's rescuer off his own yardarm an hour later, because the rescuer had seen his face." He took a breath. "Honor is a story professionals tell amateurs. I am asking you, as a professional, not to buy the story."

"And I'm telling you—" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. I steadied. "I'm telling you I have read that confession six times. Our cipher. Our *childhood* cipher, Elias — the one we invented in the attic at Wexcombe when we were nine and twelve, counting primes on our fingers. He remembered it. After eight years, after drowning, after defection, after God knows what they've done to make him write what they wanted — he still signed it in the only ink that was ever truly ours." I gestured at the harbor, at all its patient writing. "He has been paying for me since before I knew he was alive. Every day they hold him is a day he's spending so that I won't have to. I will not let him spend another one buying something I can pay for myself."

"That's not payment, that's surrender dressed as sacrifice, and you know it—"

"Yes," I said. "Yes, I know it. I know exactly what it is. I've weighed it in ounces and hours like everything else, and here is the weight: if I do nothing, they keep him until he's worthless, and then — what do you imagine happens to leverage that stops being useful? If I run, we both run, and he hangs or drowns as a warning to whoever comes next. And if I trade myself—" I stopped. Made myself finish. "If I trade myself, there's a version of this where he walks out of Cala Figuera alive. That version exists. I've checked it the way I check everything. It's thin. It might not hold. But it exists, and no other version does, and I find — I find that I cannot un-see it now that I've seen it."

The wind moved across the roof and stirred the notebook pages. Below us the town slept, or performed sleeping; somewhere a dog was complaining at nothing; the mole light burned its steady idiot white.

Elias stood looking at me for a long time. When he spoke again, the argument had gone out of his voice and something rawer had replaced it, something he clearly resented having to say aloud.

"You want to know why I'm really arguing," he said. "Not the tactics. The tactics are sound — I'll grant you the tactics, they're better than half my plans. I'm arguing because I carried letters for your family for two years, Mirabel. Peacetime letters, Wexcombe to Lisbon, Lisbon to Wexcombe. I read none of them. I promised the boy who paid me — your brother, nineteen years old, paying out of an allowance he could barely afford — I promised him his sister would get every word, safe, every time. Twenty-two deliveries. I kept that promise with nothing at stake. Now the stakes are—" He stopped, started again. "I promised him. And the promise has grown teeth since then, because somewhere between the windmill road and tonight I—" 

He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. We had been not-finishing it for weeks, the two of us, building the sentence brick by brick and leaving it unfinished like an arch waiting for its keystone.

"So don't ask me to help you do this," he said finally. "Ask me to stop you. Because helping is easy and stopping is impossible, and I have spent the whole evening discovering that I cannot do the impossible, and I hate it."

* * *

We quarreled properly after that. Not the sharp, controlled exchanges of before but the real thing — loud enough that the landlady's cat fled down the stairwell, ugly enough that we said things calibrated to wound, because love under pressure stops choosing its ammunition. He called me a martyr with a ledger. I called him a mercenary who'd finally found merchandise he couldn't price. He said the republic had trained me to believe my life was the cheapest object in any negotiation, and I said — and this was the unforgivable one, the one that hung in the air between us like smoke — "Then it's fitting I spend it on the only thing about that republic worth anything."

Silence, after. Long silence. The harbor wrote on below us, indifferent, green and red and white.

It was Elias who broke it, the way he broke everything — deliberately, with his hands. He crossed the roof, picked up the first lantern, and began checking its wick with the thoroughness he gave to rigging.

"If we're doing this," he said, not looking at me, "we're doing it my way. Not yours. Yours involves you being brave in a straight line. Mine involves you being alive in a crooked one." He counted off on his fingers, already the professional again, already burying whatever the last hour had cost him. "One: you don't signal from here. The chandlery roof is known to me, which means it's known to others, which means it's burned. We signal from the old battery point past Sant Felip — abandoned, one approach, sightline straight down the bay to the signal house. Two: I sit offshore in the fishing boat with a glass on every hull between here and Cala Figuera. Anything moves wrong, I fire the boat's flare and you walk away mid-sentence. Mid-*word*. You do not finish the message. Promise me that. Swear it like a contract, because contracts are the only scripture either of us trusts."

"I swear it."

"Three: the terms you offer are not *me for him*. Not first. First you offer the location of the dispatches — false, somewhere that takes them two days to search. You sell them distance, not yourself. Time is the only commodity we have, and I intend to spend every hour of it building the extraction at the cove." He finally looked up. "And four — four, Mirabel, non-negotiable — you tell me the true fallback. The real last resort, the thing you're holding behind your teeth right now. Because I've watched you weigh things for weeks and I know there's a final entry in your ledger you haven't shown anyone. Show me. Or I walk to that battery alone tonight and cut every wick in your lanterns."

I looked at him. He looked back, patient as ballast, immovable as one.

"The true fallback," I said slowly, "is that if the exchange breaks — if they take me and refuse him — I've left instructions with the widow Ferrer for a packet to reach the Spanish admiral at Cartagena. Contents: the confession, the map, and a full account of Aubert's double-dealing. Enough to make Port Mahon too expensive to abandon and too hot for the faction to hold. It costs me everything and saves the town and probably him. It's the move I make if I'm taken. It's why they may hesitate to take me."

Something moved through his face — not approval, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding, in another person's ledger, entries in his own handwriting.

"There she is," he said softly. "There's the woman who memorizes cipher keys and burns them." He shook his head once, almost laughing, entirely without humor. "You understand you've just planned a betrayal of your own government in front of the man who was hired to watch you. Again."

"You weren't hired to watch me. You were hired to deliver letters."

"Everything is hiring, Mirabel. The only question is who's paying, and lately—" he picked up the second lantern, checked it, shuttered it, set it in line with the first, "—lately I seem to be working for deferred wages."

* * *

We rowed out past the mole at two in the morning, under a sky scoured clean by the tramontana, the town behind us going gold at its edges where the late taverns still burned. Elias handled the boat the way he handled everything — without flourish, without waste. At the old battery point he grounded us on shale, and I climbed the broken ramp with a lantern in each hand while he took the glass and the third light down to the rocks at the waterline, where he could watch the bay and fire his flare.

The signal house stood on the far shore, a black finger against the stars. Dark. Waiting. They had said *Cala Figuera, terms possible*, and they had given me the channel, and now the channel lay open in front of me like a door in a wall I'd been leaning on for weeks.

I opened the first shutter. Red. Held it five seconds. Closed it.

Across the water, nothing. The bay breathed. A night heron complained somewhere below me.

Then — a pause long enough to doubt everything — the signal house answered. White. White. Green.

*Understood. Proceed.*

My hands were steady. I noted that with distant interest, the way you note the behavior of someone else's body. Somewhere below me in a fishing boat, Elias was watching the same light, and I knew that behind the glass of his telescope his jaw was set like a cleat, and I knew that he had lost the argument and won the position, and I understood — with the cold clarity I saved for moments that mattered — that this was what love looked like when both people refused to be rescued: two people standing in different places, aimed at the same cost, each determined to pay it first.

I raised the second lantern and began to spell out the offer.

Across the bay, the signal house flickered once, twice —

— and then, far out on the black water, well south of where any honest answer should come from, a third light bloomed. Small. Moving. Where no light had any business being.

Through the dark, faint but unmistakable, came the crack of Elias's flare pistol.
