## 14. The Exchange at Cala Figuera

The lantern signal had been answered in under an hour, which told Mirabel everything about how close the watchers were. Three flashes from the mole, three from the dark water off Cala Figuera — a conversation conducted over their heads, in a language the harbor had used since before any of them were born.

Now she stood on the shingle of the cove with the sea working at her boots and Elias a pace behind her, and she counted what could be counted: two boats drawn up above the tide line, one hers, one his; a moon three days from full and generous with its light; the black teeth of the rocks at the cove's mouth where a third boat would come through, if it came.

"You're doing the thing," Elias said quietly.

"What thing?"

"Counting. Weighing. You do it when you're frightened. You did it on the road here — I watched you price every olive tree we passed."

"I wasn't frightened on the road."

"No," he said. "You were furious. This is different."

She didn't deny it. The wind came off the water carrying salt and the far-off smell of pitch fires from the town, where the year was already being prepared for its dying — lanterns strung along the bastions, wine casks rolled out onto the quays. Above them, Port Mahon celebrated a century's end that Mirabel intended to survive into the next one, though the arithmetic for that had never looked worse than it looked tonight.

The terms had come back with the second signal: *one for one. The sister for the brother. Midnight, Cala Figuera. Come armed or don't come — it changes nothing.*

She had read it twice and felt something in her chest that was not fear. It was closer to relief, which frightened her more than fear would have.

"They'll take you and kill him anyway," Elias said now, for the fourth time since the quarry road. "Or they'll take you and keep him as further leverage. Or they'll take you both and hand you to Aubert as a New Year's gift. There is no version of this where they honor the trade."

"There's a version where they try," Mirabel said. "And there's a version where I'm standing ten feet from my brother at midnight, and whatever happens after that, happens with us in the same place. That's worth something. You'd know that if you had a brother."

"I have clients."

"Worse luck for you."

He almost smiled. Then the smile went away, because the sound came: oars, muffled — cloth wrapped on the looms, someone who knew how to approach a coast quietly — and out of the black gap between the headland rocks slid a longboat, low in the water, riding heavy.

Mirabel's heart performed a betrayal all its own, hammering against the cage of her ribs like something trying to get out.

*Steady.* She made her hands still. She thought of dispatches weighed to the ounce, of burn-times measured in minutes, of a creed that had carried her across a winter sea: *carry little, trust less, and never let them see the scale move.*

A man stepped down into the shallows — broad, bearded, a pistol tucked openly in his belt, which was itself a kind of message. He waded ashore and stopped six paces off, appraising her the way a horse-trader appraises.

"Mademoiselle Crane." His Spanish was accented but good. "You brought no soldiers. Good. Neither did we." Which was a lie, because behind him the longboat held four silhouettes, and silhouettes holding muskets have a particular shape.

"And my brother?"

"He comes when you cross the line." He pointed with one finger at the wet shine of sand between them, an arbitrary border drawn by nobody. "You walk to us. He walks to your friend. Then everyone is unhappy and alive."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we row home and everyone explains themselves to their respective employers." He shrugged. "But you won't say no. I can see it. Family — it's a weakness, no? Everyone says so." Something in his tone suggested he found this genuinely puzzling rather than cruel, and that puzzled Mirabel more than menace would have.

She took one step forward. Elias's breath caught behind her.

"Mira." Her name, in Elias's mouth, in Tomas's old nickname for her — he'd learned it from letters he'd carried years ago, in peacetime, between a house full of children and a son at sea. It landed like a hand on her shoulder. "If you cross that line and they don't release him—"

"Then you do what we agreed."

"What we agreed is insane."

"What we agreed is the only thing left." She turned her head just enough to find his eyes in the dark. "Count it, Elias. Weigh it. I'm lighter than you think."

"That's not—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Go."

* * *

She crossed the line at half past eleven, boots dragging in wet sand, and the bearded man's men closed around her with the practiced gentleness of people handling cargo they've been paid not to damage. Someone bound her wrists in front of her with cord — quick, professional, not cruel. Through the gap of two shoulders she watched the longboat's stern, waiting for the shape that would climb out of it.

It took longer than it should have. When they finally brought him up, supporting him between two men, her first thought was not joy. Her first thought was *they said he could walk and he cannot,* and her second thought was the older, deeper one, the one that had kept her alive across a winter sea:

*That is my brother.*

Thinner. Bearded. One arm bound against his chest, the sleeve stiff with something darker than seawater. But the way he held his head — chin up, eyes moving, cataloguing exits even while barely upright — that was Tomas entire, that was the boy who had once walked the sea wall at Port Mahon at seven years old refusing to hold anyone's hand.

Their eyes met across forty feet of cove.

"Mira," he said. His voice carried. It cracked in the middle of her name and he let it crack, didn't try to hide it, and something in Mirabel's chest that had been clenched for two years came apart all at once.

"Tomas."

"Walk," he said to her, hoarse. "Don't talk. Walk. They want you looking at me — look at the boat instead, always watch the boat—"

The bearded man laughed. "Brothers and sisters. Always instructing each other." He gestured, and the exchange began: Mirabel forward, Tomas lurching forward, two lines converging toward the middle of the beach like slow tides.

It was going to work. Mirabel understood that with a strange, cold clarity as the distance closed — twenty feet, fifteen — it was actually going to work, which meant of course that it was not.

* * *

The first shot came from the headland.

It went high — a warning or bad aim, impossible to know — and then the cove came apart. Torches bloomed along the ridge above the rocks, a dozen of them, and the shapes around the torches wore the grey coats of garrison regulars, and the voice that rang down over the cove belonged unmistakably to Captain Ferran, Consul Aubert's own man, the one who signed the harbor passes and smiled too easily.

"CALA FIGUERA! In the name of the Republic and by order of Consul Aubert — NO ONE LEAVES THIS COVE!"

For one heartbeat, everyone on the beach was equally surprised, and that heartbeat was the most valuable commodity on the shore. Mirabel spent it well. She dropped — not dove, dropped, deadweight, letting her bound hands hit sand before her knees did — and the second volley passed over the space where her head had been, cracking into rock, kicking sparks off stone.

Around her the cove detonated into motion. The smugglers fired back up the slope without discipline or hope; the longboat crew shoved off into the shallows; somewhere a man screamed short and stopped. And through the smoke and torchlight came Tomas — not walking now, running, lurching, his bound arm useless and his free hand hauling a fallen pistol from a dead man's belt, covering ground toward her with the terrible economy of someone who has decided exactly how much body he can afford to spend.

She got her wrists up. "Tomas — knife — left boot—"

"I taught you that trick," he gasped, dropping beside her behind the upturned hull of the first boat, and sawed through her cords with two strokes. "Never could stop you using it."

"Bullets, Tomas. Focus on bullets."

Musketry rattled down the slope in uneven volleys. The smugglers were breaking — she could hear them going, boots on shingle, the longboat pulling hard for the gap in the rocks — and in a minute the garrison would realize the cove held only two targets instead of twenty.

Elias appeared out of the smoke like something summoned, flat along the ground, and threw himself behind the hull with them, breathing hard.

"The second boat," he said. "Below the point. Fifty yards. I moved it an hour ago in case of exactly this."

"In case of—" Mirabel reloaded Tomas's borrowed pistol with hands that shook only after the work was done. "You moved a boat."

"I'm a courier. Contingencies are the whole profession." He glanced at Tomas, and something passed between the two men — appraisal, recognition, the brief arithmetic of men deciding whether to trust each other with a woman's life between them. "Can you run?"

Tomas laughed — a raw, cracked, joyful sound, absurd under gunfire. "Can I run. Mira, who is this man?"

"Elias Vane. He carried your letters."

Tomas went very still for a moment. Even with blood on his face and garrison muskets chewing the hull above their heads, he looked at Elias with an expression Mirabel recognized from childhood — the look he gave anything that had touched the family and survived it.

"Then you're the promise," Tomas said. "I wrote to him about you."

"There's no time—" Mirabel started.

"There's never time, that's the whole condition of being alive." Tomas gripped her wrist, hard, and for a moment the gunfire receded and there was only the two of them in the lee of a rotting hull, two years of grief and fury and hope compressed into a grip. "Listen to me. Whatever you were told about why I left — it's worse than that. The orders you're carrying, Mira. Look at them. Before you deliver them, *look at them.*"

"I know," she said. "I've seen enough."

His eyes searched hers. "Then you know whose side you're on now."

"I knew before you did."

Another volley hammered the beach, closer. The hull above them split; splinters rained.

* * *

They ran in relays — Elias first, drawing fire along the shingle, then Mirabel hauling Tomas by his good arm, then Tomas waving them on with the pistol while he limped as rear guard, firing twice with a marksman's economy that bought them the point. The fifty yards to the hidden boat took a year apiece.

At the point, in the shadow of the rocks, Mirabel turned to count heads — an old habit, counting her siblings on a quay, Tomas always last, always dawdling — and saw him stagger, and saw the dark bloom spread high on his side, above the bound arm, and heard the shot a half-second too late.

He went down on one knee and got up anyway.

"No," Mirabel said, and it came out of her at full voice, louder than the gunfire, louder than the sea. She ran back through open ground — she would remember later that she had done this, that some part of her had simply declined to participate in caution — and got her shoulder under his arm, and together, with Elias firing her own pistol one-handed behind them, they reached the boat.

They got Tomas over the gunwale. Elias shoved off. Musketry chased them around the point and gave up, and then there was only the black water, the rising wind, and the sound of Mirabel's brother breathing in shallow, whistling pulls against her chest.

* * *

Halfway across to the fishing anchorage on the far side of the bay, with the town's New Year lanterns burning small and golden above the cliffs, Tomas opened his eyes.

"Mira." A whisper. Blood loss was taking its toll on his voice, but not — she noticed with a fierce, absurd pride — on his insolence. "Did you bring it? My confession. Did Vane give it to you?"

"It's safe."

"And the dispatches?"

She was silent a moment. Below them, in the hold of the little boat, wrapped in oilcloth, sat the sealed orders of the republic she had served for nine years.

"I haven't opened them," she said.

Tomas studied her face — her actual brother, alive, studying her face by lantern light while bleeding into the bilge — and then he did the thing he had done since she was six years old when she was lying badly: he waited.

"I will," she said finally. "Tomorrow. Tonight we get you somewhere a bullet can't finish what it started."

"Fisherman's cellar," Elias called softly from the tiller. "Cala Sant Esteve. A woman named Rosa owes me more than money. No one will look there tonight — the whole town's watching the year die."

Tomas managed something that was nearly a grin. "New Year's Eve. We always did celebrate loudly." His eyes were closing again. "Mira. Listen. What's in those orders — when you read it — don't blame yourself. Blame them. And then—" his grip found her hand, weak but deliberate, "—then decide what the republic deserves. Not what you swore to it. What it *deserves.*"

Above the cliffs, the first midnight rockets went up early — the town unable to wait, spilling gold fire over the harbor where the great ships rode at anchor, where the defenders slept, where the orders that would doom them all lay sealed in a courier's keeping.

Mirabel held her brother and watched the fireworks and did not see them. She was reading, in her mind, the weight of the oilcloth packet, and measuring it at last against everything else she carried.

By morning, one of those things would be ash.
