## 16. Orders to Burn

The consulate kept its seals in a cedar box the size of a hymnal, and Consul Aubert kept the key on a ribbon around his neck, and for eleven days Mirabel Crane had done nothing but look at both and wait.

Waiting was a discipline. She had learned it young, in her father's counting house, where the ledgers taught you that a number entered too soon was worse than no number at all. You waited until the figure was true. Then you wrote it down once, cleanly, and never touched it again.

She sat now in the small room behind the map room — hers since arrival, though it had never been called hers, only *the clerk's room*, as if she were furniture that could take dictation. Rain worked at the shutters. Below, in the harbor, the garrison's evening gun went off late, as it had gone off late all week, and nobody in authority seemed to notice or care. That was an answer too. Decay announced itself in small failures before it confessed in large ones.

On the desk before her lay two objects.

The first was Tomas's confession, decoded in Elias's careful hand over three nights of lamplight and bad wine: the republic intended to yield Port Mahon to the enemy faction as the price of a separate peace, and the garrison — four hundred men, most of them local, most of them conscripted from the very streets outside these windows — would be surrendered with the harbor, their officers arrested as examples, their names entered into a ledger of men whose loyalty had outlived its usefulness.

The second object was her dispatch case.

It had crossed the Atlantic and half the Mediterranean sewn into her coat lining, weighed against her spare linen on a purser's scale in a leaking brig, carried through customs under a smile she had rehearsed in a mirror. She had catalogued its contents by weight and burn-time the way other women catalogued linens. She had memorized one cipher key and fed another to the sea. She had built her whole creed on the principle that paper was weight and weight was danger and the courier who forgot it died of it.

And in eleven days she had never once broken the seal on her own orders.

Not because she couldn't. The seal was wax and the wax was wax; her thumb could have opened it the first night. She hadn't because the orders were the last thing in the world that still made sense. As long as they stayed sealed, she was a courier. A courier had a task, a route, a destination. A courier did not have to decide anything. She had told herself this was discipline. She had told herself it was protocol — sealed orders opened only upon confirmed delivery conditions, per regulation, per instruction, per the small print of a service that had raised her since her brother drowned and left her nothing else to belong to.

She knew now it was neither. It was fear. She had been afraid that once she read them, she would know.

Elias had said it plainly, three nights ago, in the fisherman's cellar where Tomas lay feverish: *You already know what's in there. You've known since the windmill. You're just not ready to hold it.*

He'd been right, which was the most irritating thing about him.

* * *

Aubert found her at dusk.

He came into the clerk's room without knocking, which was his habit and his message both — the consul did not knock in his own house, and therefore she was not, in any way that mattered, mistress of even this small square of it. He carried a decanter and two glasses, which was worse. Aubert's kindnesses always arrived pre-loaded.

"Mademoiselle Crane." He set the glasses down. "You look like a woman who has not slept."

"I sleep."

"You sleep like a soldier. In snatches, armed." He poured. "I have news from the mainland. Our friends across the water are prepared to conclude terms within the fortnight. There is a ceremony planned. Signatures. Very dignified." He smiled his dry, administrative smile. "After which, I am given to understand, certain irregular personnel will be regularized. Arrested, processed, exchanged. Order restored."

"Tomas."

"Your brother among them, yes. Unless." He let the word sit in the rain-noise. "Unless the exchange you attempted at Cala Figuera is concluded properly, through me, with my mediation. I can deliver him to you, mademoiselle. Alive, unmarked, on a boat. All I require—"

"—is the dispatches."

"—is your commission discharged," he said smoothly. "Your orders executed. That is all any of us require of you. It is why you came. It is the only reason you exist here." He slid the second glass toward her. "You have carried those papers across an ocean and through a war to protect them. Surely you will not tell me you do not know what they contain."

She looked at him then, fully, the way she had learned to look at purser's scales and forged passports — reading the thing itself rather than the story wrapped around it. And Aubert, to his credit, held the look without flinching, because he believed he had already won. That was the detail she filed away: he believed it. Men who believe they've won stop counting exits.

"I'll open them tonight," she said.

Something eased in his face, a fraction, the way a ledger eases when a debt is acknowledged. "Sensible. I'll expect your confirmation by morning." At the door he paused. "Mirabel. Whatever you find in there — remember that you found it *after*. After is a country with no extradition. No one can hang you for what you learned too late."

Then he was gone, and she was alone with the decanter he'd poured and not drunk from, and the case, and the rain.

* * *

She waited until full dark. Not for secrecy — the house had ears, but the ears belonged to Aubert and Aubert wanted her to open it. She waited because the act deserved a kind of ceremony, and ceremony was the last luxury of people about to lose something.

She locked the door. She lit two candles instead of one, so that no shadow could hide a line of text from her. She took out her knife — the small one, the honest one, the one that had never been used for anything but cutting twine and bread — and she broke the seal on her own orders the way you break a fever: quickly, because the slow way is worse.

The wax parted. The paper inside was fine stock, official watermark, the republic's arms pressed soft and gray. Her own name was written on the outer leaf in the Secretary's hand — *To M. Crane, Courier First Class, with confidence* — and beneath it, the phrase they used for the worst assignments, the phrase she had seen twice before in her career and both times on documents that ended careers or lives:

*To be delivered entire, unaltered, and unread.*

She unfolded it.

The orders ran to four pages. She read them standing up, because sitting felt like agreement.

Page one was the fiction, and it was beautifully made. Confirmation of credentials. Commendation of her service. Instruction to present the enclosed annex to Consul Aubert upon verification of harbor security — *harbor security*, the phrase doing its quiet work, meaning: once the town cannot resist. She had delivered credentials to this man eleven days ago believing herself the republic's hand extended. She had been its blindfold instead.

Page two was the annex. The dispatches themselves. She read the numbers twice because the numbers refused to be believed the first time: withdrawal schedules for the fleet, timed to the hour. Artillery inventories marked *to be rendered inoperable* — spiked, buried, the powder wetted — so that whatever force inherited the harbor would inherit it disarmed. And then the list. Four hundred names. Officers of the garrison, ranked in order of precedence, each name followed by a disposition code she knew from her training: *D-1, detention; D-2, detention and transfer; D-3, transfer without return.*

Transfer without return. She had written that code herself, once, years ago, as a junior clerk copying manifests, and had asked her supervisor what it meant, and had been told it meant paperwork, don't worry your head. She was not worried now. She was something colder than worried. She was accurate.

Page three named the beneficiaries. Not the republic — the republic was not a beneficiary of anything anymore, the republic was a debtor signing over assets. The names were the faction's names, the same names Elias had traced through smugglers' tallies and Aubert's private correspondence, the men who had bought a harbor and gotten a town thrown in. Port Mahon was not being defended. Port Mahon was being *delivered*, and her dispatches were the deed of sale, and the garrison's arrest list was the warranty against future claims.

Page four was hers alone. Instructions to the courier, in the Secretary's personal hand:

*Upon execution of the above, the bearer is instructed to remain in residence until the transfer is complete, thereafter returning by the first available vessel. The bearer will be commended. The bearer's discretion regarding the nature of these operations is expected and will be rewarded.*

*Discretion.* They had written her silence into her orders. They had priced it. Somewhere a clerk had assigned a number to what it would cost to make Mirabel Crane complicit in the drowning of a town, and the number had been approved, and the approval had been signed, and the whole machinery had run smooth as a well-oiled press, and at the end of it a woman stood in a rented room in the rain holding four pages that weighed less than her knife.

She set the papers down on the desk.

She had expected rage. She had braced for it the way you brace for a wave. What came instead was worse and better: clarity. Cold, level, salt-clear clarity, the kind that comes after a storm passes and shows you exactly what the sea has done to the shore. There was no version of this in which she was innocent. There was no version in which she had been deceived — she had suspected, Elias had proven, Tomas had bled for the proof. She had carried these pages across an ocean knowing, in the place where knowledge lives below argument, what they were. She had carried them because carrying them was the last identity she had.

The republic had not betrayed her. The republic had simply shown her its books, and she had spent her whole life learning to read books.

Tomas had understood it first. That was the bitter arithmetic of it — the brother branded traitor had been the loyal one, loyal to the thing the republic said it was rather than the thing its ledgers showed. And she had spent months grieving a dead man while the living one was right, and hunted, and hers to save.

Below the window, the town went on with its evening. A dog barked near the quay. Someone laughed in the calle below, two notes of it, ordinary human noise. Four hundred names on page two, and every one of them had a laugh like that somewhere, a dog, a doorway, a mother. She thought of the garrison gun firing late all week and no one correcting it, and understood at last that the lateness wasn't neglect. It was the sound of a place being quietly written off.

Elias would come at midnight. He always came at midnight now, slipping through the consulate's service stair with the ease of a man who had been bribing its porter for a decade. He would ask what the orders said, and she would tell him, and then the two of them would have to decide — not whether, but how, and when, and what it would cost, and who would pay.

But first there was one more thing, and she did it alone.

She took the four pages to the stove. The coals were low but alive; she fed them the kindling first, then the pages one at a time, watching the watermark curl, watching the republic's soft gray arms blacken and lift. Page two burned longest — the names resisted, dense with ink, and she stood over the grate and watched every one of them go, and made herself a promise with the shape of each flame: *not yours to give, not mine to sell, not anyone's to spend.*

She kept back one thing only: Tomas's confession, in Elias's decoded hand, folded flat against her ribs where the dispatches had lived. Evidence. Witness. The difference between arson and testimony.

When the last ash settled she weighed her hands, both of them, palms up, the old habit, the courier's habit, measuring the air.

They had never been lighter.

Behind her, in the corridor, a floorboard shifted — the third stair, the one that sang — and she turned, hand finding the knife, as the door handle began to turn.

Someone had heard the stove.

Or someone had come to collect what was left of it.
