## 17. Century's Edge

The last night of the century smelled of gunpowder and frying oil, and Elias Vane stood in a boatshed below the Moll with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, calking a seam by feel because he could not afford light.

Above him, through the slats of the shed's high window, Port Mahon was making noise like a city that had never heard a cannon. Lanterns strung along the harbor wall. A band somewhere near the fish market, badly out of tune. Children running the quay with paper lanterns on sticks until an exasperated mother caught one by the collar. At midnight there would be salutes from the garrison — three guns, the consul had announced, for the new year, as if the garrison had powder to spare or a year worth saluting.

Elias ran his thumb along the fresh oakum and pressed it home.

Three boats. That was what he had. One cutter, sound, hidden under netting at Cala Figuera since the exchange went wrong in November. A llaüt borrowed from a fisherman named Bartomeu who asked no questions because Elias had once carried his son's debt north and burned the paper afterward. And this third thing, a half-rotten lateen skiff he was rebuilding with tar, canvas, prayer, and a caulking iron older than the war.

Two would carry them. The third was for if things broke badly.

He had learned to think in threes. One plan, one lie, one way out. It was how you stayed alive carrying other people's secrets, and it had served him well for eleven years, right up until the autumn a woman with a courier's flat gray eyes had stood in Consul Aubert's antechamber weighing her own hands like a grocer weighing flour, and something in his chest had quietly changed ownership.

He drove the iron home and sat back on his heels.

*One plan.* Take the confession to a neutral port — Cagliari, if the wind held; Tunis if it didn't — and put it into enough hands that it could not be smothered. Tomas Crane's testimony, decoded in a childhood cipher of numbers and saints' days, laid out plain: the republic meant to sell Port Mahon. Meant to let the harbor starve, let the garrison rot, let the town be handed over as a bargaining chip in some treaty room where no one had ever smelled this water. Mirabel had read it four times and then gone very still, the way she did, and said only: *Then we know what we are.*

*One lie.* New names, new papers, a shipping office story he'd used twice before. He was good at lies the way a mason is good at arches — you didn't notice the work, only that nothing fell.

*One way out.* These boats. The tide turned at ten minutes past midnight. If they were not on the water by then, the tide stopped being theirs.

From the town came a shriek of laughter, a bottle breaking, the band finding its key at last. Somewhere a priest was ringing a bell out of season, practicing for tomorrow.

Elias wiped his hands on his trousers and went to check the stores again, though he had checked them an hour ago. Bread wrapped in oilcloth. Water in two small casks. Mirabel's sea chest, which weighed almost nothing — she had been shedding weight all month, documents burned, ledgers returned, the courier stripped down to a woman with one folded page against her ribs and nothing else she cared to keep. He had watched her do it with the same expression she wore when measuring gunpowder, and he had understood, finally, that he was not watching grief.

He was watching relief being built, plank by plank, like a boat.

* * *

Aubert came at nine o'clock.

Elias saw him first from the shadow of the shed door: the consul's sedan chair setting down on the quay above, the two soldiers who were not really soldiers peeling off to stand where they could watch both ends of the street. Aubert himself descended carefully, holding his hat against the offshore breeze, a neat man in a neat coat, sixty years old and soft-handed, with the patient face of a clerk who had signed away cities before breakfast.

He did not come down to the boatshed. He sent a boy instead — one of the harbor orphans who ran messages for anyone with coin — and the boy delivered a folded note with a wax seal Elias recognized and despised.

The note was addressed to *Madame Lefèvre*, which was the name Mirabel wore this week.

She was not at the shed. She was up in the town, at the safe house above the tannery, sitting with Tomas, whose fever had broken but whose leg still kept him on a cot, arguing chess moves with her out of boredom and losing. Elias had left them an hour ago with the memory of lamplight on three faces and Tomas saying, *You'll take care of her, Vane, or I'll swim after your boat and sink it,* and meaning every word of it, and Elias saying, *I've survived worse threats,* which was a lie, because he hadn't. Nothing had ever threatened him the way loving these two Cranes threatened him.

He climbed the tannery stairs now with the note in his fist, taking the steps two at a time, past the reek of hides and lime, and knocked the pattern — two, pause, three — that meant *me, alone, hurry.*

Mirabel opened the door. Behind her Tomas looked up from the chessboard with a knight in his hand.

"From the consul," Elias said.

She took it without hurry. That was the thing about her that had undone him from the beginning, he thought — not the composure itself but what it cost, visible only to someone who had learned to read her the way he read water. The stillness was effortful. She broke the seal, unfolded the page, and read it standing in the doorway with the harbor noise rising faintly through the shuttered window.

Then she held it out to Tomas without a word, which was answer enough, and Elias read it over Tomas's shoulder.

*MADAME — The hour is late, and so is mine. Tomorrow the faction's commissioners arrive aboard the San Ildefonso, and whatever is found in this consulate will be found with or without your cooperation. I am authorized to offer you passage, protection, and the restoration of your commission, in exchange for the delivery of your original dispatches into my hand before midnight tonight. Your brother's name may yet be struck from certain lists. Refuse, and I regret that names are easier added than removed. You have been an honorable adversary. Do not make yourself a corpse over paper. — A.*

"He knows," Tomas said quietly. He set the knight down. "About the stove."

"He suspects." Mirabel took the note back and read it once more, and Elias watched her weigh it — actually weigh it, tilt it in her fingers as though the paper had mass, the old habit, the creed she'd carried across a storm-wrecked sea in a leaky brig: *carry little, trust less.*

"He doesn't know," she said. "If he knew, he wouldn't offer terms. He'd send the soldiers. This is a man buying insurance." She crossed to the window, eased the shutter a finger's width, and looked down at the harbor. The lanterns strung along the mole threw a trembling gold line across the black water. "Midnight," she said. "He wants it before midnight. Before the year turns and whatever he's promised whoever he's promised becomes due."

"And what will you give him?" Tomas asked. There was no accusation in it. There never was, between these two anymore. Whatever had passed between brother and sister in the fisherman's cellar — the defection explained, the sacrifice of Port Mahon laid bare, the long silence of two years broken — it had been paid out in full, and what remained between them was load-bearing.

"Nothing," Mirabel said. "But I think I'll tell him so in person."

"No," Elias said.

She turned. In the lamplight her face was calm and absolutely immovable, the courier's face, and he hated it and admired it in the same breath.

"It's the last place they'll expect me," she said. "Everyone in this town believes the courier has already fled — Aubert most of all, because a fleeing courier confirms his story. I walk into the consulate at eleven o'clock, I stand in front of him, and I tell him the dispatches are ash. Not as a threat. As arithmetic. Without those papers, his transaction fails. His faction gets nothing. The San Ildefonso sails home empty, and the commissioners have to explain to their masters why the great bargain collapsed on the last night of the century."

"And his men?" Elias said. "His *soldiers?*"

"They're celebrating. All of them. Look at the harbor." She tilted her head toward the window, toward the distant fiddle music and the firecrackers popping off the mole like small, harmless artillery. "No one starts an arrest on New Year's Eve. It's bad luck even for men like Aubert."

"That's exactly when men like Aubert start arrests," Elias said. "When everyone else is drunk and the streets are full of strangers in masks. I know how smuggling works, Mira. I know how the other side of it works too. The festival isn't cover for us. It's cover for *him.*"

For a moment neither of them moved. Tomas, from the cot, said mildly, "I could shoot him from the roof opposite the consulate. I've done worse shots with a worse leg."

"You will stay on this cot," Mirabel and Elias said together, and then looked at each other, and the look lasted a half-second longer than it should have, and Tomas grinned like a boy despite the fever-sweat still shining on his forehead.

"I'll go with her," Elias said at last. It wasn't negotiation. He heard it land in his own voice as fact, and he watched her hear it too — watched the argument go out of her shoulders, not because she yielded but because she had already accounted for his weight in the plan. That, too, was how she loved: by loading you aboard.

"Aubert won't touch me in front of witnesses," she said. "Not tonight. Tonight he needs everything quiet. But we leave the consulate separately, and we leave before the quarter-hour, and if I'm not at the foot of the Steps by quarter past eleven—"

"I carry the confession to the boats and sail without you."

The lamplight hissed. Tomas stopped grinning.

"Elias—"

"That's the price of walking into his house," Elias said. "You want me to agree to the plan? Then that's the plan. All of it. No heroics at the consulate door, no going back for anyone. The confession reaches neutral water or none of this — the burning, the ambush at the cove, Tomas's leg, any of it — means anything." He held her gaze and made his voice gentle, because the truth underneath it was gentle, or as close to gentle as he knew how to make anything. "I spent eleven years delivering things and caring about none of them. I'm not going back to that. But I can't deliver anything if I'm dead in a courtyard, and neither can you. Weigh it. You're good at weighing."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, far off, a rocket went up over the citadel and burst in red, and the light of it slid across her face through the shutter gap and was gone.

"Quarter past eleven," she said. "Foot of the Steps. And Elias—" her voice dropped, and for one unguarded instant the courier's mask slipped and it was just a tired woman on the last night of a terrible year, looking at the only man she trusted, "—if it goes wrong, don't be brave. Be fast."

"I've never been brave in my life," he said. "It's why I'm still alive."

Tomas snorted. Mirabel almost smiled. And the clock in the church of Santa Maria struck ten, each note falling through the tannery walls like a stone into still water, and the count began.

* * *

By eleven the town had gone mad in the ordinary way, which was to say beautifully.

Elias walked the lower streets with his collar up and a wine bottle in his hand for costume, and everywhere around him Port Mahon was doing what port towns do when the calendar turns: spending money it didn't have on light it couldn't keep. Oil lamps doubled along the Carrer de ses Voltes. Sailors arm in arm with girls whose ribbons matched no flag. An old woman selling roasted chestnuts from a brazier, calling out that the new century would bring good prices for salt fish, and half the crowd laughing and the other half crossing themselves, as if the century were a ship whose cargo they might share in.

Oblivious. All of it oblivious. Above them the garrison's guns stood loaded for the midnight salute, and beyond them, out past the mole, the dark shape of the San Ildefonso rode at anchor with the faction's commissioners aboard, sleeping or playing cards or writing letters, waiting for a delivery that was already ash.

Elias felt the strangest tenderness toward all of it — the chestnut smoke, the bad fiddle, the drunk midshipman singing a love song in Menorquí with his arm around a lamp post. Two hours ago he had thought of this town as a problem to be exited. Now, walking through its noise toward the consulate with a knife in his boot and a folded confession's location memorized three ways, he found himself cataloguing it like a man memorizing a face: *this is what we are saving. Not the republic. Not the commission. This. The chestnuts. The terrible singing. The old woman's faith in salt fish.*

The consulate doors stood open — open, on the last night of the year, which told him everything about how Aubert wanted the evening to look. Light spilled onto the marble steps. Inside, a servant with a tray, a couple of minor functionaries drinking the consul's wine, and at the far end of the hall, beneath a portrait of some minister nobody honored anymore, Consul Aubert in his best coat, receiving the new year like a man receiving a shipment.

Mirabel entered at seven minutes past eleven.

Elias saw her from across the square, from the shadow of an awning where he had planted himself with his bottle and his patience. She wore her gray traveling dress and nothing of the courier — no satchel, no seals, no weight at all except the single page against her ribs, and she carried even that invisibly, the way she carried everything. She crossed the marble floor and stopped before Aubert, and the two of them spoke, and though Elias could not hear a word of it across the crowd-noise, he could read it the way he read flags at distance.

Aubert's hands opened: *where is it.* Mirabel's head inclined a fraction: a courteous negative. Aubert smiled — the smile of a man showing his teeth to a mirror. Mirabel did not smile at all. She said something brief. Her hands stayed loose at her sides, palms in, empty, and Elias understood that she was telling him, in the only language the man respected: *there is nothing to take. There never will be.*

Aubert's smile did not change. But his eyes went past her shoulder, to the soldier by the door — the one who was not really a soldier — and the soldier straightened.

*Now,* Elias thought. *Leave now.*

And she did — she turned with perfect unhurried grace, collected her cloak from the servant as though she had merely come to pay respects, and walked out into the square, and the soldier followed her, casually, buttoning his coat, and a second man detached from the shadows by the customs house and drifted parallel along the arcade, and Elias was already moving, bottle abandoned on a doorstep, cutting through the festival crowd on the diagonal, counting in his head.

*Quarter past eleven.* Foot of the Steps. The tide turning at ten past midnight, the cutter under netting at Cala Figuera, Tomas on his cot with a pistol he'd sworn he wouldn't need.

Mirabel reached the top of the Steps that zigzagged down toward the harbor. The two men closed behind her like water closing behind a keel. And Elias, thirty yards out, saw her pause at the head of the stair — saw her turn and face them, unhurried, giving him time, always giving him time — and saw the first church bell of the midnight rehearsal begin to ring, early, eager, wrong.

Below in the boatshed, his three boats waited.

Behind him, the consulate doors swung shut, and every lamp in Aubert's windows went dark at once.
