## 18. What Can Be Carried

The consulate clock struck eleven, and Mirabel Crane began to weigh her life in ounces.

She had done this before — on a dock at Brest with a storm coming, in a hired room in Cádiz with a purser's boots on the stairs — but never like this. Never as an ending. She laid the dispatches out on Consul Aubert's own desk, in the light of his own lamp, and weighed them one last time.

Four packets. The heaviest, the harbor defense schedules, ran to eleven ounces of good rag paper. The arrest warrants for the garrison officers — Aubert's own signature among those to be taken — were lighter, barely seven. The naval withdrawal order, thin as a love letter, four ounces. And the smallest packet, sealed twice, addressed to no one she could name: the authority that turned Port Mahon over to whoever held it when the century turned.

Twenty-two ounces, all told. A pound and a half of paper that would cost three hundred lives if it reached its destination, or cost her neck if it didn't.

She had carried heavier things across worse distances. But she had never carried anything she meant to burn.

* * *

Elias came down from the courtyard at half past eleven, moving quietly the way he always did, though quiet was a courtesy now rather than a necessity. Above them, muffled by stone and distance, the town was celebrating. New Year's Eve, 1799, and Port Mahon intended to see the old world out properly: fireworks over the water, wine in every tavern, church bells primed for midnight. The faction's men were drinking too. Everyone drank tonight. That was the whole design of it.

"They're holding at the harbor gate," Elias said. "Two patrols, six men each. Tomas is watching the north stair with the fisherman's boys. He says—" a pause, the ghost of a smile "—he says we have until the bells stop ringing."

"And after that?"

"After that, everyone in Menorca knows what year it is." He crossed to the window, looking out at the fireworks blooming red and gold above the mole. "Aubert gave you until midnight."

"He gave me until midnight," Mirabel agreed. "He didn't say midnight of which day. I've found that ambiguity useful."

"Mirabel."

"I'm going to burn them, Elias. I've been going to burn them since I read them. I only needed to be sure." She touched the packets without picking them up. "I needed to know there wasn't some other hand I could put them in. Some officer in the garrison who'd refuse the order and hold the town anyway. There isn't. Aubert has bought or frightened all of them. The paper is the only weapon pointed at this harbor, and it's pointed the wrong way."

"So we run instead. The cutter's loaded. We could be past Cape Cavalleria by dawn—"

"With these?" She looked at him steadily. "Then someone else delivers them. Aubert has copies in his head; the faction will have their own set within a month. Burning them here buys time — real time. Without verified orders, the arrests can't proceed. The garrison commanders won't move against their own town on rumor. You taught me that, on the windmill road. Paper is only power while it exists."

"I taught you that so you'd carry less," Elias said. "Not so you'd carry nothing."

"It comes to the same arithmetic." She stood, and took off her coat — the coat with the forged papers sewn into the lining, three years of careful seams — and folded it over the chair back. Underneath she wore plain wool, clothes chosen tonight so she could move, climb, run. She had dressed for exile the way other women dressed for mass. "Help me with the stove."

* * *

The consulate stove was a squat iron thing built into the chimney breast of Aubert's private office, installed — according to the housekeeper — because the consul disliked drafts and loved his comfort. It had a grate wide enough for a manila folder and a flue that drew like a bellows. Mirabel had noticed it her first week in Port Mahon and filed it away, the way she filed everything, under *useful later*.

Later had arrived.

She knelt before the open door with the first packet in both hands. Eleven ounces. Harbor defense schedules. Batteries, magazines, the rotation of watchmen who would become prisoners at dawn if this paper lived.

"You'll want kindling under it," Elias said behind her. "Wet paper smolders. Smoldering paper smells. Smells bring people."

"Have you burned a great many documents?"

"Enough to know the technique." He crouched beside her, uninvited, and fed splinters of broken crate-wood through the grate, then a twist of oil-soaked rag. He struck a flint and held it until the kindling caught, orange light jumping between their faces. "There. Now it wants feeding, not mourning. Give it the heavy one first, while your hands are still willing."

"My hands are willing."

"Liar," he said gently. "But brave liars are my favorite kind."

She fed the packet through the grate.

It caught slowly, the wax seal bubbling first, then the corners curling black, then flame running up the folded creases like something alive escaping a trap. She watched the fire take the columns of numbers, the battery positions, the names of officers — names she knew, men who'd drunk coffee with her in this very room — and she felt the weight leave her hands and did not feel it as loss.

She felt it as balance restored.

The second packet went in. Seven ounces, the arrest warrants. The third, the naval order, thin and quick, gone almost before it touched the coals. She fed each one the way she'd once measured out cipher keys to swallow in a storm — deliberately, without ceremony, because ceremony was for people who had choices left.

Only the last packet remained. The smallest. The seal with two signatures, one of them Aubert's, authorizing the abandonment of Port Mahon to whichever power arrived first with a fleet and a bribe.

She held it over the flames and made herself read it once more, top to bottom, because she had promised herself that whatever else she burned, she would never again carry orders she hadn't read.

Then she let it go.

* * *

The bells began at midnight.

They came rolling down from the cathedral and across the rooftops and into the consulate like a tide, and above the sound of them the whole town seemed to exhale — cheering, singing, guns fired in salute from fishing boats in the harbor. The old century died in noise. Somewhere in that noise, Mirabel thought, were the defenders who would wake tomorrow still free men, still armed, still theirs, because a woman they'd never met had just committed treason against her own country in the consul's parlor.

She kept one thing back from the fire. Only one.

Tomas's confession — the pages they'd decoded together in the cellar, in his own hand, in the childhood cipher only two living Cranes could read — she wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, and pushed deep into the lining of her spare satchel, behind the false bottom she'd sewn for exactly this purpose. Evidence. Not of her brother's guilt but of the republic's. Proof that Port Mahon had been sold, and by whom, and for what. Someday, somewhere, someone might sit in judgment on all of this, and when they did, she intended to arrive with documents.

Carry little, trust less. But carry the truth. That much of the creed survived.

"The stove will smoke till morning," Elias said, rising. "Ash tells stories too, but by the time anyone reads this one—"

The front door came apart.

Not opened — *came apart*, hinges and lock and a section of frame surrendering all at once to a battering ram handled by men who had done it before. Lamplight spilled into the hall along with boots, six of them, eight, and the flat voice of Lieutenant Roque — Aubert's creature, the one who had offered Elias immunity in exchange for Mirabel — calling out with theatrical calm:

"Mademoiselle Crane. The consul regrets the hour. He requests the dispatches."

Mirabel stood up from the stove and closed its door with a click, gentle as closing a book. Through the iron she could hear the fire still eating, patient and thorough, and she thought: *by morning, ash.*

"Gone ahead of us," she called back pleasantly. "You're late."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way violence does — faster than telling it.

Roque came down the hall at a charge with two men behind him, and Mirabel put the heavy mahogany chair between herself and them, and that bought exactly the seconds it was meant to buy. Because the courtyard window burst inward — not glass, the whole shutter, torn off its pintles — and Tomas Crane came through it like weather.

Her brother. Alive, still bruised yellow-green along the jaw from Cala Figuera, moving with the economy of a man who had learned soldiering in worse places than academies. He put the first soldier down with the shutter itself, swung the second into the wall, and said, in the tone of a family dinner:

"Mira. Courtyard."

"Courtyard," she agreed, and went through the window after him, because some instructions survive any number of years apart.

Behind her she heard Elias's voice, cold and clear, addressing Roque across the wreckage of the office: "The consul sent eight men to fetch ashes. Tell him I said he should count better." Then a crash, and she trusted it the way she had decided to trust him — completely, and on insufficient evidence, which was the only way trust had ever worked between them.

* * *

The courtyard was small and walled and full of olive trees in tubs, and it was already a battlefield. Two more of Roque's men had come over the wall from the alley and met the fisherman's boys at the north stair, and the fight there was short and ugly and over. Tomas moved through it all like a man reading familiar text, putting bodies where bodies should go, and Mirabel took the lantern hook off its bracket and used it the way her father had taught her to use a boat hook, years ago, on a different sea.

Then it was quiet. Eight men down or fled. The bells still ringing. The fireworks still climbing over the mole, red and gold and indifferent, celebrating the birth of a century that had no idea yet what it was inheriting.

Tomas leaned on the wall, breathing hard, and grinned at her with blood on his teeth — their father's grin, unmistakable, unbearable.

"You always did close doors softly," he said.

"You always did come through windows." Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. Three minutes. She allowed herself three minutes of being his sister, gripping his forearm, pressing her forehead to his shoulder, feeling him laugh shakily against her hair. Then she stepped back and became again the woman who weighed things in ounces.

"Elias?"

"Coming," said the courtyard wall, and Elias came over it from inside, unhurried, straightening his sleeve, with the unhurried air of a man who had left Lieutenant Roque tied to the consul's desk with the consul's own curtain cord. "We have perhaps ten minutes before anyone thinks to check whether he's stopped shouting. The cutter's at the foot of the north stair. Tomas's fisherman is aboard and nervous."

"Nervous is good," Tomas said. "Nervous sailors live long."

Mirabel slung the satchel — Tomas's confession safe against her spine, twenty-two ounces of republic reduced to ash in an iron stove behind her — and looked around the little courtyard one last time. Olive trees in tubs. A broken window. The smell of woodsmoke drifting up from the consulate, indistinguishable from a hundred celebratory fires burning all over the city tonight. No one would notice. No one would ever need to.

She had come to Port Mahon carrying a pound and a half of death. She was leaving carrying a confession, a brother, and a man who had burned his own papers for her.

For the first time since she'd learned to count, her load weighed less than her conscience. She found she could stand upright under it.

"Bells are stopping," Elias said quietly. "New century. Time we weren't here for it."

They went down the north stair together, three shadows among the celebrating crowds, toward the water and the boats and whatever the new hundred years intended to make of them.

At the harbor gate, a sentry raised his lantern — and Mirabel saw, beyond him, at the mouth of the mole, the dark shape of a warship's boats pulling for shore, oars muffled, coming in silent against all the noise.

Roque had counted after all. Or Aubert had.

"Tomas," she breathed. "How many ways out of this harbor?"
