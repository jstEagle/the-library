## 19. The Harbor Gate

The fires started at the mole.

Tomas saw them from the fisherman's cellar door — three signal pyres on the heights above the harbor, lit in sequence, east to west, the way a garrison announces to its own ships that the port is closed. Except these weren't garrison fires. The garrison had no oil left. These were Aubert's fires, and they said something else entirely: *no one leaves*.

"They're early," Elias said, slinging the satchel of powder over his shoulder like it weighed nothing, which was a lie they both agreed to believe. "The boats won't be at the quay until the third bell."

"The boats are where I say they are," Mirabel said, "and we leave now."

Tomas loved her for it and hated her for it, in the same breath, the way he'd loved and hated her since they were children dividing an orange on their mother's kitchen table — she always took the knife, he always took the blame. Twelve years at sea and in other men's wars had not changed the arithmetic between them. She decided. He followed. And somewhere behind them both, always, was the promise: *if it comes to it, you get out and I don't.*

They had made it when he was fourteen and she was eleven, the summer before he signed his first articles, standing on the sea wall at home while their father argued with a customs man below. She'd asked him what sailors did when a ship burned. He'd said, without thinking, because he was fourteen and stupid with romance: "They get the people off first. Always the people off first." And she'd looked at him with that flat gray seriousness she still wore like a uniform, and said, "Then promise me. If it ever comes to it. You get me off first."

"You're my little sister."

"Promise anyway."

He'd promised. It had cost him nothing then. He thought about that now, running the crooked lane above the harbor with a bullet graze already stiffening along his ribs and two of Aubert's soldiers somewhere behind them in the smoke, and understood that some promises are priced cheap at the making and ruinously at the keeping.

"Left," Elias snapped, and they went left, down a stair cut into the limestone so narrow their shoulders brushed both walls, into air that smelled of burning pitch and gunpowder and the sea beneath everything, always the sea.

* * *

Port Mahon was burning in pieces, not whole. That was the trick of it — Aubert hadn't torched the town, only the things that could signal or shelter: the semaphore station, the customs house roof, the long timber sheds by the careening yard where boats might be hidden. The town itself would survive; it had been built by people who expected to be fought over, and stone remembers how to wait. But the light from the fires turned every white wall the color of meat, and the shadows ran wrong, and in the wrongness Tomas counted silhouettes.

"Two on the quay," he breathed. "One at the water stairs."

"I see them," Mirabel said.

"I see them too," Elias said, "and I see the cutter, which is the part worth seeing. She's moored short. Ten yards of open water between us and her gunwale, no more."

"No more," Tomas repeated, and almost laughed. Ten yards of open water, in a harbor full of men with orders to keep them ashore, was a country away.

They crouched in the mouth of the stair while the third bell rang out across the water, thin and clear, absurdly beautiful. Somewhere above, the town was singing — New Year's Eve, 1799, and the people of Port Mahon had decided that if the century meant to end in fire, they would sing it out anyway. The sound came down the hill in fragments: fiddles, voices, a child's high laugh chased by another. They sang above the smoke the way they'd lived under every flag for a hundred years, by refusing to notice it.

"That's what we're doing this for," Mirabel said quietly. Not a question. A ledger entry. She kept ledgers even now.

"We're doing this," Tomas said, "because you're getting on that boat."

She turned and looked at him, firelight on one side of her face and darkness on the other, and for a moment he saw their mother in her — the same jaw set against the same impossible arithmetic. "We are all getting on that boat," she said. "Or none of us are worth the walk."

"There's a version of tonight," Tomas began—

"No." Flat. Immediate. "Whatever sentence starts with 'there's a version,' swallow it. I've heard it in your voice since Cala Figuera. I've heard it every time you favor that side, every time you think I'm not counting." She stepped closer. "You spent six years being leverage so I wouldn't have to choose. Don't spend tonight trying to make the choice for me. I burn things, Tomas. It's what I do best. Don't make yourself the next thing on the list."

He had no answer to that, so he did what he'd done his whole life: he nodded and waited for the moment she wasn't looking.

It came sooner than he wanted.

* * *

The quay was chaos wearing order's coat. Aubert's soldiers had formed a line at the harbor gate — the old archway where the fishing fleet passed under on feast days, hung now with lanterns that threw hard circles of light onto the stones — but the crowd from the celebrations kept drifting through it toward the water to watch the fires, and the soldiers kept having to shove them back, and in the friction between crowd and cordon there were seams.

Elias found the seam. Of course he did. He moved through it like water finding the crack in a hull, pulling Mirabel after him by the wrist, and Tomas came behind playing the drunk, weaving, singing snatches of the same song the town was singing, close enough to the tune to pass. A soldier caught his arm at the archway.

"Where from, friend?"

"The tavern by the church," Tomas slurred, and gestured grandly upward at nothing. "They're saying the century dies at midnight. I'm going to see it drown. My father drowned. Good family trade."

The soldier, young and tired and wanting more than anything to be at the tavern himself, laughed despite himself and shoved him through. For four steps Tomas believed it would be that easy.

Then the fifth step brought him past the lantern, and the light found his face, and a sergeant by the customs shed straightened up like a man remembering a debt. Tomas knew him. Six weeks in a cellar teaches you a guard's face better than your own.

"Hold there!" the sergeant shouted. "That's the Crane brother! Stop them!"

The night tore open.

* * *

Later — much later, in the fog, in the cold, in the strange quiet of open water — Tomas would try to remember the run as a series of moments instead of one continuous roar, because the moments were bearable and the roar was not.

A musket discharged somewhere near and missed everyone, punching a hole in the night. Elias turned and fired back without breaking stride, an economy of motion Tomas would have admired in any other circumstance. Mirabel ran the way she did everything — precisely, without wasted breath, her hand locked in his as if they were children again crossing a market square.

They reached the water stairs. The cutter lay ten yards out, riding low, Elias's smuggler's boat with the smuggler's name freshly painted over. Elias whistled twice, low, and a shape aboard answered, and a grapple line came snaking through the dark to slap against the lowest step.

"Mirabel first," Elias said.

"Both of us," she said.

"Mirabel *first*—" 

And then the world swung around, because the sergeant had come down the stairs behind them with four men, and the lead soldier raised his musket not at Elias, not at the space between them, but at Mirabel — at her back, at the pale line of her neck above her collar — and took aim with the unhurried care of a man who has all night and a standing order.

There is a kind of decision that isn't a decision at all. Tomas had made his at fourteen on a sea wall. Everything since had just been the interest accruing.

He stepped into the line of the barrel.

The shot took him high in the chest, left of center, and the harbor tilted, and the fires above smeared into long red ribbons, and he heard — distantly, as if reported by someone else — Mirabel scream his name in a voice he'd never heard her use, a voice with nothing disciplined left in it at all.

* * *

Pain arrived late, the way it does at sea, after the shock has finished its paperwork. By then he was in the bottom of the cutter with Mirabel's hands pressed hard against the wound and her face very close to his, saying his name over and over in that stripped voice, and Elias was hauling the grapple in with blood on his knuckles, and the boat was swinging out into the harbor on a tide that finally, finally favored them.

Muskets cracked from the quay. One ball slapped the water nearby; another hummed overhead and was gone. Then the fog — the fog that had been waiting offshore all evening like a paid accomplice — rolled in across the harbor mouth and closed the door behind them.

"Leave me," Tomas said.

It cost him more than the bullet had. Saying it felt like swallowing glass. But the words were the words, and the promise was the promise, and a man honors a contract even against himself.

"Leave me," he said again. "I'm dead weight. Put me ashore at the point, take the tide, get clear. That was always the shape of it. That was the deal."

Mirabel stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sounds were the creak of the boat, the muffled fading of the town, and Elias breathing hard at the tiller. Then Mirabel leaned down until her forehead nearly touched his, and her eyes — their mother's eyes, gray and flat and absolutely without mercy — filled his whole world.

"When you were fourteen," she said, very quietly, "you stood on the sea wall and told me sailors get the people off first. Always the people off first. And I made you promise."

"Tess—"

"You promised, Tomas. You swore it. And I have kept every promise I ever made you — I crossed a war-torn sea carrying paper heavier than I was, I burned a century of duty in a stove, I chose you over my country, over my commission, over my name. So hear me now." Her hands pressed harder on the wound, steady as a compass. "If you die tonight, you will die *aboard this boat*, with your promise honored to the last inch, because I am not leaving you, and neither is he, and if you waste one more ounce of your blood on noble arithmetic I will never forgive you as long as I live — and I intend to live a very long time, somewhere warm, being insufferable at you. Do you understand?"

He understood. God help him, he understood. All those years he'd thought he was the one protecting her, and she had been carrying him the entire time — across seas, through sieges, over the ruins of everything they'd trusted — the way she carried everything: carefully, completely, and without once putting it down.

"Always the people off first," he whispered, and smiled, and let the fog take the edge of the pain.

Above them, the last fires of the old century burned themselves out over Port Mahon. Below, in the hold, the fever was already beginning to build, patient as a tide.

And far behind, lost somewhere in the fog and the smoke and the dying year, a boat's bell rang once — a single note, thin and wrong, that made Elias go silent at the tiller and turn his head toward the darkness, listening.

"That's not our wake," he said. "Someone's following us out."
