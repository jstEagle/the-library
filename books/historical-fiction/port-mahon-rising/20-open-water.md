## 20. Open Water

The fog came for us an hour after the signal fires died behind us, rolling off the water in slow gray walls, and I have never been so glad to see weather in my life.

I stood at the stern with the tiller line in one hand and a loaded pistol in the other, watching the glow of Port Mahon shrink from orange to ember to nothing. Somewhere back there, soldiers were dragging the harbor for us. Somewhere back there, Aubert was composing his report — three traitors fled by sea, dispatches unaccounted for, the consul's own hands conveniently clean. Let him write it. Paper had been the whole war's weapon; let it lie about us now. We were done being described.

"Anything?" Mirabel called from the bow.

"Nothing." I meant it. The fog was thick enough to drink. If a pursuit cutter was out there, it was out there blind, same as us.

She came aft along the gunwale with that walk of hers — quick, economical, a woman who had learned somewhere that hesitation cost blood — and took the tiller from me without asking. I let her. Her hands were steady. They had been steady all night, through the courtyard, through the burning, through the moment Tomas folded into the bottom of the boat like a man setting down something heavier than himself. It was only now, in the gray open water with nothing left to steer toward but a heading, that I saw the tremor start at her wrists.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you."

"I will. After."

"After what?"

I didn't have an answer. "After," I said again, and she didn't press it. That was the thing about Mirabel Crane that had taken me weeks to understand and would take the rest of my life to explain: she knew which silences were arguments and which were mercy, and she never spent the wrong one.

* * *

Tomas lay below on the boards we'd laid over the ballast, wrapped in a sailcloth blanket that had stopped being white sometime before either of us owned it. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder — high, clean, the kind of wound that killed men slowly instead of quickly, which is its own cruelty. He'd bled enough on the quay to frighten me, and I am not easily frightened. On the boat he'd bled less, because there was less of him left to bleed.

He was feverish now. That was expected. What wasn't expected was what he said when the fever talked.

"The windmill road," he murmured, eyes shut, hands moving against the blanket as if turning pages. "The olives are wrong this year. Tell Mira the olives are wrong. Father will know what it means."

"He's dead, Tomas," I said quietly. "They're both dead. You're safe."

His eyes opened partway. For a second they focused on me, clear and cold and appraising — the agent looking out through the wreck of the man — and then they clouded again.

"You're not the courier," he said. "The courier had honest boots."

"I'm whoever carried you onto this boat."

That seemed to satisfy him. He slept. Or something near sleep. I sat with him a while anyway, because a man alone with a fevered brother-in-law-to-be — no. Not that. Not yet, and maybe never, and maybe titles were just more paper to burn. A man alone with a wounded stranger who happened to be the reason the woman above decks hadn't eaten in two days — that man sat and watched the breathing and counted it, the way you count anything you can't control.

Mirabel came down once with water and hard bread soaked in wine. She fed her brother in small careful spoonfuls, the way you'd feed a fire you were trying to keep alive without letting it spread. She didn't speak to him while he was like this. I understood why. Whatever she needed to say to him belonged to the version of him that could answer, and she was too disciplined to waste words on a man who couldn't carry them.

When she finished, she looked at me across the little cabin, lamplight between us, and said: "You burned your papers."

"A long time ago."

"No. On the quay. I saw you. Your safe-conduct — you put it to the lantern flame while Aubert's men were fifty feet away. You could have shown it. You could have walked free."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The honest answer was ugly and simple: because the paper was worth more than I was. Because a smuggler with a safe-conduct is a tool, and tools get used, and I had spent one whole life being used and had found, somewhere between a windmill and a cove full of gunfire, that I preferred being a man. But honesty has a price too, and I paid it.

"Because if I'd kept it," I said, "there would always be a version of tonight where I showed it. And I didn't want that version available."

She held my eyes for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and went back up to the helm, and I understood that in her language that nod was a paragraph.

* * *

Dawn came as a rumor rather than a fact — the fog lightening from charcoal to slate to a pale wet pearl, the sea going flat and patient beneath it. We ran close-hauled under a scrap of jib, making perhaps four knots toward a coast neither of us could name aloud. Neutral waters. Everyone aboard knew the phrase; nobody aboard trusted it. Neutrality was just another flag, and flags were sewn by the highest bidder.

I took the watch so Mirabel could finally sleep, and for two hours I steered alone through a world with no edges.

It was during those hours that the calm arrived.

I want to describe it properly, because it surprised me and I don't surprise easily. There was a moment — fog thinning, sun trying, the compass card swinging lazily to settle on our heading — when I realized there was nothing left to protect. No papers. No commission. No cover story, no employer, no price on my services, no version of myself held in reserve. Everything I'd been carrying since I first signed aboard a smuggling lugger at fourteen had either been delivered, destroyed, or made worthless. The dispatches were ash in a consulate stove. My safe-conduct was ash on a quay. Even the money in my belt — and there was some, there's always some — was just metal now, not leverage.

A lighter man, I thought. I had spent years measuring cargo by weight, by value, by what it would fetch and what it would cost me. And here, with nothing, I felt lighter than I had ever felt at sea.

Then I corrected myself. Not nothing. Never nothing. There was a woman sleeping on deck under an awning we'd rigged, one hand still curled around the tiller line even in sleep, as if the boat might forget itself without her. There was a fevered man below whose breathing I could hear through the companionway hatch. Two things left to protect. Which was one fewer than most men ever manage, and infinitely more than I'd had yesterday.

The sails snapped. The fog lifted in rags. And on the northern horizon, brief as a knife-flash, I saw a sail.

* * *

"Sail hoist. North-norwest. Running large — she'll have the weather gauge."

Mirabel was awake before I finished saying it, awake the way soldiers wake, all at once and already moving. She stood beside me at the stern rail and studied the distant triangle of canvas with the same expression she'd used cataloging dispatches by burn-time.

"Warship or merchantman?"

"Can't tell. She's got too much canvas for a merchant under those conditions. Somebody in a hurry."

"Somebody chasing us."

"Or somebody chasing somebody else. This coast is full of every nation's business right now."

"We're full of every nation's business right now," she said dryly, and went forward to shake out the reef.

We gave her everything the little cutter had. She was a sweet vessel — hired in Menorca through a fisherman whose politics were mercifully for sale — thirty feet of shallow draft and clean lines, built by someone who'd loved her. With the jib drawing full and the main sheet eased just shy of straining, we slipped through the water like a rumor. Behind us the strange sail grew by degrees, slow and implacable, and I did the arithmetic I hated doing: in open water, with that wind, we could not outrun her past dark. In fog we could outrun God Himself.

So I watched the fog banks.

There. Off the eastern shore, where the land dropped away into deep water, a bank of it lay against the coast like a wall someone had forgotten to finish. Land breeze was dying with the morning; the sea haze was starting to move. By noon, if the wind did what winds do off these coasts, that wall would slide out over the water and give us forty minutes of blindness, and forty minutes was a lifetime if you spent it well.

"Tomas," I called down the hatch. "How are your hands?"

From below, cracked but lucid, the voice of the enemy agent — no, the brother, I was learning to make the distinction — drifted up: "My hands work. My shoulder doesn't care what they think."

"Good. Come up. You're going to steer."

He climbed the ladder badly, gray-faced, and Mirabel caught his elbow and steadied him with a touch that was fierce in its gentleness. For a moment the two of them stood together in the cockpit, and I looked away, because some reunions aren't for witnesses, even ones who carried the letters that started them.

Then we worked. That's what I'll remember about that hour — not fear, work. Tomas on the tiller, reading the water's mood off the back of his hand the way our fisherman had taught him in the two days we'd owned the boat. Mirabel trimming canvas by feel, calling adjustments before I asked for them. Me at the bow, sounding depths and watching the fog wall come down toward us like a curtain between acts.

At half past eleven, we entered it.

The world closed. Sail, sea, sky — gone, all of it, replaced by a gray so total I could see the drops condensing on the forestay and nothing beyond them. Sound flattened. Our wake vanished astern within a boat-length. Somewhere behind us, a ship that had us dead to rights suddenly had nothing at all.

"Come about," Mirabel said quietly. "South-southeast. Let's cross their last known bearing and let the fog argue about where we went."

We crossed. We doubled. We ran quiet, canvas barely drawing, listening until my ears ached. Once — just once — a bell struck somewhere off to starboard, muffled and lost, and all three of us froze like poachers. Then it faded, or we faded from it, and the fog kept its counsel.

By mid-afternoon the strange sail was a memory. By evening, so was the fog, burned away into a clean cold sunset with the stars coming out overhead like spilled rigging pins, and we were alone on an empty sea, running easy toward a coast none of us could see yet but all of us believed in.

* * *

That night we ate properly for the first time since Cala Figuera. Salt cod, ship's biscuit, a bottle of the fisherman's rough red wine that tasted like heaven to men who'd earned it. Tomas sat propped against the mast with his arm bound and slung, color back in his face, and told stories — not the important ones, not yet, those were still locked behind whatever confession he was saving for daylight and sobriety — but small ones. The ones that build a person.

"—and so Mirabel, aged nine, informs the entire congregation that the bishop's wig is on backwards—"

"It was," she said.

"It was not, but that's not the point. The point is she said it at volume, during the homily, and Father had to escort her out past the entire village while she explained herself to everyone individually—"

"They deserved explanations."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. And Tomas looked at me across the little circle of lamplight with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself weeks ago on a windmill road: the look of a man reassessing a stranger and finding, uncomfortably, a friend.

"She trusts you," he said. Not a question. Not entirely friendly, either. An older brother doing arithmetic.

"Your sister trusts almost no one," I said. "If I've earned a fraction of it, I intend to keep earning it."

"And if keeping it costs you?"

"It already has. That's how I know it's real."

He held my gaze a moment longer, then raised the wine bottle an inch — a salute, or a truce, or both — and drank. Above us the stars turned. Below us the sea breathed. And Mirabel, sitting with her back against the rail and her knees drawn up, watched the two of us with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because some things a man is better off discovering slowly.

Later, when Tomas had gone below to sleep like the wounded thing he honestly was, she and I stood the middle watch together. The boat steered herself mostly, balanced and content. We stood close without touching, the way you stand near a fire.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Burning the conduct. Refusing Aubert. Any of it."

I thought about giving her a clever answer. I decided she'd had enough cleverness for one century.

"No," I said. "For the first time in my adult life, everything I'm carrying fits inside me. It turns out that's what I was always trying to carry."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had something new in it — not softness exactly, but the space where softness would go, cleared and waiting.

"When we make landfall," she said, "we'll need names. New ones. Whole new lives, probably, if the reports travel the way reports do."

"I've had six names. They get easier."

"What was the first one? The real one."

I looked at her. She looked at the horizon. Neither of us moved.

"Elias," I said. "Just Elias. The rest was always decoration."

"Then we'll need a surname for Elias," she said, and there it was — the space where softness goes, filled at last, offered so casually that only a man paying attention would catch what it actually was. "Something respectable. Something boring. We're going to be terribly boring people, you understand. A shipping office. Ledgers. Weather complaints."

"The worst kind of people."

"Unbearable."

Ahead of us, low on the black rim of the world, a light appeared — a single fixed point, steady, human, exactly where the charts promised the neutral coast would be. A lighthouse. A door. A beginning wearing the costume of an ending.

"There," Mirabel said, and put her hand over mine on the rail, and steered us both toward it.
