## 3. What the Apprentice Was Told to Forget

The rain had not stopped since Corvel left it, and Odile did not light the lamp again.

She stood in the dark of the workshop with the letters in her hands—six of them, tied with tarred twine, damp at their corners as though they had crossed the harbor twice, once coming and once going—and she listened to the storm work at the shutters and understood that her life had just divided itself cleanly in two: everything before this night, when Étienne Marchand was dead; and everything after, when he might not be.

Dead men do not write letters. Dead men certainly do not write them in a living hand, on paper that still smelled faintly of the cheap tallow-and-turpentine ink sold by the stationer on the rue Crébillon. She knew that smell the way she knew the smell of her own hair. She had bought that ink for ten years, for two masters' worth of work, and every stick of it went through her hands.

So either Étienne Marchand was alive, or someone was very good at being him.

She set the bundle on the drafting table without untying it. There was a discipline to this—the first discipline he had ever taught her, before perspective, before soundings, before the mathematics of the tide: *when the hand shakes, put the instrument down*. Her hands were shaking now. She put the letters down.

And because there was nothing else in the world to do but wait for morning, and because the dark of the workshop was the same dark as another night ten years gone, Odile sat down at her stool and let the remembering come. It came the way flood water comes—through every gap at once.

* * *

She had been nineteen. The workshop had smelled then as it smelled tonight: size and pine resin, linseed oil, the vinegar bite of the etching bath, and under all of it the salt that lived in everything in Nantes the way damp lived in stone. It had been October. The chestnuts were rotting sweet in the gutters of the quays.

Étienne had been working late on the Loire sheets—she remembered that with terrible clarity, because it was one of the things she had spent ten years learning not to remember. He had been hunched over the estuary plate with his spectacles pushed up into his grey hair, which was how he wore them when he was angry at a coastline, and he had said, without looking up, *Odile, fetch me the 1739 fair copy of the Vendée approaches.*

She had fetched it. And she had seen what was written across its margins in red chalk—not soundings, not corrections, but names. Dozens of names, small and crowded, running along the coast like barnacles: *Garnier. Le Floch. The boy Aubin, fifteen. All lost off Noirmoutier, cargo never reported, hull never found.* And beside each name, in Étienne's cramped, doubled-t hand, the same word, or nearly the same word: *wrecked by arrangement. Wrecked by arrangement. Wrecked by arrangement.*

"What is this?" she had asked.

He had looked up at her then, and she would spend a decade trying to describe to herself what his face had done. Not fear exactly. Something older than fear—the look of a man who has been carrying a weight so long he has forgotten it is not part of his body, and who sees, suddenly, in his apprentice's eyes, that she has just watched him pick it up again.

"Paper," he said. "It's only paper, Odile."

But he took the sheet from her hands too quickly, and he locked it in the flat iron cabinet behind the drying rack, and he did not speak for the rest of the evening except to tell her the tide tables were misbound and she should redo the stitching, and she had thought, in the way of apprentices everywhere, *he does not trust me*, and had been wounded by it, and had gone home through the wet streets rehearsing the speech she would give him tomorrow about how she could keep secrets, how she was not a child, how she had earned—

Tomorrow never came for the speech.

The officials arrived on a Thursday, an hour past dawn, and there were nine of them, which Odile counted without meaning to, because counting was the only thing her mind would agree to do. They were not soldiers. That was the detail that had frightened her most, later, when she finally let herself think it: they were not the king's men in any uniform she knew. They were clerks and bailiffs and two gentlemen in good wool coats who stood in the doorway of the workshop with the rain steaming off their shoulders and did not touch anything, and one of whom—she saw his face clearly, she had always seen it clearly, she had simply filed it away where she did not have to go looking—gave the order.

"Seal the presses. Take the plates. All of them." A pause, mild as cream. "Particularly the coastal material."

Not *the atlas*. Not *Marchand's survey*. *The coastal material.* As though it were a shipment of contraband sugar, as though it were already catalogued, already expected. And the gentleman who said it—he was perhaps forty, well-fed, courteous, with a lawyer's soft voice and eyes like the bottom of a clean glass—smiled at Étienne Marchand with something that was almost affection, and said:

"Étienne. You've been busy."

Odile learned his name the way one learns the name of a disease. Delaunay. It was spoken twice that morning, once by a clerk seeking instruction and once by Étienne himself, who said it quietly, tasting it, the way a man says a word he intends never to say again.

They worked fast. The plates went into oiled canvas—every copper plate, ten years of engraving, the whole skeleton of the atlas—and the ledgers went into crates, and one of the clerks found the flat iron cabinet behind the drying rack and asked for the key, and Étienne gave it to him, and Odile watched the 1739 fair copy with its red-chalk graveyard of drowned sailors disappear into the same canvas as everything else, and no one looked at her at all. That was the other thing she had filed away and never examined: no one looked at her. She was furniture. An apprentice girl in an ink-stained apron was not a witness; she was part of the room's inventory, to be noted and left.

Then Delaunay turned to Étienne in the doorway, and his voice stayed gentle, and that gentleness was the worst sound Odile had ever heard.

"You'll want to make yourself scarce," he said. "A man in your position. With your... habits of industry. I'd suggest the river traffic. It's remarkable, the accidents this river has." He smiled. "I'd hate for anything official to happen to you. Officially."

That was all. That was the entire conversation. And Étienne Marchand, who could argue with a coastline for six hours, who had once reduced a naval hydrographer to tears over the placement of a sandbar, said nothing. He bowed. Actually bowed, a small dry bow, like a merchant accepting terms. And then he caught Odile's eye—one glance, half a second, and in it she read nothing she could use, nothing she could hold onto, only a kind of terrible steadiness, as if he were memorizing *her*—and said:

"Lock up when you're done, Odile."

As if it were an ordinary evening. As if the plates were merely out at the framer's.

Three days later the harbor crier called the news and she heard it from the loft above the workshop, standing among the hanging sheets: a boat found swamped below Paimbœuf, one body recovered, identified as the mapmaker Marchand, drowned in the fog. The river gives and the river takes. Even the crier's voice had been bored.

She had gone down to the water. She had stood on the quay where they brought the body ashore and she had looked at the drowned man's face and it was not his face—it was close, cruelly close, a thin grey man with a mapmaker's squint, but it was not him, the ear was wrong, the scar above the eyebrow was wrong, she had drawn that scar a hundred times in caricature on scrap paper when she was sixteen and proud of herself—and she had opened her mouth to say so.

And closed it.

Because she had replayed that Thursday morning, all nine officials, the two gentlemen in good wool, the mild voice saying *particularly the coastal material*, the gentle warning about the river's accidents, and she had understood—in one cold drop falling through her, the way understanding falls—that the man on the boards at her feet had been arranged the way the wrecks had been arranged. That whoever had drowned had been wrecked by arrangement. And that if she spoke—if she said *that is not Étienne Marchand, I know his face better than God does*—then the next arrangement would be simpler, quieter, and would involve a nineteen-year-old apprentice who had seen too much and was, conveniently, alone in the world.

She said nothing. She wept, publicly and convincingly, because everyone expected it, and the weeping was even true, and it was also armor. Grief was the one story about that week that nobody wanted to examine. She hid inside it the way a smuggler hides inside a fog bank, and she learned, in those first months, the grammar of silence: which questions to answer slowly, which to answer wrong, which never to hear at all. She learned that the safest lie is a smaller truth. She learned to finish charts of safe harbors for merchants and magistrates and to smile at Magistrate Delaunay's clerk when he came for his commissions, and to keep the atlas alive in pieces, a plate here, a sounding there, buried under honest work the way a blade is buried under bread in a basket.

Ten years. Ten years of careful forgetting, which is not forgetting at all but a kind of housekeeping, sweeping the same rooms every night so that nothing shows on the floor.

And now there were letters on her table in a hand she would know blindfolded, by weight alone.

* * *

A knock. Two knocks, actually—a knuckle and then the flat of a hand, impatient with itself. She had not heard him come back up the street; the rain had swallowed his footsteps whole.

She opened the door on Corvel, streaming, hatless now, holding something wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way a man holds a loaf in a famine.

"You didn't untie them," he said. It was not quite a question. He looked past her into the dark workshop—at the drafting table, the covered plates, the ten years of careful nothing—and something in his shoulders eased, as if he'd been braced to find the place ransacked, or worse, tidied.

"I put them down," Odile said. "My hands weren't steady."

"He told me you'd say that." Corvel stepped inside without invitation, dripping on her floorboards, and unwrapped the oilcloth on her drafting table as though it were a wound he'd been carrying. Inside lay more papers—loose leaves, a notebook swollen with saltwater and dried stiff, a stub of red chalk worn round. "There's a letter with your name on it. Has had for ten years, near enough. He wrote others. This one he rewrote." Corvel's mouth twisted. "Every year. On the anniversary of his own drowning. Like a man keeping a grave."

"Read it." Her voice surprised her—flat, commandant's-voice, the voice she used on delivery boys. "If it has my name on it, why did he send you?"

"Because—" Corvel stopped. Rain ran off his jaw. "Because he doesn't trust himself to see you. Read it yourself, then, if my voice offends you. But someone should say the words aloud. Paper can lie quiet. Voices have to commit."

He found the leaf by feel, by some private index, and held it to her lamp—he had lit the lamp himself while she stood frozen; she hadn't even noticed, and that frightened her more than the knock had—and began to read.

"*Odile.*"

Just the name. But it was the doubled t of him, the cadence of him, the way he pronounced a name the way he drew a headland—with total attention, no flourish, no mercy. The lamplight moved on Corvel's face and Odile gripped the edge of the table and made herself listen.

"*If you are reading this, then Corvel found his courage after all, which means the tide is close and the plates matter more than my comfort. Listen carefully, because I will say these things once, and I have spent ten years deciding how they sound.*

"*What you saw in the margins of the 1739 fair copy was true. Every name. The coast between the Loire and the Sèvre is a graveyard kept by respectable men, and the atlas proves it, line by line and fathom by fathom—that is why it must be finished, and why it must sail, and why I let the river have my name rather than let them have it. You saw the drowned man on the quay. You knew his ear was wrong. You said nothing. I know you said nothing, because I taught you nothing else, and I have regretted that lesson every day since with a thoroughness you cannot imagine.*

"*I told you once that silences keep people safe. That was a coward's cartography, and I apologize for it. Some silences are walls. Mine was a wall, and I built it around myself and called it protecting you, and it was protecting me. What I should have taught you is which silences are load-bearing and which are only hiding places. You have carried mine for ten years. Set it down, Odile. Set it down on paper, where it belongs.*

"*The final plates are not lost. They are hidden where no magistrate would dream of looking, in plain sight, bound in the enemy's own book. Corvel knows where. Trust him as far as the plates and no further—he is a good man who was paid to be a bad one, and that arithmetic never comes out clean. Finish the atlas. Put both our names on it. I am not asking. I have no right to ask. I am telling you what I would do if I were brave, and you are braver than I am, you always were, you were braver the day you stood on that quay and said nothing, because you did it knowing exactly what it cost.*

"*Burn this letter.*

"*—É.M.*"

Silence, except the rain.

Corvel lowered the page. His hands, Odile noticed, were not entirely steady either, and that steadied her, the way a shared tremor always does.

"'Burn this letter,'" she repeated. Her voice came out strange, sanded smooth. "He writes *burn this letter* on a letter he rewrote every year for a decade."

"He's a careful man."

"He's a *coward*." It came out of her like a spark off a grindstone, and she did not take it back. "He let me bury him. He let me stand on that quay and grieve—do you understand? I built my whole life on top of his grave. I measured my days against it. And he was *rewriting letters to me annually*—" She stopped. Breathed. The old discipline. When the hand shakes, put the instrument down. She put both hands flat on the table, on either side of the oilcloth, and looked at Corvel. "The plates. You said he hid the final plates. Where?"

Corvel met her eyes, and in his face she saw the same arithmetic she had practiced for ten years—the cost of speaking weighed against the cost of silence—and watched him begin, at last, to pay it.

"That," he said, "depends on how much you trust me. And before you answer—you should know that the handwriting isn't proof of anything. I've seen forged hands before. Good ones. So before you build ten more years on those letters, mistress, you'll want to do what he trained you to do."

"And what is that?"

Corvel nodded at the drafting table, at the flat drawers where she kept the master's surviving drafts, the practice sheets, the fair copies from the years before the seizure—the ones she had saved by lying, by hiding, by silence.

"Compare the ink," he said. "Line by line. Because if it's a forgery, mistress, it's the finest I've ever seen—and the man who made it wants your atlas finished as badly as Marchand did. Either way, someone knows his hand *intimately*." He paused, and the rain filled the pause. "The question you should be asking is: who besides you?"
