## 5. The Magistrate's Courtesy

The rain had stopped by mid-morning, leaving the streets of Nantes rinsed and shining, the gutters running brown with the residue of the night. Odile was grinding ink when she heard the carriage—too fine a sound for that quarter, the soft iron knock of wheels on wet cobbles, and then the deeper silence of horses brought to a stand.

She knew before she looked up from the mortar who had come. There were only two kinds of carriages in this city: those that carried people who wanted something, and those that carried people who intended to take it.

Through the workshop's leaded window she watched a footman hand down a gentleman in dove-grey, broadcloth brushed to a dull bloom, his hat held at an angle that managed to be both careless and exact. Magistrate Delaunay did not send letters ahead. Letters could be refused; a visit could not. He crossed the street unhurried, and Odile noticed—she made herself notice—the way his eyes went first not to the door but to the window, to her, already watching him. A small courtesy of acknowledgment, or a small demonstration that he had seen her seeing him.

*He has never set foot in this shop in ten years,* she thought, setting down the pestle with exaggerated care, as if any sudden motion might betray her. *And now he comes the morning after a stranger arrives carrying letters I burned at midnight.*

She had burned them—or most of them. One she had kept, pressed inside the false back of the drafting cabinet where Étienne's own plates had slept through a decade of quiet. She told herself it was prudence: evidence of the claim, should she need to test the hand against more samples. She told herself many things at three in the morning. The truth was simpler and worse. The letter was the first thing Étienne had touched since his drowning, and she could no more burn it than she could have burned his body if it had washed ashore.

The bell over the door rang its small honest note.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." Delaunay removed his hat as though entering a church. He was perhaps fifty-five, silvered at the temples in a way that seemed designed rather than earned, and he had the particular handsomeness of men whose faces had never been required to do anything difficult. "I hope you will forgive the intrusion. I was passing, and I said to myself—it has been too long since anyone in this city paid proper respect to the craft under this roof."

*Passing.* The harbor office stood on the far side of the quay. This street led nowhere except to workshops like hers and the tallow-chandler's at the corner.

"Monsieur le Magistrate." She bowed the way apprentices bowed—deep enough, no deeper—and kept her hands folded so their trembling would have somewhere to hide. "We are honored. Though I fear you find us at our least presentable. We are between commissions."

Something moved behind his eyes—not disappointment, not quite interest either, but the flicker of a man marking a page. "Between commissions. Yes." He turned slowly, taking in the room: the great table scarred by thirty years of compass points, the rolls of paper standing in their rack like a choir waiting to sing, the smell of gum arabic and lamp oil and the river that lived in everything within a mile of the water. His gaze traveled across the wall where finished charts hung for customers' inspection—the safe harbors, the commissioned work, Quiberon Bay rendered in loving, unremarkable detail—and paused, just barely, on the stretch of blank wall beside them.

Odile felt the pause the way one feels a finger laid lightly on a bruise.

"You have kept the place exactly," Delaunay said. "I remember it from—" He smiled, apologetic. "Well. From before. When your master was living. I attended a viewing of his work once, at the Chamber of Commerce. Magnificent coastlines. A man who drew the sea as though he had argued with it and won."

"He would have liked to hear that," Odile said. "He thought the sea won every argument."

"And yet they say he drowned." Delaunay clicked his tongue softly, a small performance of regret. "A survey sloop, wasn't it? Off the Vendée coast. Terrible weather that autumn. The Crown lost instruments, papers—an entire season's work, some said. And a good man besides." He turned back to her with sympathy worn so smoothly it might have been lacquer. "You were his apprentice then. That must have been a grief beyond describing—to lose a master *and* the work in a single night."

"The work was recovered," Odile said. Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from somewhere outside herself. "What could be salvaged. The rest is lost."

"Yes." He nodded slowly. "Lost."

The word sat between them on the scarred oak table like a third person.

* * *

He asked to see her current work, and she showed him what there was to show: a chart of the Loire's approaches commissioned by a wine merchant, accurate and dull as bread. Delaunay admired it in all the right places. He admired the hachuring of the sandbanks. He admired the neatness of her sounding figures. He remarked that her lettering had improved—that it resembled her master's without imitating it, which was, he said, the highest compliment one craftsman could pay another.

*He has looked at my work before,* Odile realized, with a coldness that began in her wrists and spread inward. *Not glanced. Studied. He knows my hand the way I know Étienne's.*

"I wonder," Delaunay said, drifting toward the window, hands clasped behind his back in the attitude of a man admiring weather, "when the workshop expects to produce its next commission. Something substantial, I mean. Something worthy of you."

"That depends on the merchants, monsieur. Trade decides these things."

"Trade." He tasted the word. "Yes. Though of course trade is not always free to decide. A merchant commissions a chart of a harbor when a chart of that harbor will make him money. He avoids commissioning—" the faintest tilt of his head, "—other subjects, when other subjects would make him *trouble*. It is remarkable, Mademoiselle Joubert, how well commerce understands what the law sometimes struggles to articulate."

"I wouldn't know, monsieur. I draw coastlines."

"You draw them beautifully." He faced her again, and now the warmth in his face was fully lit, and it was the most frightening thing she had seen in ten years, because it was genuine. Whatever else Delaunay was, in this moment he was not performing. "That is why I have come, truly. Not to threaten—I want you to hear me say plainly that I am not here to threaten—but because I have an interest in this city's welfare, and this city's welfare runs along its waterfront like a spine. Charts are how we understand ourselves. A city that maps itself honestly is a city that can be governed honestly." He smiled. "You see? Even a magistrate believes in cartography."

"Then the Crown should never have suppressed the coastal survey," Odile said, before she could stop herself.

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. She counted them by the drip of the eaves outside, each drop striking the cobbles like a slow heartbeat. Delaunay's expression did not change. That was the mastery of him: nothing changed, and yet everything underneath shifted, the way a coastline shifts after a storm—not visibly, but enough that every pilot's memory of it is suddenly wrong.

"The survey," he said at last, gently, "was not suppressed. It was *suspended*. There is a difference, though I grant it is subtle. Work of that ambition requires coordination between departments—Hydrography, Customs, the Admiralty—and coordination requires trust. There were... questions raised. Irregularities found in certain soundings. Depths recorded that did not accord with the Admiralty's own measurements. Channels marked open that our naval officers found obstructed." He shook his head, sorrowful. "One begins to wonder what purpose such errors serve. Errors in a map are never innocent, Mademoiselle Joubert. They are either ignorance—which is forgivable—or intention, which is not. A channel drawn where none exists is an invitation. To whom, one asks oneself. To smugglers? To enemy privateers? The mapmaker may intend nothing. But the map speaks regardless."

*Étienne's channels were true,* she screamed inside the closed room of her skull. *Every depth, every passage—he took the soundings twice, three times, in different weather—your officers found obstruction because your friends put it there, and the atlas tells the whole story, and that is why it sleeps in a drawer while your name sits in every gap like a watermark.*

She said: "I was nineteen, monsieur. I ground ink and ruled borders. If there were errors, they were his to answer for, and he is dead."

"Yes," Delaunay said. "Dead men answer no questions. It is one of the inconveniences of their condition." He retrieved his hat from the table where he had set it, turning the brim slowly in his fingers. "But I digress from my errand. I came, as I said, about commissions. Here is my thought." He spoke now with the brisk clarity of a man laying cards face-up. "The Chamber of Commerce will shortly require a new series of harbor charts—Nantes itself, Paimbœuf, the river as far as Couëron. Considerable work. Well paid. Prestigious. The kind of commission that would keep a talented woman comfortably busy for a year or more." He let that settle. "I mention it because I intend to recommend the Joubert workshop for it. Unreservedly."

" Monsieur—"

"One condition." Still mild. Still warm. He set the hat on his head, adjusting it with two fingers. "That when the work is done, the charts be complete. Accurate. Every bank, every shoal, every channel drawn precisely as the surveys record them—*our* surveys, mind, the official ones. No initiative. No interpretation. A mapmaker's genius lies in execution, not invention." His eyes rested on hers, gray and patient as winter light. "You understand me. Of course you do. You understood me from the moment I walked in. That is what I have always admired about this house—nothing here is ever quite what it seems, and yet nothing here is ever hidden, either. It takes discipline, that. I respect discipline."

He moved toward the door, and she moved ahead of him to open it, because her legs decided before her mind did, and courtesy was the last armor she had left.

At the threshold he paused. Rain-smell came in around him, clean and cold.

"My regards to your household," he said. "I understand you have had a visitor recently. A traveler. Wet through, by all accounts—there was quite a storm." He smiled at her over his shoulder. "I hope he found what he was looking for."

The bell rang its small honest note behind him.

* * *

She stood in the doorway long after the carriage rolled away, watching the wet street shine, feeling the shape of the sentence he had left behind like a stone swallowed whole. *A visitor. A stranger. Wet through.* Someone had reported the man at her door. Someone had watched her workshop closely enough to count her guests—and reported it quickly enough that the magistrate could arrive within hours wearing warmth instead of irons.

She went back inside and locked the door, though a lock meant nothing against a man like that. Locks were for thieves. Delaunay was not a thief. Thieves took things secretly and ran. Delaunay took things openly and thanked you, and the law called it procedure.

At the drafting cabinet she worked the false panel loose with fingers that would not steady, and drew out the letter she had promised herself she'd burn. Étienne's doubled *t*s marched across the page, alive and insistent, demanding the atlas, demanding the ship, demanding nine days. Beneath it lay the chart she had been inking at dawn—a section of the Vendée coast—and she looked at it now with new eyes, at the careful blanks she had left where Delaunay's family's warehouses crowded the shore, at the channels she had softened because softening them had kept everyone alive for ten years.

*Errors in a map are never innocent.*

No. They were not. Some errors were graves. Some were the only headstones drowned men ever got. And some—she smoothed the letter flat on the table, Étienne's living handwriting staring up at her—some were a debt, and debts, she was learning, always sent someone to collect.

Outside, the eaves had stopped dripping. The day was bright and ordinary and full of people who believed the magistrate's smile. Nine days to tide. Eight now.

And somewhere in this city, a stranger who carried a dead man's hand was waiting to tell her what he wanted in return.
