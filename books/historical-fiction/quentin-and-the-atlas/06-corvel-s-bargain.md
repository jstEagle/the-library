## 6. Corvel's Bargain

The rain had followed Corvel from the quay like a debt collector, and now it stood dripping in the corner of the Joubert workshop while the woman who had made it refused to look at him.

She had not thrown him out. That was something. In his experience, men who carried letters from the drowned were either beaten in the street or prayed over, and Odile Joubert had done neither. She had taken the letters, read the first line, gone white to the lips, and then—this was the part that unsettled him—she had set them down flat on the worktable, weighted the corners with four brass weights, and looked at him as though he were a chart she was trying to read by a bad light.

"You said nine days," she said now. "Say the rest of it."

* * *

Corvel had rehearsed this on the road from Saint-Nazaire, in the way a man rehearses a confession he does not intend to make honestly. He had rehearsed it walking, in taverns, once aloud to a mule that had regarded him with more patience than he deserved. The rehearsal had a shape: he was a courier, hired, paid, ignorant of the contents. He was a stranger to Nantes. He wanted nothing but his fee and a berth out of France.

The rehearsal had died somewhere on the stairs.

Because the workshop was Étienne Marchand's workshop. He had not understood that until he stood inside it. Ten years, and he had imagined the place gone—burned, sold, scattered—and instead here it was, breathing, the same smell of size and lamp oil and the particular sourness of iron-gall ink that he had breathed for two years aboard the sloop *L'Alouette* while Marchand cursed the fog and measured the coast of France inch by forbidden inch. There was the drafting table, scarred where the master used to drive his dividers down like a man pinning a moth. There was the shelf of rollers wrapped in oilcloth. There, on the wall, a chart of the Loire estuary with a boy's careful hand correcting the soundings—and the corrections were right, Corvel knew they were right, because he had been the man in the chains heaving the lead and calling the depths up through cold water while Marchand wrote them down.

She had been that boy. The apprentice. He had seen her once, at Saint-Gilles, when the sloop put in for water—a thin, serious child of perhaps fourteen who had looked at him the way she was looking at him now.

"You served him," she said. It was not a question. "You were on the *Alouette*."

"I was the leadsman," Corvel said. "And the oars, when the wind failed. And the cook, when the cook was drunk. On a survey sloop everyone is everything."

"Then you were there." Her voice did something he could not name—something between hunger and horror. "At the end. When he drowned."

And there it was. The reef under the water line, the one he had sailed ten years to avoid, and she had found it in four sentences.

* * *

He sat down without being asked, because his legs had decided the matter for him. The stool was the one by the door, the low one, and he remembered that Marchand had kept it there for the harbor boys who ran with messages, and that the master had never once let Corvel sit in it, because Corvel was crew and crew stood. Ten years, and the stool was still by the door. It was absurd how much that undid him.

"I was paid," he said.

"Everyone is paid."

"Not like that." He looked at his hands. They were a leadsman's hands, rope-burned and salt-stiffened, and they had taken money twice in their life that he was ashamed of. "After the officials came for the workshop—after the atlas was seized and Marchand was finished in France—there were men who came to the crew. Not the Crown's men. Other men. Men with money that didn't come from any ministry." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it went when he called a sounding. "They paid us to say he drowned. To say it clearly, to say it often, to say it to anyone who asked. The survey sloop lost her master off the Pointe de Saint-Gildas in a squall. Body never recovered. Very sad. Very final."

Odile had gone very still. It was the stillness of the drafting room, he realized—the stillness of a hand holding a pen above wet ink, afraid to breathe.

"They paid you," she said, "to kill him."

"They paid me to bury him." Corvel laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, like a rope parting. "It amounts to the same thing. A man who is dead on paper is dead, madame. Ask any smuggler on this coast. Half the men who sail out of this harbor are legally corpses, and it suits them fine until the day it doesn't."

"And was he? Drowned?"

"I never saw a body." He said it carefully, because the letters on the table said the rest, and he was not ready to say the rest himself. "I saw the squall. I saw the boat come back without him. I saw the men divide the money, and I took my share, and I told the story they gave me, and I told it so well that I believed it myself for a while. That is the trick of it. You tell a lie enough times in enough taverns and it stops being a lie and becomes your life."

"Whose men were they?"

"You know whose men." He met her eyes at last. "The same men who made sure the atlas was suppressed. The same men whose names are *not* on your master's charts, in all the places where names should be. I've spent ten years running cargo for them, madame—tobacco, brandy, indigo, once a cargo I was not paid enough to ask about—and every route I ever sailed, every cove I ever used, was a route off Marchand's plates. They didn't just suppress the atlas. They ate it. They've been navigating by stolen truth for a decade, and getting rich, and hanging the occasional sailor to keep the customs men hungry."

* * *

She turned away from him then, to the window, where the rain was writing its own illegible script on the glass. Corvel watched her back and understood that he had misjudged her entirely on the road. He had imagined a frightened woman who would clutch at any straw that floated from the sea. What stood at the window was not frightened. What stood at the window was doing arithmetic.

"The letters," she said, without turning. "If he is alive, why does he not come himself? Why send a stranger with a story ten years old?"

"Because a dead man who walks into Nantes is a dead man who gets asked questions." Corvel leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the old fear came up his throat, the fear that had ridden with him from Saint-Nazaire like a second passenger. "Because there are men in this city who would pay a great deal to be sure he stays drowned, and the surest way to un-drown a man is to let him be seen. And because—" he stopped.

"Because?"

"Because he's a coward." It came out of him raw, before he could dress it. "I know the breed. I'm one of them. He let the sea take his name rather than face the men he'd robbed with the truth, and he's been hiding behind his own handwriting ever since, and he sent *me*—he sent the man he paid to bury him—because if I turned on him, it would cost him nothing but a letter. Do you understand? He chose me because I'm disposable. I'm the part of his conscience he can afford to lose."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below in the courtyard, water was finding the drain, patiently, the way water does.

"And you came anyway," Odile said.

"I came because they want *me* dead now." He said it plainly, because there was no other way left to say it. He stood, because a man could not bargain sitting on a stool meant for boys. "Three weeks ago the *Perle* came in from the islands with a cargo that was not on any manifest, and the man who owned that cargo is the same man who owns the harbor office's ledgers, and the ledgers are missing pages, madame. Pages that were there when I helped hide them. Someone has been reading what they should not read, and someone above me has decided that the leadsman of the *Alouette* is the loose thread. Two of my old crew are already at the bottom of the roadstead. They didn't drown. I checked."

Now she turned. And he saw it—the thing he had come a hundred leagues to see, the thing he had told himself he didn't need: not pity. Interest. She was weighing him, the way her master had weighed a coastline, looking for the deep water and the shoals.

"The plates," she said. "You said in the letter there were plates. The final plates. The ones that were never recovered."

"I know where they are."

"Then say it."

"Not here. Not yet." He heard his own voice harden, and hated it, and kept it hard anyway, because a man who had nothing left to sell had to sell what he had dearly. "You don't understand what you're asking. If I say the words aloud in this room and you go and fail, I'm a corpse twice over. If I say them and you succeed, I'm still a corpse, because the men who hid those plates will know it was me the moment they're gone. There is exactly one version of this story where I live past the morning tide, madame, and I want to hear you say it."

"Say what?"

"That I sail." His mouth was dry. Ten years of cargo, of silence, of money taken and stories told, and it came down to this, standing dripping on a dead man's floor, begging an apprentice for a berth. "The *Atlas*. Your ship. Your delivery. I know you have one—I know the whole arrangement, I've known for nine days, that's why I'm here and not in Bordeaux. I want passage. Not as crew. As a man who was never born. I want to stand on that deck when the tide turns and watch France become a coastline, and I want the plates to be aboard when I do, because the only thing those men fear more than Marchand alive is Marchand's atlas in print, and the only place it can be printed is beyond their reach. You give me that, and I give you the plates. That is the bargain."

* * *

Odile came away from the window.

She did not come quickly, and she did not come softly. She came the way weather comes, and Corvel, who had spent his life reading weather, felt the pressure drop.

"You want me to understand," she said, "that my master let you buy his death for a handful of écus, that you spent ten years running smuggled goods along the very coastline he mapped, that you are hunted by the same men who killed the sailors whose names I—" she stopped herself, and he watched her stop herself, and wondered what names those were, "—and that now, because your own death has finally become inconvenient to *you*, you have decided to sell me the one thing I have spent ten years of my life trying to finish."

"Yes."

"You are a mercenary, a coward, and a liar."

"Yes."

"And the letters are in his hand."

"They are in his hand."

She stood before the worktable, before the four brass weights and the letters beneath them, and Corvel saw her look down at them the way a woman looks at a well she has drawn from all her life and has just been told is bottomless. When she spoke again her voice had changed. It was quieter. It was worse.

"I could go to the magistrate," she said. "I could tell Delaunay that the leadsman of the *Alouette* is sitting in my workshop offering to sell state secrets. He would protect me. He would hang you. And the plates would stay where they are, and the atlas would stay unfinished, and I would sleep at night. Tell me why I shouldn't."

"Because Delaunay's family name is in those ledgers," Corvel said, "and you know it, and if you go to him you go to the man the atlas was drawn to hang. And because—" he made himself say the last of it, the true last, the part the mule had heard and no one else, "—because if you don't take the bargain, I will sell the plates' location to the other side by Friday, because a drowning man grabs whatever is nearest, and I have been drowning for ten years and I am *tired*, madame. I am so tired. I would rather be the man who helped finish the atlas than the man who burned it, but I will be either. That is the whole of what I am. Choose which."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain was easing, and under the rain he could hear the harbor—rigging ticking against masts, the far bell of the pilot station, the whole breathing city of Nantes lying between this room and the sea.

Odile Joubert looked at him for a long moment. Then she lifted one of the brass weights off the letters, and turned the top sheet toward him, and tapped one line with her finger—the line where the living hand doubled its *t*s, where the dead man's habit walked again across the page.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you will take me to the harbor office. And if the plates are where you say, you sail. And if they are not—"

She did not finish it. She didn't need to. Corvel had spent his life sounding depths, and he knew a bottomless line when he heard one.

"The ledgers," he said quietly. "The plates are bound inside the customs ledgers of the harbor office. Where the whole city can watch them. Where *he* hid them, because he knew no one would ever dare look for truth in the one place in France built entirely out of lies."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was already somewhere else—already measuring, already drafting—and Corvel understood that the bargain had been struck, and that he had just sold the only honest thing he owned: the way in.

"You'd better hope," she said, reaching for her coat, "that your memory of the harbor office is as good as your memory of my master's hand. Because we go tonight, and the tide will not wait for cowards."
