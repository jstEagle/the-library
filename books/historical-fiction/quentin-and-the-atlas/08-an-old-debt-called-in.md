## 8. An Old Debt Called In

The printer's shop on the rue de la Fosse smelled the way it always had: ink and lye and lamp-black, a bitterness that got into the throat and stayed there, so that anyone who spent an afternoon in Madame Roux's back room left speaking in a voice gone slightly hoarse, as though the truth had been sitting on their chest. Odile had loved that smell as a girl. She loved it less now, coming to it with a request that could hang them both.

She had dressed plainly—grey wool, no lace, her hair pinned under a plain cap—and she had walked the long way, past the cathedral and down through the fish market, doubling back twice along the quays until she was certain no one had folded themselves into her shadow. The morning was raw, the Loire running high and brown with autumn rain, and the gulls were quarrelsome over the gutting tables. A harbor this busy should have made her feel anonymous. Instead every face seemed to be counting her steps.

The bell above Roux's door gave its cracked note—the same cracked note it had given for thirty years, never mended because Madame Roux said a bell that rang true invited customers, and a bell that rang broken invited only those who already knew why they'd come.

Inside, two apprentices were pulling a broadsheet at the press, and the great iron beast groaned through its revolution like something dreaming. Paper hung from lines strung across the ceiling, drying in sheets that turned slowly in the draft from the door, pale rectangles swaying like sails seen from below.

Madame Roux stood at the sorting case with a composing stick in her hand, setting type by touch, not looking up. She was smaller than Odile remembered—or perhaps Odile had grown—but her hands had not changed: broad-knuckled, stained black to the second knuckle, hands that looked like they had been dipped to the wrist in night itself.

"You'll want the back room," Roux said, before Odile had spoken a word. "And you'll want to wipe your feet. I've just had the floor done."

* * *

The back room held the same scarred table, the same stool, the same window of bull's-eye glass that turned the street outside into a blur of moving color. A candle burned there even at midday; Madame Roux distrusted daylight for conversations of any consequence, saying the sun encouraged people to perform.

Odile sat. Waited. Watched the older woman come in, shut the door, and stand with her back against it—not sitting, not yet, but looking at Odile the way she might look at a sheet of type she suspected held a wrong letter hidden somewhere among the correct ones.

"Ten years," Madame Roux said at last. "Ten years you've not crossed my door. And now you come dressed like a widow who's sold her good clothes, having walked past my window three times first."

"I came once directly."

"Twice past, once direct." She pushed off from the door and sat opposite, folding her stained hands on the table. "You walk like him, did you know that? Étienne Marchand, when he was working something out and didn't want it worked out in front of witnesses. Same habit of stopping to look into shop windows he had no interest in."

Odile's breath caught. She smoothed it flat. "You knew?"

"I know everything that happens on this street, girl. It's how I've kept my press running and my neck attached through two wars and one regency." Her eyes, small and quick and the brown of old varnish, did not soften. "What I don't know is what you want. And I notice you haven't asked for anything yet, which tells me it's nothing good."

There was no gentle way to begin, so Odile began the way her master would have—with the work, and only the work.

"The atlas exists," she said quietly. "All of it but the final plates. The coastal survey. The one the Crown suppressed."

The change in Madame Roux's face was very small—a stillness around the mouth, a fractional narrowing—and yet Odile felt the temperature of the room shift, the way a cellar cools when a door opens somewhere above.

"That atlas drowned with him," Roux said.

"No. It drowned with everyone who believed he drowned."

For a long moment neither of them moved. From the front of the shop came the rhythmic groan of the press, and beneath it, faintly, the cries of fishwives and the complaint of cart wheels on wet cobbles.

"You're telling me he lives." Madame Roux's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Or you're telling me someone wants me to believe he does. Those are different things, Odile Joubert, and one of them ends with us both swinging from the gibbet at the Porte Sauvetat."

"I know which things end where. I've known for ten years." Odile leaned forward. "I need harbor passes. Two. Forged well enough to pass inspection at the Porte Neuve on festival night. Passes that say we're licensed carriers moving printing stock to a ship at anchor—ink, paper, plates. Nothing that will bear close reading, because there won't be time for close reading. Not on a festival night."

"And the ship?"

"The *Atlas*."

Roux closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them again, all the warmth—if warmth there had ever been—was gone, replaced by something harder and older. "You understand what you're asking. A forged pass isn't ink on paper. It's a signature I don't have the right to make, a seal I'd have to cut, a clerk whose hand I'd have to imitate. If it's found, they trace it here. To this table. To these hands." She raised them, black-stained, and turned them over as if displaying evidence. "These hands have hanged men before. Not mine—I mean the hands that taught me. My father printed seditious pamphlets in '09 and they broke every finger he had and let him learn to set type with his teeth, which he did, God rot them, until they came back and took the rest. So when I tell you I know the price of paper, believe me. I have paid it in family."

"Then I'll ask plainly, and you can refuse plainly, and I'll leave and never trouble you again." Odile drew a breath. "I'm asking you to help finish the work Étienne Marchand gave his life for. Or his death for. Whichever of those is true."

"It matters which," Roux said sharply. "You've thought about that? Because if he's alive, then he let you mourn him for ten years, and that's a debt between you and him, and none of my business. But if he's dead and someone is wearing his handwriting like a stolen coat—" she tapped the table once, hard, "—then you're being led, child. Led by the nose toward whatever the man holding the pen wants you carrying."

"I've thought of nothing else for nine days."

"And?"

"And the handwriting is his. Down to the doubled t's. Down to the way he breaks his e's when he's writing fast and angry." Odile heard her own voice go strange with the admission. "Either he lives, or the man who forged those letters knows his hand better than I do, and I studied under him for six years. I've decided to be a fool about it. It's the only decision that lets me keep working."

Something moved behind Madame Roux's eyes—not softening exactly. Recognition, perhaps. The look of one stubborn creature acknowledging another.

* * *

Roux rose, went to the window, and looked out at the blurred street for a long time. The bull's-eye glass made her silhouette waver, as if she were standing underwater.

"Do you remember," she said without turning, "the winter of '37? When the fever came up the river?"

"I was eleven."

"Your master wasn't. He was forty-one and coughing blood into a rag, and the physicians wanted four livres a visit, and he hadn't four sous, because that man spent every livre on paper and instruments and not one sou on himself. So I paid the physician." She turned. "Four livres a visit, eleven visits, and a jar of Peruvian bark that cost more than my press is worth now, and I never told him. Never once in twenty years did I tell him, because Étienne Marchand could not abide owing anyone anything, and if he'd known, he'd have stopped taking the medicine out of pure spite and died out of pure principle."

Odile stared at her. In six years of apprenticeship she had never heard this. Had never guessed. Étienne had been tall and tireless and dry-voiced, a man who seemed built of whalebone and salt air, and she had never imagined him lying fevered, or owing his pulse to a printer's purse.

"He never knew?"

"He suspected. He was not a stupid man, whatever else he was. He brought me charts sometimes—little ones, harbors I'd no use for, drawn beautifully, worth ten times what I'd laid out. We never spoke of it. That was our arrangement: silence, kept mutually, kept cleanly." She came back to the table and sat. "So yes, Odile. I owed him. And I have carried that debt the way you carry a stone in the pocket—you stop feeling the weight after a while, but you always know which pocket it's in. You've come to collect it, and we both knew that before you wiped your feet."

"I wouldn't—"

"Don't insult me by pretending otherwise. Of course you would. It's what debts are *for*." She waved away the protest like smoke. "But hear me. I'll cut your passes. I'll forge the clerk's hand at the Porte Neuve—I've been copying official signatures since before you were born, and Delaunay's harbor clerks are illiterate men with good pens, which makes them easy. Two passes, festival night, no questions."

Odile's heart lifted, treacherously light. "Thank you—"

"*Sit.*" Roux's palm came down flat on the table and the ink bottles jumped. "That's the half of the bargain. Now the other half, and you'll not like it."

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Outside, the press groaned through another sheet.

"The ledgers," Roux said. "The customs ledgers your atlas hides in. Do you know what's written in their margins? What's written in the columns the Crown pays clerks to keep blank?"

Odile did know. She had known since Corvel first described them, and knowing had sat in her stomach like cold lead ever since. "Crew lists," she said. "Manifests. Names."

"Names of men who sailed and never came home. Men listed as *lost overboard*, *deserted at port*, *wages forfeit*—erased with a stroke of a pen so that the owners wouldn't pay out, so that no widow could file claim, so that no magistrate would ask why the *Sainte-Anne* lost six men in calm water between Nantes and Bordeaux. Six men. I knew two of them. One of them set type for my father when he was a boy, before the sea took him the way the sea takes everything this city loves and pretends not to grieve." Roux's voice did not rise; it grew quieter and heavier instead, like a hull taking water. "They aren't in any parish register. They aren't on any monument. They are nowhere, Odile, except inside ledgers that sit locked in an office guarded by the men who erased them. The Crown suppressed your atlas because it told the truth about smuggling. But smuggling needs sailors, and sailors drown cheap, and drowning men don't testify."

"What do you want?" Odile asked, though she already knew, and some part of her had known since she walked through the door—that cracked bell ringing her in.

"I want their names on the map." Roux leaned across the table, and this close Odile could see the fine tremor in her, the age she usually hid. "Not in a preface. Not in a dedication page some minister's censor can strike before binding. In the coastline itself. Where the *Sainte-Anne*'s crew went into the water, I want their names inked into the shoals, small enough to miss, deep enough to find. Let some captain a hundred years from now run his finger along that coast looking for soundings and find instead six dead men waiting for him. Let the map carry them the way the sea refused to. That is my price. Swear it."

The room was very quiet. Odile thought of Étienne's marginal notes, his careful hand, his terror of naming anything the Crown could hang him for—he had drawn coastlines for ten years and put no names on them but places. To do what Roux asked was to do the thing he never dared. It was also, Odile understood suddenly, the only version of the atlas that would be true.

And Madame Roux knew it. That was the blade of it, laid flat on the table beside the courtesy of the forged passes: loyalty invoked as leverage. And rightly so, Odile thought. Because what was loyalty worth, kept private, kept safe, kept silent? Her master had kept silent for ten years and it had made him a ghost. The drowned had been kept silent by other men's pens, and silence had made them nothing at all.

"I swear it," Odile said. "On the atlas itself. Every name the ledgers erased goes onto the coastline—in the waters where they died, in ink that will outlast the paper it's printed on. Not as a favor. As part of the survey. The map isn't finished without them."

Roux held her gaze for a long moment, hunting for the flaw in it, the way she'd hunt a wrong letter hidden in a line of perfect type. Whatever she found—or didn't—she nodded once, slow, like a verdict.

"Then we have a bargain, and may God forgive us both the methods." She was already rising, going to a cabinet in the corner, unlocking it with a key from around her neck. "Now. Tell me about this festival night, and this friend of yours—Corvel, is it? The one who smells like a man deciding whether to run." She glanced back over her shoulder, sharp-eyed. "Yes, I've seen him too. Don't look surprised. And tell me why, when you talk about him, you flinch."

"He hasn't earned trust. He's earning it. Slowly. Against his own will, I think."

"Mm." Roux drew out a shallow tray of steel punches and gravers, laying them on the cloth like surgical instruments. "Those are the worst kind to depend on and the best kind to have beside you in a dark doorway. Very well. Sit closer. I'll teach you the clerk's hand at the Porte Neuve—it's a cowardly hand, loops everything, crosses nothing twice—which suits our purposes, since a pass signed boldly invites reading, and a pass signed timidly invites nothing but pity."

They worked through the afternoon. Roux cut the passes while Odile practiced the trembling loop of the harbor clerk's signature until her own hand ached with borrowed cowardice. Candlelight replaced daylight; the press in the front fell silent as the apprentices went home; the drying sheets overhead turned slowly in the dark like a fleet at anchor.

It was nearly full dark when Roux finished the second pass, blew on the ink, and held both papers up to the candle—two ordinary-looking documents, licenses to move printing stock, bearing a signature that had never existed and a seal that had never been struck.

"There." She set them down and pressed her blackened thumb deliberately onto the corner of each, leaving a print like a small dark moon. "My mark. Not on the face of them—under the corner fold, where only a counterfeiter would think to look. If either of us swings for this, we swing knowing whose work it was."

Odile took the passes. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed everything.

At the door, with the cracked bell above her and the cold street beyond, Roux spoke once more, quietly, without looking up from the type case she had already returned to.

"One more thing, girl. Your master's letters—the living hand. Bring me one. Not tonight. Before the tide. I set type for forty years; I know what hands look like when they're tired, when they're frightened, when they're lying." At last she glanced up, and her eyes in the candlelight were the brown of deep water over a wreck. "Because if Étienne Marchand is alive, then he owes me a visit. And if he's dead, then whoever wrote those letters owes me an explanation, and I collect debts from the living and the dead alike."

Outside, the rain had begun again, fine and needling, and the lamps along the quay were blooming in the wet dark, one by one, all the way down to the harbor—where, nine days from tomorrow, a ship called the *Atlas* waited at anchor for a cargo of truth that two forged passes and a printer's oath had just become strong enough to carry.
