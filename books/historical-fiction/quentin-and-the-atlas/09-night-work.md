## 9. Night Work

### Odile

The festival came to Nantes the way the tide came—inevitable, indifferent, drowning everything in its path.

From the workshop window Odile watched the quays fill with lantern-light and grease-painted faces, watched the wine carts roll out from the Rue Crébillon and the fiddlers stake their corners, watched the whole city surrender itself to a sanctioned madness that had not been permitted since before the war. The Regent's birthday, someone said. It hardly mattered whose. What mattered was that by midnight every constable in Nantes would be drunk or distracted, and the harbor office—that squat stone building crouched at the water's edge like a toad waiting for flies—would stand all but empty.

She dressed as Roux had instructed: a servant's grey wool, hooded cloak, flat shoes that would not ring on stone. No corset stays that might snap at the wrong moment. Her hair bound tight beneath a cap. In the looking glass she saw a woman she did not recognize, and understood with a strange calm that this was the point. For ten years she had hidden her work inside other work, her truth inside commissioned charts of safe harbors. Tonight she would hide herself inside a stranger.

Corvel arrived at the appointed hour smelling of lamp oil and fear he had tried to wash off with brandy. He looked at her costume and something flickered across his face—not mockery, not quite admiration. Recognition, perhaps. As if he had expected her to falter and was unsettled that she hadn't.

"The watch changes at eleven," he said. "The clerks leave by ten unless there's a ship expected. There isn't. The porters will be in the streets with everyone else." He turned his hat in his hands, once, twice. "You understand what we're doing."

"We're going to fetch paper," Odile said. "Paper that happens to be bound in ledgers."

"And if we're caught—"

"Then I am a servant girl who took a wrong turn in the crowds, and you are whatever you have decided to be tonight." She pulled the hood up. "I've spent ten years deciding what I am when people look at me, Monsieur Corvel. One more night of it costs me nothing."

He flinched as though she'd struck him. She noted it without satisfaction and went to the door.

* * *

### Corvel

The streets were a river of noise and heat, and Corvel moved through them like a man wading upstream against his own better judgment.

He had done this sort of work before—he had done worse than this, that was the poison of it—and his body remembered the craft even as his mind recoiled from it. Keep to the shadow side of the street. Match your pace to the crowd. Never look directly at a door you mean to open. He walked three paces ahead of the woman because that was safer for both of them, because a man alone was invisible and a pair was a story, and stories got told in the morning when the wine wore off and the magistrates woke hungry.

The harbor office rose out of the dark ahead, its windows shuttered, one lamp burning above the door—the courtesy lamp, always lit, always lying. Corvel felt the old sickness climb his throat. The last time he had stood outside a government building at night with theft in his pockets, Étienne Marchand had been beside him, laughing softly at his own nerves, saying *a map is only paper until someone is afraid of it; then it becomes a weapon, and weapons have consequences.*

Marchand was dead. Officially. And here was his apprentice, walking into the lion's den to finish what the lion had killed him for.

"Side door," Corvel murmured, not turning his head. "Service entrance off the coal yard. The lock is old iron; I've opened older."

"You've broken into this building before." It was not a question.

"I worked here. After." He let the word hang. *After* meant after the sloop, after the survey, after the night he had signed his name to documents that buried the drowned men of the *Petite-Hélène* under invented cargo and let the magistrates sleep soundly. "They trusted me. That was the arrangement—I was useful, so they kept me close, and being kept close taught me where everything sleeps."

The coal yard smelled of tar and cold ash. The festival roared distantly, two streets over, a beast fed and content. Corvel knelt at the service door with his picks, and his hands were steady, which surprised him, which shamed him more than shaking would have. A man ought to tremble when he does a thing like this. Steadiness was what they had trained into him, and every steady movement tonight was a small confession that he had been theirs before he was anyone else's.

The lock gave. The door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of dust, sealing wax, and the particular staleness of rooms where men count other men's money.

"After you, mademoiselle," he whispered, and hated how much the words cost.

* * *

### Odile

Inside, the harbor office was a cathedral built to a lesser god.

Odile had imagined ledgers stacked in careless towers; instead she found order, terrible and beautiful—shelves ranked along the walls like pews, each ledger spine lettered in white, years marching backward into the dark. Somewhere among these rows lay the volumes into which Étienne had sewn his final plates, hidden in the one place no searcher would ever look: inside the smugglers' own accounts, folded against the very pages that proved their crimes. He had told her once, in another life, that the safest hiding place is always inside the thing your enemy must protect. She had thought it clever then. Now she understood it as an act of faith so audacious it bordered on despair—a dead man trusting the living to be brave enough to come and take it back.

"Third row," Corvel breathed behind her. "1741 through '44. Customs inward, coastal traffic."

Her eyes had adjusted enough to read the spines. She moved down the shelf with her fingertips, the way she read coastlines in the dark of a chart room, letting touch do what sight could not. 1740. 1741. She drew the first ledger free and felt immediately the wrongness of its weight—too heavy at the back, a stiffness in the binding that no honest account book carried. Someone had sewn stiff sheets of copperplate between the pages and rebound the spine with practiced hands.

Étienne's hands. Ten years dead and still teaching her.

"There are four," she whispered. "He wrote me—in the letters—that there were four."

"Take two each. We wrap them in the sacking, carry them low, walk out like porters who've had too much festival." Corvel was already unrolling the cloth from his shoulder bag. His voice had gone flat and professional, and Odile recognized the tone: it was the voice of a man performing competence so he would not have to feel anything. She knew it well. She had spoken in it for a decade.

She cut the stitching with her bodkin—Roux's bodkin, borrowed with a blessing and a threat—and slid the first plate free. Even wrapped in sacking she could feel its truth through the cloth: the fine tooth of good paper, the faint ridges where the engraver's burin had bitten deep. Coastlines. Her master's coastlines. The Loire bar with its true channels marked, the depths the Crown had ordered erased, the little anchor symbols where ships had anchored that official charts swore were open sea.

Her chest tightened. Not now, she told herself. Grieve later. There is a whole lifetime of grieving later, if we live.

Three plates out. She reached for the fourth—

And froze.

Boots. On gravel, outside the front of the building. Not the stumbling rhythm of festival drunks—measured, unhurried, two men walking a round they had no business walking on a night like this.

Corvel's hand closed on her wrist in the dark, hard as a manacle. Neither of them breathed. The boots crunched closer, paused at the front door, and a voice drifted through the shuttered windows—young, bored, complaining about being given the harbor rounds while everyone else drank the Regent's health. A key rattled. The great lock turned.

They had perhaps forty seconds before lamplight filled the entry hall.

Odile acted without deciding to act. She stuffed the fourth plate into the sacking with its brothers, bundled the whole weight against her stomach, and pulled Corvel toward the back of the building—not toward the coal-yard door, which was too far, but toward the records closet she had mapped in her mind from Corvel's descriptions, a narrow space behind the surveyor's old drafting room where the air smelled of mildew and forgotten voyages.

Inside, she pressed the bundle flat to her body and made herself into furniture. Corvel crowded in behind her, his breath ragged against her ear, and together they listened to the future arrive: footsteps crossing the entry hall, the scratch of a taper, lamplight blooming through the closet's louvres in thin amber blades.

* * *

### Corvel

Through the slats Corvel watched a boy of maybe seventeen set his lamp on the clerk's desk and begin, of all things, to eat a stolen pear.

It should have been funny. It was not funny, because standing in the lamplight's reach, leaning in the doorway of the inner office with his arms crossed and his face half-turned—there was Brissac.

Corvel's whole body turned to cold iron.

Brissac. Senior customs examiner. The man who had sat across the table from him seven years ago and walked him, gently, patiently, through each false line of the *Petite-Hélène*'s cargo manifest, explaining what the numbers needed to say, complimenting his penmanship. The man whose signature sat beneath Corvel's own on the document that had erased nine drowned sailors from the record and converted their deaths into a profitable fiction. Brissac had bought him the way one buys a horse—not cruelly, never cruelly, that was the horror of it—with warm wine and the promise that silence was a kind of career.

He is the reason I know where the ledgers sleep, Corvel thought. Everything I am giving her tonight, I learned kneeling at that man's table.

His knees were shaking. He could feel them through the floor, a fine vibration that any listening ear might catch, and he could not stop them, and the shame of the shaking was worse than the fear that caused it. Seven years of telling himself he had been young, coerced, left no better choice—all of it collapsing now in a mildewed closet while a boy ate a pear and Brissac checked the shutters against thieves.

Beside him, Odile shifted. Not away from him—toward him. Her hand found his sleeve and gripped it, not for comfort; he understood that instantly. It was a signal. *Steady.* The grip of an apprentice steadying a journeyman's hand over a difficult line. She had taken command of the night somewhere between the third plate and the boots on the gravel, and she held it still, silently, without asking his permission, and some drowned part of him rose toward that grip like a man rising toward air.

Brissac said something to the boy. Laughter, brief and dry. Then the lamp was lifted, the shutters checked, and the two men moved toward the front door, talking of the morrow's manifests, voices fading down the quay.

The lock turned behind them.

Still Odile did not move. She counted—Corvel could see her counting, lips moving slightly, the way Marchand used to count between a sounding and its recording—and at sixty she exhaled, slow and complete, and stepped out of the closet into the dark.

"Can you walk?" she asked him. Her voice was quiet, level, almost gentle.

He meant to answer. What came out was: "That was Brissac."

"I know. You stopped breathing. I counted for both of us." She adjusted the bundle beneath her cloak, and he saw that she had already cinched it to her body with the sacking's own ties, already turned herself into a woman carrying nothing more dangerous than laundry. "We go out the coal yard, west along the wall, into the festival crowd before the watch changes. You will keep your eyes on my shoulders and think of nothing. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Corvel said, and discovered he meant it.

Because here was the thing he would examine for the rest of his life, turning it over like a coin he could not spend: in the closet, paralyzed, he had finally understood what courage was. Not the absence of fear—he was soaked in fear, marinated in it—but the discipline of moving anyway, step by numbered step, exactly as Odile was doing, exactly as Marchand had done on the deck of a sloop with magistrates' men closing in. Fear was not the opposite of competence. Fear, properly handled, was its fuel.

"Lead," he said. "I'll follow."

* * *

### Odile

The coal yard again, the tar smell, the door swinging shut behind them with a click soft as a period at the end of a sentence.

Odile kept them in the black seam between wall and fence, counting her own steps the way she counted fathoms, and when they reached the mouth of the alley she paused and let the festival take them—not all at once, but gradually, the crowd closing around their two bodies like water closing over stones. Fireworks burst red and gold above the cathedral spire. Somewhere a choir of drunk dockworkers was murdering a sailor's song with tremendous love. Nobody looked at a tired servant girl and a stooping man in a plain coat. Nobody ever looked at the invisible.

But her heart was hammering against the plates strapped beneath her coat, four sheets of engraved truth pressed to her ribs like a second skeleton, and with every step she thought: *these were worth a man's life; these were worth ten years of my life; these had better be worth this.*

At the corner of the Rue de la Fosse she stopped in the lee of a wine stall and unwrapped the bundle just far enough to confirm what her fingers already knew. Four plates, intact, uncreased. The fireworks lit them briefly—a coastline flashing white, a channel opening like a vein of silver—and then the dark took them back.

"They're safe," she said. "Go home, Corvel. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we begin copying."

He hesitated. In the garish light of the festival his face looked ten years older than it had nine days ago at her door, and younger too, somehow, as if something frozen in it had begun, painfully, to thaw.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." He cleared his throat. "In there. When I—" He gestured vaguely backward, toward the harbor office, toward Brissac, toward the entire architecture of his guilt. "Thank you for counting."

"Someone counted for me once," Odile said. "It seems to be how the work gets done."

She left him standing in the firework-light and walked home through the celebrating city with the atlas sleeping against her heart, and it was only in her own workshop, door barred, lamp trimmed, that she allowed herself to unwrap the plates fully and spread them on the drafting table where Étienne Marchand had once stood.

The candlelight fell across the first sheet, and Odile leaned close—and stopped.

There, in the margin, in the lower right-hand corner where a mapmaker signs his proof, ran a line of small, sure notation. She knew the hand before her mind finished forming the thought. The doubled t. The downward hook of the final stroke. A hand she had imitated for ten years until it lived in her own fingers.

But it was not the handwriting that stopped her breath.

It was the date beside it, written in the same living ink:

*For Odile — the third of this month.*
