## 10. The Handwriting Again

The plates came out of the harbor office smelling of salt and other men's secrets, and Odile did not dare look at them until she had locked the workshop door, drawn both shutters, and sat with her hands flat on the table for a full minute, letting her breathing slow to the pace of ordinary work. Ordinary work. As if there were such a thing left in the world.

Lamplight first. She trimmed the wick, adjusted the shade of green glass that turned her corner of the table into a pool of submarine light—the same lamp Étienne had used for fine inkwork, the glass still clouded with ten years of her own breathing. Then the plates themselves, unwrapped from Corvel's oilcloth with fingers that refused, at first, to be steady.

Seven copperplates. Not copper at all, she saw now—tin, cheap tin, chosen because it could be bound flat inside a ledger spine and pass for binding boards. The smugglers who had hidden them had never known what they were hiding. That was the joke of it, she thought, and the grief of it: the atlas's final truth had spent a decade filed between columns of contraband brandy, catalogued by clerks who could not read what they were guarding.

She laid them in order by their edge-marks—Étienne's system, north to south along the coast—and began with the first.

And stopped.

* * *

It was not the coastlines. She knew those coastlines better than her own face; she had redrawn them from memory through ten sleepless years, and what lay etched beneath her fingertips matched her memory stroke for stroke, sounding for sounding, the little hook of the Île de Noirmoutier channel rendered in his particular shorthand—a double tick where other mapmakers used an arrow. Seeing them again was like hearing a voice on a stair in a house where everyone you loved was dead.

No. It was the margins.

Étienne Marchand kept a clean plate. That was doctrine with him, preached at her across this very table until she could recite it: *the margin is the map's conscience, and a conscience must not clutter itself.* Dates, initials, nothing else. She had obeyed the doctrine for a decade without once asking why he'd held it so fiercely, and now, tilting the first plate into the lamplight, running the pad of her finger along its lower border, she understood.

There was writing there. Fine, dry-point scratching, almost invisible unless the light raked it—the work of someone who had no burin and no time, using whatever point came to hand, perhaps a pin, perhaps the broken tip of a graver. Small letters, cramped, slanted hard to the right.

Dated 1753.

Her mind performed a small, precise refusal. 1753 was wrong. The plate had been made in '44 or thereabouts; the seizure had been in '45; Étienne had drowned—had been said to drown—in the spring of '47, ten years ago this past March, and she had stood on the quay at Paimbœuf and watched them drag the river for three days and bring up nothing but a boot and half a net. A date of 1753 scratched into a plate hidden since before his death meant one of two things, and her hands went cold around the edges of the tin because she already knew which one she believed.

Either someone had forged these notes years ago in anticipation of a moment that hadn't yet come—

—or the dead man had come back to his own maps.

*Joubert,* she thought wildly. *You are talking to tin.* But her master had taught her that a mapmaker's first discipline was to report what was there, not what she wished were there, and what was there was handwriting she would know blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. The doubled t. The way the downstroke of the d hooked left like a man glancing over his shoulder. She had spent ten years forging that hand herself, completing his plates, matching his lettering for the labels and soundings, and she knew its every trick because she had stolen every one of them. This was not her theft. This was the original.

She set the first plate down with exaggerated care, as one sets down something sleeping, and took up the second.

* * *

The notes accumulated as she worked through them, and they were not random. That was what undid her—not the existence of the writing but its *purpose*. These were working notes. Corrections. On the third plate, a warning: *shoal has shifted since survey — locals say the winter of '49 moved it a cable's length east. Verify.* On the fourth, beside the mouth of the Loire: *they have deepened the channel for the privateers' sake. Redraw. The map must lie about the river if it will not lie about the men.*

He had been maintaining the atlas. All these years, wherever he was, however he lived, some part of him had been standing watch over these buried plates the way she stood watch over the finished sheets under her floorboards—two keepers, ten years apart in the dark, each believing the other gone.

Except he had known. Hadn't he? He had scratched these notes knowing—or hoping—that someday the plates would reach her hands, and that she would read them. A message could only be a message if it expected a reader. Her eyes stung and she let them, because there was no one to see and because refusing to weep over a man twice seemed like greed.

Then the sixth plate.

She nearly skipped its margins, because the fifth plate's notes had trailed off into a single line—*if you are reading these, Joubert has them, or Joubert is dead, and either way finish it*—and she needed a moment with that, needed to press her palms against her eye sockets and breathe the smell of lamp oil and tin until her heart agreed to continue. When she picked up the sixth plate she held it at arm's length first, the way one approaches a dog that has bitten.

The margin note was longer here. Longer, and dated later than all the others: 1756, the year the workshops along the quay had begun calling her *la petite veuve du trait*, the little widow of the penstroke, because she took no commissions she couldn't finish alone and wore black more often than not.

He had written:

*Odile.*

Not *Joubert.* Not *the apprentice.* Her name, spelled the way he had always spelled it when he was pleased with her—with the final e given its full flourish, a small flag planted at the end of the word.

*Odile — if this reaches you, then you found what I could not carry out myself, which does not surprise me. You were always better at finishing than I was. Forgive the theater of my death; it was the only door they left open. Do not look for me. Looking for me is how they will find you. If I am alive, know it this way instead: every night I draw your window in my head, the third from the left, and tell myself the light means the work goes on. Keep the light burning. Finish the coasts. Name the drowned. Yours in everything I failed to be, É.M.*

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. The candle burned down through two inches of itself; the green glass shade made the whole room feel like the inside of deep water, and perhaps that was fitting, because she had spent ten years at the bottom of the sea with him and only now understood that he had been at the bottom of it too, on the surface of the earth, alive, drawing her window in his head.

The third window from the left. He had named it. She looked up at it now—her shuttered window, the one she opened on summer mornings to check the weather the way other women checked their hearts—and felt the uncanny, unbearable sensation of being seen across a decade. He had watched her light. He had counted her windows. Somewhere in this city or within reach of it, a man she had mourned annually, methodically, with the same disciplined grief she brought to everything, had been doing arithmetic with her lamplight.

Anger arrived late, as anger does after shock, and it arrived wearing grief's clothes so that for a while she could not tell them apart. He had *let* her mourn. Worse—he had engineered the mourning, staged the drowning, closed the door on his own life and left her holding the atlas like a widow left holding a debt. Ten years of silence, and the silence had been a choice, renewed nightly, each night he drew her window and did not knock. And yet the note itself confessed it—*forgive the theater, it was the only door they left open*—and she remembered, with a lurch, the night of the seizure, the magistrates' men going through the drawers, Delaunay's predecessor reading aloud the passages that proved the harbor pilots were paid to misguide revenue cutters, and how Étienne had looked at *her*, one glance across the ruined workshop, and she had understood even at sixteen that the look meant *forget me, forget this, live.* She had thought she was learning obedience that night. Perhaps she had been learning his plan.

Or he had learned nothing and planned nothing, and the note was the forgery Corvel's masters wanted it to be, bait dressed in a dead man's handwriting. The thought circled back the way doubt always does. She pushed it down—but not out. Hope, her master had told her once, is the least reliable instrument on any chart. It drifts.

She would test it. That was the answer, the only one her training offered: test the instrument. Tomorrow—no, tonight—she would compare these scratchings against the letters Corvel carried, against the surviving drafts in the cedar chest, against the plates themselves, line by line, t by doubled t. If a forger had made these, the forgery would show somewhere, because she knew Étienne Marchand's hand better than God did.

But even as she reached for the cedar chest, her eye fell on the last line of the note, the instruction he had saved for last, and her breath caught on it a second time as though reading it fresh:

*Name the drowned.*

The ledgers she had copied from—the customs books, the manifest records—those were the smugglers' ledgers, and smugglers do not record the men they lose. But she remembered the winter of '46, the cutter *Sainte-Anne* lost off Pornic with nine souls, and how no list had ever been posted, no wages paid to widows, no names entered anywhere official—as though the sea had swallowed the men and the network had swallowed the fact of them. Étienne had known those men. Had drunk with their captain. And somewhere in the margins of his mind, all along, the atlas had been meant to carry what the ledgers erased.

She looked at the seventh plate, still wrapped, untouched. The final sheet. The coast southward toward the Spanish march, the stretch where the *Sainte-Anne* had gone down. Slowly, she peeled back the oilcloth.

In the margin, in that same dry, desperate scratch, one last note. Shorter than the others. Dated barely a year ago.

*They know the plates are here. They are coming to burn the ledgers. Tell Joubert: not much time. And tell her the third window is lit tonight. It is always lit. I have never once seen it dark.*

Odile sat very still.

Tonight. Whoever had scratched that line had written it within living memory of *this* night, watching her window, knowing the ledgers were marked for destruction, knowing the clock was running—and if the note was true, then the man who wrote it was close enough to see her lamp right now, close enough that if she rose and threw open the third shutter from the left, she might catch him looking back.

Her hand went to the shutter bar.

Ten years of discipline held it there. *Do not look for me. Looking for me is how they will find you.* She pressed her forehead against the cool wood of the shutter instead, feeling the street below her—wet cobbles, distant gull-cries, a cart axle complaining somewhere near the salt warehouses—and knowing, with a certainty that frightened her more than any magistrate, that somewhere out in all that darkness, a pair of eyes was on her light.

She whispered it to the empty room, to the tin, to the man who might or might not be alive to hear it:

"Then stop counting, old man. Come and help me finish."

The lamp burned on. And below, at the corner where the rue de la Fosse bends toward the river, a figure detached itself from a doorway, looked up once at the third window, and walked away into the rain.
