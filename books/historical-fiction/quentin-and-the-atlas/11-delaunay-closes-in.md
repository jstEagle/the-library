## 11. Delaunay Closes In

There was a particular pleasure, Magistrate Armand Delaunay had found, in drinking chocolate in a room that belonged to a man who had drowned.

Not the drowned man's room, precisely—Étienne Marchand had owned nothing worth owning, and what little he had possessed had been scattered by the Crown's clerks like chaff before a fan. But the workshop on the rue des Salorges still smelled of him. Delaunay had noticed it the moment the boy showed him up the narrow stair: linseed oil and cut vellum and something older, a mineral tang like rain on slate, the smell of ink that had been mixed by a particular hand in a particular way for twenty years and could not be unlearned. Ten years, and the room had not let him go.

Delaunay set down his cup and let his gaze travel.

The workshop was smaller than he remembered from the seizure—though he had been younger then, a procureur's clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a superior's hand on his collar, and everything had seemed larger in those days, including his own importance. Now the benches were Odile Joubert's, the commissions hers, the name over the door hers. Joubert, cartographer. A modest name. A modest business. Charts of safe harbors, coastal profiles for merchant captains, the kind of work that paid a woman's rent and asked no questions of anyone.

And yet.

He turned the cup slowly on its saucer. The chocolate was excellent—she had served it without being asked, which told him she had expected him, or at least had prepared for the possibility. That was interesting. A woman genuinely surprised by a magistrate's visit would have fumbled; a woman expecting one served chocolate as if he were an uncle come for Epiphany.

"You have a fine view of the harbor," he said.

"Better in summer." Her voice was level, pleasant, pitched to carry no more than courtesy required. "The masts block it now. Half the fleet is fitting out for the Islands run."

"Half the fleet is always fitting out for something." He smiled. He had been told, in his youth, that his smile was his best feature—the kind of face that put witnesses at ease, that made juries trust him, that made men confess things they had sworn to no one. He had spent thirty years polishing that smile the way a jeweler polishes a stone, and he knew exactly what it was for. "You've done well for yourself, Madame Joubert. Or shall I say Mademoiselle? Forgive me. In the provinces one loses track of such things."

"Mademoiselle," she said. "And the provinces are kind enough not to keep track."

"No," he agreed. "They keep other things instead."

He let the silence sit between them like a third guest. She did not fill it. That was the first mark against her, and he noted it with something close to admiration. Guilty people filled silences. Innocent people filled silences too, but differently—they babbled, they laughed nervously, they offered wine and gossip and the weather. Odile Joubert did none of these. She sat with her hands folded on her apron and let him do the work of the conversation, as if she had all the time in the world and he were the one on trial.

Which, in a sense, he was. He had learned long ago that every interrogation was a trial with two benches, and the magistrate did not always occupy the higher one.

"I'll come to the point," he said, "because I know how a working day is precious to you. There was a theft at the harbor office. Nine nights ago. During the festival, when every watchman in Nantes was looking at fireworks instead of doors."

He watched her hands. They did not move. Her face did a small, correct thing—eyebrows lifting, lips parting—the face of a woman hearing news for the first time. It was a very good face. It was, he thought, almost too good; it arrived a half-beat late, the way a translation arrives after the original.

"How dreadful," she said. "What was taken?"

"Ledgers." He let the word fall and watched where it landed. "Customs ledgers. Old ones. The clerk who keeps them swears the office was locked, the key accounted for, nothing disturbed but the dust on the shelf where three volumes had stood. Someone went in during the fireworks, took three ledgers, and left everything else—coin, plate, the strongbox itself—untouched." He shook his head with the mild bewilderment of a man baffled by the criminal mind. "Who steals paper, Madame Joubert, when there is silver in the room?"

"A scholar," she said.

The answer came too fast and was too clever, and he saw her realize it a moment after she had said it, saw the faint tightening at the corner of her mouth—the self-reproach of a fencer who has riposted before she meant to. He almost laughed. Ten years of silence, and one word had cracked her composure like ice on a pond.

"A scholar," he repeated, tasting it. "Yes. Or a mapmaker."

"Or an eccentric. Nantes has its share." She rose, unhurried, and moved to the window as if the conversation were a chore she could set aside. Below, the harbor lay in its morning haze, masts like a burned forest. "I confess I don't see what this has to do with my workshop, Monsieur le Magistrate. Unless you suspect me of stealing ledgers, which I would have thought beneath even the most ambitious cartographer."

"Nothing is beneath anyone," Delaunay said comfortably, "who wants something badly enough. That is the first lesson of my profession." He joined her at the window—not too close; he had learned the geometry of menace, and proximity was a currency best spent in small denominations. "May I tell you what I think, Madame Joubert? You will correct me where I'm wrong. You seem a woman who values accuracy."

She said nothing. The silence was its own correction.

"I think that ten years ago, a man named Étienne Marchand undertook a survey of this coast that he was not commissioned to make. I think he measured depths he had no business measuring, charted inlets that appear on no other chart, and wrote down—somewhere in that obsessive, beautiful hand of his—the movements of ships that were never entered in any customs book. I think certain persons of consequence in this city found that survey inconvenient. I think it was suppressed. I think Marchand drowned—or was said to have drowned—very shortly after. And I think," he said gently, "that the woman who apprenticed in this room, who ground his pigments and cut his quills and learned his hand so well she could sign his name to a chart and fool a harbormaster, never quite stopped working on it."

The river sounds came up through the glass: a bell buoy, the cry of gulls, the groan of a hull against the quay. Odile Joubert turned from the window and looked at him, and for the first time her composure showed its seams—not in her face, which remained composed as a chart's blank margin, but in her eyes, which had gone from warm to winter in the space of a breath.

"You describe a tragedy, Monsieur le Magistrate," she said. "And a slander. My master was drowned off the Pointe du Grouin. His body was never recovered, but the sea does not often return what it takes. I finished his commissions because his widow—" she paused, and he caught the pause, filed it—"because his clients needed the work done, and because it was mine to do. That is all."

"His widow," Delaunay said softly. "Yes. Madame Marchand removed to Angers, I believe, after the seizure. You kept the workshop. Curious arrangement. An apprentice inheriting a master's premises, his clients, his tools." He returned to his chair and his chocolate, unhurried, letting her see that he had all day and the whole machinery of the Crown behind him. "Unless, of course, the workshop was never his at all in the way people supposed. Unless the premises were always meant to pass to the one person who could continue the work without anyone thinking to look."

"You have a poet's imagination," she said.

"I have a clerk's memory. It is worse." He drank. The chocolate had gone lukewarm, but he savored it anyway, the way he savored all the small luxuries his position permitted—luxuries that certain families of this city made certain he could afford, and which bound him to them more tightly than any oath. He was not a fool; he knew what he was, and whom he served, and he had made his peace with it the way a man makes peace with a limp. "Madame Joubert, I am going to offer you something, and I want you to understand that I offer it sincerely, because I think you are in danger, and because—I will admit this much—there is a part of me that has always regretted what was done in this room ten years ago."

That much was true, and he let the truth do its work. He watched it land in her: the flicker of something that was not trust but wanted to be.

"The atlas," he said. "If it exists. If it survived the seizure, if it has been hidden or completed or added to in the years since—it is contraband. Worse: it is treason. A chart of this coast in unauthorized hands is a gift to England. Every inlet, every unwatched cove, every depth and tide—a hostile squadron could run this coastline with it and never touch bottom. That is how the Crown sees it, and the Crown's view is not mine to argue." He set down the cup. "But I am not the Crown, Madame Joubert. I am a man who has watched this city for thirty years and knows which of its magistrates take bribes from which shipowners, and I am telling you, as one keeper of inconvenient knowledge to another: bring the atlas to me, and I will see it destroyed quietly, and I will see you protected. From the men who killed your master. From the men who will kill you if they think the atlas exists. From the men," and here he lowered his voice, made it almost kind, "who sent a stranger to your door with letters in a dead man's hand."

There it was. He had held it back the whole visit, the way a surgeon holds back the knife, and now he watched it go in.

Her breath stopped. Not long—a half-second, less—but he had spent his life counting half-seconds, and hers was a full confession to anyone who knew how to read. The stranger. The letters. He had said it plainly, without evidence, a stone thrown into dark water to hear how deep it was, and the depth of her silence told him everything: the man existed, the letters existed, and she had not reported either.

"You know about him," she said at last, and her voice had changed—flatter, warier, the voice of a woman stepping off solid ground.

"I know a man calling himself Corvel took a room at the Sign of the Three Anchors eight nights ago, paid in mixed coin, and has been seen twice on the rue des Salorges after dark. I know he came off a Breton coaster and that before that he served, under another name, on a survey sloop out of this port." Delaunay folded his hands. "I know that the men who own that sloop's history would very much like to know where he sleeps. I know it because they asked me—politely, over dinner, the way one asks a favor—to let them know if he surfaced. And I declined, Madame Joubert, because I do not do that kind of work for them anymore, and because I would rather this business end in my study than in a ditch."

"Then why tell me?" Her voice was steady, but her hands had left her apron and found the edge of the bench, and she gripped it the way a sailor grips a rail.

"Because you are the only person in Nantes who can end it." He rose, and let the full weight of his office settle over the room—the seals on his letter case, the King's commission in his breast, thirty years of quiet, purchased, complicit authority. "The atlas comes to me, and Corvel leaves on whatever ship will take him, and no one is hanged, and no one is drowned, and this workshop goes on making its safe little charts of safe little harbors until you die old and respected. That is what I am offering. Protection. For a dead man's paper."

"And if I refuse?"

He smiled again—the polished stone, the jeweler's work—and gathered his hat and cane with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once needed to hurry.

"Refuse?" He shook his head, almost sorrowful. "You misunderstand me. You have already refused, Madame Joubert—ten years ago, every night you lit that lamp, every plate you hid, every silence you kept. Refusal is not a decision you make today. It is a debt you have been paying all along, and today I have merely come to read you the ledger." At the door he paused, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "I will give you until the morning tide after next. Not out of mercy. Because I am curious, and curiosity is the one luxury I have never been able to afford, and I would like to see the atlas before it burns. Everyone who has ever worked on it has wanted it to be seen. Even you. Even him. It is the curse of mapmakers—you cannot bear for the truth to stay hidden, even when the truth will kill you."

He let himself down the stair, past the apprentices' benches, past the smell of linseed and vellum and the ghost of Étienne Marchand, and out into the street, where the harbor wind carried the smell of pitch and salt and the far-off thunder of a ship's canvas filling.

Behind him, in the window above, a lamp went out. He did not need to see it to know. He had counted her silences for an hour, and he knew exactly what she would do with the time he had given her.

She would spend it working.

And somewhere across the city, in a room at the Sign of the Three Anchors, a man who had already been offered his own pardon at the price of her name was running out of tides to count.
