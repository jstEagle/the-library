## 14. Six Days to Tide

The workshop had grown eyes.

Odile noticed them the way a mapmaker notices a coastline that has shifted since the last survey—not all at once, but in small wrongnesses that accumulated until the whole chart was suspect. The boy who sold chestnuts on the corner of the rue Crébillon had been there three days running, though his brazier never seemed to warm him and he never sold anything at all. A fiacre stood opposite the door each morning with its driver asleep in a way no honest driver slept, upright and attentive as a heron. And twice now, when she had opened the shutters before dawn to check the light, a man across the street had turned away too quickly, pretending interest in the shuttered windows of the glover's shop next door.

They were Delaunay's men. She was certain of it, though certainty was not proof, and proof was what the magistrate dealt in. He would not raid her yet. He would wait, patient as tide, for her to do something that could be named a crime. Which meant the workshop itself had become the most dangerous room in Nantes.

"We move tonight," she told Madame Roux, standing in the printing house among the smell of ink and hot metal while apprentices stacked formes around them like masonry. "Everything. The plates, the paper stock, the finished sheets. If they come through that door, I want them to find nothing but commissioned charts of safe harbors and an apprentice who has lost her mind with grief."

Roux looked up from the galley she was setting, her fingers black to the second knuckle, her gray hair pinned back with what appeared to be a pencil. She was sixty if she was a day, built like one of her own presses—short, iron-framed, immovable—and she had run illegal pamphlets through the diocesan censor's own courtyard during the last war without losing so much as a font of type.

"Cellar under my old shop on the quai de la Fosse," she said. "The one the flood ruined. Landlord thinks it's still full of river water. It isn't; I pumped it out myself in '43 and never told him why." She wiped her hands on her apron, which only redistributed the ink. "It will be damp enough to grow mushrooms and dark enough to hang a man. Perfect conditions for cartography."

"I need light."

"You'll have light. You'll also have cold, and wet stone, and my company, which is worse than either." Roux pulled down a lantern from its hook and began filling it with a deliberation that was, Odile understood by now, a form of affection. "Six days, you said."

"Five now. The tide doesn't negotiate."

"The tide," said Roux, "is the only honest thing left in this city." She thrust the lantern at Odile like a weapon being issued. "Go home. Sleep if you can. Come back after compline, when the streets belong to drunks and priests, and we'll carry your atlas out under a load of spoiled paper nobody wants."

* * *

They moved at night, as everything moved now.

Two carts, two trips, the plates wrapped in oilcloth and buried beneath reams of misprinted psalters that Roux had been failing to sell honestly for years. Odile walked beside the first cart with her hand resting on the cargo as though it were a sleeping animal she dared not wake. Every shadow between the printing house and the quay resolved, in her exhausted mind, into a watching figure. Once a cat spilled from a doorway and she nearly screamed; once a watchman's lantern swung toward them and Roux called out a greeting so easy and vulgar that the man laughed and waved them past.

The cellar smelled of river and old stone, of drowned years. Water still wept down one wall in a slow silver thread that Roux had channeled with a gutter into a cask, so that somewhere in the darkness water dripped with the regularity of a clock. They hung two lanterns behind screens of sailcloth so the light would not show at the street grating. They set trestles, stretched paper, laid out pens and the good ink Odile had ground herself from gallnuts and gum arabic because bad ink ruined coastlines the way bad witnesses ruined trials.

Then Odile sat down before the first unfinished sheet—the approaches to the Vilaine, where the channel split around a shoal that official charts placed half a league east of where it actually lay, east because the pilots who paid Delaunay's protection ran their cargoes over the true water and did not want it published—and began to work.

She worked by feel as much as sight. That was the thing no one who had not served ten years at a drawing board could understand: the hand remembered what the eye forgot. Her wrist knew the weight of a fair coastline, the unbroken confidence of a line drawn in one breath. Her fingers knew how hard to bear down for a line that would survive damp, how lightly to touch for a sounding that might need revision. In the cellar's imperfect light, with the ink sometimes pooling treacherously in the cold, she learned to draw the way blind men read—by pressure, by rhythm, by the faint drag of nib on rag paper telling her more than her straining eyes could.

Hour by hour the Vilaine took shape. Hour by hour the exhaustion stopped being something she suffered and became something she used. There was a clarity that came past the third sleepless night, a stripped-down state in which the body gave up complaining and simply served, and in that state Odile found she could hold an entire sheet in her head at once—every sounding, every bearing, every name—so that when her eyes failed her in the lantern-dark, the map went on building itself behind them anyway.

Roux kept her alive. The printer came down at intervals with bread and cheese and wine cut with water, and stood over her shoulder reading proofs of the lettering, and argued with her about names.

"You can't put *Pointe des Noyés* on a chart," Roux said, tapping the margin with an ink-black finger. "No hydrographer will print it. It reads like a curse."

"It is a curse. That point is named for the crew of the *Sainte-Anne*, and the ledgers say she never existed. If the atlas won't say they existed, then the atlas is just a prettier version of the ledgers."

"And if Delaunay reads that name, he knows exactly whose work this is and exactly why."

"He already knows why. He's known for ten years. What he doesn't have is proof, and I am done letting him confuse the two." Odile drew the line of the point itself, long and low and dangerous, and beneath it, in letters small enough to require a lens but permanent as any sounding: *ici périrent les hommes de la Sainte-Anne*. "He can read it or not. The sea will keep it either way."

Roux was quiet a moment. Somewhere above them a cart rattled over the quay stones, and the drip in the corner counted out its patient time.

"Étienne would have hidden those names in cipher," she said at last. "Buried them in numbers. He was always a coward about ink and brave about everything else."

"Yes," Odile said. "I'm doing the part he couldn't."

She meant it without bitterness, which surprised her. Ten years of anger had worn down, the way headlands wear, into something smoother and stranger—not forgiveness exactly, but an understanding of the shape of another person's fear. She had been afraid for ten years herself. She had simply chosen a different cowardice: hers had kept her at the drawing board instead of driving her into the sea.

* * *

On the fourth day in the cellar—it was the fifth day before the tide, though Odile had begun counting hours rather than days—Corvel came back.

She heard him before she saw him: the scrape of the outer stair, a knock arranged in the pattern they'd agreed, two and one and two. Roux lifted the lantern screen; the door opened; and Corvel came down into the light looking like a man who had swum a river in his clothes.

Not rain this time. Sweat. His face was gray, his collar torn at the back as though someone had grabbed him, and there was a fresh bruise rising along his jaw like a storm front coming up from the southwest.

"They made me an offer," he said, without preamble, without sitting, though his legs visibly wanted him to sit. "Three days ago. My pardon against you. I told them I needed time to think, which bought me three days, and today the three days ran out, and I told them no."

Odile set down her pen. The nib left a small bloom of ink on the Vilaine's eastern shore, a flaw she would have to lift with a knife later, and she noted it with the detached irritation of a craftsman even as her heart hammered.

"Why?" she asked. "You owed me nothing. You owe Étienne less—you told me yourself you took his money to stay silent."

"I did." He laughed, short and ugly. "And I've been telling myself for nine days that I'd sell you both if it kept me breathing, and meaning it, mostly. But then I sat in a room with those men and listened to what pardon sounds like when they say it aloud, and I understood that the pardon lasts exactly as long as I'm useful, and that a man who sells one mapmaker will be expected to go on selling things forever, and that eventually I'd be worth more dead than useful. Whereas—" he touched the bruise along his jaw, wincing, "—whereas saying no at least means I know who's going to kill me."

"That's not courage," Roux said from the shadows. "That's arithmetic."

"It's both," Corvel said. "I've discovered most courage is. You do the sums until the only answer left standing is the one that costs you, and then you call it honor because the alternative is calling it stupidity." He lowered himself onto a crate at last, heavily, like rigging coming down. "I refused them, Madame Joubert. Whatever else I am, whatever I did aboard that sloop and after—I refused them. Write that in your atlas if you have a margin to spare."

"There's no margin," Odile said. "But I'll remember it."

Something eased in his face—not gratitude, exactly; relief wearing gratitude's clothes. Then his eyes went past her, to the trestles, to the half-finished sheet glowing under the lantern, and she watched him take in the work itself for the first time with something close to awe. All those soundings. All those corrections. A decade of a dead man's truth lying in wet ink in a flooded cellar.

"How much is left?" he asked.

"Two sheets unfinished. One destroyed beyond saving—I've been redrawing it from memory. Lettering throughout. The title page." She calculated, and the calculation hurt. "If nothing goes wrong, I finish with a day to spare. If anything goes wrong—"

"Something's gone wrong already." Corvel leaned forward, and the lantern caught the fear in him, raw and undisguised. "When I left them today, after I refused—they didn't stop me. Do you understand? A man refuses that kind of offer, they break his arms as a receipt. Instead they let me walk. They let me walk, and I made sure I wasn't followed, I doubled back twice through the faubourg, I did everything right—and Madame, I want to be wrong about this, but a smuggler who isn't angry is a smuggler who doesn't need to be. They let me go because I'm worth more to them walking than bleeding."

"As a guide," Roux said flatly.

"As a compass needle," Corvel said. "They're not hunting me. They're sailing me. Every turn I make, they mark it down, and sooner or later my course leads here—or to wherever you move next, or to the ship, or to you." He looked at Odile, and for once there was no bargaining in his face, no angle, only a tired man reporting weather. "I should have seen it before I came. I may have brought them to your door already. I'm sorry. I'm sorry—but you needed to know, and knowing is the only thing I have left to give."

The drip in the corner counted. The weeping wall shed its thin silver thread. Odile looked down at the Vilaine, at the little bloom of spilled ink she had not yet cleaned from its shore, and felt the exhaustion that had carried her for five days suddenly sharpen to a single bright point, the way fatigue does at the end, when there is no longer any question of rest and the body finally agrees to spend everything it has.

"Then we finish faster," she said. "Roux—how many lamps can you risk?"

"Three, if I screen the grating with the sailcloth doubled."

"Then three. And Corvel—" she picked up her pen, and her hand, which had trembled an hour ago, was steady now, steady as a ruled line, "—you don't leave this cellar again. Whatever they're tracking, they track it through you. From tonight, you're cargo. You sail on the Atlas with the atlas, and you don't surface until the pilot boat takes you off at sea."

"And if they come here first?"

Odile bent over the sheet and began, with great care, to lift the spilled bloom of ink from the Vilaine's eastern shore, knife scraping feather-light, restoring the coastline stroke by stroke.

"Then they find a flooded cellar full of spoiled psalters," she said, "and a woman who has been rehearsing her ignorance for ten years."

But even as she said it, even as the knife whispered along the paper, some mapmaker's instinct in her—the sense that had warned her of shifting channels and unreliable lights all her working life—told her the chart was already wrong. Above them, faint through the stone, through the quay, through the ordinary noise of the port, came a sound she almost missed and could not unhear: footsteps on the outer stair. Not descending. Not yet.

Just arriving.
