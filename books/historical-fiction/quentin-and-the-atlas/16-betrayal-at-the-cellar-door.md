## 16. Betrayal at the Cellar Door

The ink was still wet when the knocking began.

Not the knock of a customer—customers knocked twice, politely, with the flat of a hand. This was fists, and more than one pair, and beneath it the sound of boots in the courtyard above, many boots, moving with the particular unhurried rhythm of men who already knew they were in the right.

Odile set down her pen with a care that surprised her. The sheet before her—the Pointe de Chemoulin, its soundings rendered in Étienne's own numbering system, the little crosses she had begun adding where the drowned crew of the *Sainte-Anne* had gone into the water—was half-finished. The ink glistened. She covered it with blotting paper, then with the false bottom of the drying rack, and her hands did not shake, and this too surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking.

Madame Roux looked up from the press in the corner, her spectacles pushed into her gray hair. She said nothing. Ten days of cellar work had taught them both a new language made almost entirely of silence: *hide it*, said the tilt of a chin; *not yet*, said the pause of a hand over a lamp.

The knocking stopped. That was worse.

Then the door at the top of the stairs burst open, and lamplight spilled down into the cellar along with the smell of wet wool and snuff, and Magistrate Delaunay's voice came ahead of him like something poured.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." He descended slowly, one hand on the rail, his boots finding each step as though the staircase had been built for his convenience. Behind him came four men in the plain dark coats of his constables, and behind them, held between two others by the arms—

Corvel.

He looked terrible. He looked, Odile thought with a strange detached clarity, exactly like a man who had not slept in three days, which was precisely how long he had been gone. His coat was torn at the shoulder. There was a bruise rising along his jaw like a storm front. And he would not look at her. He looked at the floor, at the press, at the low vaulted ceiling—as if somewhere among the stones he might find an explanation that could be handed to her like a letter.

"You found us," Odile said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"The city is small," Delaunay said pleasantly, "and its cellars are smaller." He walked the length of the room with his hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring another man's garden. He paused at the press. Touched the drying rack. Ran one finger along the edge of the worktable and examined the fingertip, black with ink, with the mild interest of a physician reading a symptom.

"Ink," he said. "Still wet."

"It's a print shop," Madame Roux said. "We'd worry if it were dry."

"I'm sure you would, madame." He smiled at her without warmth and turned back to Odile. "You've been very careful. I'll grant you that. The commissions delivered on time, all harbors safe and shallow, every chart a masterpiece of saying nothing at all. Your master taught you well." He let that sit. "But paper leaves traces, mademoiselle. A boy who runs errands sees a satchel. A neighbor hears a press working past midnight. A certain sailor with a guilty face asks questions in taverns about tides and departure times—and answers them, when pressed, because sailors are honest men underneath everything, and honesty can always be bought with the right fear."

Odile looked at Corvel then. She made herself do it.

"What did they threaten?" she asked. It was not what she had meant to say. She had meant to say *how could you*, or nothing at all, or perhaps simply his name in a tone that would cut deeper than either. But what came out was the question itself, bare, and she watched him flinch as though she had struck the exact wound.

"Odile—" he started.

"What did they threaten you with?"

He lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and full of something so wretched that for one absurd moment she felt sorry for him, and she hated herself for it, hated the ten years of training that had taught her to read everyone's grief before her own.

"They have the ledgers," he said hoarsely. "The ones we didn't take. The customs house moved them after the festival night—we learned that much too late. And they said—" His voice caught and restarted. "They said if I didn't give them the cellar, they'd burn them. All of them. Every ledger. The plates inside them, whatever's left, the survey sheets Étienne hid there—everything. They said they'd burn it in front of me and make me watch."

"So you gave them us instead."

"I gave them a cellar," Corvel said, and now his voice rose, cracking, desperate, a man drowning toward any shore. "I gave them empty rooms and a dead end, don't you understand? I told them the atlas was here but the plates were already gone—I bought you three days, I thought—"

"You bought yourself three days," Odile said quietly.

The words landed. She saw them land, saw them go into him below the ribs, and she did not look away, and she did not soften them, because some part of her that had been patient for ten years was done being patient.

Delaunay cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man interrupting lovers' quarrel at a dinner party. "As touching as this reunion is," he said, "my men are here for paper, not theater." He nodded to the nearest constable. "The racks. The press. Open everything."

They worked methodically, these men, and Odile had to give them that. They were not ruffians; they were clerks with truncheons. They lifted out the drying rack's false bottom in under a minute—one of them must have known presses, known the trick of it—and laid its contents on the worktable with a care that made her stomach turn: six finished sheets, six harbors of the true coast, the smugglers' channels marked in Étienne's private notation, the soundings no official chart dared record.

Delaunay bent over them for a long time.

"There it is," he murmured, almost tenderly. "Ten years I wondered if this thing actually existed, or whether Marchand's ghost was just a story we told ourselves to explain the silence. And here it is. Under a printer's floor, in a widow's cellar, drawn by a dead man's apprentice." He straightened. "Do you know what this is, legally speaking?"

"Treason," Odile said. "You've told me before. You're very fond of the word."

"It's evidence," Delaunay corrected. "It's evidence that certain channels exist which appear on no chart the Navy possesses. Evidence that certain cargoes move through those channels with remarkable regularity. Evidence that the magistrates charged with stopping such traffic have been, shall we say, geographically blind." He smiled. "You see my difficulty, mademoiselle. I cannot permit this document to exist. Not because it is false—God knows it may be the truest thing drawn in France this century—but because of what happens when it is *read*. Trials are public. Maps entered into evidence are read aloud, in effect, line by line, sounding by sounding. Do you understand? To convict you, I would have to prove your map true. And I cannot afford to prove your map true." He sighed, genuinely, and she believed the sigh. "So we arrive at the ugly arithmetic."

"And the arithmetic says?"

"That two sheets burn," Delaunay said, "and the rest come with me as contraband, and you and Madame Roux are charged with unlicensed printing, a modest offense, fines, perhaps a brief detention—provided, of course, that the remaining sheets never surface anywhere." He picked up one of the constable's lanterns. "Provided also that your master stays drowned, your sailor friend finds a ship on tomorrow's tide going anywhere at all, and the harbor office suffers no further burglaries. A tidy settlement. Everyone keeps their silence. Everyone keeps their skin." He held the lantern out toward the table, almost courteously. "Or we can do this the loud way. But the loud way burns everything, including you."

Odile heard herself say, "Which two?"

Delaunay raised an eyebrow.

"Which two sheets burn?"

"The two most incriminating," he said, as if it were obvious. "The Quiberon approaches and the Vilaine mouth. The channels your master drew that happen to be the channels my— that happen to be the channels currently in use."

Currently in use. She stored the slip away, small and cold, a coin for later.

One of the constables took the two sheets. Odile watched him carry them to the great hearth at the cellar's far end, where the glue-pot had been heated that morning, where coals still glowed under ash. He fed them in without ceremony, first the Quiberon, then the Vilaine, and the paper caught slowly, the way good rag paper does, reluctantly, curling at the edges like something trying to pull free.

Odile did not cry. She stood with her hands at her sides and she watched Étienne's soundings burn—the numbers he had bled for, the depths taken in a rocking open boat with a lead line while a lookout watched for the customs sloop—and she felt the heat of it on her face and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that rage and grief were not two things. They were one thing. They were one thing wearing different faces depending on which way you turned it, and she was turning it, turning it, unable to stop.

The Vilaine sheet curled. Along its margin, just before the fire took it entirely, she could see the little cross she had added three nights ago—the mark for Jean-Baptiste Loro, deckhand, nineteen years old, drowned off the Vilaine shoals in 1741, erased from every record, present now only in this burning ink. The cross glowed briefly, black fire, and then was ash.

Something in her went very quiet and very hard, the way the sea goes before ice.

Beside her, Corvel made a sound. Small, involuntary, like a man taking a blow he'd asked for. She did not turn to him. She would not give him even the comfort of watching her refuse to forgive him.

"Well," said Delaunay, dusting ash from his cuff with fastidious distaste. "That's done, then. The rest comes with me. You'll receive notice of charges within the week—formalities, I assure you." He paused at the foot of the stairs, and for a moment the courtesy dropped, just a finger's width, and she saw underneath it the tired, frightened, intelligent man who had spent a decade guarding a secret he despised. "Mademoiselle Joubert. One piece of advice, freely given. Whatever remains of this project—burn it yourself. Some maps cost more than they pay. Believe me. I know the price better than anyone in this city."

Then he was gone, and his men were gone, and the cellar door closed, and the smell of smoke hung in the stone room like a guest who would not leave.

Madame Roux took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her apron with slow deliberation, and put them back on.

"How long," she said, "to redraw those two sheets from memory?"

Odile blinked. "What?"

"You heard me, girl. How long?"

Odile looked at the hearth. At the ash. Two sheets. Two hundred soundings, forty miles of coastline, every cross, every name. She had studied them for nine days. She had inked them herself. And she had spent ten years carrying her master's coast inside her skull, every fathom of it, because a woman who hides a map learns it by heart or deserves to lose it.

"Three nights," she said. "If I don't sleep."

Roux nodded once, as though this were a reasonable answer to a reasonable question. "Then we start now. The press stays cold tonight; if they're watching, let them watch a quiet shop." She was already moving, pulling paper from the reserve stock, laying out pens. Then she stopped, and her eyes went to Corvel, who stood against the wall where the constables had left him, not daring to move, not daring to leave, a man waiting to learn the size of his sentence.

"And him?" Roux asked.

Odile looked at Corvel for a long moment. The bruise on his jaw. The wreck of his hands. The way he had said *they'd make me watch*—and meant it, meant every hour of it, and had chosen the plates over her anyway, or rather had chosen the only version of betrayal that left anything alive to betray.

She should send him away. She should send him away and never learn what became of him.

Instead she heard herself say, in a voice scraped flat as a shoreline after a storm:

"He's going to tell me everything they know about tomorrow's patrols. Every man, every post, every hour. And then he's going to help me carry the finished atlas to that ship—if there's anything left to carry—because he owes Étienne Marchand a debt, and I intend to collect it in full, on the quay, at dawn, with interest."

Corvel's breath went out of him. "Odile—"

"The patrol schedule," she said. "Now. While I sharpen my pens."

And outside, above the cellar door, through the small high window where the night sky showed as a slit of gray, the wind was turning. Coming around, as it did before dawn on the Loire, to the west. Toward the sea.
