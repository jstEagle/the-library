## 18. The Last Sheet

The news reached the cellar before dawn had finished deciding whether to come at all.

Madame Roux's boy arrived breathless, rain in his collar and something wilder than rain in his voice: *Marchand has given himself up. Marchand is in Delaunay's cells. Marchand traded himself for the Joubert woman.* By the time he finished, Odile was already on her feet with her coat half-buttoned, already composing the sentence she would say to the magistrate, to the constables, to anyone who would listen — *take me instead, the atlas is mine, the hand is mine* — when Roux caught her wrist with a grip that had set type for forty years and did not intend to start letting go now.

"Sit down," Roux said. "You will sit down, Odile Joubert, because if you walk out that door you spend his confession for nothing, and I did not survive three revolutions' worth of printers' ink to watch a woman waste a dead man's last bargain."

"He isn't dead."

"No. Worse. He's honest." Roux pushed her back into the chair by the drying rack, where the ink smell was thickest and the dark was kindest. "He bought your freedom this morning. The price of it is that you stay free. So sit. And work."

Odile sat.

She sat for a long time, listening to the rain find the cellar grate above and thread its way down the wall in cold silver ropes, and she understood, in the way one understands a tide — slowly, then all at once — what Étienne had done. Not for her. Not only for her. A man who had spent ten years keeping his own name off paper had finally put it on the only document left that mattered: a confession. He had made himself legible so that her maps could remain so.

"Two sheets," she said aloud. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Two of the finished sheets burned."

"Which two?"

"The Loire bar. And—" She closed her eyes and saw them, saw the flames taking the vellum the way fire takes a bird, fast and without apology. "The Croisic roads. With the soundings."

Roux pulled a stool to the table, sat opposite her, and laid two clean sheets of paper between them like cards in a game neither intended to lose.

"Then we begin," said Roux, "with what the fire could not reach."

* * *

They worked the way besieged cities eat: quickly, secretly, and with an attention to every scrap that would have seemed madness in peacetime.

The cellar belonged to Roux's press-house, below the level where daylight bothered to visit, lit by four lamps shielded with tin so their glow showed in no window. The air was warm with the body-heat of the presses and thick with linseed, lamp-black, the vinegar bite of fresh etching ground. Odile had not drawn by lamplight like this since her apprenticeship, when she and Étienne had worked winter nights over the Loire surveys with their breath ghosting above the vellum and the master's rule absolute: *the coast does not care about your comfort. Draw what is true or draw nothing.*

Her hands remembered before her mind did. That was the mercy of it. She unrolled the surviving field-notes — the ones Corvel had brought wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, the ones she had spent nine days committing to memory as though memory were a vault and not a leaky boat — and found that her pencil knew the Croisic headland's profile the way her mouth knew a psalm. The granite shoulder of it. The way the channel bent east of the third beacon like a man leaning to hear better.

"Soundings," Roux prompted, when Odile paused too long.

"Seven fathoms off the point, shoaling to four and a quarter at low water over the oyster beds." She wrote the figures small and sure. "The smugglers ran there on moonless nights because the king's cutters drew too much. That is why the ledger never recorded those depths. That is why my master—"

She stopped. In this cellar, saying *my master* in the present tense felt less like grief than like blasphemy, and she had not yet decided which sin she preferred.

"Your master measured the water honestly," Roux said, grinding ink with slow circular strokes, the pestle whispering against stone. "That was always his crime. Everything else they invented around it."

The first sheet took until the middle hours of the night. When Odile lifted the pen from the final contour line, her shoulders cracked like ship's timbers and her eyes swam so badly that the coastline appeared to breathe. Roux examined it under the strongest lamp, tilting it against the light, checking the tides tables pinned to the wall, checking Odile herself with a look that lingered longer than any inspection of paper.

"It's good," Roux said finally. "It's his hand and yours and neither, which is exactly right."

"It's missing something."

"What?"

Odile looked at the sheet. The Croisic roads lay rendered in ink finer than anything Delaunay's men could confiscate, the depths numbered, the hazards marked, the channels opened like doors. It was everything the Crown had suppressed. It was everything Étienne had nearly hanged for. And it was silent. Ten years of silence pressed into it, the same silence she had kept in the workshop while officials carried out the plates, the same silence Corvel had been paid to keep aboard the survey sloop, the same silence the ledgers kept where men's names should have been.

"The crew," Odile said.

Roux's pestle stopped grinding.

"The *Aimable Lucie*," Odile went on, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking. "Six men lost when the survey sloop was driven onto the Plateau du Four in 'thirty-seven. The ledgers recorded them as cargo damaged in transit. Six names, struck through, valued in livres. My master knew all six. He ate beside them. He trusted them with the sounding lines in weather that would have drowned him alone. And when the atlas is published, when London prints it and Paris reads it and Delaunay chokes on it — those men will still be numbers. Erased twice. Once by the ledger and once by the map."

"You want to put their names on a hydrographic chart." Roux said it carefully, testing the weight of it, as she might test a new fount of type. "Names have never gone on such sheets. Names go on monuments, on tombstones, on lists read in churches. Not on coastlines."

"Why not?" Odile asked. "A tombstone tells you where a man stopped. A chart tells you where he still matters. Every pilot who reads these depths will be steering over the place where they died. Let them steer past their names."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere above, the press-house clock counted four with the reluctance of all clocks in cellars.

"They'll call it sentiment," Roux said. "Delaunay will call it evidence. He'll say the names prove the atlas is a grievance, not a survey."

"Delaunay let my master surrender rather than face what the atlas says in open court. He is not going to start reading it now." Odile dipped her pen. "And if they call it sentiment — good. Sentiment is the one thing in Nantes tonight that isn't for sale."

* * *

She wrote the names the way Étienne had taught her to write everything: small, exact, and placed where the truth of the geography demanded.

Not in a margin. Margins could be trimmed; margins were where apologies lived. She set them into the land itself, into the fine stippled texture of the shore where the engraver's dots stood for rock and scrub and the bones of the coast. Along the stretch below Le Croisic where the *Aimable Lucie*'s masthead had last been seen, she lettered, in characters barely larger than the depth-soundings:

*GUILLOU, YVES — MATES.*
*TREMBLAY.*
*LE GOFF, PIERRE.*
*KERBRAT.*
*MOREAU, F. AND J. — BROTHERS.*

Each name anchored to its own sounding, its own grain of granite. A stranger consulting the chart would take them for place-names — little coves, little rocks, features too minor for most maps. Only someone who knew would know. And everyone who mattered would know. The pilots of the Loire. The widows in the Croisic lanes who had waited out ten years of official silence with nothing but a struck-through line in a customs book. Madame Guillou, who sold mussels on the quay and had once, years ago, pressed a coin into Odile's hand at Étienne's funeral-that-wasn't, saying, *for the candles, girl, since no one else will light them.*

By lamplight, with ink she had ground herself, Odile gave six drowned men back their geography.

"There," she said, when the last name had dried. Her throat ached around the word.

Roux leaned close, reading. The old printer's lips moved with each name the way they moved over proofs, hunting errors, and Odile realized with a jolt that Roux was not proofreading. She was praying. It had simply been so long since the printer had prayed in anything but type that the two acts had become indistinguishable.

"Étienne never dared this," Roux said quietly. "Ten years hiding, ten years of letters written in a dead man's hand, and never once did he dare put a name where the world could read it."

"He thought names were dangerous."

"They are. That's why they belong on maps."

* * *

Dawn came down the cellar grate as a gray suggestion, and with it came the arithmetic Odile had been refusing all night.

One sheet redrawn. One more to go — the Loire bar itself, the atlas's beating heart, the chart that showed exactly how contraband moved up the river under the noses of the harbor office, keyed to the very ledgers whose pages had hidden the stolen plates. She had watched it burn only yesterday. She could see the ash of it still, in her mind's eye, curling at the edges like a living thing.

But the Loire bar she did not need notes to draw. She had sounded it herself, eleven years ago, a girl with numb hands hauling the lead line while Étienne called the readings against the wind. She had inked the original with him standing behind her, his breath at her ear saying *again, again, the flood tide lies, take it once more.* If every other record in France burned tomorrow, Odile Joubert could redraw the Loire bar from the inside of her own skull, and do it truer than truth.

She told Roux this. Roux nodded, banked two of the lamps, and said the thing Odile had been waiting all night for her not to say:

"And the tide?"

Odile looked toward the grate, where the gray was warming toward pearl. Beyond it, beyond the press-house walls, the Loire was running seaward with the ebb, and riding at anchor in the Roads, the *Atlas* — Corvel's ship, their ship, the ship named for the thing itself — waited on the turn. Tomorrow morning. Perhaps the morning after, if the wind held contrary. No one knew, and not knowing was its own kind of drowning.

"We finish tonight," she said. "Whatever the tide does, we finish tonight."

"And then?"

"Then we run the quay." She heard her own voice go quiet and hard, a keel biting water. "Delaunay's men will be watching this door. They'll be watching my workshop. They'll be watching the harbor office, the customs house, every gate on the river. He let me go, but he will not let the atlas go — not when it's the one witness he can't cross-examine."

Roux began cleaning pens with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman loading a weapon. "I have a bale of printed almanacs going to a ship chandler near the Salorges at first light. Paper hides paper. It always has."

Odile almost smiled. Then she looked down at the finished sheet — the Croisic roads, the six names lying along the coast like a string of lights — and the smile failed somewhere on its way to her mouth.

Because there, in the lower corner, where a cartographer signed her work, the space was blank. She had drawn all night without deciding the question that had waited ten years for an answer. *Marchand*, alone? His atlas, his suppression, his sacrifice. Or *Joubert*, alone? Her decade, her labor, her risk. Or both names, which would tie her signature forever to a confessed criminal, a man the courts might yet hang, a name that could poison hers in every port that read it?

She picked up her pen.

She thought of the returned letter, unopened, that had taught her how long and how clumsily he had loved her. She thought of the marginal note in his hand, dated three years after his death, addressed to her by name across all that silence: *she will finish it better than I would have.* He had signed her competence with his own hope. The least she could do was answer.

She wrote *MARCHAND* first — above, as a master belongs — and beneath it, smaller but not humble, never again humble: *JOUBERT.*

Ink that argues, she thought. Ink that answers.

Above the grate, Nantes was waking. Bells, gulls, the first carts on the quay stones, the harbor shaking off its dark. Somewhere among all that ordinary noise, Delaunay's men were taking their positions at doors and gates, patient as pilings. And somewhere in a cell with a window facing the sea, a man who had chosen honesty at last was waiting to learn whether the thing he had ruined himself to protect would reach the water in time.

Odile capped the ink, rolled the sheet, and slid it into the oilskin with the rest.

"One day," she said to Roux, to the cellar, to the tide turning somewhere beyond the walls. "Maybe two. And a few hundred yards of quay between us and the sea."

Roux shouldered her shawl and blew out the last lamp.

"Then God help us both," the old printer said, "and mind the wet cobblestones."
