## 19. Morning Tide

### Odile

The tide would turn at half past six, and the atlas weighed nothing and everything.

She had carried it from Madame Roux's cellar in a satchel of oilcloth, wrapped the way Étienne had taught her to wrap plates for transport—flat against the spine, corners cushioned with felt, the whole bound in cord knotted twice because one knot was vanity and two were prudence. The satchel rode against her ribs under her cloak, and every step down the rue de la Fosse she could feel it breathing against her heart like a second heartbeat that did not belong to her.

Nantes at dawn was a city holding its breath. Fog lay on the Loire in long gray ribbons, and through it the harbor masts rose like a forest of black needles stitching sky to water. Somewhere out in that white blindness the *Atlas* waited at her moorings off the Quai de la Fosse, her crew working in silence by prior arrangement, no lamps lit, no song, no shout of ready hands—the silence of men who had been paid well enough to want nothing said aloud.

Odile walked the way she had learned to walk past Delaunay's clerks for ten years: unhurried, eyes level, a woman of no particular business going about it. But her body knew better than her face. Her palms were slick inside her gloves. The fog beaded on her lashes until the whole world softened into shapes she might have drawn herself—a quay rendered in wash, masts in drypoint, the river a single unbroken tone of pewter.

*Four hundred yards,* Corvel had said last night in the cellar, his finger moving across the chalk map they'd scratched on the flagstones. Roux's cellar smelled of ink and damp stone and the ghost of burnt paper where the raiders' torches had scorched a shelf. *From Roux's door to the ship's ladder. Four hundred yards of open quay.*

Open. As though any ground in Nantes was open anymore. She had watched Delaunay's men thicken along the waterfront for three days like sediment settling in standing water. They stood at the customs house steps in pairs. They lounged against bollards with the studied idleness of men who had been told to look bored. One of them, she was almost certain, had followed her Tuesday from the print shop, keeping always exactly far enough back to claim innocence if challenged.

But Delaunay could not search them. That was the blade's other edge, and Odile had spent the night sharpening it in her mind. A magistrate who seized an atlas he had spent a decade pretending did not exist would have to explain what it was. He would have to read it aloud—in a courtroom, before witnesses, page after suppressed page naming the coves and channels and the magistrates whose fortunes ran through them. Delaunay's power lived in the atlas remaining unread. He would stop them if he could do it quietly. He would let them pass if stopping meant speaking.

*Everything depends on him choosing silence over spectacle,* she had told Corvel.

*And if he chooses wrong?*

*Then we run.*

Now the fog gave her the first hundred yards for free. She moved through it past the sleeping wine warehouses, their doors barred, their windows blind, the smell of old barrels and tar so thick she could taste it. A cat watched her from atop a coil of rope with the flat amber judgment cats reserve for humans carrying things that matter. Gulls argued somewhere above the masts, invisible, their cries falling out of the white like small accusations.

At the corner of the Quai de la Fosse the fog began to thin, and she saw them.

Three men outside the customs house, breath steaming, lanterns still lit against the dawn—one of them Delaunay's chief clerk, Boucher, recognizable even at this distance by the stoop he affected, as though permanent apology might excuse whatever he witnessed. They stood directly athwart the stretch of quay between her and the *Atlas*'s mooring ladder. Not moving. Not looking at her either. Looking, with tremendous dedication, at nothing at all.

Her mouth went dry. Ten years of discipline kept her feet at the same pace, kept her breath even, kept her hands loose at her sides while inside her something screamed like a gull. She thought absurdly of Étienne's rule for inking coastlines: *never hesitate mid-stroke; hesitation shows in the line forever.* She put her boots down one after another and did not hesitate.

Twenty yards from them, Boucher turned.

He looked at her the way men look at weather—assessing, incurious—and then his gaze snagged, just barely, on the shape of her cloak, on the set of her shoulders, on something in her face that perhaps betrayed the second heartbeat beneath her ribs. His lips parted.

And then Corvel stepped out of a doorway ahead of her, laughing, loud, waving both arms, shouting up at a window in bad Breton-accented French about a debt of four sous and a woman named Margot, and every eye on the quay went to him, and Boucher's mouth closed, and Odile walked past three of Delaunay's men close enough to smell the tallow smoke on their coats, and none of them saw her at all.

### Corvel

Corvel had been afraid many times in his life—afloat in weather, ashore among worse men—but fear had never felt like this: not cold but hot, a furnace in the chest, a clarity like the sea at noon. He had spent nine days being a coward in careful installments, and this morning he intended to spend the whole account at once.

The plan had been his, which frightened him most of all. He had never made plans; he had survived other people's plans, taken coin for silence, rowed away from consequences. But standing in Roux's cellar watching Odile redraw two destroyed sheets from memory—watching her hand move without trembling while his own shook around a cup of wine—he had understood suddenly that some things are worth planning for, and that he had gone his whole life without finding one until now.

So: he would walk ahead of her, loud, ridiculous, conspicuous. He would be the noise that made her invisible. And when—if—it failed, he would be the wall between the satchel and whatever came for it.

He had rehearsed his drunkenness in front of a cracked mirror until even Roux laughed, a short bark she tried to swallow. Now he deployed it, bellowing about Margot and the four sous, staggering along the quay with the fine theatrical stagger of a man who has never once actually been drunk, and it worked—the clerk looked away from Odile, a constable by the bollard grinned despite himself, the whole gray morning tilted toward him like a stage accepting its fool.

Odile passed within arm's reach of him. He did not look at her. That was the hardest thing he had done all week, harder than refusing the smugglers, and he refused to examine why.

Two hundred yards remained. The fog was lifting off the water in rags, and he could see the *Atlas* now clearly—her anchor watch on deck, a boy at the rail, the mooring ladder hanging dark and wet above the tide. Two hundred yards of quay, and then the ladder, and then the sea, and then done. Done. He let himself think the word *done* and hated himself for it, because in his experience the word *done* was the exact moment the universe reached for its ledger.

It happened at the foot of the ladder.

A constable detached himself from the customs house wall—not one of the bored ones, a different man, younger, moving with the flat urgency of someone who had finally received the order he'd been waiting three days for—and came toward Odile at a walk that wanted very much to be a run. Corvel heard his boots. He didn't think. Thinking was for men with time.

He got between them.

"Madame," the constable said, reaching, "I'll have that satchel—"

"It's sewing," Odile said, and her voice was perfect, mild, insulted, and the constable's hand closed anyway, on the cloak, on the strap—

Corvel took the blow.

Not gracefully. There was nothing graceful in it. The constable's truncheon came around in a short hard arc and caught him across the shoulder and the side of the head together, and the world went white and sideways, and he went down onto the wet stones with the taste of copper blooming behind his teeth and the enormous relief of a man who has finally, at long last, paid something he owed. He grabbed the constable's ankle as he fell. It wasn't skill. It was spite. The man stumbled, swore, wrenched free—

—and Odile was already climbing.

He saw it from the ground, from the strange low angle of blood in the eyes: her boots on the ladder rungs, the satchel swinging against her back, the boy at the rail leaning down with his arms out, the fog tearing open overhead to show a sky going from pewter to pearl. She climbed like a woman ascending out of a decade. At the top she turned, once, and looked down at him lying on the quay with the constable standing over him, and her expression was not gratitude and was not pity and was not anything he had a name for, except that it held him the way a mooring holds a boat.

Then she was over the rail and gone, and the *Atlas*'s capstan began to turn, and the ship leaned toward the current like a creature remembering it was made for motion.

"Name," the constable demanded above him, hauling him up by the collar. "Your name, and what's in that bag."

Corvel spat blood onto the beautiful clean stones of the quay and grinned at him with teeth gone red.

"Margot's," he said. "Ask her yourself."

The constable hesitated—and hesitation, Étienne Marchand had once written in a margin Odile loved to quote, is a fault visible forever in the finished line—and in the space of that hesitation the law discovered it had no charge to press, no satchel in evidence, only a bleeding drunk and a departing merchantman already clearing her moorings. Behind them, at the far end of the quay, a carriage drew up and a familiar figure stepped out into the strengthening light, silver-headed, immaculate, and stopped.

Magistrate Delaunay stood watching the *Atlas* take the tide. He made no signal. He raised no hand. He simply watched, and his face wore the expression of a man reading a document he had forbidden anyone to write—and finding, against his will, that it was beautifully done.

Corvel sat down hard on the bollard and pressed his sleeve to his ear and laughed until it hurt.

Out in the channel, sails filled. The fog burned away. And the coastline of France began, at last, to tell the truth.
