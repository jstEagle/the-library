## 3. Quentin

The river woke before he did.

Quentin Vasse had learned to measure mornings by sound now — the groan of the current against the pilings, the first clatter of the cable-cranes being winched awake, the far-off whistle of the foreman's kettle drifting down from the mess shed. In the old days, before the declaration, mornings on this bank had smelled of his mother's bread and the wet iron smell of the ferry chains. Now they smelled of tar and sawdust and other men's sweat, because Quentin Vasse was dead, and the man who climbed the scaffolding each dawn was called Tomas Rehl, a laborer from the northern counties with no family worth mentioning and no history worth checking.

He had been counting since the day of the declaration. Not aloud — never aloud — but in the ledger he kept behind his eyes: one thousand eight hundred and twenty-six days as of this morning. Five years minus one. He knew the number the way other men knew their own age. He could tell you what the weather had done on day four hundred, what he'd eaten on day nine hundred and twelve (nothing; he'd been sick with fever in a workhouse in Corvel), what he'd thought about every single night when the lamps went out.

He thought about her. That was the honest arithmetic underneath all the others.

"Tomas!" The foreman's voice cracked across the yard like a whip finding its mark early. "You sleep up there or what? Third span won't hang itself."

Quentin rolled from his bunk, pulled on the rough canvas jacket that smelled permanently of pitch, and was out the door before the second call came. A man who made the foreman call twice became a name the foreman remembered. A man who was remembered got asked questions. And a man who answered questions badly ended up in front of an inspector with papers in his hand.

He had learned to be unmemorable the way Noor had learned — he supposed — to draw coastlines. Line by line, repetition until the hand did it without the mind.

* * *

The bridge rose out of the morning fog like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down in the water to die and changed its mind halfway through.

Two stone piers stood finished on either shore, square and gray and certain of themselves. Between them, the third pier — the middle one — thrust up out of the current only two-thirds built, its masonry ending in a broken crown of scaffolding poles and dangling chain hoists. Steel cables ran everywhere, sagging catenary curves that caught the fog and dripped it back into the river. From below, the unfinished span looked like a sentence that stopped before its verb.

Quentin climbed.

His hands knew the route now: the ladder rungs slicked with dew, then the cross-bracing where you went hand over hand above sixty feet of open air, then the catwalk planks that flexed underfoot like living things. He did not look down. Looking down was for men who had somewhere better to be, and everything Quentin wanted was either behind him across the water or beside him in a pressed flower in a tin box under a floorboard in a lodging house in a town he was not allowed to enter.

"Rehl. Bolt check." The foreman — Dossar, a squat man with forearms like mooring posts and eyes that missed less than Quentin liked — jerked his chin toward the near pier's capstan assembly. "Every fitting on the north arm. If we're testing the span next month I want no loose iron anywhere on my bridge."

"My bridge," Quentin noted, was what Dossar called it when the government engineers weren't listening. When they were, it was *the nation's bridge*, and everyone stood straighter and spoke louder about glory and unity and the joining of two peoples who had spent five years learning to hate each other on schedule.

He worked the bolt wrench in steady strokes, testing each fitting, and let the rhythm carry his mind downstream to the other bank, where the city of Vellmar spread along its shoreline with its crooked streets and its mapmaker's shop with the blue door.

He had seen the blue door exactly once in five years, from the deck of a coal barge he wasn't supposed to be aboard. It had been dusk, and a lamp burned in the upstairs window of the workshop, and he had told himself — as he told himself every time — that the silhouette moving past the glass was too tall to be hers, that she'd have left by now, married some surveyor or archivist with clean hands, that holding onto a lamp in a window was the behavior of fools.

Then the silhouette had paused at the glass. Just for a moment. The way someone pauses when they feel themselves looked at.

He had gripped the barge rail hard enough to bruise and said nothing to anyone, because there was no one to say anything to. That was another thing five years taught you: how to carry a moment whole and unshared until it went strange and heavy, like a coin kept so long in the fist it wore your palm print into itself.

"Rehl." Dossar again, closer than Quentin liked. "You're grinding on the same fitting for a minute. You sick?"

"Just thinking about torque."

"Don't think. Tighten." But Dossar lingered, wiping rust from his palms with an oily rag, and Quentin felt the particular prickle at the back of the neck that meant he was being measured. "You asked Jorin last week about the night rotations. Now you're asking about the span test date. Curious sort of laborer you are, Rehl."

"Man likes to know what he's building toward," Quentin said. He kept his voice level, kept his eyes on the bolt head, kept his hands moving. "My father worked ferries on this river. Before. Feels like finishing something."

It was half true, which was the only currency worth spending with Dossar. Lies were transparent here; truth was armor, in small denominations.

Dossar grunted. "Your father pick a side in the war that's coming, did he?" And he walked away down the catwalk before Quentin could decide whether the question had been casual.

The war that's coming. Everyone said it now the way they said *the rain that's coming* — inevitable, meteorological, nothing anyone could do about it. The declaration of enmity had never been rescinded; the border had never truly opened; the bridge itself was supposed to be the thing that ended all of it, the great reconciliation project, both crowns shaking hands over the water while the newspapers printed photographs of engineers embracing. But Quentin had spent five years inside the scaffolding of that handshake, and he knew what the workers whispered: that the third pier sat on flawed bedrock, that surveys had been altered, that men who asked about the pier's footings stopped being scheduled for shifts. He knew it the way you know weather in your joints.

And he knew, with a certainty that lived deeper than rumor, that if the war came, the bridge would be the first thing both sides would want — and the second thing they'd blow.

Which meant his time was not infinite. None of it ever had been. One thousand eight hundred and twenty-six days, and every one of them spent getting ready for a crossing he hadn't yet figured out how to make.

* * *

At midday he ate his bread on the high staging with his legs hanging over the water, which was against regulations and therefore the only privacy the bridge offered. Below him the river ran brown-green and patient, carrying leaves and one drowned branch shaped like a reaching hand, all of it bound for the border line invisible in midstream — a line drawn on maps, not on water. Water didn't care whose side it flowed toward. Water had been crossing freely this whole time, going wherever it pleased, while he sat on a steel beam calculating the width of a gap.

Because that was the secret he carried under his false name alongside everything else: the third pier was not connected yet. There was a gap — forty feet of open air and running water between the end of the southern approach spans and the unfinished crown of the middle pier — and at night, when the floodlamps were doused except the two on the guard barges, that gap was a darkness. And darkness, if a man studied it long enough, if a man memorized its tides and its patrol rhythms and the exact creak-temperature of the staging timbers, was a road.

He'd been studying it for eleven months.

Eleven months of night shifts volunteered-for and traded-for. Eleven months of learning that the guard barges crossed paths at nineteen minutes past every hour, leaving a blind interval on the upstream face of ninety seconds, maybe a hundred if the current was slow. Eleven months of learning that the inspection gangway on the pier's landward side had a padlock whose hasp was rusted through — he'd checked it with his thumb a dozen times, feeling the metal flake — and that the workmen's rotation left the pier unmanned between the second and third watches on nights when the moon was new.

He had a plan. What he didn't have was a reason to believe anyone would be waiting on the other side, or that the person he most needed to reach it with could even get to the water.

That was the flaw in every version of the plan, the crack that ran through it like the crack in the pier's bedrock. Crossing alone was possible. Crossing alone was, in fact, the only sane crossing. But crossing alone meant leaving her on the wrong bank of a river in a country that had just sent her household a letter — because Marta talked, everyone knew Marta talked, the landlady's gossip traveled faster than the mail boats — and Quentin Vasse, dead these five years, could not burn in good conscience while Noor Nystrom stood in the path of whatever fire was coming for the Nystrom name.

He finished the bread. He brushed the crumbs into the river, where fish rose for them without checking passports.

* * *

The flower lived in a tobacco tin beneath a loose floorboard in the lodging house, three doors down from the laborers' dormitory, in a room Quentin paid for with wages he pretended came from a cousin. He went there on rest-days, when a single man walking purposefully through town drew fewer eyes than a single man loitering.

That evening — a rest-day, grey going violet over the rooftops — he knelt, lifted the board, and took the tin out the way he always did: two hands, like it was something excavated.

Inside: a paper of matches gone soft with damp. A ferry token, brass worn featureless. And the flower.

A cornflower, pressed flat the summer they were fourteen, picked — she had picked it, he'd only watched — from the embankment above the ferry landing on the last afternoon of the world as it had been. She'd held it out to him with both hands, formal as a treaty, and said something he had repeated to himself so many times the words had worn smooth of meaning and become pure texture, like sea glass: *So you remember what color things are, when they make everything grey.*

Five years. The blue had faded to a color that wasn't quite any color — lavender, ash, dusk. The petals had gone translucent, thin as the skin of an onion, veined like a leaf. If you touched them they would crumble, and he never touched them. He turned the tin slightly so the lamplight moved across the petals, and that was all, and it was enough, and it was nowhere near enough.

One thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven days tomorrow.

They had sworn an oath on that last ferry crossing, the two of them, fourteen years old and solemn as judges while the adults around them complained about fares and schedules and didn't yet know — none of them knew — that the schedule was dying. *If they close the water,* Noor had said, *we find each other. However long. Whatever it takes. We find each other.* And he had said yes, yes, obviously yes, the way you agree to things at fourteen that turn out to cost a decade.

The borders had closed within the year. The ferry chains were cut. His family's name went onto a list, and her family's trade went onto another list, and the lists grew until they touched in the middle and became the declaration.

And still he kept the oath the way you keep a debt: not because you expect to be forgiven for it, but because not keeping it is unthinkable.

He put the tin away. He pressed the floorboard down with his palm until it seated flush.

* * *

He was almost asleep when the thought arrived — the way important thoughts do, sideways, wearing ordinary clothes.

He had been reviewing the night rotations again, lying in the dark doing the mental cartography he did every evening, drawing the bridge in his head: the gap, the barges, the ninety-second blindness, the rusted hasp. And somewhere between the second watch and the third watch of the imagined bridge, his mind had snagged on a detail it had snagged on a hundred times and finally, tonight, refused to pass over:

Elias Nystrom had surveyed this river.

Not casually — officially. Before the enmity, before the bridge was anything but argument and ambition, old Nystrom had been the crown's consulting cartographer for the whole river basin. Quentin's father had drunk with him once at the Ferryman's Rest and come home saying the mapmaker was the only man in either capital who actually understood the third-pier shoals, who'd sounded them himself, who'd written things in his margins that the ministry gentlemen found inconvenient.

Margins. Elias Nystrom wrote everything in margins. Quentin had seen the man's working maps once, years ago, spread across the workshop table — a wilderness of tiny annotations crawling around every coastline like ants around sugar, arrows and numerals and private shorthand, dense as scripture and twice as unreadable.

Noor had those maps. Noor, who had apprenticed beside the old man for years, who could read his shorthand the way priests read Latin. Noor, whose atlas of this very river was rumored to be nearing completion, five years in the making.

If anyone alive knew what lay under the third pier — knew the true depth of the bedrock, the true state of the footings, the true shape of the currents at night — it was the ghost of Elias Nystrom, preserved in ink, sitting in a workshop with a blue door on the far bank.

Quentin stared at the ceiling beam and felt the plan rearrange itself in his chest, the way a river rearranges itself around a fallen tree.

He couldn't send a letter. Letters were read. He couldn't send word through Marta — God, especially not through Marta. He couldn't cross yet; the plan wasn't ripe, and a green plan got men drowned. But the river carried more than letters. The river carried light.

Fishermen signaled each other across the border water all the time — two lanterns, three lanterns, hung off a mast or a gaff, patterns as old as the fishing fleets, older than the border. Half the guards ignored it as superstition. The other half had never learned to count.

She was a mapmaker's apprentice. Mapmakers thought in codes the way fishermen thought in knots.

Tomorrow night, Quentin decided, watching the beam overhead go silver with rising moonlight, he would hang a lantern off the end of the unfinished staging — the upstream face, where her windows faced — and he would show her three flashes, pause, two flashes. The old ferry signal. *Waiting. Safe.*

And if the lamp in the blue-doored workshop answered — if some silhouette he'd watched through glass for five years answered him in light across the water — then everything would begin.

He closed his eyes. Under the floorboards, three rooms away, a flower the color of dusk waited with the patience of pressed things.

One thousand eight hundred and twenty-seven days. Perhaps, at last, the last of them.
