## 5. Inspector Halvard Calls

The knock came at half past nine, when the fog off the river was still thick enough to blur the workshop windows into grey ovals and the ink on my desk had not yet dried from the night's work.

I knew before I opened the door that it would be him. Not because of any sound — his knock was as polite as a neighbor's — but because of what Marta had called up the stairs an hour earlier, her voice pitched carefully flat: *There's a gentleman asking after you at the corner shop, miss. Wearing a good coat.* In Vellsgard, only one kind of man wore a good coat in weather like this and had no reason to be standing on a corner watching a mapmaker's window.

I put down my pen. I capped the ink. I looked once around the room with the eyes of someone else — the eyes of Inspector Halvard — and saw everything he would see: the atlas open to plate forty-one, the unfinished coastline of the eastern shore; Elias's chair pushed back at its familiar angle; the smell of walnut oil and old paper; nothing out of place, nothing hidden, or so it appeared. The letter lay where it always lay, sewn into the false spine of a shipping ledger from 1907, beneath the shelf of coastal surveys no one had consulted in a decade. My hands were steady. That was the first miracle of the morning.

The second came when I opened the door and found that Halvard was not what I had imagined.

I had pictured a brute. Five years of living under the shadow of the crown's investigators had taught me to expect either cruelty or its cousin, incompetence. But the man on my step was neither large nor small, neither young nor old, with a face like a well-used ledger — every line entered neatly, nothing wasted, nothing legible at first glance. He wore his grey coat buttoned to the collar against the damp, and he held his hat in both hands as though it were something fragile, or perhaps as though he were.

"Fräulein Nystrom." His voice was quiet enough that I had to lean slightly toward it. "Inspector Tomas Halvard. Ministry of Interior Affairs. May I impose on your morning?"

"You may impose on my work," I said, "but I'm afraid my morning ended an hour ago."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, of a parry well made. "Then may I impose on your work?"

He stepped past me before I answered — courteously, hat still in hand, boots leaving faint wet prints on my clean floor — and I understood that courtesy was simply the shape his authority took. He did not need permission. Permission was a fiction we would maintain together for as long as this visit lasted.

* * *

He walked the workshop the way a man walks through a church: slowly, respectfully, reading everything. I watched him read. That was the discipline Elias had drilled into me across eleven years of apprenticeship — *the surveyor watches himself watch, Noor, or his instruments lie to him* — and so I stood by the stove and made tea I did not want and observed the observer.

Halvard stopped first at the atlas. Of course he did. It was the room's heart, the thing every visitor's eye went to whether they knew why or not: the great folio of plates, each one inked in Elias's hand and now, in the final pages, in mine. He turned the pages without touching them more than necessary, gloved fingers hovering at the edges.

"The Nystrom Atlas of the River Provinces," he said. "I confess I looked forward to seeing it. There aren't many men left who draw maps by hand."

"There are none," I said. "My master died in March. Five years ago." The correction came automatically, and I regretted it even as I spoke, because it told him I corrected people — that I attended to detail — that I was, in the way of all careful people, dangerous.

"Five years," he repeated, and turned another page. "A long time for an apprentice to keep a workshop alone."

"It keeps me."

"Yes." He did not look up. "I imagine it does."

The kettle began its low complaint behind me. I poured the tea and set two cups on the worktable — an instinct of hospitality I hated myself for, because it made us into two civilized people having a civilized conversation, and civilized conversations were how men like Halvard harvested confessions. He accepted the cup with a nod and drank without testing it, which told me something too: a man who fears poison checks his drink. This man feared nothing in my workshop except what he might fail to find there.

"Fräulein Nystrom," he said, settling into Elias's chair without hesitation — and I felt that as a violation more keenly than if he had struck me, though my face gave away nothing, nothing, nothing — "you will forgive a policeman his bluntness. I am investigating certain irregularities in the correspondence records of this district."

"Irregularities."

"A letter was received in this quarter of the city last month. Its routing was unusual. Its addressee—" he paused, delicately, the pause of a man laying a card face-down rather than face-up, "—was associated with your late master's name."

"My master has been dead five years," I said again, and heard how the repetition sounded, and hated it. "Dead men receive few letters."

"Some dead men receive a great many." He set down his cup and produced from inside his coat a slim leather folder, opening it on his knee with the care of a surgeon unfolding instruments. "You understand my position, Fräulein. The border commission is sensitive just now. The bridge opens within the year. There are men in both capitals whose careers depend on the appearance of order along this river, and appearances require—" he glanced up at me then, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without warmth, "—housekeeping."

"What sort of housekeeping do you imagine I perform, Inspector?"

"The ordinary kind." He drew a page from the folder. "Visitors to this address. Deliveries. Correspondence received and sent. The names of persons who have called upon you since—" a small motion toward the atlas, toward the whole silent weight of the dead man's life, "—since your master's death."

I let the silence sit. That was the first rule of the safe silences: never rush to fill them. A guilty woman fills silence with reassurance. An innocent woman fills it with questions. A careful woman lets it breathe until the other man must.

It worked, as it always works, on everyone but the best. Halvard waited me out instead. We sat in the ticking of the stove and the drip of fog off the eaves, and I understood that I was looking at the best.

Finally he said, mildly: "You have no visitors."

"I have a landlady."

"Marta Sorel." He wrote nothing down; the name was already written somewhere. "She speaks highly of you. Keeps your rooms, brings up your meals when you forget them. She tells me you rarely leave the workshop except to buy ink and bread."

"Marta is generous with her observations."

"She is thorough." Something flickered behind the mildness then — a hairline crack, gone instantly — and filed itself away in some drawer of mine for later examination. "And the bridge? You'll chart it, I suppose, for the atlas. Everyone on this river will have an opinion about that bridge."

"It is being charted," I said. "Plate forty-four. The spans, the piers, the approaches. Maps exist whether or not bridges open."

"They do indeed." He closed the folder and rose, smoothing his coat, and for a moment he stood over the atlas with his head bowed as if in respect for the dead. Then he said, without turning: "Your master died on the river, didn't he. During the survey of the third pier."

The question landed precisely where it was aimed. I felt it strike bone.

"He drowned," I said. "During a storm. The boat went down near the worksite. You know this — it will be in whatever thorough file you carry in that folder."

"It is." He turned at last, and his face was composed again, courteous, cold, a locked door painted to look like an open one. "Though there were irregularities in that account as well. A body never recovered. Instruments missing. And a witness — a young apprentice — who gave testimony that the coroner recorded as, and I quote his own hand here, 'unshakably rehearsed.'"

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, Marta's broom scraped the step, and I thought with a strange clarity: *she is listening, she has been listening for years, she is the reason this man knows the shape of my silences.*

"I was nineteen," I said. "Nineteen-year-olds rehearse grief. They've had little practice with the unrehearsed kind."

For the first time, something human crossed Halvard's face — brief, quickly mastered, but real. Recognition, maybe. Or memory. "Yes," he said quietly. "They do." And then the door of him closed again, and the inspector returned, gathering his hat. "Thank you for the tea, Fräulein Nystrom. Thank you for your patience with ministry housekeeping."

"At the door," I said, following him, wanting the interview finished and fearing its ending equally, "you mentioned a letter. Received last month. Unusual routing."

He paused on the threshold, fog curling gray past his shoulders into my hall.

"If such a letter reached this address," I went on, keeping my voice level, curious, innocent, "it never reached me. I receive bills, supply invoices, and the occasional correction from the Geographical Bureau. If the ministry has records suggesting otherwise—"

"The ministry has records suggesting everything," Halvard said. "That is the trouble with records." He settled the hat onto his head. "Good day, Fräulein. I hope the coastline goes well."

I did not ask how he knew what I was working on. I had learned already that asking Halvard a direct question was handing him the answer to it.

He descended the steps into the fog, and I watched him go — not hurrying, pausing once to look up at the river side of the building, at my windows, at the roofline, cataloguing exits perhaps, sightlines, the geometry of pursuit. Then the fog took him, and I shut the door, and leaned against it, and found my hands were shaking for the first time all morning.

* * *

I gave myself three minutes. That was the allowance — three minutes of fear, honestly spent, because Elias had taught me that suppressed fear leaks, while admitted fear can be channeled like any other force on a survey. Three minutes of shaking hands and shallow breath and the taste of copper at the back of my throat.

*Unshakably rehearsed*, the coroner had written, five years ago, about the nineteen-year-old girl who lied about how Elias Nystrom died. And Halvard had carried that sentence in his folder for five years like a pressed flower between pages, waiting for the season when it could be opened again.

Because the season had come. The bridge. Everything now was the bridge — its opening, its politics, its appearance of order — and every record, every silence, every rehearsed testimony from five years ago had been pulled from the drawers and dusted and read.

He had asked about visitors. He had asked about letters. He had sat in Elias's chair and drunk my tea and left without arresting me, and none of that meant I was safe. It meant only that he had not yet found what he needed, and men like Halvard always find what they need eventually, unless it stops existing first.

I went to the shelf of coastal surveys and drew out the 1907 shipping ledger, and held it a moment, feeling through the boards the small hard rectangle sewn into its spine. Thirty days, the letter had demanded. Testimony. Names.

Now the calendar had a second clock running inside it: the clock of Halvard's patience.

I sewed the ledger back into its shadows and climbed to the drafting table, and unrolled the atlas to the margin notes I had spent three nights deciphering — Elias's tiny coded script, the numbers that pointed to the third pier, to the gap, to the route across the water that existed nowhere on any official drawing.

*He knew,* I thought, tracing the faded figures with one dry fingertip. *He knew they would come asking someday. He built the answer into the margins.*

Outside, the fog was lifting off the river, and beneath it, invisible from my window, the third pier stood in the current with its secret flaw and its secret crossing, waiting for whoever read the water correctly.

Three days later, Marta brought up my supper and lingered in the doorway longer than usual, wiping her hands on her apron, and said, in a voice too casual by half:

"That inspector — he's asked me to send word if anything unusual comes to the house. Post, callers, packages. Nothing to trouble you over, miss. Just his thoroughness." She smiled her broad landlady's smile. "I'd never betray you, of course."

I thanked her. I ate the supper. And afterward I sat in the dark with the lamp unlit, understanding at last why Halvard had known the shape of my silences, and wondering which of the two of them — the inspector or the landlady — I should learn to fear more.
