## 6. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The ferry had a name — Noor remembered it the way one remembers a first lover's name, without trying: *The Meridian*. A squat little paddle-steamer with rust bleeding down its hull like old wounds and a wheelhouse that smelled of tar and pipe smoke, it crossed the river twice a day in those years when crossing was still an ordinary thing, as unremarkable as going to market or visiting a cousin.

Seven years ago. Noor was seventeen then, and she still thought of borders as lines drawn on paper — which is to say, she thought of them the way a mapmaker's apprentice thinks of them: as things that could be erased with a wet cloth if only someone had the courage.

She stood at the rail on the Vasse side of the river, waiting for the late crossing, her satchel heavy with proof sheets Elias had sent her to collect from the printer in Corvane. The proof sheets were an excuse. The truth was simpler and more embarrassing: Quentin Vasse had written her a letter three days earlier, four sentences long, saying the apricots were ripe and that if a certain mapmaker's apprentice happened to find herself on this bank before the season turned, there would be apricots enough for two.

She had told Elias she needed the printer's corrections verified against the master survey. He had looked at her over his spectacles with the expression he reserved for lies he found tolerable — not approving, exactly, but acknowledging their craftsmanship — and said, "Take the morning boat back, not the evening one."

She had taken the evening one anyway.

* * *

The orchards above Corvane were gold-green in the last light, and Quentin climbed the ladder like a boy who had never once considered falling. That was the thing about him, Noor thought, watching from below with her arms full of fruit she'd already picked: he moved through the world as though it had been arranged for his convenience. It wasn't arrogance. It was something rarer — a kind of unexamined faith that the world would continue being reasonable.

"You're picking the bruised ones," she called up.

"I'm picking the ones nobody else wants. That's charity."

"That's laziness wearing charity's coat."

He laughed — that easy, carrying laugh — and tossed an apricot down to her, which she caught, and ate standing in his family's orchard with the river glinting far below through the trees, and it did not occur to her, not even faintly, that she was memorizing all of it. The light through the leaves. The sticky sweetness running down her wrist. The way he came down the ladder two rungs at a time because patience had never been among his virtues.

They sat on the orchard wall until the light went copper, eating apricots and arguing about nothing — about whether the river belonged to one nation or both or neither, a favorite argument of theirs because neither of them could win it. Quentin held that water couldn't be owned. Noor held that everything could be owned; you only had to draw the line properly.

"Then draw me a map," he said, "of where the river ends and the other country begins."

"In the middle of the channel. By treaty."

"And if the channel moves? Rivers move, Noor. Everyone knows rivers move except the people who draw them."

She opened her mouth to defend the cartographer's honor and found, to her irritation, that he was right. She closed it again.

"Ha," said Quentin, with enormous satisfaction.

"Rivers move," she admitted. "Lines don't have to."

"Then maybe the lines are the problem." He said it lightly, tossing a pit into the grass, but something underneath the lightness made her look at him. In the last year the word *declaration* had begun appearing in the newspapers of both nations — always in the conditional tense, always *should circumstances require* — and adults spoke about it the way they spoke about weather coming in from the mountains: unpleasant, distant, nothing to be done. Quentin read the papers. Noor knew he read them because he'd stopped joking about them entirely, and Quentin joked about everything.

"When do you take the boat?" he asked.

"Last crossing. Nine o'clock."

He glanced west, where the sun was going down behind the hills of her own country, and for a moment — just a moment — his face did something complicated. Then it cleared, and he grinned, and said, "Then we'd better fill your satchel before I walk you down. Elias will want his proof sheets more than your company."

"Elias wants my company," Noor said. "He just pretends otherwise so I'll leave him alone with his maps."

"Your master is a wise man."

"He's a difficult man. There's a difference."

"Not much of one," said Quentin, "from where I'm sitting."

* * *

He walked her down through the orchard as the dark came up out of the hollows, and by the time they reached the landing at Corvane the lamps were lit along the quay and *The Meridian* sat low in the water, taking on its last passengers and crates. There were more people at the landing than usual. Noor noticed it the way she noticed everything now — automatically, professionally — and catalogued it: more travelers than freight, more trunks than usual, several families with the particular organized exhaustion of people who are moving rather than visiting.

"Busy night," Quentin said.

"It's market week in Corvane."

"It's not market week."

It wasn't. They both knew it wasn't. Neither of them said anything else about it.

On the gangplank, a customs officer was checking papers — not thoroughly, not yet, but checking them, which was itself new. Noor had crossed this river perhaps forty times in her life and had never once been asked for anything but the fare. The officer barely glanced at hers; an apprentice with proof sheets from a licensed mapmaker was the least interesting person on the quay. But he looked longer at the men ahead of her, and longer at the family with the trunks, and Noor saw Quentin watching from the shore with his hands in his pockets, and understood that they were both doing the same arithmetic.

The deckhand called the five-minute bell.

Quentin stepped closer. Up close, in the lamplight, she could see that the confidence had gone somewhere, or gone underground; what remained was just a boy of nineteen with river mud dried on his boots who was trying very hard to sound casual.

"So," he said. "If they close it. The crossing. Someday."

"They won't."

"If they do." He wasn't letting it go, and she realized with a small cold surprise that he had thought about this before tonight — had thought about it often, the way you worry a tooth. "What happens to us?"

"Nothing happens to us. We write letters."

"Letters get stopped. You know how I know letters get stopped? My uncle's letters stopped. All of them, in one month, four years ago, and nobody ever explained why. They didn't get lost, Noor. Letters don't get lost in batches."

The bell rang again. Passengers pressed past them up the gangplank. Noor felt the crowd moving around her like current around a stone, and heard herself say, in a voice steadier than she felt, "Then we find each other anyway."

"How?"

"I don't know. Someone finds a way. There's always a way — there's a ford above the gorge in summer, there are fishermen who cross for the salmon and no one questions a fisherman—"

"Noor."

"What?"

"Promise me properly. Not a plan. A promise." He took her hand, apricot-sticky and ink-stained both, and held it between both of his, and the lamplight caught the seriousness in his face and made it suddenly older. "Because plans are things you make about weather and fares and which boat to take. This is different. Whatever they build between us — walls, checkpoints, whatever my government says about yours and yours about mine — you come find me, or I come find you. However long it takes. However far it is. We don't let a line on somebody's map decide what happens to us."

Somewhere behind them the deckhand was shouting the last call, and the paddles were beginning to turn, churning the black water into lamplit foam.

"This is dramatic," Noor said, because her voice wanted to shake and irony was the brace she'd built for it.

"Yes," Quentin agreed. "I'm a dramatic person. Promise anyway."

She looked at him — at the orchard boy with the mud on his boots who was asking her to make a vow against governments — and every reasonable part of her mind listed the reasons this was a child's promise: borders were serious, history was heavy, love was not a bridge. And underneath all of it, quiet and certain as bedrock, another voice said: *he is right. Rivers move. Lines don't have to.*

"I promise," she said.

"Say all of it."

"I promise that if they close the crossing, I will find you, or you will find me, however long it takes, however far it is, and no line on anybody's map decides what happens to us." Her throat tightened on the last words. "There. Witnessed by a paddle-steamer."

"The Meridian," Quentin said solemnly. "May she testify well."

He kissed her then — briefly, on the mouth, the first time — tasting of apricots, and then pushed her gently toward the gangplank before either of them could make it into more than it could survive, which was the kindest and most cowardly thing he ever did, and they both knew it, and neither of them minded.

Noor went up the plank. At the rail, turning back, she watched him stand on the quay with his hands jammed in his pockets, not waving, just watching, the way you watch something you intend to see again. The lamps of Corvane shrank. The river widened into darkness. And she stood at the stern rail the whole crossing with her sticky hand curled closed, holding the shape of his grip in it, while behind them — though she did not turn to look — the lights of the border post on the far bank burned a little brighter than they had the week before.

* * *

Quentin watched the boat until the river took it, and then some time after that, until the wake was indistinguishable from the ordinary movement of the water.

His father found him on the quay an hour later, still there, throwing stones flat across the black surface — a boyish thing to do, and he did it anyway, because his hands wanted occupation and his head refused to give them any.

"She'll keep," his father said, sitting down beside him on the bollard. Quentin didn't pretend not to know who he meant. His father was not a man who tolerated pretending. "Whatever comes, that girl will keep. Nystroms are granite. You want to worry about, worry about the rest of us."

"They're talking about closing the crossing. Properly closing it. Not restricting — closing."

His father was quiet for a while. Out in the channel, the last lights of *The Meridian* crawled north toward the opposite shore.

"There's a survey party camped above the gorge," his father said at last. "Been there two weeks. Measuring. You know what they're measuring, a river that's been measured a hundred times?"

"A bridge site."

"A bridge site." His father threw his own stone; it skipped once and sank. "Bridges go two ways, son. Remember that. Everybody building one remembers only the going-toward."

Quentin thought of Noor's face in the lamplight, the way she'd recited the promise like a clause in a treaty — precise, complete, witnessed — and felt something in his chest that was too large and too certain to have any fear in it at all.

"Then I'll be the going-toward," he said.

His father looked at him sideways, and whatever he saw made him stand up and put a hand briefly on Quentin's shoulder, heavier than affection, lighter than a warning. "Come on. Your mother kept supper."

But Quentin stayed a moment longer on the bollard, looking at the dark water, at the invisible line running down the middle of the channel that no eye could see and every gun in two nations was preparing to enforce.

Above the gorge, unseen in the night, the surveyors' lanterns burned in a neat row along the bank — and downstream, at the border post, a clerk was feeding fresh orders into the signal lamp, blinking them north to the capital, blink by blink, in a code Noor Nystrom would one day read without knowing she could: *traffic increasing. recommend closure within the quarter.*

The river ran on between them, indifferent and silvered under the rising moon, carrying the smell of ripe apricots all the way down to the sea.
