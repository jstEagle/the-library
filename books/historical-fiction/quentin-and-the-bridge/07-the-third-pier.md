## 7. The Third Pier

The river kept its own hours, and so did Quentin Vasse.

He had learned them the way a thief learns the habits of a house he intends to rob — patiently, respectfully, with an ache in his chest that had nothing to do with guilt and everything to do with hope. The night watch changed at eleven and again at three. The dredge barges ran until midnight when the moon was generous, later when it wasn't. The workmen's lanterns died in stages along the scaffolding like a row of candles being snuffed by invisible fingers, and after that, for roughly forty minutes, the bridge belonged to no one.

Forty minutes. He had measured them against his own pulse more times than he could count.

Tonight the moon was a thin paring of bone above the eastern hills, just enough light to betray him if he moved wrong, not enough to save him if someone saw him first. He crouched in the reeds below the old ferry landing — the same landing where, seven years ago, a boatman named Aldric had poled the last open crossing while soldiers on both banks pretended not to notice each other. The pilings were rotten now, soft as bread. The nation on the far side might as well have been the moon.

But the pier was real. The third pier was real.

Quentin pulled his canvas jacket tighter and began to move.

* * *

He had been Joren Halde for two years — a laborer's name, a laborer's gait, a laborer's habit of keeping his eyes down and his hands calloused-looking even when they weren't. The foreman, a barrel-chested man called Doss with eyebrows like hedgerows, had hired him without a second glance because the bridge project ate men the way the river ate banks: constantly, and without much complaint from anyone who mattered. Quentin hauled timber. He held plumb lines. He learned to spit accurately off high ironwork and to swear in the flat accent of the northern districts, which was not so different from his own accent as long as he only ever said four or five words at a time.

Five years since the declaration. Five years since his family's name had gone onto a list, and the list had gone into a drawer, and the drawer had become a warrant, and the warrant had become a reason for his father to disappear one October night and never come back. Quentin counted days the way other men counted wages. One thousand eight hundred and forty-one, tonight. He did the arithmetic every morning the way other men prayed.

And through all of it — through the false name and the hauling and the spitting and the careful nothingness of his existence — he had kept a flower pressed between the pages of a prayer book he never prayed from. A blue cornflower, gone the color of old smoke now, given to him by a girl on a ferry deck when they were fourteen and the borders were still lines on paper instead of guns on banks.

Noor. Even thinking her name felt like trespass. That was the trouble with hope; it always felt like standing somewhere he wasn't allowed.

* * *

The third pier rose out of the water like a drowned giant's finger — stone and timber, half its intended height, wrapped in scaffolding that groaned softly whenever the wind came up the valley. Work on it had stopped twice this year. Officially: funding. Unofficially: Quentin had heard the workmen muttering about soundings, about the riverbed being wrong there, about engineers arriving in unmarked carriages and leaving without eating supper. Men who built bridges for a living knew what it meant when engineers stopped eating.

He climbed.

The scaffolding was second nature now; he'd been ascending it legally for two years. But tonight every handhold felt different, because tonight he wasn't climbing to build. He was climbing to learn. There was a difference, and his body knew it — a wrongness in the way his weight settled, the way his breath came shallow. Hope that feels like trespass, he thought, gripping a wet crossbeam. That's exactly what this is.

At the working platform, thirty feet above black water, he stopped and listened.

The patrol boat came around the downstream bend at a quarter past the hour — he'd timed it against the church bells in Vellmark, whose cracked bell rang eleven strokes with a stutter on the last one. Oars muffled with cloth, a single lantern hooded green, two men. They passed beneath him close enough that he could smell the tar on their hull and hear the younger guard complaining about the cold. They did not look up. Nobody looked up at the unfinished pier at night; it was scenery, it was failure, it was a monument to money wasted, and monuments to waste attracted no one.

Except, apparently, one man with a stolen future folded inside his jacket.

When the green lantern had shrunk to a spark, Quentin took out the stub of pencil and the scrap of oilcloth he'd brought, and began, in letters small enough to hide under a thumbnail, to write down everything Elias Nystrom's margin notes had promised would be here to find.

* * *

The atlas itself he had never seen. But Noor had described it once — or rather, she had described her master describing it, back when they were children sitting on the boathouse roof and she still talked about maps the way other girls talked about suitors. *Elias says a map is a promise made in ink,* she'd said, legs dangling over the river. *He says the margins matter more than the coastlines, because anyone can draw a coastline but only the maker knows what he left out on purpose.*

She'd laughed then. She'd been laughing all that summer. It was the last summer anything was funny.

Quentin hadn't thought about the margins in years — couldn't afford to think about Noor at all, most days — until three weeks ago, when a letter had reached him through channels he didn't dare examine too closely. A single line in a hand he recognized instantly, because he had watched that hand practice copperplate on scraps of butcher paper across a ferry deck:

*The third pier stands on ground that will not hold it. There is a way across at low water. Trust the margin.*

He had read it eleven times. He had burned it in the forge at the timber yard and watched the ash go up the flue, and then he had stood there feeling the absence of the paper like a wound. And every night since, he had come to the pier to make her words true.

* * *

Low water revealed things the river otherwise kept.

He went down the scaffolding's inner face — the face turned toward the bank, hidden from the patrol route — and dropped the last six feet onto a shelf of exposed gravel and mud that smelled of rot and old iron. The pier's foundation loomed above him, a mass of cut stone furred with weed, and at its base, where the waterline had withdrawn, he saw what the engineers must have seen: the bedrock simply stopped. Not eroded. Stopped. As if the river's floor were a page torn out halfway through the sentence, and someone had decided to build a thousand tons of stone on the tear anyway.

Quentin knelt and pressed his palm flat to the mud. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the ground gave slightly — a spongy give, a lie in the earth. He dug with his fingers, quick and quiet, and found what the soundings had found: gray clay over silt over nothing, a throat of soft ground going down past the reach of his arm. A pier planted here would settle. A pier that settled would crack. A span resting on a cracked pier —

He sat back on his heels, heart hammering, and understood two things at once, and they fought each other in his chest like animals in a sack.

The first thing: this bridge was going to fail. Someday, some year, with crowds on it — opening-day crowds, most likely, dignitaries and bunting and brass bands — the third pier would shift, and the span would drop, and the river would take whatever it was given. Both governments knew it. Both governments had decided the ribbon-cutting mattered more than the arithmetic. Elias Nystrom had died carrying proof of it, and Noor —

The second thing arrived slower, colder: if Noor's letter told him there was a way across at low water, and the way across ran along this failing foundation, then she had known about the flaw before he did. Which meant the flaw was in the atlas. Which meant the atlas was worth more than any border in either empire. Which meant everyone who wanted that atlas silenced — and there would be many, on both banks — would eventually find their way to the girl who kept it.

He looked across the black water to the far shore, to the smudge of lamplight that was her town, and felt the trespass of hope turn into something sharper. Fear. Not for himself; he'd spent five years learning to carry fear for himself the way a mule carries freight, without dignity and without complaint. For her.

* * *

He was writing the measurements by feel, pencil barely whispering on oilcloth, when the voice came from above him.

"Lost something, Halde?"

Doss. Standing on the platform edge thirty feet up, backlit by the rising moon, arms folded on the rail like a man settling in to enjoy a long show.

Quentin's mind performed the trick it had practiced for two years: it became empty, pleasant, slow. He let three full seconds pass before he answered, and when he did, his voice came out rough with feigned sleep. "Dropped a chock line. Off the platform earlier. Foreman docks a day's wage for lost gear."

Silence. The river filled it, sliding and chuckling against the stones.

"Chock line." Doss's voice drifted down, unhurried. "Funny. I checked the tool ledger at dusk. Nothing missing."

"Then I'll owe you a line," Quentin said, and spat, and hoped the dark hid how wet his palms had gone.

Another silence, longer. Then the hedgerow eyebrows tilted, and Doss said, in a tone that had shed all its laziness, "You've been asking questions, Halde. Three weeks of questions. About the soundings. About why we stopped pouring concrete. About which nights the inspectors come. Tonight I see you down at the base of my pier at an hour no honest man wants his boots in mud." He leaned forward, and the moonlight caught the flat shine of his eyes. "I've built nine bridges in thirty years. You know what I've learned? The men who ask about foundations aren't planning to build on them. They're planning to run across them, or blow them up, or sell them. So you're going to tell me which one you are."

Every exit closed at once. Up was death by rope and lantern; the water was death by current; the truth was death by warrant. Quentin stayed kneeling in the mud, small and harmless, and searched desperately for the fifth thing, the thing that was none of the others.

It came from somewhere deeper than strategy — from a ferry deck seven years gone, from a pressed flower the color of smoke.

"The bridge is going to fall down," he said quietly. "And whoever signs off on it knows it. And I want to know who, so I can decide whether to be afraid of them or sorry for them."

The words hung in the cold air. Doss didn't move. Somewhere upstream, the dredge barge coughed and fell silent — midnight, or near it, the beginning of the forty minutes.

"You read engineering, do you?" the foreman said at last.

"I read mud."

A long exhale from above, and then — impossibly — a short bark of laughter, quickly strangled. Doss straightened, and his voice when it came again was lower, gruffer, and aimed carefully away from the water, toward the bank, toward nowhere. "Ledger says you're owed a chock line. Come to the yard tomorrow, dawn, and I'll write you up for one." A pause. "And Halde. The inspectors come Tuesdays. Never Fridays. In case a man needed to know a thing like that for honest reasons."

Then the heavy footsteps crossed the platform, receded, and were gone.

Quentin knelt alone in the mud at the foot of the dying pier, holding measurements that could hang him and knowledge that could topple two governments, and laughed once — a single broken note that the river swallowed whole.

Tuesdays. Never Fridays.

And Friday — he counted against his pulse, against the thousand-eight-hundred-and-forty-one days — Friday was the night of the span test, when the whole city would come to the banks with lanterns and fireworks and cheering, and every eye in two nations would be fixed on the middle of the bridge instead of underneath it.

He pressed the oilcloth scrap flat, folded it small, and slid it inside his jacket, over his heart, next to the ghost of a cornflower.

Across the dark water, on the far shore, a single lamp appeared at the end of a ruined boathouse jetty. It blinked once. Paused. Blinked again.

His breath stopped.

Then it blinked a third time — and the rhythm of it, long-short-long, was a code he had no right to know and knew anyway, because somewhere in a workshop full of silences, Noor Nystrom had opened her master's atlas and found the same margin he had.
