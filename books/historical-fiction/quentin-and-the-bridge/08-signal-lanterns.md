## 8. Signal Lanterns

The atlas's code revealed itself the way all of Elias's secrets did: slowly, and only to someone who had already learned to think in his rhythms.

Noor had spent three evenings at the worktable with a candle burned down to its brass dish, tracing the marginalia with a loupe pressed so close to her eye that the world outside the lens ceased to exist. The notes in the margins of the bridge plates were not random annotations — she had known that from the first night, because Elias never wrote anything without purpose, not even the flourishes on his compass roses. But knowing they meant something and knowing *what* they meant were separated by a distance she had to cross one symbol at a time.

It was a surveyor's cipher. Of course it was. Elias had been too proud of his trade to use anything he hadn't invented himself, and what better disguise for secret writing than the language of measurement? Every number in the margins was a bearing. Every fraction, an elevation. Read them against the bridge's own plates — the unfinished third pier drawn in section, elevation, plan — and the numbers resolved into instructions. A route. His cramped hand had walked her along it in her mind three times now, until she could trace it blindfolded: down the northern stairwell of the customs house, through the drainage culvert that ran beneath the toll yard, out along the caisson at the pier's landward foot, up the iron ladder bolted to its seaward face, and then — this was the part that made her breath go shallow every time — across the open gap where the span had not yet been built. Forty feet of nothing above black water, crossed on two parallel cables strung for the workmen's trolleys, cables no wider than a man's palm.

Elias had walked it. She was certain of that now. Certain in the way she was certain of his ink formulas and his morning habits — by accumulation, by the thousand small proofs a master leaves scattered through his apprentice's years. There were chalk smudges on the original drawings where no draftsman would rest his hand. There was a note, almost playful, in the margin of plate fourteen: *Wind from the east after midnight; hold the second cable and count your heartbeats, girl.* Girl. He had written it for her. He had written the whole thing for her, five years before she needed it, the way he did everything — early, quietly, and without asking whether she would thank him.

She sat back and rubbed her eyes until colors bloomed behind her lids. The workshop was cold. She had let the stove go down again without noticing, which Marta would scold her for, which was somehow the most ordinary thought she'd had in days.

Marta. Noor turned that name over now the way she turned over a stone on the riverbank, looking for what lived underneath it. The landlady who brought her bread on Sundays and complained about the price of coal. The woman who had stood in the doorway of this very room while Halvard's letter lay on the desk — Noor remembered now, with the particular nausea of hindsight, that Marta's eyes had gone to the paper and stayed there half a heartbeat longer than politeness required. Had she read it upside down? Could she?

It didn't matter yet. What mattered was that Noor could no longer say anything aloud inside these walls that she wasn't prepared to have repeated elsewhere. The workshop had stopped being hers. It was a room with ears now, and she would have to live in it accordingly.

Which meant the river. The river couldn't be overheard.

* * *

The signal code was on plate nine, hidden in plain sight among the tide tables.

Every atlas Elias ever made carried tide tables for whatever waters it charted — heights, times, the phases of the moon rendered as neat columns of figures. This one was no different, except that certain entries were wrong. Not subtly wrong; a careful eye would catch it immediately if it knew to look. The height of the 3rd hour tide on the spring tables was given as eleven feet where no tide on this river had ever exceeded eight. And beside the erroneous figures, in the margin, Elias had written a single word in his smallest hand: *lantern.*

She had puzzled over it for a full evening before the obvious arrived, as obvious things do, all at once. Eleven feet. Eleven positions. A lantern raised or lowered through eleven stations — arm's lengths above the waterline, or above some fixed mark — spelling numbers, and numbers spelling words. Elias had taught her the counting alphabet when she was twelve, a games-master's trick for boring ferry crossings: assign each letter its place, break words into pairs, flash and pause, flash-flash and pause. He had called it *the light telegraph*, and she had thought it a charming eccentricity, the sort of thing old men invented to amuse children.

He had been building her a vocabulary for exactly this night, and neither of them had known it.

There was one problem, and it sat in her chest like swallowed ice: she didn't know whether Quentin was alive, whether he was free, whether he was within fifty miles of this river — and even if he were, she didn't know whether he remembered. Seven years since the ferry. Five since the borders closed and the letters stopped. People forgot. People *had* to forget; forgetting was how you survived a country that remembered everything for you.

And if he did remember, if he was watching — then anyone else might be watching too. The bridge works ran a night watch now; she'd seen their brazier glow from the embankment on her evening walks, a small orange eye near the toll house. If she flashed a lantern at the water and something answered, she wouldn't know what had answered.

She decided she didn't care. That frightened her more than anything else these past weeks — not Halvard, not the letter, but the ease with which she had stopped caring about the risk the moment Quentin's survival entered the arithmetic.

* * *

She chose the boathouse first, because the boathouse chose itself.

The ruins stood on the shingle below the eastern embankment, half a mile downriver from the bridge works: the gutted shell of the old ferry terminal, roofless since the last crossing, its stone walls standing to shoulder height against the wind. In peacetime it had smelled of tar and rope and the fish carts that lined up on the gravel road. Now it smelled of river rot and gull droppings, and no one went there, because there was nothing left there to want. Which made it perfect. Elias's marginalia marked it too — a small anchor symbol drawn beside the ruins on plate seven, with the notation *rendezvous, flood tide* — as though he had always intended the story to end where it had, in some sense, begun.

For signaling she needed height and darkness, and the boathouse gave her both. Its western wall still carried the stub of the old signal mast, snapped off six feet up, and above it the bluff rose steeply, crowned with a lone birch whose trunk she could climb to twice the height of any watchman's brazier. From the birch's lowest fork, the whole black ribbon of the river lay open below her, the bridge works a scatter of ember-colored lights two hundred yards upstream, and beyond them — invisible, but present the way a scar is present — the far bank. The other country. His bank.

She waited four nights.

Four nights of climbing down the back stair with her boots in her hand, of slipping along the embankment wall in the blue hour after Marta's lamp went dark, of sitting in the birch fork with her back against cold bark while the river talked to itself below. Four nights of raising the small shuttered lantern she'd bought from the chandler under the pretext of lighting her cellar stairs, opening its slide in counted rhythm toward the far shore, and getting nothing back but the water and the wind and, on the third night, the distant bark of a dog that stopped her heart for ten full seconds.

She used the counting alphabet. She kept the messages short, because short messages are harder to intercept and easier to deny: *NYSTROM. FERRY GIRL. BOATHOUSE.* Over and over, in bursts of ten minutes, then silence for an hour, then again. Anyone glancing upstream would see, at worst, a flicker of light among trees — a poacher, a lover, a drunk with a candle. She told herself this. She told herself many things, sitting in the dark with frost forming on her eyelashes.

On the fourth night, the river answered.

* * *

She had almost packed up. The cold had gotten into her knees, and her fingers were clumsy inside two pairs of gloves, and she had begun composing the speech she'd give herself on the walk home — *you lit a lantern at a ghost, Noor, and ghosts don't write back* — when she saw it.

A light. Far off, at the limit of what the eye could separate from the general darkness of the southern shore. Not steady like the work lamps. Not swinging like a patrol. It appeared, held, vanished. Appeared again.

Her hands shook so badly she dropped the shutter slide twice before she got the sequence right: *IDENTIFY.*

The far light held steady for a long moment — long enough that doubt came flooding back in, cold and total, and she thought: *it's a trick, it's Halvard, they've decoded Elias's tables, they've been waiting for you to do exactly this* — and then the light began to move, and it wasn't spelling anything at all.

It was waving. Wide, wild, sweeping arcs, side to side, the way a person waves from a departing boat when formality has abandoned them. Then it steadied, and the flashes came, slow and deliberate, a child's careful counting:

*FERRY GIRL. PRESSED FLOWER. STILL HAVE IT.*

Noor made a sound she had never heard herself make before — half laugh, half sob, loud enough in the empty dark that she clapped her glove over her own mouth. Her eyes blurred instantly, and she had to blink hard and wipe her face on her sleeve to see the answer she needed to send, because there was no time to waste being undone by joy; joy could undo her later, joy could keep, but the window couldn't.

*BOATHOUSE RUINS. TWO NIGHTS. AFTER MIDNIGHT. EAST WIND.*

She sent it three times. Three times the far light repeated it back, letter-perfect, and the third repetition ended with a sequence she hadn't sent — a short, quick pattern, flash-flash-pause, flash-pause, flash — and it took her a moment, laughing silently into her frozen glove, to recognize it. Their old game. The nonsense word Elias had invented for the two of them on the ferry, the private joke disguised as a cipher: *Q-N-R*, Quentin-and-Noor, the signature they'd signed to every message they'd ever traded across the water, back when the water was only water.

Then the light went out, and the far shore was just a shore again, and Noor sat in the birch tree with tears freezing on her cheeks and her heart hammering like someone hammering on a door, alive, alive, *alive.*

* * *

Getting home took twice as long as usual, because her legs wouldn't obey the discipline the errand required. She caught herself hurrying, corrected herself, slowed, hurried again. Twice she stopped entirely, pressed against the embankment wall, and simply breathed — great ragged gulps of river air that steamed in the lamplight spilling from Marta's kitchen window.

The window was lit. Marta was awake, then. Noor composed her face into its working mask — tired apprentice, cold walk, nothing to report to anyone — and let herself in through the courtyard door.

"You're late." Marta stood at the stove with her back to the door, stirring something that didn't need stirring. She didn't turn around. "The river air will be the death of you, walking at night."

"I lost track of time." Noor set her boots by the door. Her wet gloves she kept balled in her pocket, away from the light. "Working on the final plates. You know how the hours go."

"Mm." The spoon circled. "Inspector Halvard came by again today. While you were out."

The cold that went through Noor had nothing to do with the river. "Did he."

"Asked the same questions. Whether you'd had visitors. Whether you'd received any post besides his letter." Now Marta turned, and her face in the stove-light was carefully, elaborately neutral, a performance of blandness so complete it was itself a kind of confession — though Noor couldn't yet prove of what. "I told him you receive nothing but invoices from the bindery. I hope that was right."

"It was right," Noor said evenly. "Thank you, Marta."

"He seems very interested in you." A pause, weighted like a stone on a scale. "Or in what you're finishing. It's hard to tell with men like that. They ask about everything because they can't admit what they're really asking about."

"I'm finishing an atlas," Noor said. "That's all there is to ask about."

"Of course it is, dear." Marta smiled at her, warm as bread, and Noor climbed the stairs with the smile stuck to her skin like something adhesive and unclean.

In the workshop, she locked the door, sat down at the worktable, and put her forehead against the cool wood for a long time. Then she took out the wet gloves, spread them to dry where the stove's residual warmth could reach them, and opened the atlas to plate seven.

Two nights. After midnight. East wind.

She had a rendezvous with a ghost who had turned out to be flesh, and between here and there lay Marta's smile, Halvard's patience, thirty days shrinking toward zero, and a locked door that suddenly felt like the least secure thing in her life. She looked at the little anchor symbol beside the drawn ruins of the boathouse — *rendezvous, flood tide* — and felt the strange doubled weight of it: grief for the dead man who had mapped her escape before she knew she'd need one, and underneath the grief, rising through it like heat through floorboards, something she hadn't permitted herself in five years.

Hope. Reckless, specific, scheduled hope.

She dipped her pen, and on a scrap of blotting paper — a scrap she would burn before dawn — she wrote the only sentence that mattered, in the shorthand only she and one boy on the far shore had ever shared:

*East wind. Two nights. I'm coming.*

Outside, the river ran on in the dark, carrying the news faster than any lantern could.
