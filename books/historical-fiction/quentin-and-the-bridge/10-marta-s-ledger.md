## 10. Marta's Ledger

The rain had been falling for three days when I found the ledger.

It was an accident — the kind of accident that only happens to people who have already begun to distrust everything around them. The storm had backed up the drains in the cellar of Marta's house, where she kept preserves in crockery jars and where I stored the crates of Elias's papers that were too dangerous to keep upstairs but too precious to destroy. I had gone down with a lamp and a bucket to see what the water had reached, wading through an inch of cold river-smell, lifting crates onto the workbench one by one.

Marta's own things were stacked against the far wall: winter blankets in oilcloth, a trunk of her late husband's clothes, boxes of receipts going back twenty years because Marta Voss threw away nothing that could be counted. One box had soaked through. Water had found its way along a seam in the stone and pooled exactly there, as if the house itself had decided to open her accounts to me.

I should have left it. I tell myself now, as I told myself then, that I should have set the ruined box aside, salvaged what receipts were dry, and said nothing. But my hands were already lifting the lid, and inside, beneath a sodden layer of grocer's bills, lay a ledger bound in grey cloth with no title on the spine.

I knew the handwriting before I understood it. Everyone who has lived in a house knows the hand of the person who runs it — the firm verticals of Marta's lists pinned to the kitchen door, *bread, tallow, mending thread*, the little hooks she put on her sevens. I had seen that hand for six years on shopping notes and rent demands and, once, on a birthday card she left outside my door with a heel of honey cake.

The ledger recorded sums. That is how it began, at least. Small payments received, dated back two years, each entry in her tidy script: from the harbor office for washing done for clerks; from a widow on Tanner Street for sitting with her mother; ordinary money, honestly earned. I turned pages by lamplight while the rain hammered the trapdoor above me, and somewhere around the third signature the entries changed character.

*Received, four crowns, from the office of Inspector Halvard. For information re: tenants, correspondence, visitors.*

I sat down on an overturned crate. The water rose around my ankles and I did not move.

*Received, four crowns, re: the Nystrom woman. No visitors. Purchases of paper and ink above usual quantity.*

There it was, in ink I could have drawn from memory: my life, itemized and sold. The paper. The ink. The nights my lamp burned past midnight. Every silence I had performed so carefully in this house — every careful answer, every deliberate ordinariness — catalogued by the woman who baked bread on Fridays and complained about the price of coal and called me *child* when she thought I looked too thin.

I turned the pages faster now, though some part of me wanted to close the book and never know more. There were other names. The cobbler's apprentice who talked politics after his third glass. A schoolteacher on the east side who received foreign newspapers. All of it flowing, drop by drop, toward the same office — toward the man who had sat across from me with courteous eyes and asked about letters.

And then, near the middle, in an entry unlike all the others, written smaller, pressed hard enough into the page to emboss the sheet beneath:

*Composed and sent. The letter naming the Nystrom household. Signed with the crest of the Ministry — copied from the seal on Tomas's last conscription notice.*

My hands went still.

I read it again. And again. The lamp guttered, and I trimmed the wick with fingers that did not feel like mine.

Marta had written the letter.

Not the Ministry. Not Halvard himself. Marta — who climbed my stairs with soup when I was sick the winter after Elias died, who had held me at the funeral when my knees gave way, who had known Elias for thirty years and wept at his grave — had composed the accusation that hung over my household like a blade, forged the ministry's crest on its wax, and set it loose in the world.

Why? The question came with a cold clarity that frightened me more than fear would have. Because a lie that small takes either a monster or a reason, and I had eaten at Marta's table.

I kept reading. The answer was three pages further on, and when I found it I had to press my fist against my mouth to keep the sound inside me.

*Tomas writes that they are moving his company north in spring. To the fortifications opposite our side of the river. If the inquiry finds conspirators here, there will be reprisals — the officers have said so plainly, they will take hostages among the conscripts' families over there, or worse. But if the crown believes there is a nest of traitors on OUR side, an exchange becomes possible. They trade. I have seen it done with the fishermen. A named enemy buys a named son.*

*I am sorry, Elias. Forgive me. It is the only coin I have.*

The ledger trembled in my grip. Rain, river-water, lamplight — none of it seemed real. Somewhere across the water, in a barracks I had charted in an atlas that cost my master his life, a boy named Tomas Voss stood in a uniform of the enemy nation, and his mother had tried to buy him home with my name.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat in that flooded cellar. Long enough that the lamp burned low twice. Long enough for the water to rise another finger's width and soak through my skirt without my noticing. I read the ledger cover to cover, then again, committing it to memory the way I had memorized the first letter — because memory cannot be seized in a raid, cannot be produced on demand, cannot be burned by anyone but its keeper.

That had always been Elias's teaching. *Ink can be confiscated, Noor. Paper burns. What you carry in your skull, they must kill you to take.* He had said it lightly, tapping his temple, the day he taught me to chart coastlines without instruments. I had laughed then. I did not laugh in the cellar.

When I finally climbed the stairs, the house was quiet in the particular way of a house where someone is listening. Marta's kitchen door stood ajar. Light fell across the hall floor in a long yellow bar, and I could smell onions and bay leaf, the Friday stew she made whether there was money for meat or not.

She was at the stove with her back to me, stirring, and I stood in the doorway holding the wet ledger against my chest like a wound I was pressing shut, and I watched her shoulders move with the spoon, and I thought: *she has fed me for six years. She held my arm at the graveside. She has sold my name for four crowns a month, and she would do it again tomorrow, because her son is on the other side of that river and mine is not.*

"Noor." She had heard me; of course she had heard me. She turned, and her face did the thing faces do when they are caught between two expressions — the warmth arriving a half-second too late, the calculation underneath it visible like a fish under ice. "You're soaked through. Come by the fire, I'll get you a towel—"

"I found your ledger."

The spoon stopped. For a moment the only sound was the stew's slow bubble and the rain against the window, and I watched Marta Voss decide — visibly decide — between the lie and the truth. It took her perhaps two seconds. I have thought about those two seconds many times since. She was not a coward. She chose the truth because she had been carrying it alone for two years and some part of her, I think, had been waiting to set it down.

"Did you," she said. "All of it?"

"All of it."

She set the spoon down with great care, aligning it against the pot's rim, and wiped her hands on her apron as if preparing for a chore rather than a confession. "Then sit down, child, and let me look at you, because you've gone white as the ledger paper."

"I'm not your child."

"No." She didn't flinch. "No, I suppose you're not anymore."

We sat across from each other at the kitchen table where I had eaten a hundred suppers, and between us lay the ledger, dripping river-water onto the boards, and Marta looked at it the way people look at a grave they dug themselves.

"Ask me," she said.

"You wrote the letter."

"Yes."

"You forged the ministry's crest. You learned to do it from Tomas's conscription papers — you practiced, didn't you? There are practice seals in the box, under the receipts. Wax stubs. Dozens."

"Fifty-one," she said quietly. "It takes about fifty attempts before the impression sits right. Tomas's notice had a good deep crest. I melted it down and used the wax." She folded her hands on the table. They were a working woman's hands, knuckled and scarred from years of scalding water and hot ovens, and they did not shake. "You want to know why. Everyone wants the why first, as if the why changes the what."

"It doesn't change the what."

"No. But you'll hear it anyway." She looked past me, at the dark window where the rain ran down in ropes, toward the river neither of us could see. "Tomas was conscripted fourteen months after the declaration. Nineteen years old. He wrote to me every week until the spring, and then the letters started coming through a chaplain, censored, half their length. And in the last one — the last real one — he told me something he shouldn't have told anyone. That his officers had orders regarding families on this side of the river. That if things went badly, if there was trouble, sabotage, an incident — certain households would be taken. Held. Made examples of." Her voice stayed level the whole time, which somehow made it worse. "He listed names he'd heard. Ours was among them. The Nystrom name. Because of Elias. Because everyone remembers what Elias mapped, even if no one says it aloud."

"So you decided to be taken first. On your own terms."

"I decided to make my name worth something." For the first time her composure cracked — not into tears but into something harder, a bitterness like frost. "A household accused of treason is a currency, Noor. I've watched it spent. The fishermen's exchange, two winters ago — old Brandt swore his neighbor was running messages, and the neighbor's family paid, but Brandt got his brother back off a prison barge. Nobody punished Brandt. They *thanked* him. That's the arithmetic of this war. So I spent the only name I had that was valuable enough. Yours."

"And Halvard?"

"Halvard doesn't know I wrote it." Something flickered across her face — the first true fear I had seen in her. "Halvard thinks the letter came from a sympathetic clerk in the ministry, someone honest enough to warn the crown but afraid to sign. He's been investigating it like any anonymous denunciation. I report to him separately — tenants, correspondence, the ordinary watching — that's older than the letter, that started when the harbor office offered me money and refusing felt more dangerous than accepting. But the letter—" Her jaw tightened. "The letter is mine alone. If it unravels, it unravels to me. No one else hangs from it."

"But I hang from it. Quentin hangs from it. My household, my master's memory, a boy I've seen five times in seven years — we hang from your letter."

"Yes." She said it simply, without ornament, and I understood that this simplicity was the most terrible thing about her. Marta did not dress her sins in excuses. She laid them out flat and dared you to weigh them. "Yes. You do. And I have lain awake every night since I sealed that wax, and I would seal it again tomorrow morning, because Tomas is nineteen and his company moves north in the spring, and when they move them north, Noor, they move them within sight of this shore, and mothers on that side have already begun to disappear."

The rain filled the silence. I looked at her hands on the table, at the ledger between us swelling damply, at the stew going cold on the stove, and I searched myself for hatred and found something more complicated and less useful.

Because I remembered the funeral. I remembered her arm under mine, solid as a railing. I remembered Elias coming home from her kitchen with stories of Tomas as a boy — the boy who drew ships on every fence plank, who once rowed out to the ferry in fog just to say he'd touched the channel marker. I had liked Tomas. I had never met him, and I had liked him, the way you like a person you've only ever met in someone else's love.

And I remembered Quentin, fourteen years old on the ferry deck, pressing a wildflower into my hand as the banks slid apart, and the promise we made while the gulls screamed, and five years of silence measured in unsent letters.

Marta had done, in ink and forged wax, what I would burn a city to avoid doing. She had spent someone else's beloved to ransom her own.

"That's the difference between us," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, very quiet and very cold. "I won't pay with other people's sons."

"No," she agreed. "You won't. That's what makes you better than me, and it's also what makes you lose." She leaned forward, and her eyes — red-rimmed, exhausted, absolutely clear — held mine across the wrecked ledger. "Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then we need never speak of it again. Halvard is closer than you think. He asked me yesterday whether you receive visitors at night. Whether your lamp moves in the workshop after the house sleeps. Someone else has reported seeing lights on the water near the boathouse ruins — signals, they think, though Halvard dismissed it as smugglers. Smugglers!" She almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had ever seen. "He's wrong, and being wrong is the only reason you're still free. But he's patient, and he's thorough, and he loved you once, which makes him slower and more dangerous both. Whatever you're planning — whatever Elias left you planning — you have days, not weeks. Do you understand me? Days."

"How many days does the letter give?"

"Twenty-three now." She had counted. Of course she had counted; she had authored the deadline herself. "Twenty-three days, and then testimony, and then everything falls where it falls."

I stood. My legs ached from the cellar cold. At the doorway I stopped, because there was one question left and I needed it answered, not for leverage or strategy but for something in myself that refused to leave the room without it.

"The night of the funeral," I said. "When you held me up. Was that real?"

Marta closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she did not wipe them.

"Child," she said, "that was the truest thing in this whole rotten house."

I walked upstairs through the dark with the ledger under my arm, and behind me I heard her return to the stove and begin, deliberately, methodically, to stir the cold stew as though the evening were an evening like any other, as though betrayal were a chore like any other, and I understood that I did not forgive her — that I might stand at her grave someday still not forgiving her — and that nevertheless I would not be the one to break her, because I had seen the shape of her grief and recognized it as my own, worn on a different face, aimed at a different river.

* * *

In the workshop I locked the ledger inside Elias's iron map-case, beneath the false bottom where the master map slept, and I lit the lamp and pulled the atlas toward me and opened it to the margin where the coded notes waited — the third pier, the unfinished span, the route my dead master had threaded through the heart of the bridge like a stitch through flesh.

Twenty-three days.

Outside, the rain was easing at last. Far off, through the parting cloud, I saw it: a light on the far bank, moving along the scaffolds of the bridge — a workman's lantern, swinging steady, pausing, swinging again. Once, twice. Then it steadied, and held, and I could have sworn — I could not have sworn, I dared not swear — that it lingered longest at the place where the third pier rose black from the water.

Somewhere on that far bank, Quentin was counting the same days I was counting, though he didn't yet know the calendar had shortened.

I dipped my pen. There was a signal to compose, and a meeting to arrange, and no time left in the world for anything but speed.
