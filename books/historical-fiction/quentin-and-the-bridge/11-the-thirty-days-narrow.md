## 11. The Thirty Days Narrow

Ten days. Quentin Vasse had learned to measure time in rivets.

Each morning at the scaffolding he counted what the crew drove home — iron pins the size of a man's forearm, hammered into the span joints until the whole bridge rang like a struck bell. Four hundred rivets a day, on a good day. On a bad day, when the wind came down the river valley hard enough to make the crane cables sing, fewer. He had done the arithmetic a hundred times since the boathouse ruins, since Noor's lantern had spelled out *ten days* against the dark water, and every sum arrived at the same place: the numbers did not care that he was afraid.

He sat now in the loft above the cooperage where he lodged under the name Tomas Rehn, whittling a peg he did not need and listening to the rain work the shingles. Below him, through the floorboards, came the smell of oak staves and hot iron hoops, the ordinary music of a town that did not know his name was worth thirty years in a cell — or worse, depending on which clerk processed the paperwork. The declaration of enmity had been read aloud in every market square five years ago. He had been fourteen then, standing beside Noor Nystrom on the last ferry either nation would permit, her hand cold in his, both of them pretending the crossing was nothing more than an afternoon errand.

He still kept the flower she had pressed between the pages of his prayer book. It had gone the color of weak tea now, brittle as ash, but it held its shape: a small blue thing from the cliff path, picked in peacetime by a girl who had not yet learned to be careful with her face.

*Tomas Rehn* would have been twenty-six this year. Quentin had borrowed the name from a gravestone outside Harrow Fen, a boy dead of fever before his twelfth birthday, and worn it so long it fit like old boots. He knew its habits. *Tomas* drank sparingly, tipped his cap to foremen, lost small sums at cards without complaint, and never, ever asked questions about the river.

But *Tomas* was running out of days, because Noor was not.

He set down the knife. The peg clattered to the floorboards and rolled into shadow.

---

The letter she had shown him at the boathouse — no, she had not shown it; she had recited it from memory, word for word, the way mapmakers memorize coastlines — named her household. *Harboring the enemy's son.* Demanding testimony within thirty days. Thirty days that had already burned down to ten while he walked scaffolds and hauled cable and smiled at men who would turn him in for the price of a good coat.

Surrender himself. That had been his answer, there among the rotting boats: walk into the magistrate's office, give them the enemy's son they wanted, and let the charge dissolve around Noor like sugar in water. She had looked at him then the way she used to look at his worst ideas when they were children — the look that said *I love you, and you are a fool* — and told him no. Told him she had found something in Elias Nystrom's margins worth more than his surrender. Told him to trust the atlas.

So he trusted the atlas. And the atlas said: *wait for the span.*

---

He had spent four nights learning the third pier.

It was not sentiment that drew him there, though the pier was Elias Nystrom's legacy, marked in code along the margins of maps Quentin had never seen. It was geometry. The unfinished pier rose from midstream like a broken tooth, its granite courses climbing toward a deck that did not yet exist, wrapped in staging timber and rope guys and the black tarpaulins of the construction company. On the near shore, the workmen's gate; on the far shore, the same. Between them, three hundred yards of open water, and above the water — this was the thing, the only thing — the first completed span, stretching from the near bank to the pier's crown, a ribbon of iron and fresh planking two hundred feet long and forty feet above the current.

The span test. That was the key, and Quentin had pried it loose one evening in the site office, where he swept floors and sharpened chisels and made himself invisible in the way of men who are paid too little to notice. The engineer, a fat, decent man named Ostrava who wept openly at beautiful load calculations, had been drinking brandy over his schedule. *The eleventh,* he'd muttered to the site bookkeeper. *They want fireworks, they want the band from the garrison, they want photographs. First span tested under full live load, crowds on both banks, the minister himself coming up from the capital.*

Crowds. Fireworks. Every eye in two nations turned upward toward a ribbon of iron lit like a festival.

And underneath it all, one workman — *Tomas Rehn*, quiet, reliable, forgettable — walking out along the span in the dark before the barricades went up, or slipping past them after, moving from bank to pier to far shore while ten thousand people watched colored fire bloom over the water.

It was audacious. It was probably insane. Quentin turned it over in his mind the way he'd once turned river stones, looking for the crack that would split it open.

Here was the first crack: the barricades. Ostrava planned to rope off both approaches at sundown on the eleventh, clearing the span for the test train of loaded wagons. But the ropes went up at *sundown*, and the test itself was timed for nine o'clock, after the minister's speech. Ninety minutes, perhaps two hours, when the span stood empty but guarded by exactly two sentries per end — men chosen for their straight backs rather than their wits, because who would sabotage a bridge during its own celebration?

The second crack: the pier itself. The staging timbers climbed its full height, lashed crossways into a ladder any harbor rat could climb. Quentin had climbed it twice at night already, testing each joint, counting the rungs, learning which guy-ropes hummed in the wind and which stayed silent. From the pier's crown, a second staging dropped toward the far shore's unfinished approach — not a span yet, just the skeletal beginning of one, iron ribs reaching sixty yards across the water before ending in air. Sixty yards of rib-walking in the dark, then a drop of twelve feet onto the far abutment's rubble slope, then the cliff path Noor had described in lantern-code, up past the signal point to the road.

Twelve feet onto rubble in the dark. He could break an ankle. He could break his neck. He lay awake doing that arithmetic too, and the answer was always the same: a broken ankle on the far side of the river was a wound. A missing man on this side, with ten days left on Noor's ultimatum, was a death sentence for her entire household.

Urgency did strange things to a man's courage. It made the terrible option look like the cautious one.

* * *

Rain again on Thursday, the kind of cold horizontal rain that stopped all work, and Quentin spent the idle morning in the cooperage loft drawing the crossing on a barrel head with charcoal. Bank to span: ninety seconds if the sentries were at the far ends of their posts. Span to pier: two minutes, staying low behind the handrail timber that wasn't a handrail yet, just a spine of raw iron. Pier descent via staging: three minutes, hands cold, wood slick. Then the rib-walk, the drop, the climb. Eleven minutes, start to finish, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing would go wrong. Something always went wrong. He drew a second line beside the first: the fallback. If the span was watched too closely, go to water instead — the cooper's punt, dragged to the reed bank below the tannery, paddled downstream and under the far abutment's overhang where the patrols never looked because the current there could crack a hull. Riskier in spring melt. Quieter than iron.

He stared at the two lines until the charcoal dust got into the creases of his fingers, and thought about how every choice had narrowed to this shape: iron or water, night or night, and behind both of them the same closing door. Ten days, then nine, then eight, and somewhere across the river a woman was burning papers and keeping her face smooth for inspectors, spending herself to buy him this chance. He would not waste her purchase. That was the whole of his planning, beneath the diagrams and the minute-counts: *do not waste it.*

A knock below, then the cooper's voice, muffled: "Rehn! Visitor!"

Quentin's blood went to ice water. He wiped the barrel head with his sleeve — charcoal smearing into gray cloud — and took the ladder down slowly, arranging his face into the mild blankness of *Tomas*.

The visitor was not a soldier. It was the foreman, Brandl, a leathery man with a foreman's flat eyes, holding an oilcloth bundle under his arm against the wet.

"You left your chalk marks on the staging," Brandl said. No greeting. Rain dripped off the eaves between them.

"Chalk?" *Mild. Confused. A little slow.* "I don't chalk anything, sir. I'm below decks most days."

"Somebody's been marking the pier timbers. Little crosses. Fresh." Brandl's gaze sat on Quentin's face the way a level sits on a beam, waiting for the bubble to move. "You're up top sometimes. Nights."

"I sleep," Quentin said. "Ask anyone."

"Anyone says what I pay them to say." Brandl shifted the oilcloth bundle. For one long moment the cooperage smelled only of oak and rain, and Quentin felt the whole plan balance on a knife-edge thinner than a rivet. Then the foreman grunted, thrust the bundle forward — spare gloves, oiled canvas, the ordinary charity of a worksite in bad weather — and said, "Keep dry. Can't have my men coughing through the celebration week." He turned, paused at the door. "Eleventh will be a big day. Lots of strangers in town. You keep to the yard, Rehn. Strangers ask questions, and half of them aren't friendly."

"Yes, sir," said Tomas Rehn, and meant it as a promise in a language Brandl couldn't hear.

* * *

That night the rain cleared and the moon came up thin as a paring. Quentin went out along the cliff path above the works, to the hollow behind the gorse where he could see the whole river laid out silver and black, the span a pale seam across it, the pier a knuckle of stone. Somewhere on the far bank, in a workshop that smelled of ink and cedar, Noor was doing her own arithmetic. He wondered if she counted in rivets too, or in pages burned, or in heartbeats.

He had a message to send and no lantern of his own — light on this bank would draw the patrol boat inside an hour. But the cooperage kept signal flags for the timber barges, and tomorrow, working the loading dock, he could raise a specific pennant in a specific sequence, meaningless to the yard, legible to anyone who knew Elias Nystrom's margin-code. *Iron route confirmed. Eleventh, after the last wagon passes. Watch the north stair of the far approach — leave it unchained if you can. If I fail, burn everything and testify about nothing.*

He mouthed the words against the wind. *If I fail.* He would not say it aloud even alone; saying it gave it weight, and he needed to be lighter than the wind these days.

Ten days. Nine, when the sun came up. He thought of the pressed flower in his prayer book, tea-colored, patient, holding its shape through five years of exile. Things pressed long enough kept their form. That was all he was trying to do — keep the shape of himself, of them, until the press opened.

Below him the river ran dark and indifferent toward the sea, carrying the cold of mountains on its back. In seven days it would carry fireworks' reflections. In eight it would carry him.

He only hoped that when he reached the far bank, Noor would still be standing there — and that the door closing behind her would not close on them both.
