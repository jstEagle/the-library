## 12. Halvard's Second Visit

He came on a Tuesday, when the rain had turned the river the color of gun metal and the workshop smelled of damp paper and old ink.

Noor heard his boots on the stairs before she saw him — measured, unhurried, each step placed like a piece on a board. She had spent five days since his first visit rehearsing this moment without admitting she was rehearsing it: the angle of her shoulders at the drafting table, the letter she would be holding when he entered, the small frown of a woman interrupted at honest work.

She chose the coastal sheets. Her hands knew them so well they could lie for her.

"Inspector Halvard." She did not rise until he was already in the doorway, then rose slowly, as though her back ached from bending over the table. "You've caught me in the middle of the estuary. The Vellmark coast. It fights me every time."

"I've never trusted coastlines," Halvard said. He removed his hat and held it against his chest, letting the rain drip from its brim onto the floorboards — a small discourtesy disguised as thoughtlessness, or perhaps the reverse. "They promise you a shape and then change it with every tide."

"Elias used to say the same thing."

"Yes," Halvard said. "I imagine he said a great many things."

He crossed the room without invitation. That was the difference between this visit and the last; the first time, he had waited at the threshold like any caller, courteous as a Sunday guest. Now he moved through the workshop as if he had been measuring it in his absence — noting the drafting table, the flat files along the north wall, the iron stove with its grate of cold ash. His eyes went everywhere and settled nowhere, which was itself a kind of settling.

"You've come about the correspondence," Noor said.

Something flickered behind his gray eyes — not surprise exactly, but the pleasure of a man whose opponent has opened with the move he hoped for. "You anticipated me."

"The clerk from the Archive sent word yesterday that materials might be requested again. For verification purposes." She said it lightly, folding her hands. It was true, as it happened; a boy with ink-stained cuffs had brought the notice that morning, and Noor had read it four times before her heartbeat slowed enough to let her think. They were coming back. Of course they were coming back. "I've kept everything where you left it."

"Convenient," said Halvard.

"Orderly," said Noor. "Elias trained me to be orderly. He claimed disorder in an archive was a form of lying."

"And what did he claim silence was?"

The question came soft as a blade laid flat against skin. Noor let one breath pass — one breath, no more — and answered with a small smile. "A form of mercy, sometimes. But he also said mercy was expensive, and mapmakers should live within their means."

Halvard studied her for a long moment. Then he set his hat on the corner of the workbench, drew off his wet gloves finger by finger, and produced from his coat a leather portfolio stamped with the crown's seal in faded gold.

* * *

He laid the correspondence out across the drafting table with the reverence of a man dealing cards he already knew the value of. Forty-one letters, Elias Nystrom to various offices of the Bridge Commission, spanning six years. Noor had catalogued them herself, once, in the margin of a ledger no one would ever read. She watched Halvard's hands and counted with him, silently, as each letter found its place in the row.

Forty-one letters.

Forty pages of Elias's careful, economical hand — requests for survey access, disputes over datum points, objections to load tolerances, dry bureaucratic skirmishes fought over the heads of clerks who never answered.

And the forty-second page, the one that had lived folded against her ribs for eleven days now, sewn into the false backing of the atlas's final plate, was not among them.

Halvard reached the end of the row and paused. He did not look up. He simply stood there, fingertips resting on the last letter, and let the silence do his arithmetic for him.

"You count them too," Noor said.

"I count everything." He straightened. "Forty letters here. The Archive's register lists forty-one outgoing items for the period in question."

"Then the Archive has lost one," Noor said. "Or miscounted. Clerks are only human, Inspector. You said yourself the coast changes with every tide. Paper does worse. Paper drowns, burns, walks out of buildings inside coat pockets—"

"Inside coat pockets," Halvard repeated.

"—of men who take their work home," Noor finished smoothly. "Elias did. Constantly. Half his drafts died on that table and half were born at his kitchen table over cold coffee. If a page is missing, I'd look first to his habits, not to theft."

It was a good answer. She knew it was a good answer even as she gave it, and hated how good answers felt like winning a game she didn't want to be playing.

Halvard turned a letter toward her — the third in the row, dated fourteen months before Elias's death. "Tell me about this one."

She bent over it. Elias's hand, cramped and urgent beneath its formal surface: *I must insist the third pier be re-sounded before winter. The borings returned to my office do not match my own soundings of the riverbed, and I will not attach my name to figures I cannot reproduce.*

"His stubbornness," Noor said. "He quarreled with the Commission's engineers for years. He thought they took their measurements from horseback at low tide and called it science."

"And did they?"

"Some of them." She met his eyes. "Most of them were competent. Elias simply believed competence was a moral failing when it wasn't excellence."

The ghost of something moved across Halvard's face — not quite a smile. "You loved him."

"He raised me. Love doesn't enter into it. Or rather—" she touched the edge of the letter, lightly, "—it enters into everything, and you stop remarking on it."

Halvard nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something for him, and Noor understood with a cold slide in her stomach that whatever she confirmed, he would bend to fit the shape he had already drawn. That was what certainty looked like from the outside: not a locked door but a door being built around you, plank by plank, each answer another nail.

"May I?" He indicated the flat files along the wall.

"If you must."

He must. He worked through them methodically while Noor returned to the Vellmark coast and laid down ink she did not see, line after line of a shoreline that existed only because her hand remembered it. She listened to him open drawers, lift tissue paper, exhale through his nose at the density of Elias's archive. Once, near the bottom drawer — the drawer where the master map had slept for five years beneath false bottom boards of her own making — his hands stopped moving altogether.

The clock in the corner ticked eleven times before they resumed.

* * *

"Your landlady tells me you've taken evening walks," Halvard said, without turning from the files.

The pen did not falter. Noor had trained for that the way soldiers train for gunfire. "Marta keeps track of everyone's comings and goings. It's half of what she charges rent for."

"Where do you walk?"

"Along the embankment, mostly. Sometimes as far as the ferry ruins." A truth. Marta would have told him as much; there was no profit in contradicting a truth he could verify by looking out any window after dark. "The bridge work stops at sundown, and the river is quiet. I think better there."

"About the atlas?"

"About everything. The atlas most of all, lately. It's nearly finished, Inspector. Five years of finishing someone else's sentence. When it's done, I confess I don't entirely know who I'll be." She set down the pen and looked at him frankly. "Is that what you came for? To know whether grief walks along rivers? Because it does. It always has."

Halvard closed the file drawer. When he turned, his face had arranged itself into courtesy again, and somehow that was more frightening than suspicion.

"No," he said. "I came because a page is missing, Frøken Nystrom, and because missing pages are like missing people — they leave shapes behind them. And because eleven days ago, when I sat in that chair—" he nodded to it, "—you told me Elias received no visitors in his final year except Commission surveyors and a priest. Yet the ferryman's log from March of that year records a young man crossing twice under a name the ferryman couldn't spell and later admitted he'd been paid to forget."

The room became very still. Rain ticked against the window glass. Somewhere below, Marta's kettle began its thin whistle and was silenced.

"A young man," Noor said. "In a city of forty thousand."

"Tall. Dark-haired. A scar through the left eyebrow." Halvard lifted one of Elias's letters, considered it, set it down again with terrible gentleness. "Do you know anyone matching that description, Frøken Nystrom?"

Every instinct she owned screamed at once, a whole choir of them, and Noor stood in the middle of the noise and kept her face level by thinking of nothing at all — of gray water, of tide lines, of the blank space at the edge of charts where cartographers wrote *here be nothing* because admitting ignorance was safer than invention.

"I know a great many people," she said. "Half this city matches that description if you squint."

"Not half." Halvard picked up his gloves. "One. And he grew up on the far bank, and he was fourteen years old the last time the ferries ran, and someone taught him to fold a paper boat well enough to float a candle across a flooded millrace — because a fisherman found one in his nets two springs ago, with a pressed flower inside it, and thought it was a child's toy."

The floor tilted. Noor gripped the edge of the drafting table and felt the wood bite her palms, and was grateful for the pain, because it anchored her face.

"That was seven years ago," she said quietly. "Children do foolish things. Borders close. People forget."

"Do they?" Halvard asked. He put on his gloves, one finger at a time, watching her the whole while. "Because I've found the opposite. I've found that people remember everything — they simply arrange what they remember differently, depending on who's asking. You arrange yours very well, Frøken Nystrom. Better than anyone I've questioned in nine years of service."

"Inspector—"

"But." He took up his hat. "Arrangement isn't proof, and the Crown requires proof, and so we are both spared for the moment — you from arrest, and me from the embarrassment of being wrong. I will find the page, Noor Nystrom." It was the first time he had used her given name, and he used it the way a man sets down a card he intends to win the trick with later. "Papers want to be found. They get tired of hiding. Even the ones people burn — ash remembers its shape."

He settled the hat on his head, paused at the door, and glanced back at the drafting table, at the row of Elias's letters still laid out like a spread of evidence or a game abandoned mid-match.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the third pier. He was right about it. I've read the boring reports they buried. Whatever else your master was, he was honest in a town that punishes honesty — and if you're carrying anything of his, anything at all, you should ask yourself whether protecting it serves his memory or betrays it."

Then he was gone, boots measured on the stairs, the street door closing behind him with a click as soft as a chess clock being pressed.

* * *

Noor stood motionless until his footsteps faded, until Marta's floorboards above creaked with the particular rhythm of a woman crossing to her window, until the rain filled the silence again.

Then she went to the window herself and watched Halvard pause in the street below, hat brim streaming, face turned up toward the workshop windows for three full seconds — not searching, exactly. Memorizing.

She did not move away from the glass. Let him see a woman at a window, tired, ordinary, waiting out the weather.

When he finally walked off into the gray, she crossed to the drafting table and stood over the forty letters, and her hands, which had been steady for an hour under his eyes, began at last to shake.

*He knows.* Not everything. Not yet. But the shape of it — the scarred brow, the paper boat, the flower — he had gathered the pieces and laid them beside each other, and now he needed only the connecting line, the single stroke of proof that would draw the border around them both. The missing page. Ash remembers its shape.

Noor pulled the atlas's final plate from the flat file and carried it to the stove. Her fingers found the seam in the false backing by touch alone. The stolen page came out warm from hiding, Elias's hand marching across it — *third pier, soundings falsified, load tolerance exceeded by a factor the Commission cannot survive public knowledge of* — and for a moment she almost fed it to the coals then and there.

But her hands stopped.

Because Halvard had said one thing that mattered more than all his others, and he had probably known it would: *ask yourself whether protecting it serves his memory or betrays it.*

If she burned the page, she burned the flaw. If she burned the flaw, the bridge opened over a foundation of lies, and Elias had died — shot on the riverbank, they'd said, by smugglers, though no smugglers were ever named — for nothing at all.

But if she kept it, and Halvard found it, Quentin hanged.

Noor refolded the page along its year-old creases and slid it back into the dark behind the plate. There had to be a way to spend the truth without spending Quentin. Elias had believed maps could hold two truths at once — the coastline and the current, the road and the ravine beneath it. Somewhere in the master map he'd left her, in those dense marginalia she had only begun to decipher, there had to be such a passage.

She cleared the letters from the table, laid the master map out beneath the lamp, and began, line by coded line, to read what Elias had died trying to finish.

Outside, the rain thickened toward night, and somewhere across the black water, a lantern blinked twice and went dark.
