## 16. The Night of the Span

**Noor**

The fireworks began at dusk, and Noor counted them like heartbeats.

From the workshop window she could see the whole river burning in color — green, then gold, then a red so deep it looked like the water itself had caught fire. Below, the embankment was black with people, ten thousand of them pressed shoulder to shoulder to watch the first span of the great bridge bear its own weight and the weight of a locomotive besides. Bunting hung from every lamppost. A band played something martial and cheerful on a floating stage. Children sat on their fathers' shoulders with paper flags in their fists.

No one was looking at the river's dark edges. Everyone was looking up.

That was the whole design of the night, and Noor had built her plan inside it the way Elias had taught her to build a map: not by drawing what was there, but by understanding where the eye would refuse to go.

She dressed as a widow. It had been Marta's suggestion, offered with a face that gave nothing away — the black wool dress, the veil, the plain gloves. *No one remembers a mourner*, Marta had said, folding the dress into Noor's arms with hands that trembled only slightly. *They step around you. They look away.* Noor had understood what the trembling meant, and had said thank you anyway, because there was no other word left between them. Whatever Marta had done, she had a son across that border too, and tonight both of their boys were walking toward the same water.

In the false bottom of her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the atlas. Not all of it — only what mattered. The pages showing the third pier. The margin cipher. And beneath those, flat as a sin, the single sheet bearing Quentin Vasse's name in Elias's careful hand. She had promised herself she would burn it after. After was a country she intended to reach.

The last firework went up white, and for three seconds the whole city had no shadows at all.

Noor pulled down her veil and went out into the noise.

* * *

**Quentin**

Quentin stood in the crowd with his cap low and his hands in his pockets and let himself be carried forward by the current of bodies, because a man who moves against a crowd is remembered and a man who drifts is not.

He had crossed the city an hour before dark, wearing the foreman's coat he'd bought off a drunk for two crowns and a lie, and he had walked the length of the embankment twice, memorizing it the way Noor had once taught him to memorize coastlines — *not the shape, Quent, the logic; why the river bends where it bends* — back when they were fourteen and the world still had ferries. The lanterns along the rail were spaced too evenly. That was the flaw in the celebration, or the gift of it: light made regular becomes invisible. Only the gaps mattered. He had found four of them.

The first test span groaned above him now, a mile of iron taking the weight of a stationary engine, and the crowd gasped as one animal when the far end dipped a hand's width and held. Somewhere a priest was blessing it. Somewhere a mayor was lying about the future. Quentin felt the vibration come up through the cobblestones into his boots and thought: *that sound will be in my bones for the rest of my life, whichever side of the water I die on.*

Then he saw the mourner.

Black dress, black veil, moving along the rail with the unhurried gait of grief, and every instinct he owned stood up at once. Because he knew that walk. He had watched it for years across a schoolroom and a ferry deck and a garden wall — the walk of a person who has decided exactly where she is going and refuses to be hurried by anyone's opinion of it.

She passed within six feet of him and did not turn her head. Her gloved hand rose, adjusted her veil, and dropped again — but the fingers had spread, briefly, five points, then folded into a fist.

The fifth gap in the lanterns. The boathouse stairs.

Quentin waited until a rocket screamed overhead, and moved.

* * *

**Noor**

The boathouse ruins smelled of tar and old rope and the green rot of things the river had been chewing for years. Noor descended the broken stair with her skirt gathered in one fist, counting steps in the dark the way Elias had drilled her — *a mapmaker who cannot count in the dark is a decorator* — and at the seventh step she stopped and listened.

Water. Wind in the pilings. And behind her, soft as weather, footsteps that did not try to be silent, because silence in a deserted place is louder than noise.

"You're late," she whispered.

"I've been early for five years," Quentin said. "Tonight I decided to be punctual instead."

She turned. The fireworks lit the ruin through the broken roof in stutters — gold, gold, darkness — and in each stutter he was older. Broader through the shoulders, harder at the jaw, a workman's calluses where the boy's ink stains had been. But the eyes were the same eyes that had looked at her across a closing border seven years ago and refused, absolutely refused, to accept it.

"Noor." He said her name like a man setting down something heavy he'd carried a long way.

"Don't," she said, because her voice would break if he said anything else, and she could not afford a broken voice tonight. "Later. There's no later yet. Walk with me."

"The pier?"

"The pier."

She led him along the collapsed catwalk to the old slipway, where Elias's route began — a line no government survey had ever drawn, because it ran through ground that belonged to no one: the drowned foundations of the old ferry landing, exposed now by the summer's low water, a spine of stone and rotted piling running out toward the unfinished third pier like a causeway built by accident. At normal water it was impassable. Tonight, with the river drawn down and the whole city's attention aimed skyward, it was a door.

Elias had charted it in 1909, in a margin cipher Noor had needed four months to read, and beneath the chart he had written one line in plain ink, as if he'd known she would find it and wanted to speak to her across the years:

*A map is a promise that a way exists. Keep this one for someone who needs it.*

She had wept over that line exactly once, in March, alone, and then had gotten back to work.

"Stay behind my shoulder," she told Quentin. "Step where I step. If I stop, you stop. If I say run—"

"I know what run means, Noor."

"It means different things on water."

* * *

**Halvard**

Inspector Halvard stood at the rail of the official viewing platform, in uniform, holding a glass of the good wine he had no intention of drinking, and watched the crowd.

Not the bridge. The bridge was honest; the bridge would either hold or it wouldn't, and either way was an engineer's grief, not his. Crowds were where lies lived. He read them the way Nystrom's apprentice read charts — for the anomalies, the eddies, the places where the current moved against expectation.

There. A foreman's coat with no foreman under it — the shoulders wrong, the hands too clean at the cuffs. Moving north along the rail against the festival current, patient, unhurried, gone between one firework and the next.

Halvard set down his glass.

He had spent thirty days circling the Nystrom house like a slow planet, and everything he had learned confirmed what he already knew and proved nothing he could use. A missing page from Elias Nystrom's correspondence — page eleven, the one dealing with the river approaches — missing from the archive copy *and* from the master file, which meant someone with access had taken it twice, which meant it mattered. A landlady, Marta Soren, who reported faithfully and told him nothing true. An apprentice who answered every question correctly, which was itself an answer.

And a boy named Quentin Vasse, declared enemy of the crown at nineteen, who had vanished from every record on the day the borders closed, and whom Halvard had never seen and nonetheless recognized tonight on sight, from a photograph he should not have kept and had kept anyway, in a drawer, for reasons he declined to examine.

He descended the platform stairs without hurrying. Hurried men missed things.

At the rail he turned south, toward the boathouse quarter, away from the fireworks, into the part of the night nobody was watching.

* * *

**Noor**

The stones were slick and the river talked to them the whole way out — a long, liquid muttering under their feet, as if it knew what they were doing and found it amusing.

Twice the route required them to cross gaps where the old piling had rotted clean away, and both times Noor went first, testing with her heel, feeling for the submerged beam Elias had marked with a tiny anchor symbol and she had verified herself, twice, in daylight, with her heart in her throat. Behind her Quentin breathed in a controlled rhythm she recognized as fear being managed, not absent.

Halfway out, the fireworks stopped.

It was worse than any patrol. In the sudden ordinary darkness the river became enormous, and the far bank became a rumor, and Noor heard her own pulse in her ears like a second tide. Quentin's hand found her elbow — not gripping, just present — and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a prayer, and neither of them believed in prayers, only in maps.

Then the finale began: not single rockets but a rolling barrage, shell after shell, the whole sky tearing open in gold, and under that thunder and that light they ran.

The third pier rose out of the water ahead of them, half-built, a forest of timber legs and iron bones with scaffolding ladders going up its seaward face like stitches in a wound. The workmen's rotation was empty until midnight — Quentin had learned it, she had confirmed it, the foreman who suspected him had been given leave to attend the ceremony with his family, courtesy of a letter Noor had forged in Elias's most official hand. Every lock they'd need was opened by knowledge instead of keys: the loose board at the ladder's base, the gap in the staging where a section hadn't been planked yet, the crawl-space beneath the unfinished roadway where the ironwork made a tunnel just tall enough for a bent back and a held breath.

"Up," Noor whispered at the ladder. "You first. If anyone comes, I'm a mourner who got lost."

"And I'm?"

"A ghost. You've had practice."

He climbed. She followed. The pier swayed very slightly under them, alive, unfinished, and forty feet below the river slid past black and indifferent, carrying leaves and lamplight and, somewhere upstream, the reflected wreckage of ten thousand fireworks.

* * *

**Quentin**

Under the roadway, in the iron tunnel of the unfinished span, Quentin put his mouth close to Noor's ear and spoke the plan one final time, though they both knew it by heart, because saying it aloud was how they held each other without touching.

"We go to the end of the completed staging. The maintenance ladder drops to the towpath on the north side. The sentries are on the south approach — all eyes on the ceremony, all ears on the band. We cross the last eighty feet of finished girder above the water, we descend, we walk. You turn back at the midline. That was the agreement."

"That was your agreement."

"Noor—"

"I cross to the ladder," she said, in the voice she used on maps, the voice that did not negotiate with coastlines. "I see you onto the towpath. Then we'll discuss geography."

He almost laughed, and the laugh would have undone them both, so he swallowed it and took her hand instead — quickly, in the dark, where no one could witness it, not even the river. Her glove was off. Her hand was cold and certain and shaking.

"Five years," he said.

"Seven since the ferry. Five since the letter came about Elias." Her breath hitched, once, and steadied. "I finished the atlas, Quent. All of it. His coastline, his cipher, his — everything. It's done. Whatever happens tonight, that's done, and it's ours, and no one can unmake it."

"What's in it?"

"Everything they buried. But that's tomorrow's weapon. Tonight it's just paper, and paper doesn't float."

Above them, muffled by iron, the finale reached its crescendo — the sky hammering, the crowd roaring, the whole world celebrating a bridge while two people crawled through its skeleton like smugglers through a body.

They came out onto the final staging at 11:52 by Noor's watch. Ahead, the girder stretched out over open water to the point where finished met unfinished — a drop line marked on Elias's chart with a symbol Noor had spent weeks decoding: *the maintenance ladder, north face, fourth stanchion.* Beyond it, the far bank. Trees. Darkness. Another country.

Quentin went first, out along the narrow girder, arms out, the wind coming off the river cold enough to bite. Halfway across he stopped — Noor froze — and he knelt, and picked something up off the steel.

When he reached the ladder-head he pressed it into her palm.

A flower. Pressed flat, browned, fragile as ash. The one she had given him at fourteen, at the ferry railing, when the world was ending politely and they were the only two people who noticed. He had carried it through seven years of enmity, through a false name and a foreman's suspicion and every night of counting, and it had survived the way some things survive: by being kept against the heart where nothing could reach it.

"You'll want proof it was real," he said.

* * *

**Noor**

Later — much later, in the country of after she was only now beginning to believe in — she would remember the next minutes in fragments:

The ladder. Cold rungs, one after another, counted.

The towpath, wet gravel, the smell of the far bank's pines reaching across the water like a hand.

Quentin standing at the waterline, turning back, his face unreadable for one terrible second before it broke into the grin of the boy from the ferry — and then the shout behind her, and everything after happening at the speed of falling.

Because Inspector Halvard was standing at the top of the towpath stairs.

He had come quietly, alone, out of uniform under a civilian coat, and he stood now with his hat in his hand and his pistol holstered and his eyes going from Quentin to Noor to the river between them with the expression of a man watching a equation finish solving itself.

"Noor," he said. Just her name. Thirty days of courtesy compressed into it.

"Halvard." She stepped sideways, deliberately, placing herself between the inspector and the ladder. Behind her, below, she heard Quentin stop moving. "You're missing the fireworks."

"I saw what I needed to see." He came down the stairs slowly, boots loud on the wood, and stopped ten feet from her. "Page eleven, Frøken Nystrom. From Elias's correspondence. I have spent a month assuming you stole it because it implicated your household. I am no longer sure that's what it says at all."

The river filled the silence.

"Give me the atlas," Halvard said quietly, "and testify — not testimony against the boy. Testimony about whatever your master died documenting. Do that, and I walked the towpath tonight and found nothing but a lost mourner." His jaw tightened, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like an officer than like a man standing at the edge of his own map. "I loved you once, or near enough. Don't make me choose between what I feel and what I'm ordered to do. Give me something worth disobeying for."

Noor's hand closed around the flower in her pocket, and around the satchel strap, and around the answer.

"Come to the hearing," she said. "I'll give you something better than the atlas. I'll give you the truth — all of it — and where it points won't be where your superiors think."

Behind her, soft as a leaf on water, she heard Quentin slip into the dark of the far bank.

Halvard heard it too. He closed his eyes for the length of one long breath. When he opened them, he looked at her the way men look at bridges they have decided not to burn.

"Thursday," he said. "Nine o'clock. Bring everything."

He turned and climbed the stairs, and Noor stood alone on the towpath with the atlas against her ribs and a dead flower in her fist, listening to the last fireworks die over a bridge that had, tonight, finally begun to carry people across.
